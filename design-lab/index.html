The Berlin Blockade

In June 1948, Stalin gambled that he could starve the Western powers out of Berlin. Within a year, the gamble had hardened the very division it was meant to prevent.

When the Western Allies merged their zones and introduced the Deutsche Mark, Stalin saw a direct threat to Soviet control of eastern Germany. His response was to cut every road, rail and canal route into West Berlin — a city of two million people stranded a hundred miles inside the Soviet zone.

Rather than withdraw or risk war, the Allies chose the air. For eleven months, a plane landed in West Berlin roughly every ninety seconds, delivering coal, food and even chocolate dropped by parachute to waiting children.

Key fact
At its peak, the airlift delivered nearly 13,000 tonnes of supplies in a single day — more than had previously arrived by road.

Why it mattered

The blockade failed, but it changed the Cold War. It convinced the West that the USSR had to be contained by force of will, accelerated the formation of NATO, and made the division of Germany permanent.

"We refused to be forced out of the city of Berlin."

— President Harry S. Truman, 1949
◆ Exam technique

For a 12-mark "consequences" question, rank your points: lead with NATO's formation, then the permanent division of Germany, then the propaganda victory. Examiners reward a clear hierarchy of significance.
