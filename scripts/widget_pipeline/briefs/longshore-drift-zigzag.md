# Widget brief: Longshore drift moves sediment via repeated zigzag, not a straight current

Cluster id: `longshore-drift-zigzag`  |  serves 5 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Longshore drift results from swash moving sediment up-beach at an angle and backwash pulling it straight back, causing a zigzag net movement along the coast.

> UK Coastlines: A Quick Map The UK has more than 11,000 miles of coastline. Hard-rock erosional coasts dominate the west and north (granites of Cornwall, sandstones of Scotland), while soft-rock depositional or unstable coasts are common in the east and south (Holderness, the Suffolk coast). Wave action, geology, climate and human management all shape these landscapes — sometimes faster than people realise. Two wave types matter: destructive waves are tall, frequent and have strong backwash, eroding the coast; constructive waves are flatter, less frequent and have strong swash, building up beaches. Storm conditions usually bring destructive waves to UK coasts. > A Coast Is a Conveyor Belt UK coasts look fixed but they are constantly moving. Waves, weather and gravity break rock from cliffs, longshore drift carries the pieces along the shore, and beaches and spits build where the energy drops. The same trio — erosion, transport and deposition — that shapes rivers also shapes coasts. The difference is that the energy comes from waves, tides and wind rather than gradient and discharge. Key Fact Coasts erode in four main ways: hydraulic action (water force compresses air in cracks), abrasion (sand and pebbles scrape the cliff), attrition (load knocks together and rounds), and solution (slightly acidic seawater dissolves rock). > The Coast as a System A coastline is a working system. Energy from waves, wind and tides arrives at the shore and reshapes it constantly. The geographer’s job is to track where the energy comes from, what it does to the rock and sediment, and where the material ends up. Three families of process do almost all the work: weathering , erosion , and deposition . Between erosion and deposition sits transport, which simply moves sediment from one place to another. Waves: Constructive and Destructive Waves are the engine of the coast. Constructive waves are low and long. Their swash (the rush up the beach) is strong, while the backwash is weak, so they push sediment up the beach and build it up. Destructive waves are tall and steep.
