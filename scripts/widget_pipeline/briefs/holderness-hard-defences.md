# Widget brief: Hard coastal defences shift problems elsewhere

Cluster id: `holderness-hard-defences`  |  serves 4 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Protecting one stretch of coast with hard defences can starve neighbouring areas of sediment and increase erosion there, so 'protecting the coast' is not uniform or consequence-free.

> Britain's Fastest-Eroding Coast The Holderness coast in East Yorkshire stretches 61 km from Flamborough Head in the north to Spurn Head in the south. It is the fastest-eroding coastline in Europe. The cliffs are made almost entirely of boulder clay — a soft, glacially-deposited mixture of clay, sand and gravel that slumps easily when saturated. The coast retreats at an average of 1.8 metres per year, and in places more than 10 metres in a single storm. Around 30 villages have been lost to the sea since Roman times. Second, the coast faces north-east and has a very long fetch across the North Sea, so it receives big destructive waves — especially during winter storms with strong easterly winds. > Why People Want to Defend the Coast Around 17 million people in the UK live within 10 km of the coast. They have homes, businesses, ports and farmland to lose. Where coastal erosion eats into the land at speed — or where storm flooding threatens low-lying ground — communities demand defence. The challenge is that defending one stretch of coast often damages another, and the cheapest defences are not always the best. Hard engineering tries to stop the sea. ### 3. [geography-edexcel-b] Coastal Management & Conflict — `/lesson/geography-edexcel-b/uk-geographical-issues/3`
Triage: Students think coastal management is a local, isolated problem: protect *this* village, done. They don't see that sediment, waves and erosion form a connected system where defending one place damages the next.

> A Rising Risk Around 5 million people in England live in places at risk of flooding from the sea or rivers, and the figure is rising. The Holderness coast loses about 2 metres a year. The villages of Happisburgh in Norfolk and Fairbourne in Wales are slated for managed retreat. Climate change is making the threat worse: the UK’s sea level has risen by about 17 cm since 1900 and is projected to rise a further 30–110 cm by 2100. Storms are also becoming more frequent and more powerful. Decisions about whether and how to protect each stretch of coast are now among the most urgent in UK geography. First, rising sea levels from melting ice sheets (Greenland and Antarctica) and the thermal expansion of warmer ocean water.
