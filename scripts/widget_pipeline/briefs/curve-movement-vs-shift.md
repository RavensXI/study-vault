# Widget brief: Movement along vs shift of a curve

Cluster id: `curve-movement-vs-shift`  |  serves 3 lessons

_Merged from audit clusters demand-curve-movement-vs-shift + supply-movement-vs-shift (same misconception, two curves)._

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

A change in the good’s own price causes a movement ALONG the existing demand or supply curve; only a non-price factor (incomes, tastes, costs, technology, taxes) shifts the whole curve to a new position. "Demand increases when price falls" confuses the two: quantity demanded changes, the curve itself has not moved. One widget must serve both curves — treat demand and supply as data for the same mechanism.

> What Is Demand? In economics, demand does not simply mean wanting something. It means being both willing and able to buy it. A teenager who wants a new games console but has no money is not contributing to demand in the economic sense &mdash; there is no &ldquo;effective demand&rdquo; unless the desire is backed by the purchasing power to act on it. Economists focus on this distinction because markets respond to spending, not wishes. Demand is always for a good or service at a particular price, over a particular time period. When economists talk about &ldquo;the demand for petrol&rdquo;, they mean the total quantity all consumers are willing and able to purchase at each possible price, holding all other factors constant. This set of price&ndash;quantity relationships is captured in a demand schedule and drawn as a demand curve . ### 2. [economics-aqa] Supply — `/lesson/economics-aqa/how-prices-are-determined/2`
Triage: Students think that a movement along the supply curve and a shift of the supply curve are the same thing, or cannot distinguish which change in conditions causes which.

> What Is Supply? Supply is the amount of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at each possible price. Notice those two words: &ldquo;willing&rdquo; means producers want to sell, and &ldquo;able&rdquo; means they can actually produce and deliver it. A farmer who wants to sell 1,000 tonnes of wheat but can only harvest 200 tonnes is &ldquo;able&rdquo; to supply just 200 tonnes, regardless of willingness. Supply is always measured over a specific time period &mdash; for example, the number of loaves a bakery can supply per week, or the number of cars a manufacturer can produce per month. Supply and price are closely linked: in most markets, the higher the price, the more producers want to supply. The Law of Supply The law of supply states that there is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied, all other things being equal. ### 3. [economics-aqa] Market Equilibrium & the Price Mechanism — `/lesson/economics-aqa/how-prices-are-determined/3`
Triage: Students think that 'demand increases' when price falls, rather than understanding that price changes cause movements *along* the curve, while only non-price factors shift the curve itself.

> Where Price Comes From Every price you see &mdash; the cost of a litre of petrol, a pint of milk, a streaming subscription &mdash; is the result of demand and supply pushing against each other until they settle. That settling point is called the equilibrium price . At this price, the market &ldquo;clears&rdquo;: every seller who wants to sell at that price finds a buyer, and every buyer who wants to buy at that price finds a seller. The matching quantity is called the equilibrium quantity . Think of a street market selling fresh strawberries. The stall holder sets out 200 punnets each morning. If customers are willing to buy exactly 200 punnets at, say, &pound;2 each, the market clears perfectly. The price is at equilibrium. But markets rarely sit perfectly still &mdash; prices and quantities are always being nudged by new information, changing tastes and shifting costs.
