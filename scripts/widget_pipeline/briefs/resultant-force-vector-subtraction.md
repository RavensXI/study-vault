# Widget brief: Resultant force as vector sum, not simple addition

Cluster id: `resultant-force-vector-subtraction`  |  serves 4 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Forces in opposite directions subtract (vector combination) rather than just adding magnitudes together.

> Resultant Forces and Newton&rsquo;s First Law A resultant force is the overall force on an object once all individual forces are combined. Forces are vectors, so direction matters: two forces of 10 N in opposite directions give a resultant of zero; two forces of 10 N in the same direction give a resultant of 20 N. Newton&rsquo;s first law (Sir Isaac Newton, 1643&ndash;1727, published in Principia Mathematica , 1687) states that an object will remain at rest or continue to move at a constant velocity unless a non-zero resultant force acts on it. In other words: no resultant force means no change in motion. This is also why inertia &mdash; an object&rsquo;s resistance to change in motion &mdash; increases with mass. Non-contact forces include gravitational, electrostatic, and magnetic forces. Key Fact A force is an interaction between two objects. When two objects interact, each exerts a force on the other — these are equal in size and opposite in direction (Newton’s third law). > Newton&rsquo;s First Law Newton&rsquo;s First Law states that an object remains at rest or continues to move at constant velocity unless acted upon by a resultant force . This means: &bull; If the resultant force is zero , a stationary object stays still and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction. &bull; If there is a resultant force , the object accelerates &mdash; which means it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. A spacecraft drifting in deep space continues at the same velocity indefinitely because there is no friction or air resistance to produce a resultant force. A book on a table remains stationary because the normal contact force from the table (upward) exactly balances its weight (downward).
