# Widget brief: Piaget's stages represent qualitatively different reasoning, not just age categories

Cluster id: `piaget-qualitative-stages`  |  serves 4 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Each Piagetian stage represents a fundamentally different, more sophisticated way of reasoning about the world, not simply a set of age-linked facts to memorise.

## The lessons it will sit in

### 1. [psychology-aqa] Piaget's Theory of Cognitive Development — `/lesson/psychology-aqa/development/2`
Triage: Students think the four stages are simply age-based categories with different facts to memorize, rather than progressively more sophisticated ways of reasoning that fundamentally change how a child interprets the same situation.

> Why Do Children Think Differently? Have you ever tried to explain something straightforward to a young child, only to find they seem to be reasoning from a completely different set of rules? The Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget argued that this was not a question of children knowing less &mdash; it was a question of children thinking differently. His Theory of Cognitive Development, first published in the twentieth century, proposed that intelligence itself develops in stages, each qualitatively different from the last. Piaget&rsquo;s approach was distinctive because he did not just measure what children knew; he investigated how they reasoned. By observing his own children and later running systematic experiments, he identified a pattern of development that he believed was universal &mdash; applying to all children, regardless of culture or background. > Piaget in the Classroom Piaget&rsquo;s theory was not just an academic description of how children think &mdash; it had direct implications for education. If children at different ages are genuinely incapable of certain types of reasoning, then teaching them content that requires that reasoning is pointless. Piaget&rsquo;s key educational message was: match what you teach to the stage of thinking the child has actually reached. This influenced how schools are designed and how teachers plan lessons. Primary school classrooms with hands-on materials, practical activities and discovery learning all reflect Piaget&rsquo;s insight that children at the concrete operational stage learn best through interaction with real objects, not through abstract explanation alone.
