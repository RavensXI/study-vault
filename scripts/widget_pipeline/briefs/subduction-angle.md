# Widget brief: Subduction angle misconception

Cluster id: `subduction-angle`  |  serves 2 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

A subducting plate descends at a shallow angle while remaining in contact with the overriding plate, rather than sinking straight down like a stone in water.

## The lessons it will sit in

### 1. [geology-eduqas] Plate Boundaries and Their Products — `/lesson/geology-eduqas/structures-and-earth-dynamics/6`
Triage: Students picture subduction as a plate sinking straight down like a stone in water, rather than descending at a shallow angle while remaining in contact with the overriding plate, and don't grasp why this geometry makes the Benioff zone earthquakes deepen with distance from the trench.

> Three Kinds of Plate Boundary Tectonic plates interact along their edges, and the type of interaction depends on the direction of relative plate motion. There are three fundamental boundary types: divergent plate boundaries , where plates move apart; conservative plate boundaries , where plates slide past each other; and convergent plate boundaries , where plates move toward each other. Each type produces a distinct set of geological structures, magma types, and landforms. Key Fact Divergent: new crust created, basaltic magma (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift). Conservative: no crust created or destroyed, volcanic activity absent or minimal (San Andreas Fault). Convergent: crust destroyed by subduction, andesitic or granitic magma (Andes, Himalaya). ### 2. [geography-aqa] Plate Margins & Why People Stay — `/lesson/geography-aqa/paper-1/2`
Triage: Students think plate margins are simple line diagrams where plates either move apart, together, or sideways—missing what actually happens in 3D space: where material goes, what friction feels like, why water trapped in a descending plate causes explosions.

> Three Types of Plate Margin A plate margin is where two tectonic plates meet. There are three main types, and each one produces a different combination of hazards. Understanding what is happening at the boundary is the first step to predicting what hazards a place will face. Constructive Margins At a constructive margin two plates pull apart. Magma rises through the gap, cools and creates new crust — which is why these are also called divergent boundaries. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs the length of the Atlantic Ocean, splitting it open at about 2 cm a year. Iceland sits directly on top of this ridge, which is why it has so many active volcanoes despite being in the middle of the Atlantic. Eruptions here tend to be effusive : runny basalt lava flows steadily rather than exploding. Earthquakes are usually shallow and small, because the plates are sliding apart smoothly.
