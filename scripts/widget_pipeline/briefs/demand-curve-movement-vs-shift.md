# Widget brief: Movement along vs shift of the demand curve

Cluster id: `demand-curve-movement-vs-shift`  |  serves 2 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

A change in price causes a movement along the existing demand curve, while only non-price factors cause the whole curve to shift.

## The lessons it will sit in

### 1. [economics-aqa] Demand — `/lesson/economics-aqa/how-prices-are-determined/1`
Triage: A student thinks that when price changes, the demand curve itself moves (shifts), rather than understanding that only the position on the existing curve changes.

> What Is Demand? In economics, demand does not simply mean wanting something. It means being both willing and able to buy it. A teenager who wants a new games console but has no money is not contributing to demand in the economic sense &mdash; there is no &ldquo;effective demand&rdquo; unless the desire is backed by the purchasing power to act on it. Economists focus on this distinction because markets respond to spending, not wishes. Demand is always for a good or service at a particular price, over a particular time period. When economists talk about &ldquo;the demand for petrol&rdquo;, they mean the total quantity all consumers are willing and able to purchase at each possible price, holding all other factors constant. ### 2. [economics-aqa] Market Equilibrium & the Price Mechanism — `/lesson/economics-aqa/how-prices-are-determined/3`
Triage: Students think that 'demand increases' when price falls, rather than understanding that price changes cause movements *along* the curve, while only non-price factors shift the curve itself.

> Where Price Comes From Every price you see &mdash; the cost of a litre of petrol, a pint of milk, a streaming subscription &mdash; is the result of demand and supply pushing against each other until they settle. That settling point is called the equilibrium price . At this price, the market &ldquo;clears&rdquo;: every seller who wants to sell at that price finds a buyer, and every buyer who wants to buy at that price finds a seller. The matching quantity is called the equilibrium quantity . Think of a street market selling fresh strawberries. The stall holder sets out 200 punnets each morning. If customers are willing to buy exactly 200 punnets at, say, &pound;2 each, the market clears perfectly. The price is at equilibrium.
