# Widget brief: Microbial growth factors interact, not independent

Cluster id: `spoilage-factors-interact`  |  serves 2 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Food, moisture, warmth and time interact to enable microbial growth (e.g. warmth only matters with moisture present), rather than acting independently.

## The lessons it will sit in

Triage: A student thinks the four growth factors (food, moisture, warmth, time) act independently, and doesn't grasp that they interact—e.g., that warmth only matters if moisture is present, or that time is meaningless without the other three simultaneously satisfied.

> What Are Microorganisms? Every time you leave a bowl of leftover chicken curry on the worktop overnight, you are giving microorganisms exactly what they need to multiply at speed. Microorganisms are invisible to the naked eye, but their effects &mdash; a furry grey patch on a bread loaf, a fizzing punnet of strawberries, the sour smell of yoghurt left too long &mdash; are very familiar. The three types you need to know are bacteria , yeasts and moulds . Each behaves differently and responds to conditions differently. Bacteria are single-celled organisms that can divide roughly every 20 minutes under ideal conditions &mdash; meaning one bacterium can become millions in just a few hours. Yeasts are single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding and are responsible for fermentation (the process that makes bread rise and wine alcoholic). > Why Food Spoils Every time you leave a bag of salad too long in the fridge or forget about a loaf of bread on the counter, you are watching food spoilage in action. Food spoilage happens because of four main agents: enzyme action, mould growth, yeast activity and bacterial growth. Understanding why each agent acts &mdash; and how to slow it down &mdash; is central to safe food storage and food waste reduction. Enzymes are biological catalysts naturally present in all plant and animal tissue. After a fruit or vegetable is harvested, its own enzymes continue to work, breaking down cell walls, changing colour and softening texture. A sliced apple turning brown is classic enzymic browning : the enzyme polyphenol oxidase reacts with oxygen in the air, turning the cut surface brown.
