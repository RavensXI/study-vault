# Widget brief: SA:V ratio physically limits exchange

Cluster id: `sa-v-ratio-real-limit`  |  serves 4 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Surface area to volume ratio is a real physical constraint on diffusion rate, not just an abstract number, and explains why larger organisms need specialised exchange systems.

## The lessons it will sit in

### 1. [separate-sciences-ocr] Transport: Diffusion, Osmosis and Active Transport — `/lesson/separate-sciences-ocr/biology-paper-1/5`
Triage: Students think that as an organism gets bigger, it needs MORE surface area and MORE exchange capacity, when in fact the ratio of surface area to volume DROPS, creating a bottleneck that passive transport cannot overcome.

> Moving Substances Into and Out of Cells Cells need to transport substances across their membranes to survive. There are three main mechanisms: diffusion , osmosis and active transport . Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration — it is passive (no energy needed). Oxygen diffuses into cells for respiration; carbon dioxide and urea diffuse out. The steeper the concentration gradient , the faster the rate of diffusion. Temperature and surface area also affect the rate — higher temperature means faster-moving particles and more frequent collisions. Osmosis is specifically the movement of water molecules through a partially permeable membrane from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated solution (low water potential). ### 2. [separate-sciences-edexcel] Exchange, Transport and the Circulatory System — `/lesson/separate-sciences-edexcel/biology-paper-2/7`
Triage: Students think surface area to volume ratio is an abstract number that doesn't affect real physiology, or they picture it as a simple ratio rather than understanding why small objects have relatively MORE surface per unit volume.

> Surface Area to Volume Ratio Single-celled organisms can exchange substances directly through their cell membrane because their surface area to volume ratio (SA:V ratio) is large. As organisms grow larger, their volume increases faster than their surface area, and the SA:V ratio falls. A small cube has a higher SA:V ratio than a large cube. For a large, active organism, the surface area of the body wall alone cannot exchange gases or nutrients fast enough &mdash; specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems are therefore essential. Key Fact As organisms get larger, the surface area to volume ratio decreases. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces (e.g. alveoli, villi) and transport systems (e.g. blood) to supply all cells with oxygen and nutrients efficiently. ### 3. [science-ocr-b] Transport, Circulation and Exchange Surfaces — `/lesson/science-ocr-b/biology-paper-2/4`
Triage: Students think SA:V ratio is an abstract number that doesn't physically affect how fast substances can enter cells, or they believe larger organisms just need 'more' of everything rather than fundamentally different mechanisms.

> Why Size Changes Everything A single-celled organism like an amoeba gets all the oxygen it needs simply by diffusion across its cell surface. No circulatory system required. But a blue whale &mdash; the largest animal ever to have lived &mdash; needs a massive heart, kilometres of blood vessels, enormous lungs and elaborate exchange surfaces in its intestines. The fundamental reason is mathematics: as organisms grow, their surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) decreases. The volume (and therefore the number of cells needing supplies) grows faster than the surface area available for exchange. Consider cubes: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm 2 and a volume of 1 cm 3 &mdash; SA:V ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm 2 and volume of 8 cm 3 &mdash; SA:V ratio of only 3:1. A 3 cm cube has SA:V of 2:1.
