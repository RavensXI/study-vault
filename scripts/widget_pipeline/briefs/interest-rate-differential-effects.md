# Widget brief: Interest rate changes affect groups unevenly and with delay

Cluster id: `interest-rate-differential-effects`  |  serves 2 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Interest rate changes propagate unevenly and with time lags depending on mortgage type and decision-making, not uniformly and instantly to everyone

## The lessons it will sit in

### 1. [economics-aqa] Interest Rates, Saving, Borrowing & Investment — `/lesson/economics-aqa/national-economy-and-government-objectives/1`
Triage: Students think interest rate changes affect all households and businesses equally and immediately, not realizing that the delay, type of mortgage, and investment decision-making create different lag times and magnitudes of impact.

> What Is an Interest Rate? An interest rate is the price of borrowing money. When you take out a loan or use a credit card, the lender charges you interest &mdash; a percentage on top of the amount you borrowed. Equally, when you put money into a savings account, the bank pays you interest as a reward for depositing your money with them. Interest rates are expressed as an annual percentage. For example, if you save &pound;1,000 at an interest rate of 5% per year, after one year you will have earned &pound;50 in interest, giving you &pound;1,050. If you borrow &pound;1,000 at a rate of 10% per year, you owe &pound;100 in interest on top of repaying the original loan. The higher the interest rate, the more expensive borrowing becomes &mdash; and the more rewarding saving becomes. ### 2. [economics-aqa] Monetary Policy — `/lesson/economics-aqa/government-policy-and-the-global-economy/2`
Triage: Students think interest rate changes affect the economy immediately and uniformly, rather than understanding the staggered chain of effects (mortgages → disposable income → spending → demand → prices) that unfolds over 1–2 years.

> What Is Monetary Policy? Monetary policy is operated by the Bank of England&rsquo;s Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), which is independent of the government. The government (HM Treasury and the Chancellor) sets the inflation target &mdash; 2% CPI &mdash; but the decision on interest rates is made solely by the MPC, not by politicians. In the United Kingdom, it is therefore the Bank of England that carries out monetary policy, not the government. The Bank does not aim simply to make profit &mdash; its primary job is to keep inflation low and stable so that households and firms can plan with confidence. The key instrument of monetary policy is the bank rate , sometimes called the base rate or official bank rate.
