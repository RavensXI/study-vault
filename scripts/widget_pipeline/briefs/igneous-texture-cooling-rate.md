# Widget brief: Grain size is a cooling-rate record

Cluster id: `igneous-texture-cooling-rate`  |  serves 2 lessons

## The one thing the widget must make concrete

Crystal size in an igneous rock is direct EVIDENCE of cooling rate: slow cooling deep underground grows large crystals; fast cooling at the surface freezes small ones; two-stage cooling gives porphyritic texture. The misconception is that coarse/fine are mere descriptive labels, or that crystal size depends on "how much time the magma had" in some vague sense disconnected from depth and setting. The rock is a thermometer you can read backwards.

> Five Routes to a Mineral Minerals do not simply exist &mdash; they form by specific physical or chemical processes. Understanding how a mineral formed tells you a great deal about the environment in which it grew: temperature, pressure, the presence of fluids, and the chemistry available at the time. There are five main formation routes covered by your specification: crystallisation from a melt, metamorphic recrystallisation, evaporation, precipitation from pore waters, and deposition from hydrothermal fluids. Crystallisation from a Melt When molten rock &mdash; magma &mdash; cools, atoms lose energy and begin to join together in a fixed geometric pattern, forming crystals. The size of the resulting crystals depends almost entirely on how quickly the melt cools. Slow cooling deep underground allows atoms to migrate over long distances, producing large, well-formed crystals. > Igneous Rocks and How They Form Igneous rocks form when magma cools and solidifies. They can form deep underground, where slow cooling produces coarse-grained rocks, or at the surface where rapid cooling gives fine-grained or glassy textures. Your specification focuses on four igneous rock types: peridotite, basalt, andesite and granite. You must be able to classify these from their colour, crystal size and mineral content &mdash; the three properties tested most frequently in hand-specimen identification questions. Texture: Grain Size and Its Meaning Texture is the single most important initial observation when classifying an igneous rock. Coarse-grained rocks have crystals large enough to see with the naked eye (generally above 1 mm). Fine-grained rocks have crystals too small to see individually without a hand lens.
