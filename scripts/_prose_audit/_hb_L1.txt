Britain in the 1980s: The World of the Play The History Boys is set in 1983 in a comprehensive sixth form in Sheffield, northern England. The boys have just received their A-level results and are preparing for Oxbridge entrance exams. To understand the play, you need to understand the political and educational landscape that shaped it — and shaped Alan Bennett himself. Margaret Thatcher became Prime Minister in 1979, and her government transformed British education. Thatcher championed competition, league tables, and measurable outcomes. Schools were judged not by the quality of learning but by the results they produced. The Headmaster in the play embodies this philosophy entirely: he sees Oxbridge places as “trophies” and the boys as products to be polished for the market. His line “I am thinking of league tables ” is a direct reference to the results-driven culture that Thatcher accelerated. Key Fact The Headmaster represents Thatcher’s education philosophy: competition, measurable outcomes, and league-table success. He values Oxbridge places as institutional prestige, not as genuine educational achievement for the boys themselves. He tells Irwin: “I want results.” Grammar Schools vs Comprehensives A crucial tension in the play is the idea of selective education . Before comprehensives were widely introduced in the 1960s and 1970s, children sat the eleven-plus exam at age 11. Those who passed went to grammar schools; those who failed went to secondary moderns, which were widely seen as inferior. Bennett himself was a product of this system — a working-class boy from Leeds who passed the eleven-plus and eventually reached Oxford. The boys in The History Boys attend a state school in Sheffield, an industrial northern city. Their very attempt to get into Oxford or Cambridge is remarkable because Oxbridge was (and to some extent still is) dominated by privately educated students. Irwin later observes that Oxbridge remains a monument to social inequality — institutions that have historically favoured the wealthy. The boys are competing against candidates from Eton, Harrow, and Winchester who have been groomed for these interviews since childhood. Bennett’s Own Background Alan Bennett was born in 1934 in Leeds to a working-class family. His father was a butcher. Bennett won a scholarship to Exeter College, Oxford, where he studied history. This experience — a northern, state-educated boy entering a world of privilege — directly informs the play. Like the History Boys, Bennett felt the tension between genuine intellectual curiosity and the performative cleverness needed to impress at interview. He later described his education as “getting on” rather than “getting wisdom,” a distinction that echoes through Hector’s teaching philosophy. Bennett has spoken openly about feeling like an outsider at Oxford, surrounded by students whose confidence came from social position rather than intellectual merit. Mrs Lintott’s sardonic observation — “History is not a good career for women” — reflects Bennett’s broader awareness that Oxbridge was designed to serve a narrow group: male, southern, privately educated, and wealthy. Oxbridge and Class The Oxbridge entrance exam system in the 1980s was very different from today’s. Candidates sat a separate entrance exam and attended interviews at specific colleges. The interview process was notoriously subjective — interviewers could select on the basis of “potential” or even personality, which inevitably favoured candidates from privileged backgrounds who had been coached in the art of performing intelligence. Irwin understands this system perfectly: he teaches the boys not to think more deeply, but to perform depth. His advice is always about surface: “be different,” “take a contrary view,” “torture the evidence.” Hector, by contrast, teaches as if Oxbridge does not exist. His “general studies” lessons — filled with poetry, film, and music — have no instrumental purpose. He tells the boys: “All knowledge is precious whether or not it serves the slightest human use.” This puts Hector in direct opposition to both the Headmaster’s utilitarianism and Irwin’s performativity. For Hector, education is about enriching the soul, not winning a place at a prestigious university. Key Fact Hector’s philosophy — “All knowledge is precious whether or not it serves the slightest human use” — directly opposes the Headmaster’s results-driven approach. This tension between intrinsic and instrumental education is the play’s central conflict. Why Context Matters for Eduqas The Eduqas Component 2 exam assesses AO4 (context) alongside AO1 (response to text) and AO2 (language, form, structure). Context in The History Boys is not background decoration — it is embedded in every scene. The Headmaster’s obsession with league tables, Hector’s resistance to instrumental education, Mrs Lintott’s marginalisation as a female teacher, and the boys’ class-based disadvantage are all products of 1980s Britain. Embedding Context in Your Answers In the Eduqas exam, never bolt context on as a separate paragraph. Instead, weave it into your analysis. Rather than writing “In the 1980s, Thatcher introduced league tables,” connect it to a quotation: “The Headmaster’s fixation on ‘results’ reflects Thatcher’s education reforms, which reduced schools to league-table positions and students to statistics.” Context should explain why Bennett wrote the play this way, not just describe what life was like. Bennett wrote The History Boys in 2004, looking back at the 1980s from a distance of twenty years. This means the play is both a portrait of 1983 and a retrospective commentary on how Thatcher’s reforms shaped modern education. The epilogue, which reveals the boys’ adult careers, reinforces this double perspective — we see what education actually gave them, not just what it promised.
