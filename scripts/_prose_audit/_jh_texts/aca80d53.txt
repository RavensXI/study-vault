Language, Structure and Narrative Perspective

Language: Key Techniques Stevenson’s language is precise and purposeful. Every word choice contributes to the novella’s atmosphere of mystery, horror and moral unease. Animalistic Imagery Hyde is consistently described using animalistic imagery . He moves with “ape-like fury,” is called “troglodytic” (cave-dwelling), and hisses “like a cornered snake.” This language links Hyde to degeneration theory — the Victorian fear that humans could reverse-evolve into primitive creatures. By making Hyde animal-like, Stevenson suggests that evil is not a moral failing but a biological reality lurking inside everyone. The Language of the Unspeakable Characters repeatedly struggle to describe Hyde. Enfield says he is “not easy to describe” and gives “a strong feeling of deformity.” Utterson notes “deformity without any nameable malformation.” This technique of describing something by saying it cannot be described is called apophasis . It makes Hyde more terrifying because the reader must imagine the horror for themselves. This linguistic vagueness also reflects Victorian society’s inability to confront evil directly. The characters can sense that Hyde is wrong, but their respectable vocabulary has no words for what they are feeling. Key Fact Stevenson uses pathetic fallacy extensively. London’s fog, darkness and gaslight mirror the moral confusion and hidden evil at the heart of the story. The city itself becomes a character — concealing, distorting and threatening. Structure: Three Narratives The novella’s structure is one of its most distinctive features. It uses three different structural layers: Chapters 1–8: Third-person narrative focused on Utterson. The reader shares his limited perspective, piecing together clues alongside him. Chapter 9: Lanyon’s first-person letter. A shift to direct testimony from someone who witnessed the transformation. Chapter 10: Jekyll’s first-person “full statement.” The final revelation, told by the man at the centre of the mystery. Why This Structure Matters Each narrative layer reveals more of the truth. Utterson sees the outside; Lanyon witnesses the transformation; Jekyll explains everything. This structure mirrors the peeling away of a disguise — the deeper we go, the closer we get to the horrifying reality beneath the respectable surface. The use of epistolary elements (letters and written statements) is also significant. The truth can only be told in writing, after death. In Victorian society, some truths were literally unspeakable — they could only be committed to paper and sealed away. Key Fact The novella ends with Jekyll’s statement, not with Utterson’s reaction. The reader never learns what happens after Jekyll’s death. This open ending forces the reader to draw their own conclusions about the meaning of the story. Narrative Perspective and Reliability Stevenson carefully controls what the reader knows and when. Utterson is a reliable narrator in the sense that he reports honestly what he sees. But his perspective is limited — he cannot understand what is happening because it is beyond rational explanation. Jekyll, by contrast, is an unreliable narrator . Even in his “full statement,” he distances himself from Hyde (“He, I say — I cannot say I”) and minimises his own responsibility. The reader must decide how much to trust Jekyll’s version of events. Gothic Conventions in the Novella Stevenson draws on several Gothic conventions : The doppelgänger — Hyde is Jekyll’s dark double, a common Gothic motif representing the hidden self. The mad scientist — Jekyll, like Frankenstein, conducts an experiment that creates a monster beyond his control. The discovered manuscript — The truth is revealed through found documents (Lanyon’s letter, Jekyll’s statement), a technique used by Gothic writers to create a sense of authenticity. Atmosphere of dread — Fog, darkness, locked doors and screaming winds create a persistent sense of menace.
