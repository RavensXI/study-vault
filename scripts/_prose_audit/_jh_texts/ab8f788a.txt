Chapters 1-4: Mystery & Terror

Chapter 1: The Door and Enfield’s Story The novella opens with two respectable gentlemen, Mr Utterson (a lawyer) and his kinsman Mr Enfield, walking through London. They pass a sinister door on a rundown street, and Enfield tells a strange story: he once witnessed a man “calmly trampl[e] over a child’s body” at a street corner. The man was Hyde. The crowd surrounded him, and Hyde paid compensation through a cheque signed by the respectable Dr Jekyll. Enfield’s reaction to Hyde is visceral: “I had taken a loathing to my gentleman at first sight.” Stevenson uses mystery as his primary structural device. The reader, like Utterson, is given pieces of a puzzle without the full picture. We know Hyde is connected to Jekyll but not how. We know the door leads to Jekyll’s laboratory but not why Hyde uses it. The first four chapters build a web of second-hand narration : Enfield tells Utterson, Utterson investigates, but nobody speaks to Jekyll directly. This narrative structure mirrors the Victorian culture of secrecy — gentlemen do not ask uncomfortable questions. Key Fact Hyde tramples a young girl “calmly” — without remorse or hesitation. The word “calmly” is crucial: it shows that Hyde’s violence is not passionate rage but cold indifference to human suffering. He treats the child as though she does not exist. This establishes Hyde as a figure without empathy or conscience — the purely selfish part of human nature unleashed. Chapter 2: Utterson’s Investigation Utterson reads Jekyll’s will and discovers that everything is left to Hyde — including the provision that if Jekyll “disappears for any period exceeding three calendar months,” Hyde inherits. This alarming clause suggests either blackmail or something worse. Utterson visits Dr Lanyon, Jekyll’s old friend, who dismisses Jekyll’s recent scientific work as “unscientific balderdash.” The disagreement between the two doctors hints that Jekyll has crossed a moral boundary that Lanyon refuses to follow. Utterson’s First Sight of Hyde Utterson stakes out the sinister door and finally meets Hyde. His reaction is the same indefinable revulsion that every character experiences: “Mr Hyde was pale and dwarfish, he gave an impression of deformity without any nameable malformation.” Hyde produces a “disgusting” impression that Utterson cannot explain: “God bless me, the man seems hardly human! Something troglodytic.” The word troglodytic links Hyde to prehistoric humanity, reinforcing the degeneration theme. Notice that characters consistently cannot describe what is wrong with Hyde. This is Stevenson’s deliberate technique: by making Hyde’s evil indefinable, he makes it universal. Hyde does not look like a specific type of villain — he embodies a feeling of wrongness that everyone recognises but nobody can articulate. In the exam, this is a powerful AO2 point about Stevenson’s language choices. Chapter 3: Jekyll’s Reassurance Utterson confronts Jekyll, who dismisses his concerns: “I can be rid of Mr Hyde… whenever I choose.” This is deeply ironic because the reader later learns that Jekyll is already losing control. Jekyll’s confidence is the confidence of an addict who believes he can stop at any time. Stevenson uses proleptic irony throughout: statements that seem reassuring on first reading become ominous once the truth is revealed. Chapter 4: The Carew Murder Hyde’s violence escalates dramatically. He murders Sir Danvers Carew, a respected MP, in a frenzied attack witnessed by a maidservant: “Mr Hyde broke out of all bounds and clubbed him to the earth. And next moment, with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim underfoot and hailing down a storm of blows, under which the bones were audibly shattering.” The language is extraordinarily violent: “ape-like fury,” “trampling,” “bones audibly shattering.” Stevenson moves from the cold trampling of a child to a savage murder, showing Hyde’s escalating loss of control. The Significance of Carew’s Murder Carew is described as “an aged beautiful gentleman with white hair” who approaches Hyde “with a very pretty manner of politeness.” The contrast between Carew’s gentleness and Hyde’s savagery is absolute. Carew represents everything that Victorian respectability values: age, beauty, courtesy, and public service. Hyde’s destruction of him symbolises the assault of the primitive, selfish self on civilised values. The maidservant who witnesses the murder does so by moonlight — she is at an upstairs window, looking down. This vertical perspective (observer above, crime below) mirrors the structure of Jekyll’s house and Victorian society itself: respectability lives upstairs, while violence happens in the streets below. Key Fact The Carew murder is described with escalating violence: “with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim underfoot and hailing down a storm of blows, under which the bones were audibly shattering.” “Ape-like” links to degeneration, “trampling” echoes the child incident (showing escalation), and “bones audibly shattering” uses sound imagery to make the violence disturbingly real.
