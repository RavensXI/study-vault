Setting, Structure & Narrative

London as a Gothic Landscape Stevenson transforms London into a character in its own right. The city in Jekyll and Hyde is fog-choked, gas-lit, and full of shadows. The most famous description captures this perfectly: “The fog still slept on the wing above the drowned city.” The personification of fog as a sleeping creature hovering over a “drowned” city creates an oppressive, suffocating atmosphere. London is not just a backdrop — it is a Gothic space where truth is obscured, identities are hidden, and evil thrives in the gaps between respectability. The fog serves a dual purpose. Literally, it obscures visibility — characters cannot see clearly, just as they cannot see the truth about Jekyll. Symbolically, it represents the moral fog of Victorian society, where appearances matter more than reality. When the fog lifts briefly during the Carew murder, the moonlight reveals the violence that normally remains hidden. Stevenson suggests that moments of clarity are rare and often horrifying. Key Fact Stevenson uses pathetic fallacy throughout the novella. Fog, darkness, wind, and shifting light mirror the characters’ psychological states and the moral darkness that pervades the story. When Utterson visits Hyde’s rooms in Soho after the Carew murder, the district is shrouded in a “great chocolate-coloured pall” of fog — the darkness of the setting reflects the darkness of Hyde’s actions. The Door: Threshold Between Two Worlds The sinister door in Chapter 1 is one of the novella’s most important symbols. It is “equipped with neither bell nor knocker” and is “blistered and distained.” It faces a busy street but belongs to a neglected, windowless wall. This door is the back entrance to Jekyll’s house — the entrance Hyde uses. The front of Jekyll’s house, on a respectable square, is grand and welcoming. The contrast is the novella in miniature: a polished public facade concealing a decaying, secret interior. The door functions as a threshold between Jekyll’s public and private selves. When characters pass through it, they cross from the respectable world into the hidden one. Hyde enters and exits through this door; Jekyll never uses it publicly. The physical separation of the two entrances mirrors the psychological separation Jekyll attempts between his two identities — a separation that ultimately fails. The Locked Cabinet: Jekyll’s Final Prison Jekyll’s cabinet — the private room connected to his laboratory — becomes his prison in the final chapters. He locks himself inside, refusing visitors, desperately trying to prevent Hyde from emerging in public. The locked door that once protected Jekyll’s secret now traps him. When Poole and Utterson break down the door with an axe, they are literally breaking through the barrier between the public and private worlds. What they find inside — Hyde’s body, not Jekyll’s — confirms that the secret has consumed the man who created it. The cabinet also contains a mirror. Jekyll describes looking into it and seeing Hyde’s face staring back. The mirror is a classic Gothic motif — the doppelgänger confronting the self. The mirror forces Jekyll to acknowledge that Hyde is not someone else but a version of himself. This moment of recognition is central to the novella’s meaning: you cannot escape your own nature by giving it a different name. Contrasting Spaces: Cavendish Square vs Soho Stevenson uses London’s real geography to reinforce his themes. Jekyll’s house is in a wealthy, respectable area (loosely based on Cavendish Square). Hyde’s lodgings are in Soho — a district associated in the Victorian imagination with poverty, crime, and vice. The two addresses mirror Jekyll’s dual nature: the respectable doctor lives in the grand square, while his dark alter ego inhabits the dangerous slum. When Utterson visits Hyde’s Soho rooms after the murder, he finds a disturbing mixture of luxury and squalor: good paintings, fine wine, and silver cutlery alongside ransacked drawers and a pile of burnt papers. Even Hyde’s private space reflects duality. The elegance comes from Jekyll’s taste; the chaos comes from Hyde’s nature. Stevenson reinforces the point that duality exists everywhere — not just within people but within the spaces they inhabit. Narrative Structure: The Mystery Form Stevenson structures the novella as a mystery with delayed revelation. For the first eight chapters, the reader shares Utterson’s perspective and investigates alongside him. The truth is not revealed until Chapters 9 and 10, in two embedded documents: Lanyon’s letter and Jekyll’s confession. This structure means the reader experiences the same shock of discovery as the characters — though modern readers usually know the twist in advance, the structure still shapes how we understand the themes. Key Fact Stevenson uses three narrative perspectives: third-person (Chapters 1–8, focused on Utterson), Lanyon’s first-person letter (Chapter 9), and Jekyll’s first-person confession (Chapter 10). Each narrator gets progressively closer to the truth but is also progressively more damaged by it. Utterson never fully understands; Lanyon understands but dies; Jekyll understands completely but is destroyed. The structure implies that complete knowledge of human duality is incompatible with survival. Embedded Narratives and Sealed Letters The novella is full of documents that contain and control information: Jekyll’s will, Hyde’s forged letter, Lanyon’s sealed letter, and Jekyll’s full statement. These embedded narratives function as textual locked rooms. The truth is literally sealed away, only to be opened after death or disappearance. This mirrors Victorian society’s approach to uncomfortable truths: contain them, lock them up, and only confront them when there is no alternative. The sealed letters also create suspense — the reader knows that crucial information exists but must wait for it to be revealed. Form: The Novella Stevenson chose the novella form deliberately. At around 25,000 words, it is tightly compressed — there is no padding, no subplots, and very little description that does not serve the central theme. The brevity intensifies the claustrophobic atmosphere. Every detail matters: the description of a door, a hand, a piece of fog. Stevenson reportedly wrote the first draft in three days, then burned it and rewrote it in another three. The result is a narrative as concentrated and dangerous as Jekyll’s potion itself.
