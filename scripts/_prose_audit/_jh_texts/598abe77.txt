Chapters 1-4: Mystery & Terror

Chapter 1: The Story of the Door Stevenson opens with the lawyer Mr Utterson and his cousin Enfield walking in a “by-street” in London. The contrast is deliberate: the street is well-kept, “clean”, lively — except for one “sinister” building with “a blistered and distained” door. Stevenson uses the Gothic technique of the uncanny: a single wrong detail in an ordinary scene makes the reader suspicious of everything around it. Key Fact The neglected door is Stevenson’s first symbol of duality. Opening the novella with a building rather than a person signals that the whole story will be about hidden rooms — literal and psychological. Enfield tells the story of a “small man” who “trampled calmly” over a young girl and gave him “a look so ugly it brought out the sweat on me like running.” The repeated word “trample” stays with the reader — the violence is described less in physical detail than in the witnesses’ reactions. Stevenson uses this reflected horror to make Hyde feel more disturbing than any explicit description could. Chapter 2: Search for Mr Hyde Utterson, disturbed by the story, reads Jekyll’s will: on Jekyll’s death or “disappearance” of more than three months, everything is to go to “Edward Hyde.” The legal language — precise, formal — makes the strangeness worse. Utterson begins to dream of Hyde “crushing” children, then sets out to hunt him by the door on Bye Street. When they finally meet, Hyde is described in strikingly unstable terms. Utterson says: “God bless me, the man seems hardly human! Something troglodytic, shall we say?… he gives a strong feeling of deformity, although I couldn’t specify the point.” Stevenson deliberately refuses detail. Hyde is felt rather than seen. This indirect characterisation echoes Victorian anxieties about degeneration and the unspeakable inside the respectable. The ‘Hide and Seek’ pun Stevenson puns on Utterson’s name (“he will be Mr Seek”) and on Hyde’s (“to hide”). The playfulness is deliberate: under the surface of every Victorian gentleman is something hidden. Stevenson was writing in 1886, the year after W. T. Stead’s “Maiden Tribute” exposé revealed widespread sexual exploitation by respectable London men. The theme of public respectability concealing private vice was everywhere. Chapter 3: Dr Jekyll was Quite at Ease Two weeks later Utterson dines at Jekyll’s and raises the will. Jekyll’s refusal is polite but final: “the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr Hyde.” The claim is the first lie. Stevenson lets the reader hear it as confident reassurance and only later realise it is false. Utterson is presented as the rational Victorian gentleman — lawyer, discreet, loyal — the kind of man readers were invited to trust with the story. Chapter 4: The Carew Murder Case The novella’s first moment of clear violence comes with Sir Danvers Carew’s murder. A servant girl watches from a moonlit window as Carew is “trampled” and “clubbed… with ape-like fury” by Hyde. Stevenson’s simile — “ape-like” — draws on Victorian degeneration theories. Darwin’s Descent of Man (1871) had shaken confidence that humans were truly separate from animals. The setting amplifies the horror: a full moon, a silent street, a respected MP killed for no reason. Stevenson chooses the victim carefully. Carew is an “aged” gentleman with “silvery hair” — the symbol of Victorian respectability. Hyde’s violence is not crime but sacrilege. Key Fact Stevenson uses three key phrases in the Carew murder: ‘trampled’, ‘ape-like fury’ and ‘bones were audibly shattered’. Each word strips away civilised language and pushes Hyde closer to the animal. Narrative structure: the gathering of voices Stevenson builds these chapters through embedded narration : Enfield tells Utterson, who reads the will, who hears Poole, who hears the maid. No one has the whole story. The readers have to assemble it — a deliberate technique that mirrors the novella’s theme: nothing is as single or stable as it appears. The delayed revelation is also a narrative suspense machine. We suspect a link between Jekyll and Hyde long before it is confirmed. Stevenson’s 1886 readers, reading the novella in a small red shilling edition, would have experienced this as a shock. Modern readers, knowing the twist, can analyse how the structure creates the possibility of that shock. Why These Chapters Matter The first four chapters teach the reader how to read the whole novella. Stevenson trains us to distrust surfaces: the tidy street with one wrong door; the respectable Jekyll with one dubious beneficiary; the quiet night with a moonlit murder. The doubling that will later be explicit is already present in the structure.
