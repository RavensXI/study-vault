Chapters 9-10: The Revelations

Chapter 9: Dr Lanyon’s Narrative Stevenson finally delivers the confirmation. Lanyon’s sealed letter is a first-person account of a single terrible night. He receives a desperate note from Jekyll asking him to go to the cabinet and retrieve a drawer of chemicals. At midnight, a “small” stranger calls for the drawer. Lanyon describes him in now-familiar terms: “something abnormal and misbegotten… something seizing, surprising, and revolting.” This is the first time we meet Hyde through Lanyon’s professional, scientific eyes. Key Fact Stevenson withholds the transformation until Chapter 9 so that the reveal lands on a scientific, rational character (Lanyon) whom the reader has been taught to trust. The horror is not that Hyde changes — it is that a man of medicine watches it happen. Hyde drinks the prepared potion. Lanyon describes what follows in stunned clauses: “he reeled, staggered, clutched at the table… and before my eyes — pale and shaken… there stood Henry Jekyll!” The dashes and staccato verbs mimic Lanyon’s shattered narration. Stevenson uses the sentence rhythm to stage the horror in real time. The Cost of Knowledge Lanyon writes: “my life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me… I cannot, even in my own thoughts, dwell on it without a start of horror.” The knowledge kills him — a direct echo of the Faust story, where the scholar who seeks forbidden knowledge is damned. Stevenson’s warning is moral: some truths are not survivable for those committed to empirical medicine . Chapter 10: Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case The final chapter is Jekyll’s own long confession. Stevenson shifts voice entirely — the controlled, professional tone of Lanyon gives way to Jekyll’s restless self-examination. Jekyll explains that he was born with “duplicity of life”: a respectable surface and appetites “foreign” to it. He describes his scientific research and the creation of a drug that “could cast off the lower elements” of his personality. The first transformation brings “incredibly sweet” freedom. As Hyde, Jekyll feels “younger, lighter, happier in body”, and experiences a “solution of the bonds of obligation.” Stevenson is careful: the freedom is pleasurable, which makes the moral warning more interesting. If evil felt only terrible, nobody would pursue it. Key Fact Jekyll’s central claim is that ‘man is not truly one, but truly two’. He believed he could separate the good and evil selves — but the evil, given its own body, grows stronger while the good weakens. The drug reveals the truth of duality but cannot manage it. The Loss of Control Gradually Jekyll loses the ability to return. After the Carew murder he vows never to take the drug again — but wakes in a park one morning as Hyde without taking it. Stevenson dramatises the addiction : Jekyll’s “powers seemed shaken”, the drug stops working reliably, and the original supply of the salt runs out. What saved him no longer exists. The final transformation is involuntary. Jekyll’s confession ends with an extraordinary narrative move: he describes his own impending death in present tense. “This, then, is the last time… that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts or see his own face… in the glass.” Stevenson uses the last-words framing to make the reader complicit: we are reading a note that ends with the writer’s death. Duality and Victorian Anxiety Late-Victorian Britain was obsessed with doubles: the respectable husband who kept a mistress, the clergyman with a secret life, the civilised man who was also an animal. Stevenson crystallises this fear in one body. The novella was published in 1886, a year of high public tension about hidden vice, and the story became popular immediately. Jekyll’s confession reads like a Victorian nightmare turned inside out: the gentleman does the evil, not a stranger. Modern criticism often reads Hyde as a figure for repressed Victorian sexuality, addiction, or class anxiety. Stevenson himself called the book ‘a fable’ — inviting interpretation. A strong essay treats the text as open to several readings rather than a puzzle with a single key. Structure and Effect Stevenson ends with a written confession because writing is what makes the story survivable. Jekyll’s words are preserved even as he dies; Utterson reads them and gains knowledge that Lanyon could not. The reader is a second Utterson, piecing the evidence into a whole. Structurally, the novella moves from street-level rumour to professional lawyer to scientific eyewitness to the subject’s own pen. Each stage gets closer to the truth — and each stage loses more of the person being described. Why the Ending Matters The ending is tragic rather than triumphant. There is no cure, no moral redemption, only a confession. Stevenson refuses the comfort of exorcism: Jekyll dies, Hyde dies, but the double remains as a lesson. The novella closes not with peace but with the reader’s new suspicion — of the doctors, gentlemen and lawyers who seemed so solid in Chapter 1.
