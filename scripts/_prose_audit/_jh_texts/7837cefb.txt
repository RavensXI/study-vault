Chapters 9-10: The Revelations

Chapter 9: Dr Lanyon’s Narrative Lanyon’s letter, opened after his death, reveals what killed him. Jekyll (as Hyde) sent Lanyon instructions to collect chemicals from the laboratory. When Lanyon witnesses Hyde drink the potion and transform into Jekyll before his eyes, the shock destroys him: “My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me; the deadliest terror sits by me at all hours.” Lanyon’s testimony gives the reader the first direct description of the transformation — and confirms that Jekyll and Hyde are the same person. Stevenson delays this revelation until Chapter 9 of 10 — a structural choice that maximises impact. The reader has spent eight chapters constructing theories (blackmail? murder? a secret relative?), and the truth is more disturbing than any of them. Lanyon’s horror is not just at the physical transformation but at its implications: if a respectable doctor contains a murderer, what might anyone contain? The truth about duality is literally lethal to the Victorian mind. Key Fact Stevenson delays the truth until Chapter 9, making the reader experience the same process of discovery as the characters. This delayed revelation maximises shock: eight chapters of mystery resolve into a truth more disturbing than any theory. Lanyon’s death proves that the Victorian world cannot survive the knowledge of its own duality. Chapter 10: Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case Jekyll’s confession is the novella’s final chapter and its richest for analysis. He describes his life before the experiment: “I was in no sense a hypocrite; both sides of me were in dead earnest.” He was genuinely good and genuinely drawn to vice. The experiment did not create evil — it separated it from the restraints of conscience. Hyde was “pure evil” because he was Jekyll without the moral part: “Edward Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil.” Jekyll’s Confession: Key Quotations Jekyll describes his initial pleasure in being Hyde: “I felt younger, lighter, happier in body… I knew myself, at the first breath of this new life, to be more wicked, tenfold more wicked… and the thought, in that moment, braced and delighted me like wine.” The language of pleasure and intoxication (“lighter,” “delighted,” “wine”) reveals that Jekyll enjoyed being Hyde. He does not present himself as a victim of his experiment but as someone who chose to indulge his darkest impulses and found it addictive. Hyde is smaller than Jekyll because, as Jekyll explains, “the evil side of my nature… was less robust and less developed than the good.” This is because Jekyll had spent his life exercising virtue and suppressing vice. But once released, evil grows: “I began to be tortured with throes and longings, as of Hyde struggling after freedom.” The metaphor of addiction is unmistakable — Hyde is a craving that intensifies with every indulgence. The Failure of the Experiment Jekyll’s experiment fails for a specific reason: he cannot create a potion that separates the good side. The transformation only works one way — it releases evil but cannot isolate virtue. Jekyll explains: “Had I approached my discovery in a more noble spirit… all must have been otherwise.” He acknowledges that his motives were selfish from the start — he wanted freedom from moral responsibility, not genuine self-improvement. The novella argues that you cannot escape moral accountability by splitting yourself in two. Jekyll’s Death and Hyde’s Suicide Jekyll’s final transformation is permanent because the original salt that powered the potion was impure — he cannot replicate it. He runs out of the crucial ingredient and knows that the next transformation will be his last. His statement ends with the knowledge that “this is the last time… that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts.” When Utterson breaks down the door, he finds Hyde dead by poison. Jekyll and Hyde die together because they are, and always were, one person. The suicide is significant: Hyde chooses death rather than face justice. Even Hyde’s final act is selfish — he kills himself to avoid consequences, not out of remorse. Jekyll’s tragedy is that he created something he could not control and could not undo. The novella ends not with moral redemption but with destruction — a warning, not a comfort. Key Fact Hyde is smaller than Jekyll because “the evil side of my nature was less robust and less developed than the good.” Jekyll has spent years exercising virtue, so evil is stunted. But once released, Hyde grows stronger: the novella argues that evil is not a fixed quantity but a force that expands with indulgence. Repression does not destroy evil — it only stores it.
