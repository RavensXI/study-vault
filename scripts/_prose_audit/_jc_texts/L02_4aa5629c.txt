The Art of Persuasion: Cassius and Brutus Acts 1 and 2 are dominated by a single, devastating act of persuasion: Cassius convincing Brutus to join the conspiracy against Caesar. Shakespeare presents Cassius as a skilled manipulator who understands exactly which emotional buttons to press. Brutus is honourable, idealistic, and deeply committed to the republic. Cassius exploits every one of these qualities. In Act 1, Scene 2, Cassius begins by flattering Brutus, telling him that many ‘groaning underneath this age’s yoke’ wish that Brutus could see his own worth. He then plants the idea that Caesar is no better than them: ‘I was born free as Caesar; so were you.’ He tells a story about Caesar nearly drowning in the Tiber and crying ‘Help me, Cassius, or I sink!’ — reducing the mighty Caesar to a weak, dependent figure. The anecdote may be exaggerated or invented, but it serves its purpose: it makes Caesar seem undeserving of supreme power. Key Fact Cassius uses three key tactics: flattery (‘many of the best respect in Rome’ look to Brutus), belittlement of Caesar (the Tiber story, Caesar’s fever in Spain), and an appeal to Brutus’s ancestry (his ancestor Lucius Junius Brutus expelled the last Roman king). Each tactic targets Brutus’s specific values. ‘The Fault, Dear Brutus’ Cassius delivers one of the play’s most famous lines: ‘The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.’ This is a powerful piece of rhetoric . Cassius rejects the idea that fate controls human destiny — instead, he argues that people are responsible for their own positions. If Brutus and Cassius are ‘underlings’, it is because they have allowed Caesar to rise above them. The implication is clear: they have the power to act. Notice how Cassius frames this as a shared problem (‘ourselves’, ‘we’). He positions himself alongside Brutus as an equal, not as someone issuing orders. This collaborative language is crucial to Cassius’s success — Brutus would never take instructions, but he will respond to an appeal between honourable equals. The Forged Letters Cassius does not rely on persuasion alone. In Act 1, Scene 2, he reveals in soliloquy that he will throw forged letters through Brutus’s window, written ‘in several hands’ (different handwriting) as if from multiple Roman citizens begging Brutus to act against Caesar. This is deception, not honest persuasion. Cassius admits he is exploiting Brutus’s ‘honourable mettle’ — his very honour makes him vulnerable to manipulation. The soliloquy reveals Cassius’s true, calculating nature beneath the surface of republican idealism. The forged letters work. In Act 2, Scene 1, Brutus reads one and is convinced: ‘Shall Rome stand under one man’s awe?’ Shakespeare shows how propaganda can push even honourable people into extreme action. Brutus’s Soliloquy: The Orchard Scene Act 2, Scene 1 opens with Brutus alone in his orchard at night, unable to sleep. His soliloquy reveals the torment of a man trying to justify murder through reason. He admits Caesar has not yet acted tyrannically: ‘to speak truth of Caesar, / I have not known when his affections swayed / More than his reason.’ Caesar has been rational and fair. But Brutus argues that power itself is dangerous: ‘It is the bright day that brings forth the adder, / And that craves wary walking.’ This serpent metaphor is crucial. Brutus compares Caesar to a snake that might become venomous once it has full power. He decides they must ‘kill him in the shell’ — destroy the threat before it matures. The reasoning is entirely hypothetical: Caesar might become a tyrant, so they must act now. Shakespeare invites the audience to question whether this logic is sound or whether Brutus is rationalising a decision already made under Cassius’s influence. Key Fact Brutus’s soliloquy admits Caesar has done nothing wrong yet. His argument is preventative: Caesar might become dangerous, like a serpent in the egg. This hypothetical reasoning is the moral weakness of the conspiracy — they kill a man for what he might do, not what he has done. The Soothsayer and Omens In Act 1, Scene 2, a soothsayer calls out from the crowd: ‘Beware the Ides of March.’ Caesar dismisses him as a ‘dreamer.’ This moment establishes one of the play’s central motifs: characters are warned repeatedly but fail to listen. Caesar’s dismissal shows his arrogance and perhaps his belief that he is above mortal danger. The audience, who already know how the story ends, experience powerful dramatic irony . Portia’s Plea In Act 2, Scene 1, Brutus’s wife Portia confronts him about his disturbed behaviour. She kneels before him and pleads: ‘Within the bond of marriage, tell me, Brutus, / Is it excepted I should know no secrets / That appertain to you?’ Her argument is that marriage should be a partnership of equals, not a relationship where the husband hides his inner life. To prove her strength, Portia reveals she has deliberately wounded herself in the thigh: ‘I have made strong proof of my constancy, / Giving myself a voluntary wound / Here, in the thigh.’ This shocking act demonstrates her willingness to endure pain and her refusal to be dismissed as a weak woman. Shakespeare uses Portia to challenge Elizabethan gender expectations — she insists on being treated as Brutus’s equal, not his subordinate. Portia and Calpurnia: A Comparison Shakespeare pairs Portia and Calpurnia as wives who try to influence their husbands. Both are ultimately ignored. Portia demands to share Brutus’s burden; Calpurnia begs Caesar to stay home after her prophetic dream (Act 2, Scene 2). Both women show insight and emotional intelligence that their husbands lack, yet both are overruled by male pride and political pressure. Portia acts with physical courage (the self-inflicted wound), while Calpurnia appeals through fear and intuition. Neither can prevent the tragedy, suggesting that in this political world, women’s voices — however wise — are silenced.
