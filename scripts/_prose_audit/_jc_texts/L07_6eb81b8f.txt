Theme: Power and Ambition Power is the engine that drives every relationship and every conflict in Julius Caesar . The central question — was Caesar a tyrant or a great leader? — is never definitively answered. Caesar has been appointed ‘dictator perpetuo’, which horrifies the republican senators, but the people love him and he has refused the crown three times (though Casca notes he was ‘loath to lay his fingers off it’). Shakespeare shows that power corrupts in every direction. Caesar’s power breeds arrogance (‘I am constant as the northern star’). The conspirators’ power over life and death corrupts their moral reasoning (killing on the basis of what Caesar might do). Antony’s rhetorical power becomes a weapon that destroys innocent lives (Cinna the Poet). The triumvirate’s political power produces the proscription list. The play argues that power itself — regardless of who holds it — tends toward violence and injustice. Key Fact Power corrupts across the play: Caesar’s political power breeds hubris, the conspirators’ power over life and death leads to murder based on hypothetical danger, Antony’s rhetorical power unleashes mob violence, and the triumvirate’s power produces cold-blooded proscription. Shakespeare suggests that the problem is not who holds power but the nature of power itself. Theme: Honour and Loyalty Honour is the most contested word in the play. Brutus kills Caesar because he believes it is the honourable thing to do for Rome. Cassius manipulates Brutus by appealing to his sense of honour. Antony weaponises the word by calling the conspirators ‘honourable men’ until it becomes an insult. By the play’s end, Antony restores the word’s sincere meaning: ‘This was the noblest Roman of them all.’ Loyalty is tested throughout. Brutus is loyal to Rome over his friend Caesar. Cassius is loyal to himself more than to the republic. Antony is loyal to Caesar’s memory but not to moral principles (the proscription list). Portia is loyal to Brutus to the point of self-destruction. Shakespeare presents loyalty as admirable but dangerous — misplaced loyalty destroys as effectively as betrayal. Theme: Manipulation and Rhetoric Almost every character in the play manipulates someone else through language. Cassius manipulates Brutus into joining the conspiracy (flattery, forged letters, appeals to ancestry). Decius manipulates Caesar into going to the Senate by reinterpreting Calpurnia’s dream. Antony manipulates the crowd into rioting against the conspirators. Even Brutus manipulates himself — his orchard soliloquy is an exercise in self-persuasion, talking himself into murder through hypothetical reasoning. Shakespeare presents demagoguery as the play’s most dangerous force. A skilled speaker can make murder seem like patriotism (Brutus), turn grief into a weapon (Antony), or disguise jealousy as principle (Cassius). The play warns that in a world where rhetoric is power, the truth is whatever the most persuasive speaker says it is. Theme: Fate vs Free Will The tension between fate and free will runs through every act. Cassius explicitly rejects fate: ‘The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings.’ He insists that human beings control their own destiny. But the play’s omens — the soothsayer’s warning, the storm, Calpurnia’s dream, Caesar’s ghost — suggest that some events are predetermined. Shakespeare does not resolve this tension. Caesar has the choice to stay home but chooses to go to the Senate. Brutus has the choice to reject Cassius but chooses to join the conspiracy. Every character exercises free will, but the outcomes seem fated. This ambiguity is deliberate — Shakespeare invites the audience to decide whether the characters are masters of their fate or merely actors playing parts already written. Key Fact Free will evidence: ‘The fault is not in our stars, but in ourselves’ (Cassius), Caesar choosing to go to the Senate despite Calpurnia’s pleas, Brutus deciding to join the conspiracy. Fate evidence: the soothsayer’s prediction comes true, Calpurnia’s dream is fulfilled literally, Caesar’s ghost appears at Philippi announcing doom. Shakespeare leaves it to the audience to decide. Exam Technique: The Extract + Whole Play Essay The Shakespeare question typically gives you a printed extract of around thirty lines and asks you to write about how Shakespeare presents a theme or character in the extract and in the play as a whole . Plan your time so that you leave enough space for a sustained, developed response — check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact timing and mark weighting. The exam is closed book — the extract is printed for you, but you must know the rest of the play from memory. This means you need to memorise key quotations from across the entire play, not just the famous scenes. Your response is assessed on how well you use the text to build an argument, your analysis of Shakespeare’s language, form and structure, your use of context, and your accuracy of spelling, punctuation and grammar. Structuring Your Essay: A Step-by-Step Method Step 1 — Introduction (2–3 sentences): State your argument clearly. What is Shakespeare doing with this theme/character? Give a thesis that runs through your whole essay. E.g. ‘Shakespeare presents power as inherently corrupting, showing that the assassination merely replaces one form of tyranny with another.’ Step 2 — The Extract (2–3 paragraphs): Analyse specific quotations from the extract. For each quotation: identify the technique, analyse the effect on the audience, and embed context. Use the PEA-Z method: P oint, E vidence (quotation), A nalysis (language/structure), Z oom in (word-level analysis) + context. Step 3 — The Whole Play (2–3 paragraphs): Extend your argument to other parts of the play. Use memorised quotations from different acts. Track how the theme/character develops across the play — this shows sophisticated understanding. Connect back to your thesis. Step 4 — Conclusion (2–3 sentences): Return to your thesis. What is Shakespeare’s overall message about this theme/character? How does the ending of the play reinforce or complicate that message? Key Quotations to Memorise These quotations are versatile — each can be used for multiple themes and characters: ‘Beware the Ides of March’ — fate, omens, Caesar’s arrogance ‘Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look’ — Caesar’s perception, Cassius’s ambition, power ‘The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves’ — fate vs free will, manipulation, rhetoric ‘Cowards die many times before their deaths; the valiant never taste of death but once’ — courage, Caesar’s philosophy, hubris ‘Et tu, Brute? — Then fall, Caesar’ — betrayal, loyalty, acceptance of fate ‘Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears’ — rhetoric, manipulation, power of language ‘This was the noblest Roman of them all’ — honour, Brutus’s tragedy, moral complexity ‘Let me have men about me that are fat’ — political awareness, Caesar’s character, suspicion
