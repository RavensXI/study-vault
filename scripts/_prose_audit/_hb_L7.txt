Bennett’s Language: Why Dialogue Matters The History Boys is a play driven by dialogue. There is very little physical action: no fights, no chases, no dramatic entrances. The drama comes from what characters say and how they say it. Bennett gives each character a distinctive register that reveals their values, their intelligence, and their relationship to education. Analysing these different registers is essential for AO2 (language, form, structure) in the Eduqas exam. The play’s language is also densely comic. Bennett is one of Britain’s finest comic writers, and The History Boys is packed with wit, wordplay, and irony . But the comedy always serves a purpose: it reveals character, advances argument, and disarms the audience before delivering emotional or moral blows. Understanding how Bennett uses comedy is as important as understanding the serious themes. Key Fact Bennett gives every character a distinctive language register: Hector speaks in literary quotation and metaphor; Irwin uses rhetorical questions and provocative imperatives; the Headmaster uses corporate jargon; Mrs Lintott uses dry, epigrammatic wit; the boys switch between irreverent slang and the literary language Hector has taught them. These registers reveal values as clearly as actions. Hector’s Literary Register Hector rarely speaks in his own voice. Instead, he communicates through quotation: Hardy, Larkin, Housman, Auden, and the dialogue of old films. When he describes the experience of reading, he says: “The best moments in reading are when you come across something — a thought, a feeling, a way of looking at things — that you’d thought special, particular to you. And here it is, set down by someone else.” This is one of the few moments where Hector speaks genuinely in his own voice, and it is revelatory: his deepest belief is that literature connects isolated individuals. Hector’s habit of speaking through others’ words is itself revealing. He cannot express his own feelings directly — his sexuality, his loneliness, his sense of failure — so he channels them through poetry and song. His teaching method is also his coping mechanism . When he tells the boys “Pass the parcel. That’s sometimes all you can do,” the metaphor carries personal weight: Hector is passing on the literature that sustained him because he has nothing else to give. Irwin’s Performative Language Irwin’s language is the opposite of Hector’s. Where Hector quotes, Irwin commands. His typical speech acts are imperatives (“Torture the evidence,” “Distance yourselves”) and rhetorical questions (“What is truth?”). His vocabulary is drawn from television and marketing: “good television,” “ratings,” “selling it.” He never quotes poetry. He never refers to art or music. His language reveals a mind that is sharp, strategic, and completely devoid of emotional depth. Irwin’s most characteristic technique is the volte-face : he sets up an expected conclusion, then swerves. He tells the boys their essays are “competent” — then adds “but dull.” This linguistic structure mirrors his teaching method: take the obvious position, then reverse it. His language is his method. Bennett makes Irwin’s dialogue structurally performative, so that even the way he constructs a sentence reflects his approach to history. The Boys’ Language: Irreverence and Fluency The boys are extraordinarily code-switchers . In Hector’s lessons, they recite poetry and perform film scenes with genuine emotion. In Irwin’s lessons, they deploy contrarian arguments with confident fluency. With each other, they use profanity, sexual innuendo, and the rapid-fire banter of teenage boys. This linguistic flexibility is itself a product of their education: Hector gave them cultural literacy; Irwin gave them rhetorical skill; their own intelligence gives them the ability to move between registers at will. Dakin’s language is particularly revealing. He uses the vocabulary of military conquest and sexual possession: “I’m going to have you,” he tells Irwin. His language treats relationships as campaigns to be won. Posner, by contrast, speaks hesitantly, often trailing off or being interrupted. His most powerful speech — “I’m a Jew. I’m small. I’m homosexual. And I live in Sheffield. I’m fucked” — uses short, declarative sentences that gain power through simplicity. Bennett gives Posner the play’s most devastating line precisely because his usual register is so quiet. Key Fact The boys are linguistic code-switchers: literary in Hector’s class, rhetorical in Irwin’s, and profane with each other. Dakin speaks in the language of conquest (“I’m going to have you”); Posner speaks in short, vulnerable declarations. Bennett uses each boy’s register to reveal their character and emotional state. Mrs Lintott’s Dry Irony Mrs Lintott speaks in epigrams : short, sharp, perfectly balanced sentences that expose uncomfortable truths. “History is a commentary on the various and continuing incapabilities of men.” This single sentence redefines the word “history” as both an academic discipline and a catalogue of male failure. It is simultaneously a serious feminist statement and a dry joke. Mrs Lintott’s register is the play’s most controlled: where Hector gushes and Irwin performs, she is precise, sardonic, and devastatingly economical. Her line “I have not lived with these issues as they have, but I know about them” is an example of understatement that carries enormous weight. She is saying: I am aware of Hector’s abuse, Irwin’s sexuality, the boys’ emotional turmoil — but as a woman, I am invisible in this male drama. Bennett uses Mrs Lintott’s language to expose the play’s own gender blindness. She is the character who sees most clearly, but whose vision matters least to the men around her. The Headmaster’s Corporate Register The Headmaster’s language is the play’s most consistently satirised. He speaks in the vocabulary of business: “results,” “shelf,” “display,” “selling points,” “a service the school provides.” He never uses the language of learning, curiosity, or intellectual growth. When he discusses Hector’s abuse, he calls it “the odd grope” — an euphemism that minimises sexual abuse to a trivial inconvenience. His language reveals that he processes everything through institutional self-interest: abuse is a PR problem, Oxbridge places are marketing assets, and teachers are employees whose value is measured in results. Bennett makes the Headmaster comic through bathos : he reduces grand educational ideals to mundane corporate language. When he says “I’m thinking of shelf, Hector. Shelf and display,” the audience laughs because the gap between what education should be (Hector’s vision) and what the Headmaster reduces it to (a product on a shelf) is so wide. This bathos is Bennett’s sharpest satirical weapon.
