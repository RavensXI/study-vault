Theme 1: What Is Education For? The play’s central question — “What is education for?” — is debated through three competing answers. Hector believes education is for enrichment: “All knowledge is precious whether or not it serves the slightest human use.” His teaching fills the boys with poetry, film, and song that have no exam value but deepen their humanity. The Headmaster believes education is for utilitarian outcomes: league tables, Oxbridge places, institutional prestige. Irwin believes education is for performance: the ability to argue cleverly, to take unexpected positions, to impress those who hold power. Bennett does not declare a winner. The epilogue reveals that all three approaches produced mixed results. Hector’s teaching enriched the boys emotionally but could not protect Posner from loneliness. Irwin’s teaching got them through the door but gave them no lasting values. The Headmaster’s utilitarian vision reduced everyone — teachers and students alike — to instruments of institutional ambition. Bennett’s refusal to resolve this debate is itself an argument: education, like life, does not yield simple answers. Key Fact Hector: “All knowledge is precious whether or not it serves the slightest human use” (intrinsic value). Irwin: “Torture the evidence” (performance). The Headmaster: “I want results” (utilitarian). Bennett refuses to let any single philosophy win, insisting that education’s purpose remains genuinely contested. Theme 2: Truth vs Performance The tension between truth and performance runs through every aspect of the play. Irwin teaches the boys to perform intellectual originality rather than pursue genuine understanding. His question “What is truth? said jesting Pilate” is both a literary allusion and a philosophical position: truth is unknowable, so all that matters is how persuasively you argue. Hector, by contrast, teaches that some truths are sacred — that the emotional truth of Hardy’s poetry or the moral truth of the Holocaust cannot be “tortured” into something else. This theme extends beyond the classroom. Irwin performs heterosexuality (he is closeted). The Headmaster performs concern for the boys (he actually cares about league tables). Dakin performs confidence and sexual power. Even Hector performs a kind of nostalgia — his cultural references are all from an earlier era, suggesting he is performing a vision of education that has already passed. The only characters who do not perform are Posner (who is painfully honest about his identity) and Mrs Lintott (who sees through everyone’s performances). Bennett suggests that the most authentic characters are also the most marginalised. Theme 3: Sexuality Sexuality is woven into the play’s fabric. Hector’s abuse is sexual: he touches boys on his motorbike. Irwin is attracted to Dakin but conceals it. Posner is openly gay and in love with Dakin. Dakin is casually heterosexual but propositions Irwin, using sexuality as a form of power. Bennett presents sexuality as complex, messy, and resistant to simple moral judgements. The play was written in 2004 but set in 1983, before Section 28 (1988) but during a period when homosexuality, while legal, was still socially stigmatised. Posner’s openness about his sexuality is brave for the era. Irwin’s closetedness is understandable but linked to his wider dishonesty: a man who hides his sexuality also hides his intellectual convictions. Bennett draws a parallel between sexual and intellectual authenticity: those who perform in one domain tend to perform in the other. Theme 4: What Is History? The play asks what history is — not just as a school subject but as a way of understanding the world. Irwin argues: “History is a performance. It’s entertainment.” For him, the past is raw material to be shaped into persuasive narratives. Mrs Lintott counters: “History is a commentary on the various and continuing incapabilities of men.” For her, history is a record of gendered power. Hector does not define history explicitly, but his teaching implies that history is cultural inheritance — the poems, songs, and stories that connect us to the past and to each other. The play itself demonstrates these competing definitions. It is a performance (Irwin’s definition) that the audience watches in a theatre. It is a commentary on male failure (Mrs Lintott’s definition) played out through Hector’s abuse and Irwin’s dishonesty. And it is an act of cultural inheritance (Hector’s definition): Bennett passes on the poems, songs, and ideas that his characters discuss. The audience leaves the theatre carrying Hector’s parcel. Key Fact The play offers three definitions of history: performance and entertainment (Irwin), a commentary on male failure (Mrs Lintott), and cultural inheritance passed between generations (Hector). The play itself enacts all three simultaneously, refusing to privilege any single definition. Theme 5: Growing Up Beneath the intellectual debates, The History Boys is also about adolescence. The boys are on the threshold of adult life — about to leave school, leave Sheffield, and enter a wider world. Their relationships with Hector and Irwin are part of growing up : learning from flawed adults, discovering that authority figures are not infallible, and negotiating the gap between what school teaches and what life demands. The epilogue reveals that growing up does not mean finding answers — it means learning to live with questions. Exam Technique: Extract and Wider-Text Response Close extract analysis. When you are given a printed extract, focus on how Bennett presents a character, theme, or idea in that specific passage . Focus on: specific quotations with word-level analysis (AO1/AO2), language techniques (register, imagery, sentence structure), structural features (where the extract sits in the play, how it builds tension), and embedded context (AO4). Write 3–4 paragraphs with a clear focus on how Bennett creates meaning, not just what happens. Wider-text analysis. When you are asked to discuss the same theme or idea “elsewhere in the text,” this is your chance to range across the whole play. Select 2–3 key moments from different parts of the play (not just from the same act as the extract). Integrate quotations, analyse language and structure, and link to context. The best answers create an argument — not just a list of relevant moments, but a point of view about what Bennett is saying. Time management: Share your time roughly evenly between the extract task and the wider-text task. Close extract work rewards close, detailed analysis of a short passage; wider-text work rewards breadth, range, and the ability to construct an argument across the whole play. Both test AO1 (response), AO2 (language/form/structure), and AO4 (context). Check your exam board's specimen paper for the precise question format and mark allocation.
