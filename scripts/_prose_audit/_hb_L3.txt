Act 2: The Stakes Rise Act 2 of The History Boys accelerates the play towards its climax. The Oxbridge interviews loom, Hector’s inappropriate behaviour is finally exposed to the Headmaster, and the play’s central philosophical debate reaches its most intense moment in the Holocaust scene. Bennett structures Act 2 as a convergence of crises: professional, moral, and personal. Everything the characters have been repressing — desire, dishonesty, abuse — comes to the surface. Where Act 1 established the two-act tension between Hector and Irwin, Act 2 complicates it. Irwin’s method is tested against a subject that resists being “tortured” (the Holocaust). Hector’s moral authority collapses when his abuse is confirmed. Neither teacher emerges intact, and Bennett denies the audience the comfort of choosing a hero. Key Fact Act 2 brings three crises together: Irwin’s philosophy is morally tested in the Holocaust scene; Hector’s abuse is exposed to the Headmaster; and the boys must face their Oxbridge interviews, where they discover that both teachers’ methods have value — and limitations. Irwin’s “Torture” Approach to History Throughout Act 2, Irwin pushes his contrarian method further. He instructs the boys to treat the dissolution of the monasteries as a positive development, and to argue that the First World War was not a tragic waste but a catalyst for social progress. The boys are initially excited: Rudge says he likes Irwin’s lessons because “it’s torture,” and Timms adds “torture works.” Bennett uses the word “torture” repeatedly — a deliberate setup for the Holocaust scene, where the same word takes on a horrifying double meaning. Irwin’s approach is intellectually stimulating but morally hollow. He never asks the boys what they believe — only what they can argue . When Scripps asks whether they should worry about the truth, Irwin responds: “What is truth? said jesting Pilate.” This quotation from Francis Bacon is deeply ironic: Pilate, who condemned Jesus, is not a figure the audience would want to emulate. Bennett signals that Irwin’s relativism is intellectually clever but morally dangerous. The Oxbridge Interviews The interview scenes are among the play’s most comic and revealing moments. The boys deploy Irwin’s contrarian technique to varying effect. Rudge, the least academic, gets into Oxford by being straightforwardly honest — his grandfather was a soldier at a relevant battle, and the interviewer is charmed by his authenticity. This is deeply ironic: the boy who ignores Irwin’s methods is the one who succeeds most naturally. Bennett suggests that genuine personal connection (Hector’s territory) may ultimately impress more than intellectual performance. Other boys blend Hector and Irwin’s methods. Posner recites Hardy’s Drummer Hodge at interview — Hector’s teaching — and one boy uses Irwin’s technique of arguing against the obvious interpretation. All the boys get in, which raises the question: was it Hector’s enrichment, Irwin’s strategy, or simply their own intelligence that earned the places? Hector’s Abuse Exposed Hector gives boys lifts home on his motorbike, during which he touches them inappropriately. The boys have always known about this and treat it as a joke — they draw straws for who has to ride with him, calling it “the routine .” When the Headmaster finally discovers the abuse (after the Headmaster’s wife sees Hector groping a boy at a traffic light), he uses it as a pretext to force Hector out. But Bennett makes the Headmaster’s response morally dubious: the Headmaster does not act out of concern for the boys. He has been looking for a reason to remove Hector because he cannot measure the value of general studies. The boys’ reaction is striking. They are not traumatised — they defend Hector. Dakin says: “It’s just the odd grope. It’s nothing.” Bennett does not endorse this view, but he refuses to simplify it. The boys are shown simultaneously as victims and as agents who minimise their own abuse. A modern audience may find this deeply uncomfortable, which is precisely Bennett’s intention: he forces us to hold contradictory responses at the same time. Key Fact Bennett refuses to simplify the abuse: the boys minimise it (“just the odd grope”), the Headmaster exploits it for institutional convenience, and the audience is denied a clear moral position. This moral complexity is central to the play’s examination of how we judge people who are both admirable and deeply flawed. The Motorbike Crash and the Ending The play ends with Hector’s death in a motorbike accident. Irwin, who is riding pillion, survives but is paralysed and ends up in a wheelchair — a detail revealed in the play’s framing device, where the adult Irwin is a television historian . The crash functions symbolically: Hector’s death makes him a kind of martyr, preserved forever in the boys’ memories as “the teacher who changed their lives,” while Irwin survives but is diminished — literally and figuratively. The epilogue fast-forwards to show the boys’ adult lives. Some succeed; some don’t. Posner, the most vulnerable, ends up lonely and mentally fragile. Scripps becomes a journalist. Dakin becomes a tax lawyer. Hector is remembered with deep affection. Irwin becomes a television presenter — the ultimate embodiment of his performative approach to knowledge. Bennett uses the epilogue to ask: what did education actually give these boys? The answer is ambiguous. Hector’s teaching enriched them emotionally; Irwin’s got them through the door. Neither was sufficient alone. Symbolic Readings of the Crash Hector’s death on the motorbike carries multiple symbolic resonances. The motorbike is the site of his abuse, so his death occurs at the very point where his moral failure was most visible. Irwin is on the pillion — the two teachers are literally and symbolically bound together at the moment of destruction. Hector dies and is immortalised in memory; Irwin survives and is diminished. Bennett may be suggesting that passionate, flawed sincerity (Hector) leaves a deeper mark than polished, strategic performance (Irwin), even though sincerity alone is not enough. The crash also prevents the audience from reaching a neat moral conclusion. If Hector had lived, the play would have to address his future — would he be prosecuted? Shamed? His death freezes him in the boys’ memories as a beloved teacher, a status his behaviour does not entirely deserve. Bennett deliberately denies us closure.
