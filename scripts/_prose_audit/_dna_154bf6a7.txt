Act 3: Adam Returns

The Shocking Reversal Act 3 delivers the play’s most dramatic twist: Adam is alive. He has been living in a hedge, surviving on scraps, mentally broken but physically present. The boy the group assumed was dead — the boy they built an entire cover-up around — is found by Cathy. This discovery should be good news. Instead, it is the worst thing that could happen to the group, because Adam’s survival threatens the lie that has been holding them together. Kelly uses this reversal to expose the moral rot at the heart of the group. A living Adam is more dangerous than a dead one, because a dead Adam requires only silence — a living Adam could speak. The group’s first instinct is not relief or joy but fear. “What are we going to do?” becomes the desperate refrain again, and once again all eyes turn to Phil. Key Fact Adam’s survival in Act 3 is the play’s central reversal. Rather than bringing relief, it creates a deeper crisis: the group has framed an innocent man, and a living Adam could expose everything. Their response — fear rather than joy — reveals how far they have fallen morally. Phil’s Darker Orders Phil’s response to Adam’s reappearance marks a turning point. In Acts 1 and 2, his plan was morally wrong but arguably designed to protect the group. In Act 3, his instructions become explicitly cruel. He orders the group to keep Adam hidden — to take him to a disused building and ensure he never comes forward. “I’ve got nothing to say,” Phil tells Leah when she pleads with him to stop, a line that echoes his earlier silence but now carries a darker weight. His refusal to engage is no longer mysterious; it is wilful detachment . The group follows Phil’s orders again, but this time the cracks are visible. Richard questions the plan. Lou is uneasy. Even Brian, who has been having a breakdown throughout, seems to recognise that this is different from the original cover-up. Kelly shows that there is a threshold of evil beyond which even passive followers begin to hesitate ��� but hesitation is not the same as refusal, and nobody actually walks away. Cathy’s Violence: The Cigarette Scene Cathy emerges as the play’s most overtly violent character in Act 3. When Adam is found, she is the one who takes charge of controlling him. She tortures Adam with a lit cigarette, burning him to establish dominance and ensure his silence. This is the play’s most physically violent moment — and significantly, it is one of the few acts of violence that occurs onstage (or very close to it, depending on staging), breaking Kelly’s earlier pattern of keeping brutality offstage. Cathy’s enthusiasm for violence is not reluctant obedience — it is pleasure. She volunteers for the worst tasks and relishes the power they give her. Earlier, she enjoyed being interviewed by a TV reporter about the “missing boy.” Now she enjoys inflicting pain directly. Kelly presents Cathy as a sociopath who has found, in the group’s crisis, the perfect environment for her worst instincts to flourish. Where the Stanford Prison Experiment showed that ordinary people become abusive when given power, Cathy suggests that some people were already waiting for the opportunity. The Group Fractures Act 3 is where the group begins to break apart. John Tate, the original leader, has effectively disappeared from the action — his authority shattered. Richard starts to question Phil openly: “Happy? Is that what you are? Happy?” This is the first time anyone directly challenges Phil, and the question is devastating because it suggests Phil may not simply be pragmatic but may be enjoying his control, just as Cathy enjoys her violence. Danny and Lou remain silent followers, doing what they are told without protest but also without enthusiasm. Brian continues his breakdown. Mark and Jan, the street-scene narrators, report events with increasing detachment , as though they are distancing themselves from what they are describing. Their choral function becomes increasingly ironic: they report atrocities in the same flat tone they used to describe the initial bullying. Key Fact Act 3 shows the group fracturing: Richard challenges Phil (“Happy?”), John Tate has vanished, and Mark and Jan report events with increasing detachment. However, nobody leaves the group or goes to the authorities — Kelly shows that awareness of wrongdoing does not equal the courage to act. Adam’s State: The Living Victim Adam barely speaks in Act 3. He has been living rough, surviving alone after his fall. When found, he is described as being in a traumatised state. He is not the same person who walked across the grille — the experience has broken him. Kelly uses Adam’s silence to mirror Phil’s, but where Phil’s silence is power, Adam’s silence is powerlessness. He has no voice, no agency, no ability to help himself. The group treats him as a problem to be managed rather than a person to be saved. This dehumanisation is one of Kelly’s most disturbing achievements. Adam has gone from a boy who wanted to belong so badly he would endure bullying, to a “living dead” figure who exists only as a threat to the group’s safety. The other characters never ask how he is feeling, what he has been through, or whether he needs help. They ask only: what do we do with him? Act 3 is the play’s moral nadir. The group has moved from accidental harm to deliberate cruelty, from covering up a death to imprisoning a living person. Phil’s authority has become absolute, Cathy’s violence has been unleashed, and Leah’s moral objections have been completely ignored. The audience watches the last traces of decency drain away from the group, replaced by the cold logic of self-preservation at any cost.
