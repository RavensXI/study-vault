Language & Silence

Silence as the Most Powerful Language In most plays, the characters who speak the most hold the most power. In DNA , the opposite is true. Phil barely speaks, yet he controls everything. His silence is not passive — it is an active strategy of control . By refusing to speak, Phil forces the group to come to him, to beg for instructions, to depend on him. The repeated question “What are you going to do?” shows that Phil’s silence creates a vacuum that only he can fill. When Phil does speak, his words are devastatingly effective. His instructions are short, flat, and imperative: “Take his jumper. Leave it there. Go home. Say nothing.” There is no emotional language, no justification, no persuasion. He does not explain why his plan is necessary; he simply states what must be done. This minimal directive language makes his orders feel inevitable rather than debatable. The group complies because Phil’s speech style removes the option of discussion. Key Fact Phil’s silence is not absence but strategy. When he does speak, his flat, imperative sentences (“Take his jumper. Leave it there. Go home. Say nothing.”) remove the possibility of debate and make criminal actions feel like simple instructions to be followed. Leah’s Verbal Desperation Leah is the opposite of Phil: she fills every silence with words. Her monologues are long, rambling, and increasingly frantic. She talks about bonobos: “We share 99.6% of our DNA with them.” She talks about happiness: “Are you happy? Is that what you are?” She talks about friendship: “You need me, Phil. Everyone needs someone.” Each monologue is an attempt to provoke a response from Phil, and each one fails. Kelly uses Leah’s speech as a form of verbal filling . The more Phil refuses to respond, the more Leah talks. Her language becomes a mirror of her emotional state: as her desperation increases, her sentences become longer, more repetitive, more fragmented. She jumps between topics, circles back, contradicts herself. This linguistic disintegration tracks the group’s moral disintegration — as things fall apart, so does coherent speech. Euphemism and Avoidance The group’s language is full of euphemism . Mark and Jan describe the bullying as “having a laugh.” The group refers to Adam’s fall as “what happened.” John Tate bans the word “dead.” Phil’s instructions avoid naming the crime: he says “take his jumper” and “leave it there,” not “plant evidence to frame an innocent man.” This linguistic avoidance is a form of moral distancing . By refusing to name what they have done, the characters create a buffer between themselves and the truth. If they do not say “murder” or “torture” or “frame,” then perhaps those things are not really what they did. Kelly shows that language is not just a tool for communication — it is a tool for self-deception. The group lies to itself through the words it chooses and the words it avoids. Fragmented Dialogue and Interruption Much of the dialogue in DNA is fragmented. Characters interrupt each other, leave sentences unfinished, and talk over one another. Mark and Jan’s street-scene dialogue is the clearest example — they share sentences, each picking up where the other left off, creating a breathless, anxious pace : “MARK: So we were — JAN: Having a laugh. MARK: Having a laugh, we were, and — JAN: He fell.” This shared dialogue serves several purposes. It creates naturalism — real teenagers speak in fragments, especially under stress. It creates urgency — the overlapping rhythm makes events feel chaotic and out of control. And it shows that Mark and Jan are so enmeshed in the group that they cannot even narrate independently; their identities have merged into a single choral voice. Key Fact Kelly uses language in four key ways: Phil’s silence as control, Leah’s verbal desperation as failed conscience, euphemism as moral distancing, and fragmented dialogue as naturalism and urgency. Each technique reveals character and theme simultaneously. “I’m Not Saying Anything” — The Paradox of Speech Phil’s line “I’m not saying anything” is one of the play’s richest paradoxes . By saying he is not saying anything, he is saying something. He is declaring his position: silence is his chosen stance. This line encapsulates the play’s exploration of communication and its failures. Phil communicates through absence. Leah communicates through excess. The group communicates through euphemism and fragmentation. Nobody in DNA says what they actually mean, and this failure of honest communication is both a symptom and a cause of the moral breakdown. Incomplete Sentences and Unspoken Truths Throughout the play, sentences are left incomplete. Characters trail off, change direction mid-thought, or are interrupted before they can finish. This is not careless writing — it is a deliberate representation of the unsayable . The things the group has done are so terrible that language itself breaks down. They cannot describe the bullying without euphemism. They cannot discuss Adam without evasion. They cannot name the moral horror because naming it would force them to confront it. Kelly suggests that language fails when moral reality becomes too uncomfortable to articulate. The gaps in the dialogue — the pauses, the unfinished sentences, the things not said — are where the real meaning lives. An attentive audience reads the silences as carefully as the speech.
