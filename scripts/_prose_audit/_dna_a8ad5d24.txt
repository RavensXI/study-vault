Key Themes

Guilt and Responsibility Guilt in DNA is not evenly distributed. Kelly creates a spectrum of responses: Leah is consumed by moral anxiety, Phil shows no visible guilt at all, and Cathy feels nothing but excitement. Danny and Brian develop anxiety and depression. John Tate seeks refuge in religion. The range of responses is Kelly’s way of asking: does feeling guilty matter if you still do nothing to make things right? The concept of diffusion of responsibility is central to the play. Because the crime is collective, no individual feels personally accountable. Each member can point to someone else: “I wasn’t the one who pushed him” or “Phil told us to do it.” Kelly shows that shared guilt is not the same as shared remorse — the group distributes the blame but nobody actually takes responsibility. Key Fact Kelly presents guilt as a spectrum: Leah agonises, Phil shows nothing, Cathy feels excitement, Danny develops anxiety. The concept of diffusion of responsibility means nobody feels individually accountable, even though every member is complicit. Leadership and Power The play examines two models of leadership. John Tate leads through intimidation: he shouts, threatens, and demands obedience. His leadership collapses the moment a real crisis arrives because bluster cannot solve practical problems. Phil leads through detachment and strategy: he listens, thinks, and provides clear instructions. The group follows Phil not because they fear him but because he offers them what they desperately need — a plan. Kelly’s exploration of power connects directly to Milgram’s obedience experiment. Phil is the authority figure in the white coat; the group members are the willing participants. The play suggests that in any group, people will gravitate towards whoever appears most confident and competent, regardless of the morality of their decisions. Power flows to those who provide certainty. Phil’s Power: Strategy or Manipulation? A key debate is whether Phil is a manipulator who deliberately exploits the group’s fear, or simply a pragmatist who sees what needs to be done and does it. Kelly leaves this ambiguous. Phil never lies to the group or flatters them; he simply presents his plan as the logical solution. But the result is the same as manipulation: the group does what Phil says without questioning the morality of his instructions. Whether Phil intends to exploit them or not, the effect of his calm authority is to override their individual moral judgement. Morality and Amorality Kelly raises the question of whether morality is innate or learned — and whether it can be abandoned under pressure. Most characters in the play have some sense that what they are doing is wrong, but this awareness does not translate into action. Leah knows, but cannot act. Danny knows, but obeys. Even John Tate, who leaves the group, does not confess — he simply removes himself. Phil and Cathy represent two forms of amorality . Phil appears to have no moral framework at all — he treats the crisis as a puzzle to be solved, not a wrong to be righted. Cathy, meanwhile, actively enjoys the transgression. Both characters function without a conscience, but for different reasons: Phil through emotional shutdown, Cathy through the pleasure of power. Peer Pressure and Conformity The group in DNA is held together not by friendship or loyalty but by fear of exposure. Each member knows that if one person confesses, they all go down. This creates a form of mutual entrapment — the group cannot disband because each person holds information that could destroy the others. Peer pressure in the play is not about wanting to be liked; it is about survival. Kelly connects this to real-world conformity . Once the group has committed to Phil’s plan, dissenting becomes almost impossible. To speak out would mean admitting personal guilt, betraying the group, and facing consequences alone. The social cost of telling the truth is higher than the moral cost of staying silent. Key Fact The group is held together by mutual entrapment, not friendship. Each member knows that confessing would expose everyone. Dissenting means admitting guilt, betraying the group, and facing consequences alone. Kelly shows that the social cost of truth can be higher than the moral cost of silence. Silence and Complicity Silence operates on multiple levels in DNA . Phil’s literal silence in the field scenes shows his emotional detachment. The group’s collective silence about their guilt enables the cover-up to continue. Leah’s eventual permanent silence (her disappearance) represents the death of the moral voice. Adam’s silence — he barely appears and never speaks in a meaningful way — represents his dehumanisation by the group. Kelly uses silence to explore complicity . The play argues that doing nothing is itself a moral choice. Every character who stays silent enables the cover-up. Even Leah, who speaks constantly, is ultimately complicit because her words never translate into action. Kelly’s message is clear: silence and inaction are not neutral — they are active contributions to injustice. The Many Forms of Silence in DNA Phil’s silence is strategic — he speaks only when he has a plan, and his silence gives his words greater weight. The group’s silence is self-protective — they stay quiet to avoid consequences. Leah’s silence (when it finally comes, through disappearance) is defeat — she has given up trying to be heard. Adam’s silence is imposed — he is silenced by the group who treat him as an object. Each form of silence serves a different dramatic purpose, but all contribute to the play’s central argument that silence enables moral catastrophe.
