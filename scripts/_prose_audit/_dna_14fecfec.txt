Acts 3-4: Escalation & the Ending

Act 3: Adam Is Alive The turning point of DNA comes in Act 3 when the group discovers that Adam is not dead. He survived the fall and has been living in the woods, alone and traumatised. This should be good news — nobody has actually committed murder. But the group’s reaction is the opposite of relief. Adam’s survival threatens the cover-up. If he comes forward, the group will be exposed: they have already framed an innocent postman, and the man living rough has been arrested as a false witness. Kelly uses this reversal to expose the depth of the group’s moral corruption. Instead of being grateful that Adam is alive, they now see him as a problem to be solved. The cover-up has become more important than the person at the centre of it. This is one of the play’s most devastating insights: once a lie takes hold, the truth becomes the enemy. Key Fact Adam is found alive in Act 3, but the group sees his survival as a threat, not a relief. If Adam talks, their cover-up unravels: the framed postman, the false witness, and their own lies will all be exposed. The cover-up has become more important than Adam himself. Phil’s Darker Orders Phil’s response to Adam’s survival marks a significant moral escalation . In Act 1, his plan involved deception — framing an innocent person, which is serious but does not directly harm anyone physically. In Act 3, Phil considers a far darker solution. He instructs the group to keep Adam hidden and, it is strongly implied, orders them to kill him. The exact nature of Phil’s instructions is left ambiguous by Kelly. The audience must infer what happens from the characters’ reactions and the events of Act 4. This deliberate ambiguity is a key feature of Kelly’s writing style. By not spelling out the order, he forces the audience to confront the possibility themselves — and in doing so, makes them complicit in imagining the worst. The Escalation of Phil’s Leadership Phil’s trajectory across the play mirrors the group’s moral decline. In Act 1, he is a strategist; by Act 3, he is potentially ordering murder. Each act requires a greater moral compromise. The group, having already crossed the line with the cover-up, finds it psychologically easier to take the next step. This echoes the psychological concept of the foot-in-the-door effect — once people have complied with a small request, they are more likely to comply with a larger one. Kelly also shows that Phil’s power has become self-sustaining. By Act 3, nobody questions him — not because he threatens them, but because they have already invested too much in his leadership to turn back. They are trapped by their own earlier compliance. Cathy’s Violence Cathy is the most disturbing character in DNA . While other group members show varying degrees of guilt, Cathy is energised by the situation. In Act 3, she reports back from the woods with visible excitement, describing events in graphic terms. She shows no remorse and appears to enjoy the power that violence gives her. Kelly uses Cathy to explore the idea that group pressure does not simply force people into immoral acts — for some, it provides permission to reveal impulses that were already there. Cathy thrives in the moral vacuum created by the crisis. She volunteers for the most unpleasant tasks and takes pleasure in them. By Act 4, she has joined a media career, cheerfully discussing the events as though they were an interesting story rather than a moral catastrophe. Kelly suggests that in a society that rewards confident, assertive people, characters like Cathy can succeed precisely because they lack empathy. Key Fact Cathy is the only character who actively enjoys the violence and chaos. She shows no guilt, volunteers for the worst tasks, and is excited by events others find horrifying. Kelly uses her to suggest that group pressure can give some people permission to express cruelty they already possessed. Act 4: The Bleak Ending The final act of DNA offers no redemption. Mark and Jan’s street scene reveals the aftermath: the group has fragmented. John Tate has “found God.” Danny and the others are on medication for anxiety and depression. Leah has disappeared entirely. Phil sits alone in the field, eating, silent, staring into space. Only Cathy appears unaffected — she has moved into television. Richard, who has replaced Leah in the field scenes, tries to talk to Phil, mirroring Leah’s earlier attempts. Phil does not respond. Richard asks: “What are you thinking?” Phil says nothing. The play ends on this image of total emotional shutdown. Why the Ending Is So Disturbing Kelly deliberately avoids a conventional resolution. Nobody is punished. Nobody confesses. The innocent postman’s fate is unresolved. Adam’s fate is implied but never confirmed. The characters simply move on with their lives, carrying varying degrees of damage. Kelly’s message is deeply pessimistic: society does not automatically correct moral failures. People can do terrible things, avoid consequences, and continue to function. The replacement of Leah with Richard in the final field scene is particularly significant. The play’s moral conscience has been silenced and replaced by someone who is more naively curious than ethically tormented. Kelly suggests that the capacity for genuine moral questioning (Leah) is rarer and more fragile than passive acceptance (Richard). Leah’s Disappearance Leah’s absence in Act 4 is one of the play’s most haunting details. She simply vanishes. Kelly provides no explanation — has she left the group voluntarily, or has something happened to her? The audience is left to speculate. Given the play’s trajectory of escalating violence, her disappearance carries sinister overtones. At the very least, it confirms that the one voice of moral conscience has been silenced, and the group is poorer for it. Richard’s cheerful report that Cathy is “going into television” while others are on medication creates a bitter irony . The character who showed the least remorse is the one who thrives. Kelly uses this to make a pointed social commentary: in a world that rewards confidence and punishes doubt, moral sensitivity is a disadvantage.
