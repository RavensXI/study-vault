“How Do I Teach the Holocaust?” The Holocaust scene is the moral crux of The History Boys . It is the moment where Irwin’s contrarian method is pushed to its breaking point, where Hector’s belief in the sacredness of certain knowledge is most powerfully expressed, and where Posner’s personal identity makes the intellectual debate suddenly, painfully real. Bennett uses this single scene to crystallise every theme in the play: truth, performance, education, history, and the limits of intellectual detachment. Irwin asks the class how they would approach the Holocaust in an Oxbridge interview. His suggestion is characteristic: take the unexpected view. Rather than expressing horror and reverence, argue that the Holocaust was “not unique” — that it can be compared to other genocides, contextualised historically, analysed dispassionately. Irwin frames this as intellectual courage: “torture the evidence.” But Bennett is asking the audience: is there a point at which intellectual detachment becomes moral obscenity? Key Fact The word “torture” is used throughout the play for Irwin’s method (“torture the evidence”). In the Holocaust scene, this word takes on a horrifying double meaning : the same word that describes clever argument also describes the systematic infliction of suffering on millions. Bennett forces the audience to confront the gap between intellectual games and real human pain. Irwin’s Position: History as Material For Irwin, the Holocaust is simply another historical event that can be approached from an unusual angle. He suggests the boys could argue that the Holocaust has been “overdone” in school curricula, or that focusing exclusively on the Holocaust obscures other genocides. His point is not that the Holocaust was acceptable — it is that an Oxbridge interviewer would be impressed by a candidate who could step back from emotional responses and think analytically. Irwin tells the boys: “But this is history. Distance yourselves.” Irwin’s language is revealing. He uses the vocabulary of television (“good television,” “ratings”) and marketing (“selling it”), not the vocabulary of scholarship. Bennett positions Irwin as a man who treats history as content to be packaged and presented, not as truth to be understood. His approach works well for the Reformation or the Industrial Revolution, but it collapses when applied to genocide because genocide demands a moral response, not a strategic one. Hector’s Response: “This Is Not Torture. This Is Real.” Hector is present during the Holocaust discussion, and his reaction is visceral. He refuses to treat the Holocaust as material for argument. For Hector, some things are sacred — they demand reverence , not analysis. His response embodies his entire educational philosophy: knowledge is not a tool; it is something you feel. The Holocaust, for Hector, is not a topic you “do” at interview — it is a human catastrophe that demands silence, empathy, and humility. Hector’s position is emotionally powerful, but Bennett does not let it go unchallenged. The boys point out that reverent silence does not help them pass their interviews. They need strategies, not feelings. This is the play’s central dilemma : Hector is morally right but practically useless; Irwin is practically effective but morally bankrupt. Neither alone serves the boys well. Posner’s Pain The scene’s emotional climax comes from Posner. He is Jewish, gay, small, and acutely aware of his own vulnerability. When Irwin suggests treating the Holocaust as debatable, Posner responds: “I’m a Jew. I’m small. I’m homosexual. And I live in Sheffield. I’m fucked.” This is the play’s most devastating moment. Posner collapses the distance between the historical and the personal. The Holocaust is not an abstract topic for him — it is a catalogue of the very categories that define his identity and his suffering. Bennett uses accumulation in Posner’s speech: “I’m a Jew. I’m small. I’m homosexual. And I live in Sheffield.” Each short sentence adds another layer of vulnerability. The final “I’m fucked” is brutally colloquial, stripping away all intellectual pretension. The profanity is a deliberate jolt — it shatters the classroom’s academic tone and forces the audience to feel what Posner feels. In this moment, Irwin’s intellectual detachment is exposed as not just inadequate but cruel. Key Fact Posner’s speech — “I’m a Jew. I’m small. I’m homosexual. And I live in Sheffield. I’m fucked” — is the play’s emotional turning point. It proves that intellectual games have human consequences. Each identity Posner lists would have made him a target during the Holocaust, and each still makes him an outsider in 1980s Sheffield. “Torture” vs Torture: Bennett’s Double Meaning Bennett’s most devastating literary technique in this scene is his use of the word “torture.” Throughout the play, “torture” means Irwin’s method — pushing evidence into unexpected shapes, making the unreasonable sound reasonable. But in the context of the Holocaust, “torture” means physical suffering inflicted on millions. When Irwin tells the boys to “torture the evidence,” Bennett is asking: what is the moral difference between manipulating historical truth and the actual torture of human beings? This is not a casual pun. Bennett has been building towards this moment from the opening scenes. Every time a character says “torture” in the classroom context, the word accumulates weight. By the Holocaust scene, it has become unbearable. The audience realises that Irwin’s entire method rests on a metaphor of violence — and that metaphor, when applied to the Holocaust, collapses into literal truth. Bennett uses extended metaphor to devastating effect: the intellectual “torture” of evidence is finally confronted by the real torture of human beings. The Scene’s Wider Significance The Holocaust scene raises questions that extend beyond the classroom. If history is just a matter of argument and perspective (as Irwin teaches), then there is no objective truth — and Holocaust denial becomes just another “contrarian” position. Bennett is warning against a culture of moral relativism in which everything is debatable and nothing is sacred. This is particularly resonant in the context of twenty-first-century media, where Irwin’s approach — take the provocative angle, generate controversy, attract attention — has become the default mode of public discourse. Bennett does not offer a simple solution. He shows that Hector’s reverence is inadequate for an exam system that demands analysis, while Irwin’s analysis is inadequate for a subject that demands reverence. The boys — and the audience — are left to negotiate this tension themselves.
