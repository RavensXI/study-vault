Williams: Main Title from Star Wars

The opening cue of Star Wars Episode IV: A New Hope (1977), for full symphony orchestra in a bright B flat major. It has two connected halves: a concert-style opening of fanfare, main theme, contrasting idea and return, then action underscore for the chase between the rebel ship and the Imperial Star Destroyer.

In 1977 film scoring had drifted towards pop songs and electronics. George Lucas asked for something grand and melodic instead, and Williams answered with recurring themes for a full orchestra. The film recording was made by the London Symphony Orchestra; this performance is by the Vienna Philharmonic.

1 · The curtain-raiser

Before any theme, a short brass gesture. Horns and trumpets leap upward through fourths and fifths, wide open intervals that project brightly, launched by triplet upbeats. A fanfare is not a melody; its job is to seize attention and fix the key, and it does both within four seconds.

Listen for: the direction of the leaps. Rising fourths and fifths, not steps: that is what makes it sound heroic.

2 · The A theme

The fanfare runs straight into the theme everyone knows. Horns and trumpets stride upward through the notes of the chord, and this idea will return across the whole saga as a leitmotif for heroism and the Rebellion, a technique Wagner developed in opera and film composers borrowed wholesale.

Listen for: how the tune climbs. The theme is the film's engine, not just its title music.

3 · The contrast

The texture changes and the strings take over with a gentler idea. It moves by smaller intervals, at a softer dynamic and a slower pace, so it gives the ear a rest from fanfare energy. In the film this is the crawl still moving away into deep space: scale and mystery rather than action.

Listen for: strings replacing brass. Naming the family that carries each theme is the quickest structure mark in this cue.

4 · The theme comes back

The brass reclaim the main theme. That gives the opening half a clear shape — fanfare, A, B, A — which is why this part of the cue works as concert music as well as film music. What follows is not a repeat but a transition: the music will change function rather than stop.

Listen for: the return of a theme you already know, in the instruments that first played it.

5 · The music changes job

The loud title music falls away and something quiet and uneasy takes its place. The crawl has finished and the camera has found the planet. The music does not stop and restart: tension builds in the strings with brass interjections until the chase is under way and the cue has become underscore.

Listen for: the join. Recognising where concert music becomes underscore is exactly the structural detail examiners reward.

6 · The finish

The chase music drives to its close. Across the whole cue Williams has done three jobs at once: set the heroic tone of the saga, introduce a theme that will return for hours of film, and glue the written crawl to the first on-screen action. On this recording the audience answers immediately.

Listen for: how far the music has travelled from the opening fanfare without ever leaving its key for long.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: symphonic film score, main title cue. Forces: full symphony orchestra, brass-led. Key: B flat major. Metre and tempo: firm regular metre with a march-like drive. Structure: fanfare, A theme, lyrical B section, return of A, transition, action underscore. Texture: mostly homophonic, melody doubled in octaves over accompaniment. Harmony: functional and major, with quartal touches. Context: 1977, written at George Lucas's request in a style reviving Korngold's Hollywood scoring and Holst's orchestral power, using Wagner's leitmotif technique.

Say which family plays what, and where. “It uses a fanfare” earns little; “horns and trumpets open with rising fourths and fifths before the strings take the contrasting theme” earns the mark.
