The same cue, element by element

John Williams’s opening cue for Star Wars Episode IV (1977), for full symphony orchestra in B flat major: fanfare, main theme, lyrical contrast, return, then action underscore for the chase.

The previous lesson followed the cue section by section. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on: rhythm and metre, melody, orchestration, harmony and tonality, dynamics and timbre.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · It marches

Listen to the rhythm before you listen to the tune. Phrases are launched by triplet upbeats, three quick notes springing into the strong beat, and the theme itself carries dotted rhythms that give it a crisp, military edge. The metre is firm and regular, which is why the cue feels like advancing rather than dancing.

Listen for: the three-note run into each phrase. Name it as a triplet upbeat, not just ‘a fast bit’.

2 · Built from a chord

The main theme does not wander by step; it outlines the notes of the chord, which makes it triadic. There is a wide upward leap near the start of the phrase, so the tune reaches rather than creeps. The effect is close to a bugle call: confident, open and instantly memorable.

Listen for: the gaps between notes. Triadic outline plus a wide leap is the answer to almost any melody question here.

3 · Who plays what

The brass step back and the strings take the lyrical theme. That division of labour runs through the whole cue: brass carry fanfare and heroism, strings carry lyricism and later tension, and percussion punctuates the structure. The same family will do a completely different job later on.

Listen for: which family has the tune. Naming the family and its dramatic role together is the fastest mark in this set work.

4 · Secure, with one strange colour

The theme returns over harmony that leaves no doubt about the key: strong triads in a bright major. But Williams also slips in chords and fragments built from stacked fourths rather than thirds, which is quartal harmony. It sounds slightly modern and unsettled, hinting at scale and mystery without loosening the key.

Listen for: a chord that sounds hollow rather than warm. Spotting and naming quartal harmony separates a strong answer from an average one.

5 · The biggest drop

The loudest and the quietest points of the cue are seconds apart. The full orchestra falls away to almost nothing as the crawl ends, and Williams then rebuilds from that silence rather than staying loud. Dynamics here are structural: the drop is what tells you the music has changed job.

Listen for: how far the volume falls, and how quickly. Describe the contrast and what it signals, not just the loud part.

6 · Tension made of tone colour

By the close the same orchestra sounds nothing like the fanfare. Strings play tremolo and rapid figuration, brass throw in syncopated stabs off the beat, and timpani punch the accents. No new theme is needed: the tension is built entirely from tone colour, rhythm and register.

Listen for: short accented chords landing between the beats. That is the sound of the underscore, not of the title music.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: triadic, with wide leaps and triplet upbeats; smoother and narrower in the B section. Harmony and tonality: confident B flat major with strong triadic harmony, coloured by quartal chords. Texture: mostly homophonic, with the melody doubled in octaves. Rhythm and metre: firm regular metre, march-like, with dotted rhythms and later syncopated stabs. Orchestration: brass for fanfare and heroism, strings for lyricism and tension, percussion for punctuation. Dynamics: extremes, including a sudden fall as the title music ends.

Name the device, then what it does. “Quartal harmony” earns little; “chords stacked in fourths give a hollow, modern colour that suggests the vastness of space” earns the mark.
