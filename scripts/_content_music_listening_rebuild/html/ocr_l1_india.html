One region, two rhythmic systems

Track one is North Indian classical music: a solo on the tabla, the pair of hand drums, in teentaal, the 16-beat cycle. Ustad Tari Khan is a Pakistani master of the gharana, the Punjab school of tabla playing. Behind him a harmonium repeats a short melody so the cycle can always be counted. Everything you hear is measured against that cycle.

Track two is bhangra, the Punjabi folk music that became a British pop genre. ‘Aaja Billo’ was released in 2000 by Balwinder Safri, founder of the Safri Boyz and one of the biggest names in UK bhangra. The double-headed dhol drives it, and around the dhol sit a tumbi, a harmonium, synthesisers and a drum machine.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Use the track buttons to swap recordings, and tap any number to jump.

1 · Said before it is played

Tari Khan talks to the audience and recites the composition out loud before he plays it. Those syllables are bols: each one names a particular stroke, so a tabla player can speak a piece as accurately as play it. It is how the repertoire is taught, and why the tradition needs no notation.

Listen for: the same pattern spoken and then played. If you can hear the match, you have heard how the music is passed on.

2 · The cycle starts turning

The solo begins and the harmonium comes in with a short repeating melody, the lehra. Its job is to mark the cycle so the tabla can leave it and come back. Find the steady pulse first, then find the strongest stroke: that is sam, beat one, where every cycle restarts. Count on and the heavy stroke returns after sixteen.

Listen for: the pulse first, then the strongest stroke. Count the beats between strong strokes to get the cycle length.

3 · A theme, then its variations

The opening pattern is now being reworked. A kaida is a short theme developed through a strict series of variations, or paltas, which reorder and expand the bols while keeping the cycle exactly sixteen beats long. Beat nine is khali, the empty group: the low drum lifts off and the sound thins.

Listen for: the moment in each cycle where the bass drops out. That is khali, and it tells you where you are in sixteen.

4 · The tempo climbs

This is the densest, fastest playing in the solo. Indian musicians call tempo laya, and a solo of this kind climbs steadily rather than jumping. The cycle has not changed length: the beats are simply closer together, and more strokes are being fitted between them.

Listen for: the cycle staying sixteen beats long while the pulse quickens. Speed is not the same as a change of metre.

5 · Landing on sam

The solo closes with a tihai: a phrase played three times so that its final stroke arrives exactly on sam. That is why the ending feels so decisive, and why the audience applauds on the beat. It is the standard way to finish a passage in this music, and naming it is worth a mark.

Listen for: three repetitions of the same phrase, then a stroke that lands with the harmonium. Count them.

1 · Everything at once

A spoken sample, then the whole rhythm section lands together. That is the first thing to say about modern bhangra: the dhol is played alongside a drum machine and synthesisers, so a folk drum from Punjab is sitting inside a studio-built dance track. The impact of technology on traditional music is examinable, and it is audible here.

Listen for: two drum sounds at once: one struck by hand, one programmed. Name both.

2 · The chaal, and the swing

The vocal comes in and the chorus hook comes round. Under it the dhol plays the chaal, the eight-beat bhangra groove. Listen to the pairs of quick notes between the beats: they are not even. Each pair is long then short, a swung feel, and that lopsided lilt is what makes bhangra sound like bhangra.

Listen for: swing. Are the pairs of quick notes even, or is each pair long-short?

3 · One string, high above

An instrumental break, and the tumbi is clearly on top. It has a single string, plucked with one finger, and it plays repeating high figures rather than chords. Alongside it the dhol’s two heads are doing different jobs: a thin stick on the treble head, a heavier one on the bass head. That is why one drum sounds like two.

Listen for: the two sides of the dhol. High and dry on one head, deep and booming on the other.

4 · The drum takes over

The singing stops and the percussion drives the track alone. In a live bhangra performance this is where the dancing takes over, and the modern recording keeps the shape even though nobody is dancing in a studio. The chaal is unchanged: what has been removed is the melody, not the groove.

Listen for: the groove continuing untouched with the voice gone. The rhythm was never accompaniment.

5 · Round again

The hook returns for the last time. Bhangra structure is built from repeats: verse, chorus, break, chorus, with boliyan sung couplets in the older folk style feeding the verses. The song does not develop in the way a Western pop song does; it circles, exactly as the tala circles in track one.

Listen for: how little changes between one chorus and the next. Repetition is the structure, not a shortage of ideas.

Say it precisely in the exam

Indian classical: tabla and harmonium; teentaal, 16 beats in four groups of four; sam on beat one, khali at beat nine; bols, kaida and paltas, tihai; lehra or nagma; laya rising through the solo; the Punjab gharana. Bhangra: dhol playing the eight-beat chaal with swung quavers; tumbi, harmonium, synthesisers, drum machine and studio production; boliyan; harvest-dance origins in Punjab and a British Asian revival from the 1980s. OCR asks for performers by name: Ustad Tari Khan and Balwinder Safri will both do.

Do not mix up the two systems. Raga is melody, tala is rhythm, and chaal belongs to bhangra, not to classical music. And never stop at naming a feature: “it uses a tala” earns nothing, while “the tabla plays a 16-beat teentaal cycle, with the bass lifting off at khali on beat nine” earns the mark.
