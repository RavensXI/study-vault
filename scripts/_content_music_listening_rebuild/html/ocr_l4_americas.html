Two carnivals, one rhythmic idea

Both recordings are carnival music, and both are built on syncopation inside a duple frame. Track one is a Rio de Janeiro bateria: the percussion band of a samba parade. The song is Salgueiro’s 1993 samba-enredo, ‘Peguei Um Ita No Norte’, played here live by the Rio group Monobloco. Track two is a steelband from Trinidad.

The Trinidad All Stars are playing their Panorama arrangement of ‘Curry Tabanca’, a calypso by Winsford Devine, arranged for the band by Leon ‘Smooth’ Edwards. Calypso carries news, gossip and political comment in its words; the steelband takes the tune and rebuilds it for a competition stage.

1 · The whole band, at once

A whistle and the repinique, the leader’s calling drum, bring the band in. Within two seconds everything is playing: surdos on the beat and the deepest one answering, tamborins cutting semiquavers across the top, agogo bells, caixa and shakers. Confirm the metre first. This is duple, two beats in a bar.

Listen for: which beat the deepest drum owns. In samba the low surdo answers on the second beat, not the first.

2 · The song arrives

The singing enters over a groove that does not change to accommodate it. A samba-enredo is written to be sung by thousands of people while they walk, so the tune is simple, the range is narrow and the refrain comes round quickly. Listen to how fast the crowd joins in: that is call and response in a stadium.

Listen for: the percussion carrying on underneath, unaltered. The song sits on the groove; it does not lead it.

3 · The break

The groove stops and the whole bateria plays one figure together, then drops back in as if nothing happened. Brazilians call this a break or paradinha. It is rehearsed, cued by the leader, and it is the clearest evidence you can give that a bateria is one instrument played by many people.

Listen for: everybody stopping on the same semiquaver. Nobody drifts, because the leader called it.

4 · The groove does not move

A later verse of the song. Compare it with the first: the words and the melody have moved on, the percussion has not. Samba structure is a repeating ostinato under a song in verses and refrains, and the interest comes from the breaks, the singers and the sheer weight of the band, not from the pattern changing.

Listen for: 3+3+2 accents pulling against the steady pulse while everything else stays put.

5 · The refrain, everybody

The last full statement of the refrain, sung by the whole crowd. In a parade this is what the judges and the public hear most, so a samba-enredo is judged partly on how singable its refrain is. Structure worth naming: introduction, verse, refrain, break, verse, refrain — strophic, with the bateria constant throughout.

Listen for: the density at the end. More voices, the same groove, and a surdo still answering on beat two.

1 · A hundred oil drums

The announcement finishes and the orchestra starts. Steel pans are tuned from oil drums, sunk and hammered into note patterns; a large band has tenor pans on the melody, double seconds and cellos in the middle, and bass pans underneath. Behind them the engine room — irons, scratcher and drum kit — keeps the rhythm.

Listen for: the irons cutting through the pans. That metallic pulse is the engine room, and it never stops.

2 · The tune itself

The calypso melody arrives, high in the tenor pans. Calypso came out of Trinidad’s carnival and out of West African call and response song; the words carry news, teasing and political comment, and a calypsonian can make a national argument in a chorus. Here the words are gone and the tune alone has to carry it.

Listen for: syncopation in the tune: the melody notes land between the beats the irons are marking.

3 · Variation, not repetition

A new section of the arrangement. A Panorama piece takes a calypso lasting three minutes and rebuilds it into eight: new counter-melodies, new countermelody, changes of key and changes of texture. The tune stays recognisable and everything around it is rewritten.

Listen for: the tune underneath the new material. Say what has changed around it, and what has not.

4 · The lift

The whole band lifts into a new key. A modulation late in a Panorama arrangement is a standard device: it raises the excitement without changing the tune, and on a stage with no amplification it is the loudest thing an arranger can do. Name the effect and the reason, not just the fact.

Listen for: the same melody suddenly sitting higher and brighter. Everything moves up together.

5 · Strumming

The middle pans take the foreground with repeated strummed chords rather than the melody. This is where a steelband sounds least like an orchestra and most like a giant guitar section: the texture is chordal and rhythmic at once, with the melody instruments answering around it.

Listen for: chords on the off-beats. The strumming is a rhythm part as much as a harmony part.

6 · The finish

The arrangement drives to its closing chords and the crowd takes over. Both recordings end the same way: not with a fade, but with a full ensemble stop that everybody has rehearsed. In carnival music the ending is part of the performance, because a competition is being won or lost on it.

Listen for: the last chord and how tightly it is placed. Then compare it with how the samba track ends.

Say it precisely in the exam

Samba: 2/4, semiquaver subdivision, surdo answering on beat two, tamborim, agogo, caixa, repinique and ganza; a bateria of a samba school playing a strophic samba-enredo with rehearsed breaks. Calypso and steelband: duple metre with heavy syncopation and 3+3+2 accents; tenor pans, double seconds, cellos and bass pans, with an engine room of irons, scratcher and kit; Panorama arrangements add countermelodies, strumming sections and a late key change. Context: both are carnival music, both African-descended, both competitive.

The marks are in the detail. “It is lively and syncopated” earns nothing. “The low surdo answers on the second beat while the tamborims cut semiquavers across it” earns the mark, because it names the instrument, the beat and the relationship between the two.
