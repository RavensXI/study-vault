Afro Celt Sound System: Release

The title track of the 1999 album Volume 2: Release. Three traditions at once: Irish traditional instruments, West African griot instruments and electronic dance production. There is no verse and chorus here; the track is built by layering and taken apart again.

The band formed in the mid-1990s around the producer and guitarist Simon Emmerson, with close links to Peter Gabriel’s Real World Studios and the WOMAD festival. Fusion is not a side effect of that history: it is the band’s whole purpose.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Atmosphere before pulse

Nothing here is in time yet. A sustained synthesiser drone holds a fixed note underneath a few exposed fragments, and that is the whole texture. Drones belong to Irish traditional music and to West African and Asian traditions alike, so the track opens on ground both sides already share.

Listen for: how little is playing. This sparseness is the measure against which everything later is judged.

2 · The engine starts

Percussion arrives and the track finds its pulse. Underneath sits a programmed beat, made in the studio rather than played live, which will not change for the rest of the piece. It is the anchor: however much the other rhythmic layers pull against each other, this keeps them locked together.

Listen for: the steady electronic pulse under the hand percussion. Say ‘programmed’, not ‘drum kit’.

3 · The lament

The voice enters over layers that are already running, which is why it feels like an arrival rather than a start. The style is close to sean-nós, the unaccompanied Irish tradition of highly ornamented, rhythmically free singing, and the intense wailing tone is described as keening. It works as a lament, not a pop hook.

Listen for: ornament on almost every long note. Name the style, and say the vocal is a lament rather than a chorus.

4 · The traditions meet

With the voice out of the way you can hear the fusion working. Irish uilleann pipes and whistle offer a phrase; the West African kora or drums answer or overlap it. Nobody plays the same tune together: the traditions keep their own identity and pass material between them over the programmed pulse.

Listen for: phrases handed from one tradition to the other. That dialogue is the fusion, not the fact of playing at once.

5 · Built back up

The singer comes back and the layers rebuild around her. Notice that nothing new has been composed: the same loops, drones and patterns are simply muted and reintroduced. That is dance-music architecture, a breakdown followed by a rebuild, applied to acoustic folk material.

Listen for: which layer returns first. Being able to name the order of entries is worth more than calling the track busy.

6 · Everything at once

The final section brings every strand back together: Celtic melody, West African cross-rhythm and the electronic engine underneath. The track does not end on a decisive chord; it fades. That is a production decision as much as a musical one, and it is typical of the dance tradition this band draws on.

Listen for: the density at its greatest, then the fade. Compare it directly with the opening drone you heard first.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: fusion of Irish traditional music, West African griot music and electronic dance production. Forces: uilleann pipes, whistle, fiddle and accordion; kora, talking drum and djembe; voice; synthesisers, loops and programmed beats. Structure: a layer-by-layer build, a breakdown, a rebuild and a fade, with no verse or chorus. Texture: drone foundation with melodic dialogue above it. Rhythm: Irish lilt and West African cross-rhythm over a steady programmed pulse. Context: 1999, from Volume 2: Release, made at Real World Studios.

Pair each instrument with its tradition and its moment. “There are lots of instruments” earns little; “the drone underpins the texture while whistle and kora exchange phrases above it” earns the mark.
