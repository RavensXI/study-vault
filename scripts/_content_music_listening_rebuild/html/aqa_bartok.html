Four pictures of Hungarian village life

Hungarian Sketches, Magyar Képek, is an orchestral suite Bartók assembled in 1931 from five short piano pieces written about twenty years earlier. Each movement is a small picture of rural life: a village at dusk, a dance, a drunk stumbling home.

Your exam asks for four of the five: Evening in the Village, Bear Dance, Slightly Tipsy and Swineherd’s Dance. Movement 3, Melody, is not on the specification. From 1906 Bartók travelled Hungarian and Romanian villages with a phonograph, recording peasant song with Zoltán Kodály; the modal scales, short repeated phrases and dance rhythms he found there are what you hear in all four.

1 · A sigh at the end of the day

The movement is in arch form — A B A1 B1 A2 — a symmetry Bartók was known for. The A sections are marked Lento rubato, slow and speech-like, and the melody is a mainly descending legato phrase on solo woodwind, built from the F sharp minor pentatonic scale and the Aeolian mode on F sharp.

Listen for: one woodwind line over very little else. The scoring here is only woodwind, two horns and strings.

2 · Not everyone's evening is calm

The B section is marked Allegretto, nearly twice the speed. Its melody is the opposite of the A melody in every respect: staccato and conjunct quavers, moving by step, over short pizzicato rather than long held notes. Both melodies still come from the same F sharp minor pentatonic scale.

Listen for: the change of articulation. Same scale, opposite character, is the point of the contrast.

3 · The slow music, varied

The Lento rubato music returns, but varied rather than copied. Throughout the movement the texture is melody and accompaniment, with different woodwind instruments taking the solo line in turn over chordal accompaniment — either long held notes or pizzicato crotchets. Dynamics run from ppp to forte.

Listen for: which woodwind has the tune this time. Bartók rotates the solo colour rather than repeating it.

4 · The quick music, decorated

The Allegretto material comes back, and the giveaway detail is the decoration. Trills are added that were not there the first time. Underneath, Bartók avoids traditional cadences and uses non-functional chords, so however folk-like the tune sounds, the key stays deliberately unclear.

Listen for: trills on the returning dance tune. Naming what is added on a return earns more than spotting the return.

5 · Closing the arch

The final slow section completes the shape: A B A1 B1 A2. The movement is mostly 4/4, but bars of 3/4 and 2/4 near the end unsettle the ending, so the arch closes without quite squaring up. That refusal to resolve neatly is deliberate, and it matches the harmony.

Listen for: the metre going out of step near the close. Say ‘bars of 3/4 and 2/4’, not ‘the rhythm changes’.

6 · Follow the pedal note

Bartók described this as the impression of a bear dancing to its leader’s song, growling to a drum. It is in varied strophic form, and the easiest way to hear the sections is to follow the pedal note: A sits on a D, held in cellos, double basses, horns and tuba, and the melody above uses the Aeolian mode on D sharp.

Listen for: one low note that will not move. Everything else is measured against it.

7 · A tritone away

The pedal moves to A flat, now in the second violins, and the melody uses the Lydian dominant scale on D. D to A flat is a tritone, the most dissonant interval there is, so the shift is deliberately harsh. Phrases stay mostly four bars long, and the dissonance is what gives the dance its excitement.

Listen for: the pedal jumping to a new note in a higher instrument. Name the interval, not just the change.

8 · Back to D, plus a triangle

The D pedal returns and with it a new colour: a triangle joins here. Bartók adds two trumpets, two trombones, tuba, timpani and two side drums to the forces of the first movement, and he is exact about how they play — the brass sometimes muted, the strings both bowed and plucked.

Listen for: the triangle. On a return, an added instrument is the clearest thing you can name.

9 · Snares on

The A flat pedal comes back, and the side drums, which began without snares, now switch them on. The texture revolves around the pedal notes, which work as a rhythmic drone: repeated quavers in the pedal and the snare build anticipation while the melody moves mainly in crotchets, harmonised in thirds, sixths and octaves.

Listen for: the drum changing from a dull thud to a rattle. That is the snares being engaged, and it is examinable.

10 · The bear stops

The final D section closes the symmetry and the movement ends on a D major chord. The crotchet melody carries the rhythm of the Hungarian kanász folk dance. Dynamics run from piano to forte with sforzando stabs, and there is a great deal of staccato throughout.

Listen for: a clear D major chord at the end. After all that tritone dissonance, the ending is unusually plain.

11 · Stumbling, not walking

True to its title, this movement portrays someone stumbling rather than walking straight. It is in ternary form. Almost every note of the opening melody is decorated with acciaccaturas, and that single feature does most of the work in creating the tipsy feel. The melody is built on the interval of a perfect fourth.

Listen for: the violins col legno, muted and at the tip of the bow for a thin, quiet sound.

12 · Two motifs, one key change

The B section sets two ideas against each other: an off-beat accompaniment pattern alternating with a smooth legato melody, the second motif descending by step. The music begins major and moves to G minor here, with parallel chords, chromatic harmony and dissonant added-note chords keeping the tonality ambiguous.

Listen for: the tonality refusing to settle. Name G minor and the added-note chords rather than saying it sounds odd.

13 · The same, but drunker

The A material returns significantly developed rather than simply repeated. The marking is Allegretto rubato, so the music speeds up and slows down; it is mostly 4/4, but a few bars of 2/4 and one bar of 5/4 unsettle the phrasing, and slur-staccato articulation makes the whole movement sway.

Listen for: the pulse leaning and recovering. Dynamics swing from pianissimo to forte with sudden changes.

14 · The tune arrives in pieces

The suite ends with the only genuine collected folk tune in it: Bartók recorded it in Tolna County in 1907. The movement opens over a drone, with fragments of the melody appearing before it properly arrives. The drone sets the tonal centre on A, and the mode is mixolydian on A.

Listen for: a held note underneath and scraps of tune above it. The drone is doing the work of a key signature.

15 · The folk melody

The first half of the collected melody enters, bars 1 to 16. It opens with leaps of fourths and fifths and then moves by step, sitting quite high. The woodwind carry it — piccolo, flute, oboe and clarinet — over strings playing both bowed and pizzicato, muted and unmuted.

Listen for: the shape: wide leaps first, then stepwise motion. That contour is what Bartók collected, not what he invented.

16 · The drone moves

The second half of the melody, bars 17 to 33. The drone shifts to D before returning to A, and G sharps in this half lean the music towards G major. The metre is 2/4 throughout and the marking is Allegro molto: quick and lively, with moments of calm and changes of tempo.

Listen for: the drone note changing. In a piece with no key signature to speak of, that is the modulation.

17 · Round again

After a reworked second half and a four-bar link, the first half of the melody returns. An ostinato rhythm — quaver, crotchet, quaver — drives many sections, while the horns and trumpets carry syncopated melodies against it. This movement has more sections than any other in the suite.

Listen for: the three-note rhythm underneath. Count the sections: introduction, A, B, A1, B2 and coda.

18 · Held back for the coda

The second half returns fragmented and developed, and Bartók holds back its final two bars. The coda is then built on exactly those two bars, so the tune only completes itself at the very end. Right at the close, two solo violins play harmonics.

Listen for: a melody deliberately left unfinished, then finished. Dynamics run from pianissimo to fortissimo throughout.

Say it precisely in the exam

Evening in the Village: arch form A B A1 B1 A2, Lento rubato against Allegretto, F sharp minor pentatonic and Aeolian on F sharp, woodwind with two horns and strings. Bear Dance: varied strophic, sections marked by a D pedal against an A flat pedal a tritone away, ending on D major. Slightly Tipsy: ternary, acciaccaturas on almost every note, col legno strings, Allegretto rubato with bars of 2/4 and 5/4. Swineherd's Dance: a genuine folk tune collected in 1907, mixolydian on A over a drone, 2/4 Allegro molto, ending on violin harmonics.

Name the element or technique, give its correct technical term, and say exactly where it happens. “Bartók uses folk music” is too general. “Bartók builds the Allegretto dance tune of Evening in the Village on snap rhythms” names the feature, uses the term and locates it — and make sure each term is attached to the right movement.
