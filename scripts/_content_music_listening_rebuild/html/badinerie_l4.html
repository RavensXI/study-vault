The same movement, element by element

Bach’s closing movement of the Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor, BWV 1067: solo flute with strings and continuo, in B minor, 2/4, fast, and cast in binary form.

The previous lesson followed the form from A to the final cadence. This one stops five times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on here: melody, texture, tonality, dynamics and tone colour.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · What the flute is made of

Take the line apart and it is three things. Rising and falling scales; broken-chord patterns; and short ideas repeated immediately a step higher or lower, which is sequence. All of it in unbroken semiquavers at speed, which is what makes the part virtuosic and why players need double-tonguing and long breath control.

Listen for: scale, arpeggio, sequence. Name which one you are hearing rather than calling the part ‘fast’.

2 · One line that matters

Underneath the flute, the strings are doing very little: sustained chords or simple quaver patterns. That is melody-dominated homophony, one melody plus accompaniment, and it is deliberate, because the flute must never compete for attention. Occasionally the violins echo a fragment of the flute’s idea, a brief touch of imitation.

Listen for: how simple the accompaniment is. Then listen for the violins answering, which is the only counterpoint here.

3 · Two keys, and how they relate

The movement uses two main key areas. It starts in B minor and the first half closes in F sharp minor, the dominant minor: a closely related key, one sharp away, not the relative major. The second half sets off from there and touches D major, which is the relative major, before coming home.

Listen for: the change of colour at the halfway cadence. Name F sharp minor as the dominant minor and you have the mark.

4 · Steps, not slopes

Volume here changes in blocks. A phrase is played loudly and then repeated softly, an echo effect, because Baroque instruments could not swell gradually the way a modern piano can. That is terraced dynamics, and it is the only dynamic device the movement uses.

Listen for: an immediate quiet answer to a loud phrase. If you hear no crescendo at all, that is the correct observation.

5 · The sound of the period

This is a period-instrument performance, so the colours are not modern ones. The soloist plays a traverso, a wooden flute with a softer, breathier tone than a metal one. Beneath it, the harpsichord realises the figured bass: it improvises chords from the written bass line rather than reading them out.

Listen for: the woody flute tone and the plucked harpsichord underneath. Name the continuo instruments, not just ‘the accompaniment’.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: continuous semiquavers built from scales, arpeggio figures and sequences, virtuosic throughout. Harmony and tonality: B minor to F sharp minor, the dominant minor, across A, then through related keys including D major back to B minor. Texture: melody-dominated homophony with occasional imitation from the violins. Rhythm, metre and tempo: 2/4, fast, with almost no rests in the solo line. Dynamics: terraced, with echo effects. Timbre: Baroque transverse flute, strings, and continuo of cello with harpsichord realising the figured bass.

Attach each term to a moment. “It uses sequence” earns little; “the flute repeats a broken-chord figure a step lower each time, driving the harmony forward” earns the mark.
