The same number, element by element

Stephen Schwartz’s act one finale from Wicked (2003), for two solo voices with a pit orchestra of strings, woodwind and brass plus a rock rhythm section of electric guitar, bass, drum kit and keyboards.

The previous lesson followed the number section by section. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on: rhythm, melody, motif, texture, tonality and dynamics with orchestration.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · A pulse at last

The opening had no steady beat, because the lines followed the rhythm of speech. Now the accompaniment settles into continuous quavers, a repeating rhythmic ostinato under everything. Against that busy pattern the singer holds long notes, so the two layers pull in opposite directions: still voice, restless band.

Listen for: held notes above driving quavers. That contrast is the texture examiners most often ask you to describe here.

2 · A tune that climbs

The title phrase is set to a rising figure with a wide upward leap on the key word. Schwartz also colours several phrases with the pentatonic scale, a five-note scale with no semitone steps, which sounds open and unforced even while the harmony underneath is doing something more complicated.

Listen for: the shape rising while the accompaniment stays busy. Name the leap and say what it depicts.

3 · The idea inside the idea

This short rising motif returns through the number, each time a little higher, which is sequence. Schwartz has said it nods to the opening interval of the famous rainbow song from The Wizard of Oz, but he changes the rhythm and where it falls in the bar, so it echoes rather than quotes.

Listen for: the same shape arriving higher each time. Call the film reference a disguised homage, never a direct quotation.

4 · Layers you can count

Two voices sound together over the full band. Count what is there: two vocal lines, sustained string and keyboard pads, and the rhythm section underneath. Musical theatre builds its textures the way pop does, by adding layers, and the number of layers is what changes across the song.

Listen for: how many separate parts you can pick out. Say the number and what each one is doing.

5 · Keys that lift

The music steps up into a higher key for the last stretch. That is a step-up modulation, standard in musical theatre. What is not standard is that at least one of the shifts moves to a key a third away with a change of mode, a chromatic mediant, which shares few chords with the old key and so sounds sudden and slightly magical.

Listen for: the whole texture sitting higher. Naming the chromatic mediant is the detail that lifts an answer.

6 · Everything, at once

This is the loudest and thickest point in the number. The full pit orchestra doubles the melody, the rock section drives underneath, and on stage a chorus is added. The soloist belts a sustained note above it all. Nothing here is marked gently: the volume is built by adding players and pushing the voice.

Listen for: the scoring at its fullest under the highest note. Describe the build as a thickening of texture, not just as ‘louder’.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: rising shapes with wide upward leaps, pentatonic colouring, and a recurring motif extended by sequence. Harmony and tonality: minor-tinged uncertainty at the start, step-up modulations including a chromatic mediant, ending firmly in D flat major. Texture: sparse chords, then held vocal notes over a driving quaver ostinato, then two voices with full band. Rhythm: free speech rhythm becoming continuous quavers. Orchestration: pit orchestra with rock rhythm section, thickening throughout. Dynamics: built by adding layers, to a belted climax.

Attach each term to a moment. “It uses sequence” earns little; “the rising motif returns a step higher each time, so the tune seems to climb as the lyric describes flight” earns the mark.
