The same track, element by element

Esperanza Spalding’s 2008 recording of a 1962 song by Baden Powell and Vinicius de Moraes: a bossa nova in a minor key, sung in Portuguese, for voice with acoustic bass guitar, acoustic guitar and strings.

The previous lesson followed the arch from the solo bass to the thinned close. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on here: texture, melody, rhythm, improvisation, harmony and dynamics.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Two parts, one musician

Count the parts: bass, then voice, and nothing else. That is a duet texture. The bass is doing two jobs at once, supplying the chord roots that hold the harmony and answering the singer with shaped melodic phrases. Naming that double function is worth more than calling the opening sparse.

Listen for: the bass moving from harmony to melody and back. One performer, two musical roles.

2 · The line

The vocal line moves in long, flowing phrases, mostly by step, with very little decoration. Even once the tempo arrives it keeps a rubato freedom, leaning slightly ahead of or behind the beat. The Portuguese lyric is kept from the original, which is what anchors the recording in its Brazilian identity.

Listen for: how unhurried the phrasing is. Describe it as lyrical and flexible, not as slow.

3 · Off the beat, on purpose

The accompaniment now plays a repeating chordal pattern, and its accents fall slightly off the main beats. That syncopation is the rhythmic fingerprint of Brazilian popular music, and this way of playing it is called comping. Above it the vocal stays smooth and long-noted, so tight and relaxed rhythms run at the same time.

Listen for: the gap between a busy accompaniment and an unhurried voice. That contrast is the bossa nova style itself.

4 · Invented on the spot

The singing stops and an extended solo takes over. The soloist improvises: the melody is being invented now, over the chord pattern the song has already set up. That procedure comes from jazz, not from the Brazilian song tradition, and it is the clearest single sign of the fusion.

Listen for: the familiar chords continuing underneath an unfamiliar tune. Say what stays fixed as well as what is invented.

5 · Chords with extra notes

The key is minor, which gives the song its reflective, bittersweet colour. The chords are not plain triads though: bossa nova and jazz both favour extended chords, sevenths and ninths and more, voiced smoothly so that the added notes colour rather than clash. Spalding keeps that sophistication instead of simplifying it.

Listen for: chords that sound richer than three notes. Name the tonality and the extension together.

6 · Restraint as a style

Across five minutes nothing is ever pushed. The dynamic range is narrow and the vocal stays soft and unforced even when the ensemble is at its fullest, and the track ends by removing layers rather than by getting louder. Set that beside Release, where the same fusion problem is solved by density.

Listen for: how little the volume changes. Restraint here is a deliberate style, not a lack of intensity.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: long, flowing, largely stepwise phrases sung in Portuguese with rubato freedom. Harmony and tonality: minor key with extended jazz chords, sevenths and ninths. Texture: a duet of voice and acoustic bass guitar, thickening slightly with comping and strings and thinning again. Rhythm: rubato at first, then syncopated bossa nova comping under a smooth vocal line. Improvisation: an extended solo invented over the established harmony. Dynamics: consistently soft and unforced, with the close made by subtraction.

Attach each term to a moment. “It is syncopated” earns little; “the chordal accompaniment accents just off the beat while the vocal line stays smooth above it” earns the mark.
