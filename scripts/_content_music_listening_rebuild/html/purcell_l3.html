The same song, element by element

Purcell’s theatre song of 1692: one voice over a ground bass, with the bass pattern repeating from the first bar to the last while the vocal line above changes. The continuo realises chords from a figured bass over a cello or bass viol.

The previous lesson followed the shape from the bare ground to the closing cycles. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on: texture, phrasing, tonality, melody, harmony and ornamentation.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Two layers, nothing hidden

One singer, one accompanying group. That is melody-dominated homophony: a single melodic line over harmony. Because there are so few layers, every small gesture in the voice is exposed — a late entry, a leaning note, a rest. Occasionally the voice echoes a shape from the bass, which adds a brief imitative.

Listen for: how little is sounding. Purcell chose that transparency so the word painting can be heard.

2 · Phrases that do not fit

The bass repeats in fixed lengths. The voice refuses to agree with it. A vocal phrase ends part-way through a bass cycle, so the joins between repetitions are covered over. That is phrase overlap, and naming it is the quickest way to show you have heard more than ‘a bass that repeats’.

Listen for: one bass cycle beginning while the singer is still in the middle of a line.

3 · A ground that moves

The pattern in the bass has not changed, but its pitch level has. Purcell lifts the whole ground into a different key as the text turns to the Furies, so the harmony darkens without the bass ever stopping. The music modulates and later works its way home.

Listen for: the familiar bass shape sounding higher or lower than before. Say that the ground has changed key, not merely that the mood changes.

4 · The line above

The vocal writing is mostly conjunct, moving by step, which gives it its smooth, consoling quality, with occasional leaps saved for important words. Here a single syllable is spread over a run of notes: a melisma, used for text about things that do not end.

Listen for: the syllable that will not stop. Name it as a melisma and say which idea in the text it illustrates.

5 · Tension and release

The bass is fixed, so Purcell’s invention goes into the chords he builds above it. Listen for a suspension: a note held over from the previous chord that clashes, then falls by step on to a note that fits. He also colours the harmony with chromatic notes at the darkest points of the text.

Listen for: the clash and its resolution, in that order. A suspension is heard as tension, then release.

6 · What the singer adds

The voice has finished and the continuo plays the ground out. Look back over what the singer did to the line: trills and appoggiaturas at cadences and phrase endings. Much of that was not written down. Baroque performers decorated by convention, so no two performances of this song are identical.

Listen for: how much decoration falls at the ends of phrases. That placement is the convention, not an accident.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: mostly conjunct with expressive leaps and melismas on significant words. Harmony: largely diatonic, with suspensions and chromatic colouring as the text darkens. Tonality: the ground itself shifts key part-way through. Texture: melody-dominated homophony, voice and continuo only, with brief imitation of bass shapes. Rhythm and phrasing: vocal phrases overlap the joins of the repeating bass. Ornamentation: trills and appoggiaturas at cadences, largely added by the performer.

Every term needs a moment. “There are suspensions” earns little; “a note is held over the changing bass, clashes, and falls by step on to the new chord” earns the mark.
