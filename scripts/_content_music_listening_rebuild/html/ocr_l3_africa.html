Layers that lock together

Two West African traditions sit side by side here. Track one is Malinke drumming from Guinea: a lead djembe, the three dundun drums with bells fixed to them, and singers. Track two is the kora, the 21-string harp of the Mande jeli families, played by Sona Jobarteh of the Gambia. Both are learnt by ear and handed on by oral tradition.

The method is the same in both. Short repeating patterns, ostinati, are stacked one on another. Because the layers group the same bar in different ways they pull against each other — that is polyrhythm, and where two groupings collide head on it is cross rhythm. A lead player calls the music in, signals the changes and improvises above it.

1 · The call, then the layers

The lead djembe opens alone with a short call. That phrase is the signal to start, and within five seconds the bells and the three dunduns — dundunba, sangban and kenkeni — have entered and the groove is running. The pulse is compound: every beat splits into three, so the music rolls rather than marches.

Listen for: the ORDER in which the layers enter. Follow one layer at a time before you judge the whole texture.

2 · Call and response

A single voice sings a phrase and the group answers with a fixed reply. That alternation is call and response, and it is not only a vocal device: the lead drummer calls and the ensemble answers in exactly the same way. Underneath, the accompanying drum patterns carry on unchanged, so the answer always lands in the same place.

Listen for: alternation, not accumulation. One voice, then the group, over a drum pattern that never breaks.

3 · The master drummer takes over

The lead djembe pulls clear of the ensemble and improvises. Listen to how the accompaniment behaves: the bell, the dunduns and the second djembe repeat their ostinati exactly as before. That is the point of an ostinato-based texture. Because the ground stays fixed, the solo can cut across it and the cross rhythm stays audible.

Listen for: conflicting groupings of the same bar. The solo is measured against the layers, not against a conductor.

4 · Song, solo, song

The singing comes back and the drums drop into their supporting role. The structure of the whole piece is this alternation of sung sections and drum solos, and there is no score deciding when each one arrives. The lead drummer signals the changes, the ensemble hears them and follows. That is oral tradition working live.

Listen for: the join between solo and song. Somebody cued it, and the cue is audible in the lead drum.

5 · The texture thins

Parts fall away and the lead drum is left with far less around it. Texture is the exam word here, and it is the easiest mark in the topic to score badly: say which layers stopped and which carried on. A thinned texture in this music is a deliberate structural event, not a mistake or a fade.

Listen for: which parts drop out. Name them, and name what is left.

6 · Everyone stops together

The piece ends with the whole ensemble stopping on the same stroke. Nobody counted a final bar: the lead drummer played a break that the players recognise, and they finished on it. In an answer about how these musicians work together, that ending is your strongest single piece of evidence.

Listen for: the break that warns the ending is coming, and the exactness of the stop that follows it.

1 · Twenty-one strings

The kora plays alone. It has 21 strings running over a bridge above a calabash resonator, and the player plucks with both thumbs and both index fingers, so one pair of hands can hold a bass line, a repeating pattern and a melody at once. The pattern you can hear looping is the kumbengo.

Listen for: one player producing several layers. The kumbengo is an ostinato, exactly like the drum patterns opposite.

2 · Adding parts

Guitar and calabash percussion come in over the kora. The principle is the one you met in track one: the music grows by adding layers to an ostinato that is already running, not by changing key or theme. Notice that the kora keeps its kumbengo going underneath the moment the others arrive.

Listen for: how texture builds with added parts. Say which instrument enters, and over what.

3 · The jeli sings

The voice enters. Sona Jobarteh comes from one of the Gambia’s principal griot families, and she is among the first women to perform professionally on an instrument traditionally handed from father to son. A jeli is a musician, a praise singer and an oral historian, which is why this repertoire carries so much history in it.

Listen for: the voice riding over a pattern that never stops. The kora does not pause to accompany it.

4 · Birimintingo

The singing stops and the kora breaks into fast decorative runs above the accompaniment. That improvised flourishing is called birimintingo, and it is the counterpart of the master drummer’s solo: a fixed ground beneath, invention on top. Both traditions build their solos the same way.

Listen for: the kumbengo continuing underneath the runs. If it stopped, this would not be birimintingo.

5 · Winding down

The performance ends by letting the pattern run out rather than by a Western cadence. Structure in this music is cyclic: sections are as long as the players decide, and the end is agreed in performance. Saying so, and giving the evidence, is worth more than naming a form that was never used here.

Listen for: the absence of a final chord. The music stops; it does not resolve.

Say it precisely in the exam

Instruments: lead djembe (bass, tone and slap), dundunba, sangban and kenkeni with attached bells, and the 21-string kora over a calabash. Rhythm and metre: compound pulse, ostinato layers, polyrhythm and cross rhythm. Texture: layered, built by adding parts, thinned deliberately. Structure: cyclic, alternating song and solo, with no notation. Devices: call and response, improvised djembe solos, kumbengo and birimintingo. Context: Malinke ensemble music from Guinea, and the hereditary jeli or griot tradition of the Mande world.

Marks come from mechanism, not atmosphere. “It sounds tribal” earns nothing. “The bell plays an unchanging timeline while the lead djembe improvises across it, so the two groupings cross” earns the mark, because it names the parts, says what each is doing and says how they relate.
