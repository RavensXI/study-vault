Beethoven: Pathétique Sonata, first movement

Composed in 1798 for solo piano, in C minor and common time: a slow Grave introduction, then an Allegro di molto e con brio in sonata form. Beethoven titled it himself — Grande Sonate Pathétique — and dedicated the sonata to his patron Prince Karl von Lichnowsky.

Haydn and Mozart wrote sonatas for the drawing room. Beethoven writes this one as public theatre, and the radical stroke is that the slow introduction refuses to stay at the beginning: it comes back twice inside the fast movement.

1 · The introduction

C minor, very slow, and immediately dramatic. A loud dotted rhythm chord is answered by a hushed one; the harmony is unsettled by diminished seventh chords that belong to no single key. The texture is homophonic, so nothing distracts from the gesture itself. Fix this sound: you will meet it twice more.

Listen for: the loud-then-quiet pairs. That contrast, not the tune, is what the introduction is made of.

2 · The storm arrives

Tempo, dynamic and texture all change in a single bar. The first subject climbs restlessly in the right hand over a hammering tremolo in the left, in the tonic key of C minor. This is the exposition of the sonata form beginning, and the shock comes purely from placing it against what went before.

Listen for: the left hand shuddering underneath. If you hear fast repeated notes under a rising tune, you are in the first subject.

3 · The wrong minor

A bridge passage modulates away and a smoother, more singing theme appears. The expected key would be E flat major, the relative major of C minor. Beethoven gives E flat minor instead, so the contrast arrives still in shadow; only later does it brighten into E flat major, where a closing theme finishes the exposition.

Listen for: a theme that sings rather than drives, but stays dark. Naming E flat minor rather than E flat major earns the mark.

4 · Everything again

If this sounds familiar, it should: the pianist takes the repeat and plays the whole exposition a second time. Use it. Listen through the repeat with the three landmarks in mind — first subject, bridge, second subject — and check that you can hear each join without looking at the numbers.

Listen for: the same music in the same order. This is the free practice run the movement gives you.

5 · The introduction breaks in

The fast music stops and the Grave comes back. In 1798 an introduction was disposable; Beethoven brings his back to open the development, so the slow and fast worlds of the movement turn out to belong to each other. Its diminished sevenths return with it.

Listen for: the tempo collapsing. A sudden change of tempo mid-extract is almost always the Grave coming back.

6 · Working the material

The fast tempo is running again and the development is under way. No new tune appears: Beethoven fragments the first subject and the bridge, passes motifs between the hands and drives them through unstable keys by sequence. The recapitulation then brings the first subject home to C minor.

Listen for: short scraps of the first subject rather than the whole theme, and harmony that refuses to settle.

7 · The last word

The Grave appears once more, for a few bars only. By now the recapitulation has run its course, taking the second subject through F minor before settling in C. What follows is a short coda built from first-subject material at the same fierce tempo, ending abruptly in C minor. The dark key wins.

Listen for: the moment of held-breath stillness before the final rush. Nothing brightens; the movement simply stops.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: Classical solo piano sonata, first movement. Forces: one pianist. Key: C minor, with the second subject in E flat minor turning to E flat major, and F minor in the recapitulation. Metre and tempo: common time throughout, Grave against Allegro di molto e con brio. Structure: slow introduction plus sonata form, with the Grave returning at the start of the development and again before the coda. Texture: homophonic, melody over tremolo. Harmony: chromatic, with diminished sevenths. Context: 1798, Vienna, dedicated to Prince Karl von Lichnowsky.

Name the section and the key together. “It changes key” earns little; “the second subject arrives in E flat minor rather than the expected E flat major” earns the mark.
