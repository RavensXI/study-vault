Three Queen tracks, equal weight

Your study pieces are Bohemian Rhapsody, Seven Seas of Rhye and Love of My Life. Questions can ask about any of them, so know all three equally. The band: Freddie Mercury (lead vocals, piano), Brian May (electric guitar, vocals), John Deacon (bass guitar) and Roger Taylor (drum kit, vocals).

Bohemian Rhapsody and Love of My Life come from A Night at the Opera (1975); Seven Seas of Rhye from Queen II (1974). The set versions are the 2011 remasters. The spread is deliberate: one multi-sectional epic, one compact hard rock single, one ballad. Revise them side by side.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Use the track buttons to switch songs, and tap any number to jump.

1 · Voices alone

The song opens a cappella, voices with no instruments at all, moving in homorhythmic block chords. It is in B flat major, and the very first chord is a B flat 6, an unusual added-sixth. Watch the metre too: the introduction contains single bars of 5/4 and 9/8 among the 4/4. Listen for the portamento slide on ‘reality’.

Listen for: one rhythm shared by every voice. The whole song is through-composed: no repeating chorus anywhere.

2 · Piano takes over

The piano enters and the ballad begins, still in B flat major, with arpeggiated accompaniment rippling under the voice. The word-setting is almost entirely syllabic. Mercury had a four-octave range and uses it: soaring falsetto in places, belted chest voice in others. Listen for the Scotch snap on ‘sympathy’.

Listen for: the melody rising at the line about looking up — word painting, the music acting out the words.

3 · May takes a chorus

At the end of the ballad the guitar takes over with a virtuosic solo. Name what makes it so: fast scalic passages, slides, bends, vibrato and distortion, played high on the instrument. It is a composed melodic statement in its own right rather than filler between vocal sections.

Listen for: the guitar singing rather than strumming. Name two techniques, not just ‘a guitar solo’.

4 · The opera section

The key jumps to A major, unrelated to the home key — an abrupt modulation. The ‘Galileo’ motif uses repeated notes and a rising semitone; ‘Bismillah!’ works as question and answer, the refusal landing in octaves and the group replying in homophonic chords. The drums copy the voices’ rhythms rather than keeping a beat.

Listen for: voices panned across the stereo field. The staccato crotchet opening imitates the comic characters of real opera.

5 · The rock section

Everything changes at once: E flat major, heavy overdubbed electric guitar, and a shift into 12/8. That is a compound time signature, so each of the four beats now splits into three. Dynamics swing to fortissimo interjections here, the loudest the song gets.

Listen for: count four strong beats, each carrying a triplet. The rest of the song is almost all 4/4.

6 · It ends unfinished

The music falls away to piano for a quiet, reflective close. The song ends on an F major chord — the dominant of B flat major, the key it began in. That is why the ending feels unresolved rather than settled: the harmony is left pointing home without ever arriving.

Listen for: the last chord, and ask which key it belongs to. Ending away from home is the point, not an accident.

1 · Ten bars of piano

A hard rock song in D major, released in February 1974 — the single that made Queen’s name. It opens with a ten-bar piano introduction: an arpeggiated riff in octaves, in semiquavers, with the right hand rippling against the left in a cross-rhythm. Power chords are already there underneath.

Listen for: the two hands clashing deliberately. Name the cross-rhythm rather than calling it ‘busy’.

8 · The verse hits

The band arrives and the verse lands on power chords, bare root-and-fifth chords with no third, which is the hard rock sound. Listen for blues notes colouring the vocal line. There is no chorus anywhere in this song: instead a vocal hook closes each verse.

Listen for: a chord with no major or minor quality to it. That missing third is what makes a power chord.

9 · The bridge

This is the contrast section. The mode shifts, a new melody breaks the verse pattern, and the passage ends with a long held vocal cry that launches the guitar solo. Listen for palm-muting on the guitar in the first bridge, which damps the strings for a tighter, drier attack.

Listen for: the change of mode and the held cry at the end. A bridge is defined by contrast, not by position.

10 · Away and back

The guitar takes the melodic lead between sung verses. Track the keys: the solo moves to B flat major before returning to D, and the second bridge modulates to G major. A delay effect closes the solo — the signal repeated back at itself a fraction later.

Listen for: the key shifting under the solo. Naming B flat major beats saying ‘it changes key’.

11 · A different world

The song dissolves into a music-hall singalong of ‘I Do Like to Be Beside the Seaside’, played on a stylophone. Ending a hard rock single on a Victorian seaside tune is deliberate pastiche: the style flips completely, and the joke is the point.

Listen for: the genre switching in a single bar. Name the borrowed tune and the instrument playing it.

12 · Home, almost

The ballad of the three, and the one to choose for a texture or sonority question. Home is F major, but the harmony travels constantly, and naming where it goes is where the marks are. The short introduction, bars 1 to 7, implies F major and then slips to B flat major in its second phrase.

Listen for: a key established and immediately left. Give the bar numbers if you can — this song is examined on detail.

13 · The verses

Bars 8 to 31. The two verses start in C major and modulate to F major. Around Mercury, who sings in his tenor range with moments of falsetto, sit layered backing vocals, harp and arpeggiated piano. The harp is played by Brian May, who taught himself the instrument for this song.

Listen for: an orchestral harp inside a rock band. That is not a normal rock sonority, and it is examinable.

14 · An echo

Bars 31 to 35, in B flat major. This passage echoes the second phrase of the introduction, so material you heard in the first seven bars comes back without the voice. Recognising a returning idea, and saying which earlier passage it belongs to, is worth more than describing the sound.

Listen for: a phrase you have already heard, now instrumental and in B flat major.

15 · Into the minor

Bars 36 to 44. The music turns to D minor, the relative minor of F major, passing through G minor on the way. This is the darkest point in the song’s harmonic journey, and it is reached by step rather than by a jolt.

Listen for: the shift from major to minor colour. Name D minor and the route through G minor, not just ‘it gets sadder’.

16 · Three keys

Bars 45 to 58. The guitar solo begins in F major, moves to C major, then B flat major. Three keys in fourteen bars is the clearest demonstration in the set of how restless this song’s harmony is, and every one of those keys is nameable in an answer.

Listen for: the ground shifting under the solo three times. Location plus key name is the full mark.

17 · Home at last

Bars 59 to 67. The coda begins in D minor and finishes in F major: the song only settles at home in its last bars. Set that against Bohemian Rhapsody, which deliberately ends away from home, and you have a comparison that writes itself.

Listen for: the final arrival in F major. Two of the three tracks have no chorus; this one has no early resolution either.

Say it precisely in the exam

Bohemian Rhapsody: through-composed, no chorus; a cappella and ballad in B flat major, opera in A major, rock section in E flat major and 12/8, ending on F major; roughly 180 vocal overdubs. Seven Seas of Rhye: hard rock in D major, February 1974; ten-bar piano introduction, power chords, palm-muting, a solo through B flat and a stylophone seaside pastiche. Love of My Life: F major ballad travelling through C, B flat, D minor and G minor, with harp, layered backing vocals and arpeggiated piano.

Marks come from three things: name the element, give its exact technical term, and say where in the track it happens. “The harmony is interesting” earns nothing. “The opera section shifts abruptly to A major, an unrelated key” earns the mark — element, term, location.
