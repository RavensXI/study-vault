The same finale, element by element

Bach’s finale of about 1721 sets a concertino of flute, violin and harpsichord against a string ripieno with continuo. D major, Allegro, 2/4, and built as a fugue inside a da capo A B A frame.

The previous lesson walked the movement from start to finish. This one stops at six moments, one for each element of music an examiner can ask you about: melody, dynamics, tonality, timbre, texture and metre.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · The shape of the subject

Every bar of this movement grows from one tune. Its shape is worth learning: running quavers and semiquavers, mostly conjunct, with small leaps that spell out the chord underneath. There are almost no long notes. Later Bach stretches it by sequence, lifting the same short pattern up or down a step at a time.

Listen for: the flute repeating the violin’s tune note for note. Fix that rising-then-falling shape in your ear — you must spot it later in any instrument.

2 · Volume in steps, not slopes

Listen to what happens to the loudness here, and to what does not. The sound jumps, then stays. A Baroque string band cannot swell gradually, so Bach changes volume by adding or removing players: three soloists, then the whole ensemble. That is terraced dynamics, and it is the only dynamic device this movement needs.

Listen for: a step up in volume, not a slope. If you hear no crescendo anywhere in the movement, that is the point, not an accident.

3 · Name the keys

The movement is anchored in D major, the home key of the whole concerto. Here it turns to B minor, the relative minor. The central section also touches A major, the dominant, before working back. Baroque tonal planning is exactly this: leave the tonic, visit closely related keys, return decisively.

Listen for: the change of colour from bright to shadowed. In the exam, write ‘it modulates to B minor, the relative minor’ — naming the key earns the mark, ‘it modulates’ does not.

4 · Telling them apart

Three solo timbres, three different sounds. The harpsichord is plucked, so every note starts sharply and dies away at once; the flute is breathy and sustained; the violin is bowed, and can hold and swell a single note. One trap to avoid: the famous unaccompanied harpsichord cadenza belongs to the first movement, not to this one.

Listen for: the plucked attack cutting through. Decide by attack and decay, not by how high or low the line is.

5 · The full weave

The ripieno is back, and the texture is at its thickest. Even now the lines stay independent, which is what makes this polyphonic rather than a tune with chords: each part carries its own material, and entries imitate each other. Underneath it all the continuo bass keeps the harmony moving.

Listen for: how many separate lines you can follow at once. Answers score when you say who imitates whom, not just ‘polyphonic’.

6 · Count it in two

The movement rushes to its close, and the lilt is unmistakably that of a gigue. That is the trap: the character is dance-like, but the metre is 2/4, a simple duple beat, not the 6/8 a gigue usually takes. Tap along and you will find two beats in a bar, each divided into ordinary halves and quarters.

Listen for: two firm beats per bar under all that semiquaver motion. Feel the pulse, then count it, before you write down a time signature.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: one fugal subject, conjunct with chordal leaps, extended by sequence and decorated with trills. Dynamics: terraced, made by adding or removing players. Tonality: D major, through B minor and A major and back. Timbre: plucked harpsichord against sustained flute and bowed violin. Texture: polyphonic and imitative, with a continuo bass; thickest when the ripieno plays. Metre and tempo: 2/4, Allegro, gigue-like in character. Harmony: functional, driving to perfect cadences.

Each of those words is worth a mark only when it is attached to something audible. “Terraced dynamics” earns little; “the volume jumps at bar 29 because the ripieno is added, with no crescendo” earns the mark.
