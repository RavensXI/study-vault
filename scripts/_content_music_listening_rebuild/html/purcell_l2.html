Purcell: Music for a While

A theatre song of 1692 for solo voice and basso continuo, built over a ground bass: a short bass pattern repeated from the first bar to the last. Broadly a ternary shape sits on top of that unbroken bass, so two structures run at once.

Purcell wrote it as %s for a revival of Dryden and Lee’s tragedy Oedipus. In the play the song is sung to raise the spirit of a murdered king, so it has to be beautiful and slightly uncanny at the same time.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · The pattern, on its own

Purcell gives you the ground bass before anything else, unaccompanied by any voice. It is a short phrase, mostly moving by step with a falling shape at its start, and it will now repeat without a break until the song ends. The continuo realises chords above it from a figured bass.

Listen for: the shape of the bass. Everything that follows is measured against this one pattern.

2 · Voice over ground

The singer comes in and the texture becomes two layers: one melody over accompanying harmony, which is melody-dominated homophony. Notice at once that the vocal phrases do not stop where the bass pattern stops. That deliberate mismatch is called phrase overlap, and it is what stops strict repetition sounding mechanical.

Listen for: the joins. Try to hear where each bass cycle restarts, and how rarely the voice restarts with it.

3 · The Furies arrive

The words turn from easing pain to Alecto and the dead. Purcell answers by letting the ground move out of its home key for a while, so the fixed pattern is not as fixed as it seemed, and by colouring the harmony with notes from outside the key. The ground does not change; its key does.

Listen for: the same bass shape sounding at a new pitch level. That is the moment to say the ground has changed key.

4 · Drop, drop, drop

This is the passage everyone quotes. On the word describing the snakes falling, Purcell writes a repeated, falling figure so that the music does what the word says. That is word painting, and it is the single clearest example in the set work. Elsewhere he uses long melismas for words about endlessness.

Listen for: the repeated descent. Name the device and the word together, and say what the effect is.

5 · Back to the beginning

The first words come back and the song rounds itself off. This is the return that makes the shape ternary, but it is not a copy: Purcell varies the vocal line, which is why it is labelled A prime rather than a second A. The ground has been running underneath the whole time, unbroken.

Listen for: the tune you know, decorated differently. Say ‘varied return’, not ‘repeat’.

6 · The last cycles

The voice stops and the instruments are left with the pattern. Purcell ends the way he began, which frames the whole song in the ground bass, and the last statements settle on to a closing cadence. The stillness is the point: nothing has changed underneath from first bar to last.

Listen for: the same bass you heard before the singer entered. That symmetry is worth naming in an answer.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: Baroque theatre song, incidental music. Forces: solo voice with basso continuo, a chordal instrument realising harmony over a bass instrument. Structure: a continuous ground bass beneath a broadly ternary vocal shape, A B A prime, framed by instrumental statements of the ground. Texture: melody-dominated homophony, two layers, with phrase overlap disguising the joins. Harmony: mostly diatonic, with suspensions and chromatic colouring as the text darkens, and one shift of key in the ground. Context: 1692, for a revival of Dryden and Lee's Oedipus.

Tie the label to the moment. “There is a ground bass” earns little; “the ground is heard alone before the voice enters and again after it finishes, framing the song” earns the mark.
