Three Spalding tracks, three fusions

Esperanza Spalding is an American jazz bassist, singer and composer. I Know You Know and I Adore You come from Esperanza (2008); Little Fly comes from Chamber Music Society (2010). In 2011 she became the first jazz artist to win the Grammy for Best New Artist.

All three are fusions. They mix jazz harmony and improvisation with Brazilian samba rhythm, and, in Little Fly, with chamber-music strings. Be precise about the bass: a fretless bass guitar in I Know You Know, a double bass in the other two.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Use the track buttons to switch songs, and tap any number to jump.

1 · The riff everything grows from

The strings enter one by one over a bass line that will dominate the song. It is a chromatically descending pizzicato riff played with double stopping, so one instrument sounds two notes at once. That riff returns about twenty times — forty of the piece’s sixty-three bars.

Listen for: two notes from one bass. Count the scoring: voice, double bass and a string trio — four string instruments, but not a string quartet.

2 · Blake, set syllabically

The words are William Blake’s poem ‘The Fly’, and the setting is strophic: every verse takes the same music. The voice opens with a descending three-note pattern in E major, repeated, moving mostly by step and setting the words syllabically. The riff carries on without the double stopping, then stops altogether.

Listen for: the word ‘oh’ at the verse end, falling in three chromatic steps.

3 · The ensemble answers

The same vocal melody returns, but the texture around it has grown. A violin countermelody joins, the ensemble adds interjections, and the viola recalls its own melody from the introduction. Spalding sings in the alto range, moving into head voice with vibrato in the second half of each verse.

Listen for: the strings using trills to suggest the fly itself. The poem’s first four verses make the song’s two.

4 · Improvised close

Blake’s last verse becomes the coda. The trio plays semibreve tremolos while the voice and bass improvise together over a sustained arco chord. After a whole song built on one strict repeating riff, the ending is the one passage where the players are free.

Listen for: the bow replacing the pluck. That change of technique is the clearest signal that the coda has begun.

5 · One line, no key

The track opens monophonic: a solo fretless bass riff with nothing accompanying it. The riff avoids the first beat of every one of its seventeen bars, and it opens on dominant Cs. The home key of F major is being withheld from the very first note, and it will stay hidden until the chorus.

Listen for: a bass that slides between notes rather than stepping. That is a fretless bass.

6 · Still hiding

Very fast 4/4, around 176 beats per minute. The chords that join the riff dodge the chord of F, and the first sung note is G, the supertonic. Underneath, the samba lives in the rhythm section: ganza, a shaker; caixa, a Brazilian snare drum; and a triangle.

Listen for: percussion, not a drum kit. Name ganza and caixa rather than calling them shakers and drums.

7 · F major, at last

The chorus finally lands on F major, and it does so through the standard jazz cadence ii–V–I — G minor 7, C7, F — with every chord carrying a seventh. The melody adds blue notes, especially A flat and D flat. Off-beat piano chords give a double-time samba feel, and the syncopations layer into polyrhythm.

Listen for: the moment the harmony settles. Naming that first chorus chord as the arrival of F major is a ready-made answer.

8 · Somewhere else entirely

The interlude modulates to B major, a distant key from F. Over it sits a vamp — a short chord pattern repeated underneath improvisation — for piano and fretless bass solos. Improvisation is built into the form here, not added on top of it.

Listen for: how far the harmony has travelled. B major and F major share almost nothing, which is why the shift is startling.

9 · Back and out

The music snaps back to F for a double chorus and ends. Production here is light but nameable, which is exactly what an answer needs: some reverb, some panning across the stereo field, and EQ shaping the balance. This is a studio jazz recording, not a live capture.

Listen for: the chorus arriving twice over. Say ‘double chorus’ rather than ‘it repeats the chorus’.

10 · No key at all

Samba instruments fade in: surdo and ocean drum among them. Nothing here has a definite pitch, so the track literally begins in no key. That is unusual enough to be worth a sentence on its own, and it is answered by the ending, which does the same thing in reverse.

Listen for: percussion with no pitch. You cannot name a key here because there is not one yet.

11 · Voice as instrument

The voice enters, then the double bass with the main groove, and the vocals move in thirds. There are almost no conventional lyrics: the singing is scat and vocalise throughout, and the spoken rhythm of the title phrase forms the main melodic hook.

Listen for: a voice used as an instrument rather than as a storyteller. Name scat and vocalise separately.

12 · Three motifs at once

Three repeating motifs are layered together here, and this is the chorus of the piece. The music settles into G mixolydian, a major-sounding mode with a flattened seventh. Even now the tonality stays ambiguous, with dissonant piano interjections cutting across the voice.

Listen for: how many separate repeating patterns you can pick out. Name the mode, not just ‘it sounds major’.

13 · Passing it round

The piano solos first, over drums and bass; later the voice and double bass take over. Improvisation is shared rather than owned by one player, which is a jazz convention operating inside a samba texture — the fusion working at the level of who plays, not just of what is played.

Listen for: the solo changing hands. Say which instrument has it and what the rhythm section is doing underneath.

14 · Ending in no key

The samba instruments are left alone and the track fades. No final chord ever confirms a key, so the piece ends exactly as it began: unpitched percussion and nothing to analyse harmonically. That symmetry is a strong exam sentence, because it is a structural observation and a harmonic one at once.

Listen for: the absence of a final chord. A fade-out on percussion is a decision, not an accident.

Say it precisely in the exam

Little Fly: Blake's poem set strophically in E major for voice, pizzicato double bass and string trio, built on a chromatically descending riff in double stopping. I Know You Know: samba-jazz at about 176 bpm that hides F major until a ii-V-I chorus, with fretless bass, ganza, caixa and triangle, and a B major solo vamp. I Adore You: scat and vocalise over G mixolydian, opening and closing on unpitched samba percussion so no key is ever confirmed.

Precision with instruments matters and is easy to lose: fretless bass guitar in I Know You Know, but double bass in Little Fly and I Adore You; ganza and caixa, not just percussion; a string trio plus bass, not a string quartet.
