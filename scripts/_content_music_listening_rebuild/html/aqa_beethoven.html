A symphony that starts in the wrong key

Beethoven’s Symphony No. 1 in C major, completed and first performed in 1800, is the compulsory set study piece. A late-Classical orchestra, C major, and a first movement built as a slow Adagio molto introduction followed by an Allegro con brio in sonata form.

Beethoven wrote it as a former pupil of Haydn and an admirer of Mozart, using the four-movement plan and standard orchestra they had established. What he does inside that inheritance is the point: the piece is often described as standing on the boundary between the Classical era and the Romantic one he helped create.

1 · The wrong chord

The movement opens slowly, bars 1 to 12. The very first chord is a dominant seventh — but it belongs in F major, not in the symphony’s home key of C major. For 1800 that was a striking way to begin, and the introduction goes on touching harmonic areas without settling in any of them.

Listen for: a chord that pulls somewhere, then does not go there. Most symphonies before 1800 state the home key at once.

2 · C major, at last

The wait ends and C major finally lands, with far more force for having been delayed. The music is in alla breve, written 2/2, so there are two strong beats a bar rather than four. Short repeated motifs drive it, sudden sforzando accents jolt the texture, and a rapid crescendo pushes it forward.

Listen for: the release of tension. Name the arrival of the tonic, not just ‘it gets faster’.

3 · The wind answers

A contrasting theme arrives in a related key: the second subject. Listen to who plays it. The orchestra is a standard late-Classical one — pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, pairs of horns and trumpets, timpani and strings — and Beethoven gives the wind its own melodic material rather than doubling the strings.

Listen for: wind tone carrying the tune. Clarinets were still a new addition to the symphony orchestra in 1800.

4 · Both subjects again

The whole exposition is played a second time, as Classical convention expects. Use it: you now know what the first subject and the second subject sound like, so listen through the repeat and check you can hear the join between them and the change of key that comes with it.

Listen for: the same two ideas in the same order. This repeat is the free practice run the movement gives you.

5 · Taken apart

No new theme appears here. The development takes fragments of what you have already heard and pushes them through unstable, shifting keys, so a tune you know keeps surfacing in unfamiliar surroundings. Recognising material in pieces rather than whole is the skill this section tests.

Listen for: scraps of the first subject rather than the whole theme, and harmony that will not settle.

6 · Home

The themes return, and this time they stay in C major. That is what a recapitulation does: it restates the exposition’s material with the tonal argument resolved, so the second subject no longer moves away to a related key. After all the delay at the start, the home key now sounds settled rather than withheld.

Listen for: the same themes, but with the key fixed. Say which section you are in and what the key is doing.

7 · Hammering it home

A closing passage rounds the movement off. The coda drives emphatic cadences that leave no doubt about C major — the key the opening chord deliberately avoided. Across nine minutes Beethoven has turned the home key from something withheld into something insisted upon.

Listen for: how firmly it stops. Compare that certainty with the ambiguous chord you heard at the very beginning.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: Classical symphony, first movement. Forces: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, pairs of horns and trumpets, timpani and strings. Key: C major, approached through an introduction that avoids it. Metre and tempo: Adagio molto, then Allegro con brio in alla breve. Structure: slow introduction, then sonata form with the exposition repeated, a development, a recapitulation and a coda. Dynamics: sforzando accents and rapid crescendos. Context: 1800, written by a pupil of Haydn on the edge of the Romantic era.

Support each point by naming the element, using its technical term and locating it in the music. Weak: “the music sounds exciting and grand” — no element, no term, no location. Strong: “the first subject, from around 1:50, creates energy through short repeated motifs and sudden sforzando accents, before a rapid crescendo drives the music out of the ambiguous Adagio molto introduction.”
