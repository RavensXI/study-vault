The same track, element by element

Afro Celt Sound System’s title track from Volume 2: Release (1999): Irish traditional instruments, West African griot instruments and electronic dance production, built by layering rather than into verses and choruses.

The previous lesson followed the build from end to end. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam sets questions on here: harmony, rhythm, melody, instrumentation, texture and technology.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · A fixed foundation

There is no chord sequence pushing this music forward. Instead a drone holds a fixed note, or a narrow group of notes, while everything else moves above it. That is how harmony works in Irish traditional music and in many African and Asian traditions too, so the drone is common ground rather than one side’s idea.

Listen for: the note that never changes. Say the harmony is drone-based, not that the track ‘has no harmony’.

2 · Two traditions, one pulse

Three rhythmic layers are working at once. The Irish melodic lines carry a lilt, the swinging dance feel of jigs and reels. The West African percussion adds cross-rhythm, accents that do not line up with the main beat. Underneath, an unchanging programmed pulse holds both in place.

Listen for: two rhythmic feels pulling gently against each other. The programmed beat is what stops them coming apart.

3 · Decorated lines

Melody here is not a tune with a chorus; it is a decorated line. The vocal is close to sean-nós, the unaccompanied Irish style with free rhythm and heavy ornament, and the pipes and whistle decorate in the same way, with rolls, cuts and grace notes. The vocal functions as a lament over the groove.

Listen for: how few notes are left plain. Name the ornament type, not just ‘it sounds decorated’.

4 · Both sides, by name

Marks come from pairing each instrument with its tradition. From Ireland: uilleann pipes, tin whistle, fiddle and button accordion. From West Africa: the talking drum, whose pitch bends as the player squeezes its cords, the kora, and the goblet-shaped djembe played with bass, tone and slap strokes.

Listen for: which family answers which. Learn the six names and which side each belongs to.

5 · Dialogue, not unison

The layers come back one at a time. Above the drone the texture is built from dialogue: a phrase from the whistle or pipes is answered or overlapped by the kora or the voice, over repeating ostinato patterns in the percussion. The traditions converse; they almost never play the same line together.

Listen for: call and response between named instruments. That is the sentence examiners want, with the names in it.

6 · The studio as a player

Treat the production as an instrument, because the specification does. Short sections are looped so percussion and bass run continuously while live playing is added on top; sampling, reverb and filtering blend acoustic timbres with electronic ones; and muting layers is what creates the breakdown and this final build.

Listen for: how seamlessly acoustic and electronic sit together. That blend is a production achievement, not an accident.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: ornamented Irish lines and a sean-nos style vocal lament, with rolls, cuts and grace notes. Harmony: drone-based, with no driving chord progression. Rhythm: Irish lilt and West African cross-rhythm over a steady programmed pulse. Texture: drone foundation with call-and-response dialogue and layered ostinato patterns. Instrumentation: uilleann pipes, whistle, fiddle and accordion against kora, talking drum and djembe. Technology: loops, samples, reverb and filtering, used to build, break down and rebuild the track.

Pair the term with the tradition and the moment. “It uses cross-rhythm” earns little; “the West African percussion accents beats off the programmed pulse, so two rhythmic layers pull against each other” earns the mark.
