The same movement, element by element

Beethoven’s first movement of 1798, for solo piano: C minor, common time, a Grave introduction followed by an Allegro di molto e con brio in sonata form, with the Grave returning twice inside the fast music.

The previous lesson followed that shape from end to end. This one stops six times, once for each element of music the exam asks about: dynamics, texture, tonality, harmony, melody, and tempo with metre.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Loud, then suddenly quiet

The first chord is heavy and the answer to it is hushed. That is an fp attack: a strong sound cut off into a quiet continuation. Beethoven also drops sforzando accents on to beats you do not expect. There is no gentle swelling here, only sudden change, and that is the emotional temperature of the piece.

Listen for: the size of the drop between the two halves of each gesture. Describe the contrast, not just the volume.

2 · One pianist, one orchestra

The Allegro is a melody over an accompaniment, but listen to what the accompaniment is doing. The left hand plays a tremolo — a note repeated so fast it becomes a shudder — imitating tremolo strings. Beethoven also uses wide-spaced chords that span most of the keyboard, so a single player fills an orchestral space.

Listen for: the repeated notes underneath rather than the tune on top. Melody over tremolo is the sound of the first subject.

3 · The key you did not expect

A second theme arrives, and what matters is its key. From C minor the standard move is to E flat major, the relative major. Beethoven writes E flat minor, so the contrast keeps the shadow, and only later opens into E flat major. In the recapitulation the group appears in F minor first.

Listen for: a change of theme that is not a change of mood. The mark is for naming the minor, not for hearing a modulation.

4 · Chords with nowhere to go

The Grave’s harmony comes back with the Grave itself. A diminished seventh chord is built entirely from minor thirds, so it belongs to no single key and can be pushed in several directions at once. Beethoven uses it, with other chromatic chords, to keep the ear from ever settling.

Listen for: harmony that sounds tense without resolving. Say why it is unstable, not just that it is.

5 · Themes taken apart

The development introduces no new tune at all. Beethoven takes short pieces of the first subject and the bridge and repeats them a step higher or lower, which is sequence, tossing them between the hands in imitation. Recognising a familiar theme in fragments is exactly what the exam tests.

Listen for: scraps rather than whole themes. If you know the tune but cannot find all of it, you are in the development.

6 · Two speeds, one bar length

Grave breaks in one final time before the close. Notice what changes and what does not: the tempo swings between Grave and Allegro di molto e con brio, but the metre stays common time throughout. The coda ends the movement quickly and abruptly, in C minor.

Listen for: a change of tempo, not of time signature. Say ‘the tempo changes to Grave’, not ‘the metre changes’.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: rising first subject, lyrical second subject, fragmented by sequence and imitation in the development. Harmony: chromatic, driven by diminished sevenths. Tonality: C minor, second subject in E flat minor turning to E flat major, F minor in the recapitulation. Texture: homophonic, melody over tremolo, with wide-spaced chords. Dynamics: sudden fp contrasts and sforzando accents rather than gradual change. Tempo and metre: Grave against Allegro di molto e con brio, common time throughout. Timbre: the full range and weight of the piano.

Attach every term to a moment. “Chromatic harmony” earns little; “diminished sevenths in the Grave leave the key uncertain, which is why the opening sounds so unsettled” earns the mark.
