Irregular beats, decorated melodies

Track one is Arabic art music from Aleppo in Syria. A takht — the small classical ensemble of oud, qanun, ney, violin and riq — plays a samai. It opens with a solo taqsim: pure improvisation with no pulse at all. The melody follows a maqam, and some of its notes fall between the notes a piano can play.

Track two is a kalamatianos, a Greek circle dance, played by clarinet, violin, santouri, laouto and tambourine. This Area of Study also asks for Palestinian and Israeli folk music, and all of it shares the same two signatures: irregular metres built from unequal groups of equal fast pulses, and melodies decorated far more heavily than a Western tune would be.

1 · No pulse at all

The oud plays alone, and there is no beat to count. A taqsim is improvised: the player works through the notes of the maqam, testing each one, pausing where the phrase wants to pause. Listen to the pitches. Several of them sit between the keys of a piano, and those microtones are what makes the mode sound like this one and no other.

Listen for: pitches between the piano’s notes, and the absence of any pulse. Free rhythm is itself the answer.

2 · Ten beats, and everyone at once

The whole takht arrives with the percussion and a cycle starts turning. The rhythmic pattern, or iqa, has ten beats, unequally grouped, so it never settles into a march or a waltz. Notice the texture: every instrument is playing the same melody, but each one decorates it differently. That is heterophony.

Listen for: count the fastest steady pulse per bar. Ten, in unequal groups, means an irregular cycle.

3 · A section for one player

A single instrument steps forward and the rest fall back to accompany. A samai is built from separate sections, and each one gives different material to the ensemble; some are shared, some belong to one player. The cycle underneath does not change, so the soloist is measured against a beat you can still count.

Listen for: which instrument has the melody, and how the others change what they play to make room for it.

4 · The refrain comes back

The opening ensemble music returns after the contrasting section. That alternation is the whole structure of a samai: a new section, then the refrain, then another new section, then the refrain again. The effect is closer to a rondo than to anything that develops, and it makes the form easy to describe in an answer.

Listen for: music you have already heard, returning unchanged. Name it as a returning refrain, not as a repeat.

5 · Into a faster six

The closing section shifts into a quicker, lilting metre in groups of three, and the riq and darabuka are at their most exposed. A metre change at the end is standard in this form. Say which way it moves: from an unequal ten to an even, rolling six, and from stately to dancing.

Listen for: the change of grouping, then the change of character. Both are worth marks; only one is obvious.

1 · Count to seven

The dance begins immediately, with the clarinet leading. Count the fastest steady pulses in a bar and you get seven, not eight, grouped 3+2+2. That long group at the front is why the dance limps forward: the first step is longer than the two that follow it. Try marching in two or waltzing in three and neither will fit.

Listen for: the accent pattern. Where is the LONG group? At the front, and that is what makes it a kalamatianos.

2 · A new tune, same seven

A different melody, over exactly the same rhythm. Greek dance tunes are built from short strains that are repeated, decorated and handed round the band. The metre never changes: it is the ground the dancers hold on to, and changing it would stop the circle.

Listen for: the metre continuing underneath a completely new tune. Melody changes; the seven does not.

3 · Passing it round

A third melody, and the lead moves between the instruments. Behind them the laouto and the tambourine hold the rhythm, while the santouri fills the gaps. This division — melody instruments in front, plucked and struck instruments behind — is how a Greek dance band works, and it is worth naming as texture.

Listen for: which instrument has the tune at any moment. The answer changes more than once.

4 · Round it comes

The opening strain returns. The structure here is alternation, not development: tunes come back rather than growing. That is true of the Arabic samai on the other track as well, and of the tala and chaal cycles in the India and Punjab lesson. Traditional music across this whole Area of Study is built on return.

Listen for: a melody you recognise from the beginning. Say which one and where it came from.

5 · It stops on the beat

The final phrase arrives and the dance ends. There is no fade and no Western cadence: the tune finishes its phrase, and because the dancers need to know when to stop, the ending is exact. Compare it with the Arabic track, which also stops rather than resolving.

Listen for: the last accent. Everything in this music, including the ending, is measured in fast pulses.

Say it precisely in the exam

Arabic: takht of oud, qanun, ney, violin, riq and darabuka; taqsim, free rhythm and improvised; maqam with microtonal notes; heavy ornamentation; heterophonic texture; the iqa, a ten-beat cycle, changing to a faster six at the end; sections alternating with a returning refrain. Greek: kalamatianos in 7/8 grouped 3+2+2, an irregular or additive metre; clarinet and violin leading, laouto and tambourine driving, santouri filling; strains alternating. Both are dance and social music, learnt by ear and spread by recording and broadcast.

Never write “it sounds Middle Eastern”. Count first and name the number: seven fast pulses grouped 3+2+2, or a ten-beat cycle. Then name what the melody does: microtonal steps, heavy ornamentation, heterophony. Number plus grouping plus one melodic feature is a complete answer in two lines.
