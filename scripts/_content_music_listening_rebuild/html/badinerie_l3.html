Bach: Badinerie

The last movement of Bach’s Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor, BWV 1067, for solo flute with strings and continuo. B minor, 2/4, fast, and cast in binary form: two halves, each played twice. The French title means playful jesting.

A Baroque suite collected stylised dances. Around its four standard movements composers slipped in lighter galanteries — minuets, gavottes, a badinerie — and this one has outgrown the suite it belongs to.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Straight in

No introduction: the flute is already running. The movement opens in the tonic, B minor, with the solo line in continuous semiquavers over strings and continuo. Before this half is over the harmony will have moved to F sharp minor, the dominant minor, and closed there with a perfect cadence.

Listen for: the flute never stopping. Fix the opening shape now — you will hear it again in about fifteen seconds.

2 · The same half again

The music jumps back and plays the first half again. That repeat is written into the form, not a performer’s choice: binary form means A A B B. Use the second hearing to follow the journey from B minor to the cadence in F sharp minor, which is the point of this half.

Listen for: the arrival on a new key at the end of the half. Naming that key is what turns a form label into a mark.

3 · The second half

The B section begins from the F sharp minor that A reached. It does not stay: Bach steers it through related keys, touching D major, the relative major of B minor, before turning firmly for home. Nothing from the opening is restated wholesale, which is exactly what separates binary form from ternary form.

Listen for: the harmony travelling. This half is longer than the first, and it is longer because it is doing more work.

4 · And again

The second half is played through a second time. In Baroque performance a repeat is an invitation: players often add ornaments the second time round, so listen for anything decorative that was not there before. The written notes are identical; the performance need not be.

Listen for: small differences between the two hearings. If you hear none, that is an observation too.

5 · Home

The music lands on a perfect cadence in B minor, the key it started in. The whole movement has lasted under a minute and a half, and in that time it has left the tonic, cadenced in a related key, wandered further and returned. That departure and return is what binary form is for.

Listen for: how decisively it stops. Then say the shape out loud: A, A, B, B, B minor to F sharp minor and back.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: the closing galanterie of a Baroque orchestral suite. Forces: solo transverse flute, first and second violins, violas, and continuo of cello with harpsichord. Key: B minor, moving to F sharp minor, the dominant minor, at the end of A, and back through related keys including D major during B. Metre and tempo: 2/4, fast. Structure: binary form, A A B B, with no restatement of the opening inside B. Texture: melody-dominated homophony with occasional imitation. Dynamics: terraced. Context: Bach, BWV 1067, the only one of his four orchestral suites led by a solo flute.

Name the keys, not just the form. “It is in binary form” earns little; “section A modulates from B minor to F sharp minor and section B works back to B minor” earns the mark.
