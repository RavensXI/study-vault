The same song, element by element

Killer Queen, from Queen’s 1974 album Sheer Heart Attack, written by Freddie Mercury: lead vocal, stacked backing vocals, piano, electric guitars, bass and drums, in a bright major key and a rolling 12/8, shaped as verse-chorus form with a guitar solo.

The previous lesson followed the song section by section. This one stops six times, once for each element of music an examiner can set a question on: rhythm and metre, texture, harmony, timbre and studio technology, melody and dynamics.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Four beats, each split in three

Snap along and you will find four beats in the bar. Then listen to what fills each beat: three notes, not two. That is compound time, and it is why the song rolls rather than marches. The piano adds syncopation, pushing notes off the beat, which gives the accompaniment its music-hall strut.

Listen for: the triple subdivision inside each beat. If it swings but you can still count four, the answer is 12/8, not 4/4.

2 · From two layers to many

In the verse there were two layers: a voice and a piano, which is melody-dominated homophony. At the chorus that becomes a block of sound, because separate recordings of Mercury, May and Taylor singing harmony are stacked together. The harmony has not changed. Only the number of layers has.

Listen for: how many distinct vocal parts you can pick out. Say how many layers and what each is doing, not just ‘the texture gets thicker’.

3 · Chords with a twist

The song sits securely in a major key. Mercury colours it with chromatic chords that step outside the key and resolve straight back, and with runs of chords whose roots fall by fifths, the circle of fifths, which pulls the ear towards the cadence at the end of a phrase. Pop function, classical furniture.

Listen for: a chord that sounds momentarily wrong and then right again. Name it as chromatic, and say it resolves back to the key.

4 · The sound of the guitar

May plays a guitar he built himself, the Red Special, through a treble booster, which drives the amplifier into a singing, sustained tone. He layers several such lines by overdubbing, and adds ringing harmonics to imitate bells. Panning spreads them left and right; flanger and wah-wah pedal shape the tone further.

Listen for: several guitars moving in parallel, not one. Name the effect and describe what it does to the sound.

5 · The line and the delivery

The lead vocal is deliberately theatrical. It leaps widely rather than moving by step, stretches and clips the rhythm against the beat, and exaggerates consonants. That delivery is a kind of word painting: the manner of singing acts out the glamour the words describe: how a phrase is sung carries meaning.

Listen for: the size of the leaps and the freedom of the rhythm. Describe the effect, not the lyric.

6 · Loudness built from layers

The last chorus is the loudest point in the song, and nobody is playing harder. The increase comes from layering: backing vocals, guitar parts and the fuller mix are all kept in at once. Studio pop builds a climax by addition, where an orchestra would build one by playing louder.

Listen for: the difference between the first verse and this. Same tune, four times the sound, no crescendo marked anywhere.

Say it precisely in the exam

Melody: wide leaps and flexible rhythm, delivered theatrically. Harmony: major, with chromatic passing chords and circle-of-fifths movement into cadences. Texture: melody-dominated homophony in the verses, thickening to dense layered vocal and guitar harmony in the choruses. Rhythm and metre: 12/8 compound time with a rolling, syncopated lilt. Timbre and technology: Red Special guitar with treble booster, multitracked and harmonised guitar lines, harmonics, panning, flanger and wah-wah, produced with Roy Thomas Baker. Dynamics: built by layering rather than by playing louder.

Every one of those words needs a moment attached to it. “Multitracking” earns little; “the backing vocals at the first chorus are several recorded takes stacked together, which is why the texture thickens instantly” earns the mark.
