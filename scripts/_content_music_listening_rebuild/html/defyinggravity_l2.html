Schwartz: Defying Gravity

The act one finale of Wicked, the 2003 musical with music and lyrics by Stephen Schwartz, based on Gregory Maguire’s novel. Voices of Elphaba and Glinda over a pit orchestra of strings, woodwind and brass combined with a rock rhythm section of electric guitar, bass, drum kit and keyboards.

Its dramatic job is fixed by its position: Elphaba decides to reject the Wizard’s regime and accept being called wicked, and the music has to carry her from uncertainty to defiance in about five minutes, then send the audience into the interval.

Press play and the cards follow the music. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Not a song yet

This does not begin like a song at all. The vocal lines follow the rhythm and rise and fall of speech over sparse, sustained chords, a style close to recitative. There is no strong pulse and no repeating pattern, so your ear has nothing to hold on to. That is deliberate.

Listen for: words winning over melody. Say ‘recitative-like’ or ‘speech-rhythm’, not ‘the singing is quiet’.

2 · Melody takes over

The lines lengthen, the phrases start to shape themselves, and underneath them the accompaniment settles into continuous quavers. After the free opening, that regular pulse arrives as a release. From here the number runs on a verse-chorus core, and each time round it is bigger than the last.

Listen for: the moment a pulse appears. The change from free to measured is one of the clearest structural signposts in the set work.

3 · The hook

The title phrase is sung for the first time, and Schwartz sets it to a rising figure with an upward leap on the key word. In a stage number nothing is decorative: the tune climbs because the character is talking about flight. This is the material the whole rest of the song will keep transforming.

Listen for: the shape of the phrase, not just the words. A wide upward leap here is a picture of what is being sung about.

4 · Two characters at once

Until now the singers have taken turns. Here the two voices sound together, so the disagreement between them becomes audible as music rather than as dialogue. The ensemble writing is a reminder that this is a scene: the song is carrying a plot, not just a mood.

Listen for: two independent lines at once. Say who is singing what, and what the combination tells you about the drama.

5 · The key steps up

The whole texture moves up a level. Schwartz raises the key more than once across the number, each rise arriving at a higher emotional stake, and the final statement lands in D flat major. The step-up modulation makes the listener feel a physical ascent, which is exactly what the words describe.

Listen for: everything sitting higher than before. Name the device as a rising key change, and say why it fits the lyric.

6 · The last note

The final section abandons verses and choruses. Chorus material comes back transformed — higher, louder, doubled by the full pit orchestra and rock section, with a driving ostinato bass beneath — and the singer holds one sustained, belted note above it. The pitch never falls back before the end.

Listen for: the texture at its thickest under the highest note. That combination is what makes this the most quoted extract in the song.

Genre: musical theatre act one finale, a power ballad. Forces: two solo voices with a pit orchestra of strings, woodwind and brass plus a rock rhythm section. Structure: recitative-like dialogue, then a verse-chorus core that intensifies each time, then a transformed final section and a sustained coda. Tonality: rising step-up modulations, ending in D flat major. Texture: sparse chords at the start, thickening to full orchestra and voices. Rhythm: free at first, then continuous driving quavers. Context: Wicked, 2003, music and lyrics by Stephen Schwartz.

Every device here has a dramatic job. “The key changes” earns little; “the key steps up before the final section so the whole texture rises with Elphaba’s decision” earns the mark.
