Queen: Killer Queen

A 1974 single from the album Sheer Heart Attack, written by Freddie Mercury. Lead vocal, stacked backing vocals, piano, electric guitar, bass and drums, in a bright major key and a rolling 12/8, and shaped as verse-chorus form with a guitar solo.

It reached number two, Queen’s biggest hit to that point and a year before Bohemian Rhapsody. It is piano-led and closer to music hall and cabaret than to hard rock, and it was built in the studio with producer Roy Thomas Baker out of layers no four musicians could play at once.

Press play and the cards follow the music. The first five seconds are the television introduction. Tap any number to jump to that moment.

1 · Voice and piano

The song opens with finger snaps and piano before Mercury enters. What you hear now is the plainest texture in the whole song: one lead vocal carried on a syncopated, music-hall piano part, which is melody-dominated homophony. Nothing is competing with the voice, so the wit in the words lands. Remember this thinness — the song is measured against it.

Listen for: just two layers. Count them now, so you can hear how many are added later.

2 · The chorus arrives

The chorus does not simply get louder; it gets thicker. Mercury, May and Roger Taylor recorded take after take of backing vocals which were stacked into a choir-like block of sound behind the lead. The band called this the studio doing what the stage could not, and it became the sound people recognise as Queen.

Listen for: the moment the single voice becomes a crowd. That switch is the clearest structural signpost in the song.

3 · The same, but not the same

Verse two returns to the tune of verse one. Listen to what has changed underneath it: guitar lines have been added, and parts are spread left and right across the mix by panning. Each returning verse is a varied strophe, so the song grows without altering its harmony or its shape.

Listen for: new layers arriving rather than a new tune. That is how Queen keep a repeat from sounding mechanical.

4 · The solo takes over

The second chorus is cut short and the guitar takes the song. This is not an improvised rock solo: May harmonises the line with himself, overdubbing several guitar parts into a written-out instrumental chorus, complete with bell-like harmonics. It carries thematic weight, in the way a composer would use it.

Listen for: several guitars moving in parallel rather than one. That layering is the point of the passage.

5 · The voice comes back

The solo ends and a third verse begins. The song has now been through every section it owns: introduction, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, solo, verse. Nothing new is coming, so from here Queen build only by adding, and the outro that follows is the fullest sound in the record.

Listen for: the accompaniment already heavier than it was in verse one, before the last chorus has even started.

6 · The varied close

The last chorus is not a copy of the first. Mercury changes his delivery, the backing vocals are denser and the guitar layers stay in. That is why the closing section is called a varied chorus: the sense of climax comes from texture and dynamics, not from any new melody or chord.

Listen for: how far this is from the piano and single voice you heard at the start. Same tune, four times the sound.

Say it precisely in the exam

Genre: glam-era pop rock with music-hall and cabaret leanings. Forces: lead vocal, multitracked backing vocals, piano, electric guitars, bass, drums. Key: a bright major key. Metre and tempo: 12/8, a rolling compound lilt at a moderate pace. Structure: introduction, three verses and three choruses with a guitar solo after the second chorus and a varied final chorus. Texture: melody-dominated homophony that thickens by layering. Harmony: functional, coloured by chromatic chords. Context: 1974, from Sheer Heart Attack, a year before Bohemian Rhapsody.

Structure marks come from labels tied to what you can hear. “Verse-chorus form” earns little; “the second chorus is cut short after nine seconds so the guitar solo can begin” earns the mark.
