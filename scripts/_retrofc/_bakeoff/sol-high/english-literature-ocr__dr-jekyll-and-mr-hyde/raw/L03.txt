--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 5: Incident of the Letter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the Carew murder, Utterson visits Jekyll in his “cabinet” above the laboratory. Jekyll is “deadly sick”, but insists he is “done with” Hyde. He produces a letter signed by Hyde absolving him of blame. Utterson’s clerk, Mr Guest, compares the handwriting to Jekyll’s own and declares it a forgery — the letter Jekyll wrote <em>as Hyde</em> matches Jekyll’s hand in every feature except the slope. Stevenson plants <dfn class="term" data-def="Clues given to the reader to suggest or hint at later events.">foreshadowing</dfn>: the handwriting is literally the same because the writer is.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain, in one sentence, why Stevenson uses the handwriting clue rather than a direct confession at this stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The forged letter is Stevenson’s cleverest hint. It lets the reader register suspicion without fully understanding the truth — training us to accept the later revelation as earned, not imposed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Chapter 6: Remarkable Incident of Doctor Lanyon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Jekyll withdraws from society, then emerges briefly as his old self. Suddenly he refuses to see Utterson again. Utterson visits Lanyon instead and finds him transformed: “a death warrant written legibly upon his face… he had his death-warrant written there.” Lanyon says he has suffered “a shock” and refuses to name its cause. He dies within weeks, leaving a sealed letter marked “not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr Henry Jekyll.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Stevenson uses Lanyon as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character used to highlight qualities in another by contrast.">foil</dfn> to Jekyll. Both are men of science, but Lanyon is “hide-bound”, sticking to “the practical side of medicine”. He cannot survive the knowledge Jekyll has gained. The contrast argues that some boundaries exist for good reason.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Victorian Science and the fear of transgression</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The 1880s were a decade of rapid scientific advance and anxiety: Darwin’s ideas were reshaping biology, and newer ideas about psychology (including those of William James and early Freud) were questioning the unity of the self. Stevenson’s Jekyll stands for the <dfn class="term" data-def="Choosing to cross accepted moral or natural limits in search of knowledge.">transgressive</dfn> scientist who goes too far. Lanyon is the cautious alternative — and neither survives untouched.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The fear is not science itself, but the dissolution of the boundary between respectable professional and hidden appetites. This is why Stevenson makes the story about a <em>doctor</em>: the figure most trusted by Victorian society becomes the figure most dangerous.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Chapter 7: Incident at the Window</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Utterson and Enfield again walk by the cursed court. Jekyll appears at the upper window, “infinite sadness” on his face. Utterson invites him down; Jekyll declines. Then “the smile was struck out of his face and succeeded by an expression of such abject terror and despair, as froze the very blood of the two gentlemen below… the window was instantly thrust down.” Stevenson compresses the whole tragedy into a single moment of horror and silence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The chapter is short and placed centrally in the novella for good reason. Jekyll’s transformation must have started. Utterson and Enfield leave in silence, not daring to speak. Stevenson uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="Leaving something significant unsaid so the reader feels the weight of it.">silence</dfn> to signal the limits of respectable language: there is nothing Victorian gentlemen can say about what they have just seen.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Chapter 8: The Last Night</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Poole, Jekyll’s butler, arrives at Utterson’s in “white and shaky” terror. For a week a stranger has been locked in the cabinet crying out for a chemical that cannot be found. Poole is convinced the voice is not Jekyll’s: “That thing was not my master.” Utterson hears a voice whining: “For God’s sake have mercy!”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Utterson and Poole break down the door with an axe. They find Hyde’s body on the floor, a phial in his hand. There is no sign of Jekyll, but two documents: Jekyll’s will (rewritten to leave everything to Utterson), and a sealed packet — the “Full Statement” that forms Chapter 10. Stevenson leaves the chapter on a cliffhanger: the reader, like Utterson, does not yet know why Jekyll’s body is Hyde’s.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three pieces of evidence that convince Poole the voice in the cabinet is not his master’s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Poole notices three pieces of evidence: the voice sounds wrong, the hand glimpsed on the stair is ‘dwarfish’ and ‘hairy’, and a servant girl saw a creature ‘crying out like a rat’. The details rhyme with earlier descriptions of Hyde.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Setting: the Cabinet and the Laboratory</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Jekyll’s cabinet sits above the dissecting theatre of an old surgeon. Stevenson deliberately layers the house: a respectable front, a working laboratory, an inner cabinet locked away. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Mapping the layout of a building onto the structure of the self.">architectural metaphor</dfn> mirrors the Freudian-sounding model of the mind that the novella anticipates — surface, thought and hidden appetite.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why These Chapters Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Chapters 5–8 are the unravelling. Stevenson switches from planting clues to accumulating them until the reader can no longer resist the conclusion. The forged letter, Lanyon’s death, Jekyll’s face at the window and Hyde’s body on the cabinet floor are arranged as a ladder down from respectable surface to exposed depth.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The forged letter and Lanyon’s decline are carefully placed clues that teach the reader to read suspicion into every scene.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The window scene condenses the whole tragedy into a moment of wordless horror — Stevenson uses silence where language cannot go.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The last-night break-in provides bodily evidence but not explanation, forcing the reader into the written confessions of Chapters 9–10.</li>
</ul>
