--- DESCRIPTION ---
The door on Bye Street, Enfield’s story, the Carew murder, and Utterson’s growing dread.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 1: The Story of the Door</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Stevenson opens with the lawyer Mr Utterson and his cousin Enfield walking in a “by-street” in London. The contrast is deliberate: the street is well-kept, “clean”, lively — except for one “sinister” building with “a blistered and distained” door. Stevenson uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="Writing that creates a threatening, uncanny atmosphere through setting and suggestion.">Gothic</dfn> technique of the uncanny: a single wrong detail in an ordinary scene makes the reader suspicious of everything around it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain, in one sentence, why Stevenson chose to open the novella with a door rather than a character.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The neglected door is Stevenson’s first symbol of duality. Opening the novella with a building rather than a person signals that the whole story will be about hidden rooms — literal and psychological.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Enfield tells the story of a “small man” who “trampled calmly” over a young girl and gave him “a look so ugly it brought out the sweat on me like running.” The repeated word “trample” stays with the reader — the violence is described less in physical detail than in the witnesses’ reactions. Stevenson uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="Writing that reveals emotions or events through the reactions of onlookers rather than direct description.">reflected horror</dfn> to make Hyde feel more disturbing than any explicit description could.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Chapter 2: Search for Mr Hyde</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Utterson, disturbed by the story, reads Jekyll’s will: on Jekyll’s death or “disappearance” of more than three months, everything is to go to “Edward Hyde.” The legal language — precise, formal — makes the strangeness worse. Utterson begins to dream of Hyde “crushing” children, then sets out to hunt him by the door on Bye Street.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When they finally meet, Hyde is described in strikingly unstable terms. Utterson says: “God bless me, the man seems hardly human! Something troglodytic, shall we say?… he gives a strong feeling of deformity, although I couldn’t specify the point.” Stevenson deliberately refuses detail. Hyde is <em>felt</em> rather than seen. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Description that suggests meaning without specifying detail, leaving the reader's imagination to complete it.">indirect characterisation</dfn> echoes Victorian anxieties about degeneration and the unspeakable inside the respectable.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The ‘Hide and Seek’ pun</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Stevenson puns on Utterson’s name (“he will be Mr Seek”) and on Hyde’s (“to hide”). The playfulness is deliberate: under the surface of every Victorian gentleman is something hidden. Stevenson was writing in 1886, the year after W. T. Stead’s “Maiden Tribute” exposé revealed widespread sexual exploitation by respectable London men. The theme of public respectability concealing private vice was everywhere.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Chapter 3: Dr Jekyll was Quite at Ease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Two weeks later Utterson dines at Jekyll’s and raises the will. Jekyll’s refusal is polite but final: “the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr Hyde.” The claim is the first lie. Stevenson lets the reader hear it as confident reassurance and only later realise it is false. Utterson is presented as the rational Victorian gentleman — lawyer, discreet, loyal — the kind of man readers were invited to trust with the story.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chapter 4: The Carew Murder Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The novella’s first moment of clear violence comes with Sir Danvers Carew’s murder. A servant girl watches from a moonlit window as Carew is “trampled” and “clubbed… with ape-like fury” by Hyde. Stevenson’s simile — “ape-like” — draws on Victorian <dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that civilised humans can regress to a more primitive or animal state.">degeneration</dfn> theories. Darwin’s <em>Descent of Man</em> (1871) had shaken confidence that humans were truly separate from animals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The setting amplifies the horror: a full moon, a silent street, a respected MP killed for no reason. Stevenson chooses the victim carefully. Carew is an “aged” gentleman with “silvery hair” — the symbol of Victorian respectability. Hyde’s violence is not crime but sacrilege.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three words or phrases Stevenson uses to describe Hyde’s killing of Carew.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson uses three key phrases in the Carew murder: ‘trampled’, ‘ape-like fury’ and ‘bones were audibly shattered’. Each word strips away civilised language and pushes Hyde closer to the animal.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Narrative structure: the gathering of voices</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Stevenson builds these chapters through <dfn class="term" data-def="A story told through multiple framed accounts rather than a single narrator.">embedded narration</dfn>: Enfield tells Utterson, who reads the will, who hears Poole, who hears the maid. No one has the whole story. The readers have to assemble it — a deliberate technique that mirrors the novella’s theme: nothing is as single or stable as it appears.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The delayed revelation is also a narrative <dfn class="term" data-def="A structural build-up that increases tension by withholding information.">suspense</dfn> machine. We suspect a link between Jekyll and Hyde long before it is confirmed. Stevenson’s 1886 readers, reading the novella in a small red shilling edition, would have experienced this as a shock. Modern readers, knowing the twist, can analyse how the structure creates the possibility of that shock.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why These Chapters Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The first four chapters teach the reader how to read the whole novella. Stevenson trains us to distrust surfaces: the tidy street with one wrong door; the respectable Jekyll with one dubious beneficiary; the quiet night with a moonlit murder. The <em>doubling</em> that will later be explicit is already present in the structure.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Stevenson opens with a symbolic door and an embedded narration that train the reader to distrust respectable surfaces.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Hyde is made disturbing by what is withheld — ‘deformity’, ‘troglodytic’, ‘ape-like’ — rather than described.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Carew murder uses a moonlit setting and ‘ape-like fury’ to push Hyde into the realm of Victorian degeneration theory and animalistic fear.</li>
</ul>
