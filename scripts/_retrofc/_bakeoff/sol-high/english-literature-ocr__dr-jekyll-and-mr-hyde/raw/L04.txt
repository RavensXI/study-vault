--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 9: Dr Lanyon’s Narrative</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Stevenson finally delivers the confirmation. Lanyon’s sealed letter is a first-person account of a single terrible night. He receives a desperate note from Jekyll asking him to go to the cabinet and retrieve a drawer of chemicals. At midnight, a “small” stranger calls for the drawer. Lanyon describes him in now-familiar terms: “something abnormal and misbegotten… something seizing, surprising, and revolting.” This is the first time we meet Hyde through Lanyon’s professional, scientific eyes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why Stevenson waits until Chapter 9 to show the transformation itself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson withholds the transformation until Chapter 9 so that the reveal lands on a scientific, rational character (Lanyon) whom the reader has been taught to trust. The horror is not that Hyde changes — it is that a man of medicine watches it happen.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Hyde drinks the prepared potion. Lanyon describes what follows in stunned clauses: “he reeled, staggered, clutched at the table… and before my eyes — pale and shaken… there stood Henry Jekyll!” The dashes and staccato verbs mimic Lanyon’s shattered narration. Stevenson uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="The pace and structure of sentences, controlling how fast or urgent the prose feels.">sentence rhythm</dfn> to stage the horror in real time.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Lanyon writes: “my life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me… I cannot, even in my own thoughts, dwell on it without a start of horror.” The knowledge kills him — a direct echo of the Faust story, where the scholar who seeks forbidden knowledge is damned. Stevenson’s warning is moral: some truths are not survivable for those committed to <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of only investigating and treating what science accepts as physically measurable.">empirical medicine</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Chapter 10: Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The final chapter is Jekyll’s own long confession. Stevenson shifts voice entirely — the controlled, professional tone of Lanyon gives way to Jekyll’s restless self-examination. Jekyll explains that he was born with “duplicity of life”: a respectable surface and appetites “foreign” to it. He describes his scientific research and the creation of a drug that “could cast off the lower elements” of his personality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The first transformation brings “incredibly sweet” freedom. As Hyde, Jekyll feels “younger, lighter, happier in body”, and experiences a “solution of the bonds of obligation.” Stevenson is careful: the freedom is pleasurable, which makes the moral warning more interesting. If evil felt only terrible, nobody would pursue it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in one sentence the single most important idea in Jekyll’s confession about the structure of human nature.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jekyll’s central claim is that ‘man is not truly one, but truly two’. He believed he could separate the good and evil selves — but the evil, given its own body, grows stronger while the good weakens. The drug reveals the truth of duality but cannot manage it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Loss of Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Gradually Jekyll loses the ability to return. After the Carew murder he vows never to take the drug again — but wakes in a park one morning as Hyde without taking it. Stevenson dramatises the <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance or habit that gradually controls a user and overpowers their will.">addiction</dfn>: Jekyll’s “powers seemed shaken”, the drug stops working reliably, and the original supply of the salt runs out. What saved him no longer exists. The final transformation is involuntary.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Jekyll’s confession ends with an extraordinary narrative move: he describes his own impending death in present tense. “This, then, is the last time… that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts or see his own face.” Stevenson uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="Writing from the character's perspective at the very moment before their end, giving the reader a live record of the final hours.">last-words framing</dfn> to make the reader complicit: we are reading a note that ends with the writer’s death.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Duality and Victorian Anxiety</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Late-Victorian Britain was obsessed with doubles: the respectable husband who kept a mistress, the clergyman with a secret life, the civilised man who was also an animal. Stevenson crystallises this fear in one body. The novella was published in 1886, a year of high public tension about hidden vice, and the story became popular immediately. Jekyll’s confession reads like a Victorian nightmare turned inside out: the gentleman does the evil, not a stranger.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Modern criticism often reads Hyde as a figure for repressed Victorian sexuality, addiction, or class anxiety. Stevenson himself called the book ‘a fable’ — inviting interpretation. A strong essay treats the text as open to several readings rather than a puzzle with a single key.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Structure and Effect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Stevenson ends with a written confession because writing is what makes the story survivable. Jekyll’s words are preserved even as he dies; Utterson reads them and gains knowledge that Lanyon could not. The reader is a <em>second</em> Utterson, piecing the evidence into a whole. Structurally, the novella moves from street-level rumour to professional lawyer to scientific eyewitness to the subject’s own pen. Each stage gets closer to the truth — and each stage loses more of the person being described.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why the Ending Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The ending is tragic rather than triumphant. There is no cure, no moral redemption, only a confession. Stevenson refuses the comfort of exorcism: Jekyll dies, Hyde dies, but the double remains as a lesson. The novella closes not with peace but with the reader’s new suspicion — of the doctors, gentlemen and lawyers who seemed so solid in Chapter 1.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Lanyon’s account confirms the transformation through the eyes of a trusted scientist — the horror lies in its being witnessed, not in its happening.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Jekyll’s claim that ‘man is not truly one, but truly two’ is the novella’s central thesis about the divided self.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Stevenson uses the first-person confession and its final present tense to leave the reader in the moment of death — no peace, only knowledge.</li>
</ul>
