<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Duality of Human Nature</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The central theme of the novella is expressed in Jekyll’s declaration: “Man is not truly one, but truly two.” Stevenson argues that every person contains both good and evil impulses. Victorian society demanded that people suppress their darker urges and present a unified, moral exterior. Jekyll’s experiment is an attempt to resolve this tension by literally dividing himself into two separate beings. It fails catastrophically because evil, once released, cannot be contained.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The theme of <dfn class="term" data-def="The concept that every individual contains two contrasting natures — typically good and evil — existing in tension within a single identity.">duality</dfn> extends beyond Jekyll himself. London is divided between wealthy squares and squalid slums. Jekyll’s house has a grand front and a sinister back. The novella’s structure is split between investigation and confession. Even minor characters display duality: Utterson suppresses his own pleasures, and Enfield lives a double social life. Stevenson’s message is universal: duality is not Jekyll’s personal pathology but the human condition.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the key contextual details and how they connect to the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson does not present Jekyll as uniquely evil. Jekyll confesses: “I learned to recognise the thorough and primitive duality of man.” The word “man” is universal — this applies to all people, not just Jekyll. The novella’s horror is not that one man has a dark side, but that <em>everyone</em> does. The Victorian pretence that respectability equals goodness is the real delusion Stevenson attacks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme 2: Repression and Its Consequences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The act of suppressing or restraining thoughts, desires, or behaviour, especially under social pressure. In the novella, Victorian repression is the root cause of Jekyll’s experiment.">Repression</dfn> is both the cause and the catastrophe of the novella. Jekyll does not create Hyde because he is unusually wicked — he creates Hyde because Victorian society forces him to bury all his “undignified” desires. The pressure to appear perpetually virtuous is what drives him to seek a scientific escape. Stevenson implies that repression does not eliminate dangerous urges; it stores them up until they explode with greater violence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Hyde’s escalating brutality — from trampling a girl to murdering Sir Danvers Carew — demonstrates this principle. Each act of indulgence does not satisfy Hyde’s desires but intensifies them. Jekyll describes a “leap of welcome” with each transformation, the language of addiction. Stevenson’s warning is clear: a society built on repression breeds monsters, because the desires it forces underground do not disappear — they grow.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The novella is obsessed with secrets. Every major character keeps them: Jekyll hides his experiment, Lanyon seals his letter, Utterson refuses to speak about what he has seen at the window, Enfield agrees never to discuss the trampling incident. Reputation is the currency of Victorian professional life, and every character works to protect it — sometimes at the cost of truth and safety.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This culture of secrecy enables Jekyll’s double life. Nobody investigates too closely because doing so would risk exposing uncomfortable truths — not just about Jekyll, but about the entire class of respectable gentlemen. Utterson suspects blackmail because it is a socially understandable crime; the actual truth (one person containing two identities) is too threatening to the Victorian worldview to even consider. Stevenson suggests that Victorian society’s commitment to <dfn class="term" data-def="The desire to maintain a good public image and social standing, which in Victorian society often took priority over truth, honesty, or personal wellbeing.">reputation</dfn> creates a system where dangerous secrets are not just tolerated but actively protected.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Theme 3: Science and Morality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jekyll’s experiment raises profound questions about the limits of science. He uses chemistry to divide the human soul — an act that crosses the boundary between science and the sacred. His creation of Hyde echoes the <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring character type in Gothic literature: a scientist or scholar who pushes beyond the limits of human knowledge, defying natural or divine law, and is ultimately destroyed by their creation.">overreaching scientist</dfn> of Gothic tradition — most obviously Victor Frankenstein, who also creates a monster by transgressing natural boundaries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Lanyon represents the opposing view: cautious, conventional science that stays within accepted limits. He dismisses Jekyll’s work as “unscientific balderdash.” Yet Lanyon is also destroyed — not by his own experiment but by witnessing Jekyll’s. Stevenson implies that there is no safe position in the science-versus-morality debate: pushing too far is fatal, but so is being confronted by someone else’s transgression. The novella was published in the same decade as Darwin’s legacy debates and the rise of experimental medicine, making its anxiety about science’s moral limits sharply topical.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe Jekyll development across the text in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jekyll’s experiment fails not because of a scientific error but because of a moral miscalculation. He assumed evil could be separated and contained. Instead, it grows stronger when given freedom. The impure chemical that made the experiment possible cannot be reproduced, suggesting that the transformation was partly accidental — Jekyll never fully understood what he was doing. Stevenson presents scientific ambition without moral restraint as inherently dangerous.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Theme 4: Violence and the Primitive Self</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hyde’s violence is described in animalistic terms: “ape-like fury,” “troglodyte,” and other language that connects him to <dfn class="term" data-def="The concept of reverting to an earlier, more primitive evolutionary stage. Hyde’s animalistic behaviour reflects Victorian fears about human regression inspired by Darwin.">atavism</dfn>. This reflects the post-Darwinian anxiety that beneath civilised behaviour, humans remain animals capable of savage violence. Hyde is physically smaller than Jekyll, suggesting the primitive self is normally kept small by social constraint. As Hyde is indulged, he grows — both in physical stature and in dominance over Jekyll’s consciousness.</p>

    <span>Exam Technique: Approaching the Two-Part Question</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Extract-based analysis:</strong> Questions on the novella often begin by giving you a printed extract of around 400 words. Focus on <em>language analysis</em>: individual words, phrases, imagery, sentence structure. Use the PEA/PEEL framework but go deeper: what does this specific word choice reveal? What effect does it have on the reader? How does it connect to Stevenson’s wider purpose? Allow yourself enough time to produce three or four developed paragraphs rather than rushing through six superficial ones — check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact timing.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Whole-text response:</strong> Most exam questions also require you to explore a theme, character, or idea across the novella as a whole. Range confidently across the text, using memorised quotations from at least three or four different chapters. Structure your response thematically, not chronologically. For example, for a question on duality, you might discuss: the physical duality of Jekyll/Hyde, the geographical duality of London, the structural duality of the narrative, and the psychological duality within all the characters. Each point should be supported by a short, precise quotation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Closed book strategy:</strong> Most GCSE literature papers are closed book, so memorise 10–15 key quotations that cover the major themes and characters. Short quotations (3–6 words) are best: “ape-like fury,” “Satan’s signature,” “not truly one, but truly two,” “slowly losing hold.” Each quotation should be versatile enough to use for multiple questions. Always analyse the specific language of the quotation — do not simply drop it in as evidence without comment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">The five key themes are duality, repression, science and morality, violence and atavism, and secrecy and reputation. Every theme connects to the others — duality drives repression, which fuels the experiment, which unleashes violence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The Edexcel exam has two parts: Part (a) extract analysis (focus on language) and Part (b) whole text exploration (range across the novella with memorised quotations).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Memorise 10–15 versatile short quotations. Analyse the specific language of each one. Connect every point to Stevenson’s wider purpose and the novella’s themes.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">For the highest marks in both parts, you must analyse Stevenson’s methods, not just retell the story. Methods include: word choice, imagery, sentence structure, narrative perspective, symbolism, and structural choices. Every point should follow the pattern: <em>what</em> the method is, <em>how</em> Stevenson uses it (with a quotation), and <em>why</em> — what effect it creates and what it reveals about Stevenson’s message. Context should be woven in naturally, not as a separate paragraph.</p>
