--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 9: Dr Lanyon’s Narrative</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novella’s final two chapters abandon Utterson’s perspective entirely. Chapter 9 is Lanyon’s sealed letter, opened after his death. It reveals what destroyed him. Jekyll had written to Lanyon with urgent, desperate instructions: go to Jekyll’s cabinet, collect a specific drawer of chemicals, and bring it to Lanyon’s house. At midnight, a visitor would come to collect them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The visitor who arrives is Hyde — small, dressed in clothes far too large for him (Jekyll’s clothes), shaking with desperate need. Hyde mixes the chemicals into a potion and offers Lanyon a choice: leave the room, or stay and witness something that will “stagger the unbelief of Satan.” Lanyon, driven by scientific curiosity, chooses to stay.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and trace Hyde character development across the text from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hyde drinks the potion and transforms into Jekyll before Lanyon’s eyes. Lanyon describes his reaction: “like a man restored from death.” But this is not a resurrection — it is the opposite. Seeing the transformation destroys Lanyon’s entire worldview. Everything he believed about science, about the separation of mind and body, about the nature of reality, is shattered in a single moment. He never recovers. Stevenson implies that some truths are too terrible for the human mind to survive.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The phrase “like a man restored from death” is richly ambiguous. On the surface, it describes Jekyll’s reappearance. But it also suggests that Jekyll is not truly alive — he has been living a kind of death since creating Hyde. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison between two things using ‘like’ or ‘as,’ drawing attention to a shared quality.">simile</dfn> hints at resurrection, but Stevenson subverts the religious connotation: this is not a divine miracle but a scientific abomination.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Shift in Narrative Voice</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Chapters 1–8 are narrated from Utterson’s perspective — a measured, rational, legal viewpoint. Chapter 9 shifts to Lanyon’s voice: still rational but now fractured by horror. This structural shift is deliberate. Utterson’s calm, investigative narration cannot contain the truth. Stevenson needs a different voice — someone who has witnessed the transformation firsthand. Lanyon’s narrative is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A document or narrative that provides direct evidence of events, written by someone who was present.">testimony</dfn> from a man who has seen the impossible. His clinical language (“I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard”) struggles to process the experience, and this struggle makes the horror more convincing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Chapter 10: Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The final chapter is Jekyll’s own confession, written as he feels the last transformation approaching — the one from which he will never return. It reveals everything. Jekyll explains that from a young age, he was aware of a profound <dfn class="term" data-def="The condition of having two contrasting aspects or qualities existing within a single entity.">duality</dfn> in his nature. He felt torn between his desire for pleasure and his need to maintain the respectable public image demanded by Victorian society.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Jekyll writes: “Man is not truly one, but truly two.” This is the novella’s central statement. Jekyll discovered, through his experiments, that the human personality is not a single, unified self but a battleground between opposing forces. He believed that if he could separate these forces — extract the evil and contain it in a separate body — both halves could live freely. The good Jekyll could be virtuous without the weight of dark desires; the evil Hyde could indulge without guilt.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe Jekyll development across the text in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jekyll’s experiment fails for a devastating reason: evil is stronger than good. Hyde is pure evil, but Jekyll is not pure good — he remains a mixture. Over time, Hyde grows stronger while Jekyll weakens. Jekyll confesses: “I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self.” The balance tips irreversibly. The transformations begin happening involuntarily, without the potion, and Jekyll can no longer control when Hyde emerges.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Loss of Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Jekyll describes the terrifying moment he woke up one morning and realised he had transformed into Hyde in his sleep. He looked down at his hand and saw it was “lean, corded, knuckly” — Hyde’s hand, not his own. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a part is used to represent the whole, or vice versa. Hyde’s hand represents his entire identity.">synecdoche</dfn> of the hand is powerful: hands represent agency, identity, and moral choice. Jekyll’s hand becoming Hyde’s hand means he has lost control over his own body and moral identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">From this point, Jekyll needs the potion not to become Hyde but to become himself again. The default state has shifted. He is trapped: running out of the original chemical compound, unable to reproduce it because a crucial impurity in the original batch cannot be replicated. Jekyll writes with increasing desperation, knowing that the next transformation will be permanent.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Hyde Grows Stronger</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Stevenson offers a psychological explanation for Hyde’s increasing dominance. Each time Jekyll becomes Hyde, he indulges desires that were previously repressed. Rather than satisfying these desires, the indulgence strengthens them. Hyde is smaller than Jekyll at first because the evil side has been suppressed for years. But as Hyde is given more freedom, he grows — both in physical stature and in power over Jekyll’s consciousness. Stevenson’s message is clear: evil does not diminish when it is fed. Repressed desires, once released, do not return quietly to their cage.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Jekyll’s confession also reveals that he enjoyed being Hyde. He describes the “leap of welcome” he felt with each transformation — the exhilaration of freedom from social constraint. This enjoyment makes Jekyll complicit in Hyde’s crimes. He is not an innocent victim of his experiment; he is an addict who cannot stop returning to the drug.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Final Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Jekyll’s statement ends mid-sentence. He writes: “I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.” The reader understands that as Jekyll writes these final words, Hyde is taking over for the last time. There is no dramatic death scene for Jekyll — he simply ceases to exist, consumed by the creature he created. Hyde, trapped in the cabinet and facing arrest for Carew’s murder, takes cyanide rather than face justice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Stevenson denies the reader a conventional resolution. Jekyll does not defeat Hyde. Good does not triumph over evil. The novella ends with destruction: Jekyll gone, Hyde dead, Lanyon dead, and the truth contained in documents that the reader has been allowed to witness but the wider world of the story has not. The secrets of Victorian gentlemen die with them.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Lanyon witnesses the transformation and dies from the psychological shock. His fate shows that Victorian rationalism cannot survive contact with the truth about human duality.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">“Man is not truly one, but truly two” — Jekyll’s central discovery. His experiment fails because evil is stronger: Hyde grows more powerful while Jekyll weakens, until the transformations become involuntary.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The novella ends without resolution. Jekyll is consumed, Hyde is dead, and the truth is locked in documents. Stevenson denies the comforting Victorian narrative that good always triumphs over evil.</li></ul>

<p data-narration-id="n19">In the Edexcel exam, the most successful responses to Part (b) treat the novella’s structure as a method worth analysing. Do not just discuss what happens — discuss <em>how</em> and <em>why</em> Stevenson tells the story in this order. The shift from Utterson’s investigation (Chapters 1–8) to Lanyon’s testimony (Chapter 9) to Jekyll’s confession (Chapter 10) is a deliberate structural choice. Each narrator gets closer to the truth, but each is also destroyed or limited by what they discover.</p>
