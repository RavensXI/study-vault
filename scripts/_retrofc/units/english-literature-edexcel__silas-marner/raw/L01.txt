<h2 data-narration-id="n1">George Eliot and the Pen Name</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Silas Marner</em> was published in 1861 by <dfn class="term" data-def="The pen name of Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), who adopted a male pseudonym to ensure her work was taken seriously in a literary world dominated by men.">George Eliot</dfn>, the pen name of Mary Ann Evans. At the time, women writers were rarely taken seriously by critics. Publishing under a male name allowed Evans to be judged on the quality of her writing rather than her gender. Other female authors of the period, including the Brontë sisters (who published as Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell), made similar choices. Eliot’s decision reflects the rigid gender expectations of <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), a period marked by rapid industrialisation, strict social hierarchies, and strong moral codes.">Victorian</dfn> society, where women were expected to write light romances, not the serious social novels Eliot produced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Mary Ann Evans grew up in rural Warwickshire, and the landscape of her childhood deeply shaped <em>Silas Marner</em>. The fictional village of Raveloe, with its farming community, parish church, and slow rhythms of life, is drawn directly from the rural Midlands Eliot knew. She wrote about a world that was already disappearing by 1861, as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of rapid economic and technological change from the late eighteenth century onward, when Britain shifted from an agricultural economy to one based on factories, machinery, and urban industry.">Industrial Revolution</dfn> transformed England from an agricultural society into an industrial one.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the key contextual details and how they connect to the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans. She adopted a male pseudonym because Victorian society did not take women novelists seriously. Her rural Warwickshire upbringing directly inspired the setting of Raveloe in <em>Silas Marner</em>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Rural England and the Threat of Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The novel is set in the early nineteenth century, during the Napoleonic Wars, but Eliot published it in 1861. By then, the Industrial Revolution had radically changed English life. Factories were replacing handloom weavers, railways were connecting remote villages to cities, and mass migration was emptying the countryside. Eliot deliberately set the novel in an earlier period to capture a rural world that her readers were losing. Raveloe represents a pre-industrial <dfn class="term" data-def="A close-knit group of people living in the same area who share customs, values, and mutual support — a central theme in Silas Marner.">community</dfn> where everyone knows each other, customs are passed down through generations, and life revolves around the church, the harvest, and the local gentry.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This is not a sentimental picture. Eliot shows that rural communities could also be suspicious of outsiders, slow to change, and bound by rigid class rules. Silas Marner arrives in Raveloe as a stranger, and the villagers treat him with distrust for years. The novel explores both the warmth and the limitations of close-knit community life.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Before Silas arrives in Raveloe, he lives in a northern industrial town called Lantern Yard, where he belongs to a <dfn class="term" data-def="Protestant groups who separated from the Church of England, often practising a stricter, more intense form of worship in small, tight-knit congregations.">Dissenting</dfn> religious congregation. Dissenters were Protestant groups who rejected the established Church of England. Their communities were intense, close-knit, and governed by strict moral codes. Silas is a devoted member — he believes absolutely in God and in the community’s justice.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">When he is falsely accused of theft and the drawing of lots declares him guilty, Silas loses everything: his faith in God, his trust in people, and his sense of belonging. Eliot uses Lantern Yard to show the danger of blind religious faith. The community’s claim to divine authority is exposed as arbitrary and cruel. Silas’s cry — “there is no just God that governs the earth righteously, but a God of lies, that bears witness against the innocent” — marks his complete break from organised religion.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Class and the Social Hierarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Victorian society was sharply divided by class. In Raveloe, this hierarchy is clearly visible. At the top sits Squire Cass and his sons, who own land and hold social authority. Below them are the respectable villagers — farmers, tradespeople, and the parish establishment. At the bottom are labourers and outsiders like Silas, who has no family connections, no land, and no social standing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Eliot uses the Cass family to criticise the <dfn class="term" data-def="The wealthy landowning class in rural England who held social power and influence through inherited property rather than merit or effort.">gentry</dfn>. Squire Cass is a lazy, self-indulgent father. His sons — Godfrey and Dunsey — are morally weak, driven by self-interest and the desire to maintain their social position. In contrast, the lower-class characters like Dolly Winthrop show genuine kindness, moral strength, and practical wisdom. The novel suggests that moral worth has nothing to do with social rank.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eliot criticises the class system through the contrast between the morally weak Cass gentry and the morally strong working-class villagers. Squire Cass and his sons have wealth and status but lack integrity, while Dolly Winthrop shows genuine compassion and wisdom despite her low social position.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Women in Victorian England had limited legal rights: they could not own property after marriage, could not vote, and were largely defined by their roles as wives and mothers. Eliot challenges these expectations subtly. Nancy Lammeter is presented as morally upright but constrained by her class and gender. Dolly Winthrop, though uneducated, is the novel’s moral compass. And Eppie’s final choice — to stay with Silas rather than accept Godfrey’s wealth — is the most powerful act of agency in the novel. Writing as a woman under a male name, Eliot understood the tension between social expectation and personal integrity better than most.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Closed Book Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For Edexcel 1ET0 Component 2, Section A, you will answer a two-part question on <em>Silas Marner</em>. Part (a) asks you to analyse an extract of around 400 words, focusing on how Eliot uses language and structure to create meaning (AO1 + AO2). Part (b) asks you to explore a theme, character, or idea across the whole novel (AO1 + AO2). This is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An exam condition where students do not have a copy of the text and must rely on memorised quotations and knowledge of the whole novel.">closed book</dfn> exam, so you must memorise key quotations. Context is not a separate assessment objective, but embedding contextual understanding into your analysis will strengthen your AO1 marks for a personal, informed response.</p>
