<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Betrayal at Lantern Yard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before Silas Marner arrives in Raveloe, he is a deeply religious young man living in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrow, enclosed community — here referring to the strict Dissenting chapel where Silas worshipped before his exile, governed by rigid moral codes and collective judgement.">Lantern Yard</dfn> congregation in a northern industrial town. Silas is devoted to his faith: he attends services regularly, trusts absolutely in God’s justice, and considers William Dane his closest friend. When the church deacon falls ill and Silas sits up with him through the night, he has one of his <dfn class="term" data-def="A medical condition in which a person becomes rigid and unresponsive, appearing to be in a trance. Silas’s fits are initially seen as holy visions by Lantern Yard, then used as evidence of guilt.">cataleptic fits</dfn> — he falls into a trance and loses consciousness. While Silas is unconscious, the deacon dies and the church money is stolen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">William Dane plants Silas’s knife at the scene to frame him. The congregation decides Silas’s guilt through the drawing of lots — a method they believe reveals God’s will. The lots declare Silas guilty. He cries out: “there is no just God that governs the earth righteously, but a God of lies, that bears witness against the innocent.” This is one of the most devastating moments in the novel. Silas has not simply been accused by people; he believes God Himself has condemned him. His faith collapses completely.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Silas is falsely accused of theft at Lantern Yard. The drawing of lots — which the congregation believes reveals God’s will — declares him guilty. Silas loses his faith entirely, crying that God is “a God of lies, that bears witness against the innocent.” William Dane, who actually stole the money, also marries Silas’s fiancée Sarah.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Exile in Raveloe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">After the betrayal, Silas leaves Lantern Yard and settles near the village of Raveloe. But he does not join the community. He lives in a stone cottage by the deserted stone-pits, working as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who weaves cloth on a loom. In the novel, Silas’s trade as a linen-weaver defines his identity and isolates him from the farming community of Raveloe.">linen-weaver</dfn>, producing cloth for the local women. Eliot describes his existence as mechanical and joyless. He has no friends, attends no church, and speaks to no one beyond the minimum required for trade. The villagers find him strange and frightening — his cataleptic fits, his solitary habits, and his knowledge of herbal remedies make them suspect he has uncanny powers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Eliot writes that Silas’s life narrowed to “the monotony of his loom and the repetition of his web.” The loom becomes a symbol of his mechanical, empty existence. He weaves cloth endlessly, but he weaves no human connections. He has retreated from all the things that gave his life meaning: faith, friendship, community, and love.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Silas’s cataleptic fits serve a crucial structural purpose in the novel. At Lantern Yard, his fits are interpreted as moments of spiritual vision — the congregation initially sees them as evidence of holiness. But when the deacon’s money goes missing during a fit, the same condition becomes evidence of guilt. Later, at Raveloe, Silas has a fit on New Year’s Eve with his door open. While he stands unconscious, Eppie crawls through the open door towards the light of his fire. The fits are a plot device that enables key events, but they also symbolise Silas’s vulnerability — during his trances, the world acts upon him without his knowledge or consent.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Gold: A Substitute for Human Connection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">With no faith, no friends, and no family, Silas pours all his emotional energy into his gold. He is paid for his weaving in gold coins, and he begins to hoard them. Every evening, after the day’s work, he takes the coins from their hiding place beneath the floorboards and counts them. Eliot describes his relationship with the gold in almost intimate terms: “The gold had kept his thoughts in an ever-repeated circle, leading to nothing beyond itself.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This is a key quotation for the exam. The gold represents a closed, sterile existence. It demands nothing, gives nothing, and connects Silas to no one. He touches the coins, arranges them, and finds comfort in their familiar weight and shape. Eliot compares his attachment to the gold to an insect’s mindless, repetitive behaviour. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong attachment to something that provides emotional comfort but prevents growth or genuine human connection — Silas’s relationship with his gold coins before Eppie arrives.">fetishism</dfn> of the gold is Eliot’s way of showing what happens when a person is cut off from all human bonds: they transfer their need for love onto objects.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the symbol or technique, then explain its effect from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“The gold had kept his thoughts in an ever-repeated circle, leading to nothing beyond itself.” Silas’s gold-hoarding replaces all human connection. The circular imagery suggests stagnation: the gold gives Silas routine but no growth, comfort but no love. It is a substitute for the faith, friendship, and community he lost at Lantern Yard.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Silas lives in Raveloe for fifteen years before the theft of his gold. During this time, he barely engages with the community. He does not attend church, refuses social invitations, and interacts with villagers only through his trade. Eliot presents this period as a kind of living death — Silas exists but does not live. His days are identical: weave, eat, count gold, sleep. The repetition mirrors the circular imagery of the gold itself. When his brown pot — his only domestic companion — breaks, he keeps the pieces and props them in their old place, showing how desperately he clings to even meaningless objects. Eliot is preparing the reader to understand the magnitude of what the theft of the gold will mean: it removes the only thing Silas has left.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Loss of Faith</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Silas’s loss of faith is not a gradual process — it is sudden and absolute. At Lantern Yard, he believed completely; after the drawing of lots, he believes not at all. Eliot uses this to explore the difference between institutional religion and genuine <dfn class="term" data-def="In the novel, the quality of trusting in something or someone — Silas loses religious faith at Lantern Yard but eventually finds a new, secular faith through human love and community.">faith</dfn>. The Lantern Yard congregation claimed to speak for God, but their judgement was manipulated by William Dane. Silas’s loss of faith is really a loss of trust in people disguised as a loss of trust in God.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In Raveloe, Silas does not find a new religion. Instead, he gradually discovers a different kind of faith — faith in human kindness, represented by Dolly Winthrop and, above all, by Eppie. Eliot’s narrator describes this process using the novel’s opening quotation: “In old days there were angels who came and took men by the hand and led them away from the city of destruction. We see no white-winged angels now.” The angels are replaced by ordinary people. Redemption, Eliot argues, comes not from above but from beside us.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Silas is betrayed at Lantern Yard through a rigged drawing of lots, losing his faith in God, his community, and his fiancée in one devastating moment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">“The gold had kept his thoughts in an ever-repeated circle, leading to nothing beyond itself” — Silas’s gold-hoarding replaces human connection but traps him in a sterile, repetitive existence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Eliot uses Silas’s cataleptic fits as a structural device: at Lantern Yard they enable the theft and false accusation; at Raveloe they enable Eppie’s arrival through the open door.</li></ul>
