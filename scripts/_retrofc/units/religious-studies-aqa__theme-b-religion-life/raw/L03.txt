Sanctity of life, quality of life, and contrasting beliefs on abortion and euthanasia.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sanctity of Life & Quality of Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Two key concepts underpin religious and ethical debates about abortion and euthanasia. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that all human life is sacred and God-given, and therefore must be protected and valued from conception to natural death.">sanctity of life</dfn> is the belief that all human life is sacred because it is created by God. Christians point to Genesis 1:27: “God created mankind in his own image.” Muslims believe Allah breathes a soul (<em>ruh</em>) into every person at a key stage of foetal development; Qur’an 32:9 describes this in the context of Adam’s creation: “Then He fashioned him and breathed into him of His Spirit.” The dominant Hanafi position places ensoulment at 120 days; some Maliki and Hanbali scholars place it at 40 days. Qur’an 6:2 (“It is He who created you from clay, then determined a term for you”) establishes that lifespan is divinely appointed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The extent to which a person’s life is comfortable, meaningful and free from suffering, sometimes used to argue that ending a life of extreme suffering is compassionate.">quality of life</dfn> is a different concept — it considers whether a person’s life is worth living based on their level of comfort, independence, happiness and freedom from pain. Some people argue that when quality of life is extremely poor, ending suffering may be the most compassionate response.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and define sanctity of life and quality of life from memory. State which one religious traditions tend to emphasise.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The tension between sanctity of life and quality of life is at the heart of both the abortion and euthanasia debates. Those who prioritise sanctity argue that life must <em>never</em> be deliberately ended. Those who emphasise quality of life argue that sometimes ending suffering is the most loving and compassionate thing to do.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Abortion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The deliberate ending of a pregnancy, usually before the foetus is 24 weeks old, legal in England, Scotland and Wales under certain conditions.">Abortion</dfn> is the deliberate termination of a pregnancy. In England, Scotland and Wales, abortion is legal up to 24 weeks under the Abortion Act 1967 (amended 1990), provided two doctors agree that continuing the pregnancy poses a risk to the physical or mental health of the mother or existing children. After 24 weeks, abortion is only permitted if the mother’s life is at risk or the foetus has a severe abnormality.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Catholic Church:</strong> Abortion is always wrong because life begins at conception and is sacred. The Catechism states: “Human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception.” The only exception is if a medical procedure to save the mother’s life indirectly causes the death of the foetus (the principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ethical principle that an action with a good intended outcome (e.g. saving the mother) is morally acceptable even if it has an unintended negative consequence (e.g. death of the foetus).">double effect</dfn>).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Church of England:</strong> Abortion is generally opposed but accepted in certain circumstances, such as when the mother’s life is at risk, in cases of severe foetal abnormality, or after rape. The decision should be made with compassion and careful thought.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Liberal Christians:</strong> Some argue that the most loving thing may be to allow abortion in difficult circumstances. They apply Jesus’s teaching of compassion and the principle of “the lesser of two evils.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Most Muslim scholars oppose abortion after <dfn class="term" data-def="In Islamic teaching, the moment at which the soul is believed to enter the foetus, traditionally held to be at 120 days (approximately 17 weeks) of pregnancy.">ensoulment</dfn> (when the soul enters the body), traditionally believed to occur at 120 days. Before this point, some scholars permit abortion in certain circumstances, such as when the mother’s life is in danger. The Qur’an (17:31) warns: “Do not kill your children for fear of poverty.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">If the mother’s life is at risk, most scholars agree that her life takes priority because she has existing responsibilities and relationships. However, abortion for convenience or non-medical reasons is generally considered <dfn class="term" data-def="An Arabic term meaning forbidden or sinful in Islam; actions that are prohibited by the Qur’an or Hadith.">haram</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Euthanasia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The deliberate ending of a person’s life to relieve suffering, sometimes called ‘mercy killing.’ It is illegal in the UK.">Euthanasia</dfn> means deliberately ending someone’s life to relieve suffering. It is sometimes called “mercy killing.” There are different types: <strong>voluntary euthanasia</strong> (the person requests it), <strong>non-voluntary euthanasia</strong> (the person cannot give consent, e.g. in a coma), and <strong>assisted suicide</strong> (helping someone end their own life). All forms of euthanasia and assisted suicide are illegal in the UK.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="From memory, define the three types of euthanasia. State the legal status of each in the UK.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both Christianity and Islam oppose euthanasia. Christians believe life is a gift from God and only God should decide when it ends. Job 1:21 says: “The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away.” Islam teaches that only Allah determines the time of death: “No soul can die except by Allah’s leave” (Qur’an 3:145). However, both religions support <dfn class="term" data-def="Specialist medical care focused on relieving pain and providing comfort for people with terminal illnesses, rather than curing the disease.">palliative care</dfn> to relieve suffering without hastening death.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Arguments For & Against Euthanasia</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Arguments for:</strong> People should have the right to choose how and when they die. Forcing someone to live in extreme pain is cruel. Quality of life matters — when there is no hope of recovery, euthanasia may be the most compassionate option. Some argue Jesus’s Golden Rule (“Do to others what you would have them do to you” — Matthew 7:12) supports ending suffering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Arguments against:</strong> Life is sacred and belongs to God — humans do not have the right to end it. Euthanasia could lead to a “slippery slope” where vulnerable people feel pressured to die. Modern palliative care can manage pain effectively. The Qur’an teaches patience in suffering: “Allah does not burden a soul beyond that it can bear” (2:286).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Death & the Afterlife</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Religious beliefs about what happens after death influence attitudes towards euthanasia and the value of life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Christianity:</strong> Most Christians believe in an <dfn class="term" data-def="Life after death; the belief that the soul continues to exist in some form after the physical body dies.">afterlife</dfn>. The soul continues after death — the righteous go to heaven (eternal life with God) and the unrighteous face judgement. Jesus said: “I am the resurrection and the life. The one who believes in me will live, even though they die” (John 11:25). This belief gives Christians hope and comfort in the face of death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Islam:</strong> Muslims believe in <dfn class="term" data-def="The Day of Judgement in Islam, when Allah will resurrect all people and judge their deeds to determine whether they enter paradise (Jannah) or hell (Jahannam).">Yawm al-Din</dfn> (the Day of Judgement), when Allah will resurrect all people and judge their deeds. The righteous will enter <em>Jannah</em> (paradise) and the wicked will be sent to <em>Jahannam</em> (hell). This belief means Muslims see life as a test and believe suffering has a purpose — it builds patience and strengthens faith.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Afterlife Beliefs Affect Views on Euthanasia</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Belief in an afterlife reinforces opposition to euthanasia. If this life is a preparation for eternal life, then cutting it short may have spiritual consequences. Christians may fear that taking one’s own life or assisting death goes against God’s plan. Muslims believe that accepting suffering patiently is rewarded in the afterlife. However, some non-religious people argue that without an afterlife, reducing suffering in this life becomes even more important — supporting the case for euthanasia.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain how Christian, Muslim, and Humanist beliefs about the afterlife shape their views on euthanasia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Humanists and atheists, who do not believe in an afterlife, argue that moral decisions should focus on reducing suffering in this life. They may support euthanasia because they believe people have the right to die with dignity. This contrasts with religious views that see suffering as meaningful and life as belonging to God.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25">The sanctity of life (all life is sacred) and quality of life (suffering matters) are the two key principles in abortion and euthanasia debates.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Both Christianity and Islam generally oppose abortion, but there is a range of views. Catholics oppose it absolutely; the Church of England and many Muslims accept it in certain circumstances, especially when the mother’s life is at risk.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Euthanasia is illegal in the UK and opposed by both religions. Christians and Muslims believe only God should decide when life ends, and both support palliative care as an alternative.</li></ul>
