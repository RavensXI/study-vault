--- DESCRIPTION ---
How carbon and water cycle through ecosystems, and the vital role of decomposers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Carbon Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Many materials cycle through the abiotic and biotic components of an ecosystem (B4.1a). The <dfn class="term" data-def="The biogeochemical cycle by which carbon moves between the atmosphere, living organisms, oceans and the Earth's crust through processes like photosynthesis, respiration, combustion and decomposition.">carbon cycle</dfn> is one of the most important. Carbon enters living organisms when plants absorb CO\(_2\) during <strong>photosynthesis</strong>. It passes through food chains when animals eat plants. Carbon is returned to the atmosphere through <strong>respiration</strong> (by all living organisms), <strong>combustion</strong> of fossil fuels and wood, and <strong>decomposition</strong> of dead organisms (B4.1c).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Over millions of years, some carbon becomes locked in <strong>fossil fuels</strong> (coal, oil, natural gas) and <strong>sedimentary rocks</strong> like limestone. Burning fossil fuels releases this stored carbon rapidly, increasing atmospheric CO\(_2\) concentrations. The oceans also act as a carbon sink, dissolving CO\(_2\) from the atmosphere, but rising levels are causing <strong>ocean acidification</strong>, which threatens marine life such as coral reefs and shellfish.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four key processes in the carbon cycle: which ones remove CO2 from the atmosphere and which ones release it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Carbon enters living organisms via photosynthesis and is returned to the atmosphere by respiration, combustion and decomposition. Only photosynthesis removes CO\(_2\) from the atmosphere. Fossil fuels lock carbon away for millions of years until combustion releases it.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Water Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous movement of water between the Earth's surface and atmosphere through evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection.">water cycle</dfn> describes how water moves continuously between the Earth’s surface and atmosphere. Water <strong>evaporates</strong> from oceans, lakes and rivers (heated by the Sun), forming water vapour. Plants also release water vapour through <dfn class="term" data-def="The loss of water vapour from the surface of leaves through tiny pores called stomata.">transpiration</dfn> from their leaves. As water vapour rises, it cools and <strong>condenses</strong> into clouds. Water returns to the ground as <strong>precipitation</strong> (rain, snow, hail) and is collected in rivers, lakes and underground stores before the cycle repeats.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The water cycle is essential because it provides fresh water for drinking, agriculture and industry. It also transports dissolved minerals through the soil, making nutrients available to plant roots. Human activities such as deforestation reduce transpiration, which can alter local rainfall patterns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The <strong>nitrogen cycle</strong> is another critical nutrient cycle. Nitrogen gas (\(N_2\)) makes up 78% of the atmosphere but plants cannot use it directly. <strong>Nitrogen-fixing bacteria</strong> in the soil and in root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover) convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia (\(NH_3\)) and then into nitrates (\(NO_3^-\)), which plants absorb through their roots for making proteins and DNA. When organisms die, <strong>decomposers</strong> break down the nitrogen-containing compounds, and <strong>denitrifying bacteria</strong> convert some nitrates back into nitrogen gas, completing the cycle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Understanding nutrient cycles is essential for sustainable agriculture. Farmers who grow legumes in rotation add nitrogen to the soil naturally, reducing the need for synthetic fertilisers. This mirrors how natural ecosystems recycle nutrients without human intervention.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Human activities disrupt these cycles. Burning fossil fuels increases atmospheric carbon faster than photosynthesis can remove it. Excessive use of nitrogen fertilisers leads to nutrient run-off into waterways, causing <strong>eutrophication</strong> — algal blooms that block light and deplete oxygen, killing aquatic organisms. Understanding both cycles helps explain why sustainable land management is so important.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In laboratory experiments, you can investigate decomposition by measuring the rate of decay of milk using <strong>pH indicators</strong> (as bacteria decompose lactose, they produce lactic acid, lowering pH) or by monitoring the mass loss of a compost bag over several weeks. Controlled variables include temperature, moisture and initial mass. These experiments demonstrate the real-world principles of nutrient recycling in a measurable, quantitative way.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of dead organic matter by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi), releasing nutrients back into the soil and CO2 into the atmosphere.">Decomposition</dfn> is the breakdown of dead organic matter by <strong>bacteria</strong> and <strong>fungi</strong> (known as decomposers). These microorganisms secrete enzymes onto dead material, digest it externally, and absorb the soluble products. Decomposition releases CO\(_2\) back into the atmosphere through the decomposers’ respiration and returns essential minerals (such as nitrates, phosphates and potassium) to the soil, where they can be taken up by plant roots.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Without decomposition, dead organisms and waste would accumulate, and the nutrients locked within them would never be recycled. This would eventually starve living plants of the minerals they need for growth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four conditions that speed up decomposition, explaining why each one helps decomposers work faster.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead matter, recycling carbon and minerals back into the ecosystem. Without decomposition, nutrients would remain locked in dead organisms.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting Decomposition and Composting</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The rate of decomposition depends on several factors (B4.2b). <strong>Temperature</strong> — warm conditions increase enzyme activity in decomposers, but very high temperatures denature the enzymes. <strong>Moisture</strong> — water is needed for chemical reactions and for decomposers to survive. <strong>Oxygen</strong> — aerobic decomposers respire faster with more oxygen, breaking down material more quickly. <strong>Surface area</strong> — smaller pieces decompose faster because more surface is exposed to enzymes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of deliberately creating conditions to speed up decomposition of organic waste, producing nutrient-rich compost for gardening and farming.">Composting</dfn> applies these principles deliberately. Gardeners and farmers shred material (increases surface area), turn compost heaps regularly (introduces oxygen) and keep them moist and warm. The resulting compost is rich in minerals and can be used as a natural fertiliser, reducing the need for chemical alternatives.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">In <strong>anaerobic</strong> conditions (without oxygen), decomposition still occurs but more slowly, and produces <strong>methane</strong> rather than CO\(_2\). This is why waterlogged landfill sites produce methane, a potent greenhouse gas.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down what happens to decomposition rate when temperature, moisture, oxygen and surface area each increase.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Decomposition is fastest in warm, moist, oxygen-rich conditions with high surface area. Composting uses these principles to recycle organic waste into nutrient-rich soil.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The carbon cycle moves carbon between the atmosphere, living organisms, oceans and rocks through photosynthesis, respiration, combustion and decomposition.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The water cycle continuously moves water through evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and collection.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Decomposition by bacteria and fungi recycles nutrients; its rate depends on temperature, moisture, oxygen and surface area.</li>
</ul>
