Communicable diseases, pathogens, the immune system and vaccination.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Health and Disease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Health is a state of physical and mental well-being, not just the absence of disease (B6.3a). <dfn class="term" data-def="Diseases that can be spread from one organism to another, caused by pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists.">Communicable diseases</dfn> are caused by <dfn class="term" data-def="Microorganisms that cause disease, including bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists.">pathogens</dfn> — microorganisms that infect and cause illness. Pathogens include <strong>bacteria</strong>, <strong>viruses</strong>, <strong>fungi</strong> and <strong>protists</strong>. Communicable diseases can be spread by direct contact, airborne droplets, contaminated water or food, and animal vectors such as mosquitoes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Diseases that cannot be passed from person to person, such as cancer, cardiovascular disease and diabetes.">Non-communicable diseases</dfn> cannot be spread between people. Examples include <strong>cancer</strong>, <strong>cardiovascular disease</strong>, <strong>type 2 diabetes</strong> and <strong>asthma</strong>. Risk factors for non-communicable diseases include smoking, poor diet, lack of exercise, obesity and excessive alcohol consumption. Some non-communicable diseases have genetic components too.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall four types of pathogen, two ways communicable diseases spread, and three examples of non-communicable diseases with their risk factors.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Communicable diseases are caused by pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists) and can spread between organisms. Non-communicable diseases (cancer, CVD, diabetes) cannot be passed on and are linked to lifestyle risk factors.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Body’s Defence Systems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The body has several lines of defence against pathogens (B6.3b):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Non-specific defences</strong> (first line) prevent pathogens from entering the body. These include: <strong>skin</strong> (a physical barrier), <strong>mucus</strong> in the nose and airways (traps pathogens), <strong>cilia</strong> (tiny hairs that sweep mucus out of the airways), <strong>stomach acid</strong> (pH 1-2, kills most pathogens in food), and <strong>tears and saliva</strong> (contain the enzyme lysozyme, which breaks down bacterial cell walls).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">If pathogens breach the first line, the body’s <strong>second line of defence</strong> — the inflammatory response — is activated. Damaged cells release chemicals that cause blood vessels to dilate and become more permeable. This increases blood flow to the area (causing redness and swelling) and allows white blood cells to reach the site of infection more quickly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The different types of pathogen cause illness in different ways. <strong>Bacteria</strong> reproduce rapidly in the body and produce <strong>toxins</strong> that damage cells and tissues. <strong>Viruses</strong> are much smaller — they invade host cells, take over the cell’s machinery to replicate themselves, and burst out of the cell (lysis), destroying it in the process. <strong>Fungi</strong> can cause surface infections (e.g. athlete’s foot) or systemic infections in immunocompromised patients. <strong>Protists</strong> include the <em>Plasmodium</em> parasite that causes malaria, transmitted by mosquito vectors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The interplay between communicable and non-communicable diseases is significant. For example, a weakened immune system (from HIV, a communicable disease) increases susceptibility to infections and certain cancers (non-communicable). Poor nutrition (a risk factor for non-communicable diseases) also weakens the immune system, making individuals more vulnerable to infections.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">OCR Gateway also requires knowledge of specific diseases and how they spread. <strong>Salmonella</strong> is a bacterial disease spread through contaminated food. <strong>HIV</strong> is a virus spread through body fluids (blood, semen). <strong>Cholera</strong> is a bacterial disease spread through contaminated water. Understanding transmission routes helps develop prevention strategies such as vaccination, hygiene practices and vector control programmes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Drug development follows a rigorous process. New medicines are first tested on cells and tissues in the lab (<strong>preclinical testing</strong>), then on animals to check for toxicity, before <strong>clinical trials</strong> on human volunteers. Phase 1 tests safety on healthy volunteers, Phase 2 tests effectiveness on patients, and Phase 3 involves larger groups with double-blind placebo controls. Only after passing all phases and regulatory review can a drug be approved for widespread use.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">If pathogens get past the non-specific defences, <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of white blood cell that produces antibodies to target specific pathogens.">lymphocytes</dfn> mount a <strong>specific immune response</strong>. Each pathogen has unique proteins on its surface called <dfn class="term" data-def="Proteins on the surface of a pathogen that are recognised as foreign by the immune system, triggering an immune response.">antigens</dfn>. Lymphocytes recognise these foreign antigens and produce <dfn class="term" data-def="Y-shaped proteins produced by lymphocytes that bind to specific antigens on pathogens, marking them for destruction or clumping them together.">antibodies</dfn> — Y-shaped proteins that bind to the specific antigen, marking the pathogen for destruction.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Antibodies work by: <strong>clumping pathogens together</strong> (agglutination) so phagocytes can engulf them more easily, <strong>neutralising toxins</strong> produced by pathogens, and <strong>marking pathogens</strong> for destruction by other immune cells. After infection, some lymphocytes become <strong>memory cells</strong> that remain in the body for years. If the same pathogen enters again, memory cells produce antibodies much faster, often preventing illness entirely.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Phagocytes</strong> are white blood cells that carry out <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens or debris, forming a non-specific defence mechanism.">phagocytosis</dfn> — they engulf and digest pathogens. This is a non-specific response (phagocytes attack any foreign cell).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the three roles of antibodies. Then describe what memory cells do and why a second infection is fought off faster.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Lymphocytes produce antibodies specific to antigens on pathogens. After infection, memory cells allow a faster response to reinfection. Phagocytes provide non-specific defence by engulfing pathogens.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Vaccination and Antibiotics</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Introducing a small or inactive form of a pathogen into the body to stimulate the immune system to produce memory cells and antibodies, providing future immunity.">Vaccination</dfn> introduces a dead or weakened form of a pathogen (or its antigens) into the body. The immune system responds by producing antibodies and memory cells, without causing the disease. If the real pathogen enters later, memory cells trigger a rapid immune response. Vaccination programmes have eradicated smallpox and greatly reduced diseases like measles, polio and tetanus (B6.3c).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Antibiotics</strong> are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them reproducing. They work by targeting bacterial structures (e.g. cell walls) that human cells lack. Antibiotics do <strong>not</strong> work against viruses because viruses reproduce inside human cells and lack the structures antibiotics target. Overuse and misuse of antibiotics drives <strong>antibiotic resistance</strong>, creating ‘superbugs’ like MRSA (B6.3d).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Painkillers</strong> (e.g. paracetamol, ibuprofen) treat symptoms but do not kill pathogens. They relieve pain and reduce fever while the immune system fights the infection.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how vaccination works in three steps (injection, immune response, memory cells). Then state why antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vaccines introduce weakened pathogens to produce memory cells without causing disease. Antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses. Overuse drives antibiotic resistance.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Communicable diseases are caused by pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists); non-communicable diseases (cancer, CVD, diabetes) are linked to lifestyle factors.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The body defends itself with non-specific barriers (skin, mucus, acid) and specific immune responses (lymphocytes producing antibodies + memory cells).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Vaccines create immunity using weakened pathogens. Antibiotics target bacteria only — not viruses. Overuse causes resistance.</li>
</ul>
