<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Non-Communicable Diseases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Diseases that cannot be spread from person to person; they are often caused by the interaction of genetic, lifestyle and environmental factors.">Non-communicable diseases</dfn> (NCDs) cannot be passed from one person to another (B6.3b). Unlike communicable diseases caused by pathogens, NCDs are caused by the interaction of multiple factors including genetics, lifestyle and environment (B6.3l). Major NCDs include <strong>cardiovascular diseases</strong> (heart disease and stroke), <strong>many forms of cancer</strong>, <strong>lung diseases</strong> (such as bronchitis), <strong>liver diseases</strong> (such as cirrhosis), and <strong>type 2 diabetes</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Non-communicable diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide. Understanding the <dfn class="term" data-def="Factors that increase the likelihood of developing a disease, such as smoking, poor diet, obesity, lack of exercise and excessive alcohol consumption.">risk factors</dfn> that contribute to these diseases is essential for prevention and public health.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Lifestyle Risk Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The OCR Gateway specification requires you to analyse the effect of lifestyle factors on the incidence of NCDs at local, national and global levels (B6.3n). The key lifestyle factors are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Smoking</strong> — damages the lining of arteries, leading to atherosclerosis (fatty deposits). Increases risk of cardiovascular disease, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and bronchitis. Tar in cigarette smoke is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that can cause changes in DNA leading to cancer.">carcinogen</dfn> — a cancer-causing substance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Diet</strong> — a high-fat, high-salt, high-sugar diet increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and obesity. Too much saturated fat raises cholesterol levels, which contributes to atherosclerosis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Alcohol</strong> — excessive alcohol consumption damages the liver, leading to cirrhosis (scarring of liver tissue). It also increases the risk of liver cancer and cardiovascular disease.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Lack of exercise</strong> — physical inactivity increases the risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Regular exercise helps maintain a healthy weight and strengthens the heart.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Learn four lifestyle risk factors (smoking, diet, alcohol, exercise) and link each to specific diseases. Smoking → CVD + lung cancer; Diet → CVD + type 2 diabetes; Alcohol → liver disease; Inactivity → obesity + CVD.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key lifestyle risk factors for NCDs include smoking (cardiovascular disease, lung cancer), poor diet (CVD, type 2 diabetes), excessive alcohol (liver disease), and lack of exercise (obesity, CVD) (B6.3n).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Cardiovascular Disease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A group of diseases affecting the heart and blood vessels, including coronary heart disease and stroke, often caused by atherosclerosis.">Cardiovascular disease</dfn> (CVD) is a major cause of death globally. It occurs when fatty deposits (atherosclerosis) build up inside coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing blood flow to the heart muscle. This can cause <strong>angina</strong> (chest pain) or, if the artery becomes completely blocked, a <strong>heart attack</strong>. A stroke occurs when blood supply to the brain is interrupted.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Lifestyle changes</strong> — improving diet, exercising more, stopping smoking, reducing alcohol. Low risk, but requires long-term commitment from the patient.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Medical treatments</strong> — <strong>statins</strong> lower blood cholesterol levels, reducing atherosclerosis. <strong>Blood thinners</strong> (anticoagulants/antiplatelets) reduce the risk of blood clots. Medication is effective but may have side effects.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Surgical treatments</strong> — <strong>stents</strong> are small tubes inserted into narrowed arteries to hold them open, restoring blood flow. <strong>Bypass surgery</strong> uses a blood vessel from another part of the body to create a new route for blood around the blocked artery. Surgery is effective but carries risks of infection and complications.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>CVD treatments range from lifestyle changes (low risk, requires commitment) to medication (statins, blood thinners) to surgery (stents, bypass). Each has benefits and risks that must be evaluated (B6.3m).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Cancer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A disease caused by uncontrolled cell growth and division, resulting from changes (mutations) in the DNA that controls the cell cycle.">Cancer</dfn> is the result of changes in cells that lead to uncontrolled growth and division (B6.3o). Mutations in the DNA that controls the cell cycle cause cells to divide rapidly, forming a tumour. <strong>Benign tumours</strong> do not spread to other parts of the body. <strong>Malignant tumours</strong> invade surrounding tissues and can spread to other organs through the blood — this is called <strong>metastasis</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Risk factors for cancer include smoking (lung cancer), UV radiation (skin cancer), certain viruses (e.g. HPV and cervical cancer), and genetic predisposition. Not all cancers have known causes.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">When analysing data on lifestyle and disease, it is crucial to distinguish between <dfn class="term" data-def="A statistical relationship between two variables where they change together, but one does not necessarily cause the other.">correlation</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A direct link where one factor is proven to directly cause another, established through controlled experiments.">causation</dfn>. A <strong>correlation</strong> means two factors are linked (e.g. countries with higher smoking rates also have higher lung cancer rates). <strong>Causation</strong> means one factor directly causes the other. Correlation does not always mean causation — other variables (confounding factors) may be involved. Causation is only established through controlled experiments.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Stem cells</strong> have potential benefits in medicine (B6.3p). They can divide and differentiate into specialised cell types, so they could be used to replace damaged tissues. However, there are risks (e.g. tissue rejection after transplantation) and ethical concerns about using embryonic stem cells. The spec also asks about gene technology in medicine (B6.3q) and the importance of understanding the human genome for predicting disease likelihood and targeting treatments (B6.3r).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Correlation vs causation is a favourite exam topic. Always state that correlation alone does NOT prove one thing causes another — you need controlled experiments. Give an example to show you understand.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Correlation means two variables are linked; causation means one directly causes the other. Proving causation requires controlled experiments. Many NCD studies show correlations, not proof of causation.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Non-communicable diseases are caused by the interaction of genetic, lifestyle and environmental factors — not pathogens.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Lifestyle risk factors include smoking, poor diet, excessive alcohol and lack of exercise, each linked to specific NCDs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Correlation does not prove causation — controlled experiments are needed to establish a causal link.</li>
</ul>
