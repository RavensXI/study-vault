--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Genes, Chromosomes and DNA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The entire set of genetic material (DNA) in an organism, containing all the genes needed to build and maintain that organism.">genome</dfn> is the entire set of genetic material in an organism. In humans, DNA is packaged into 23 pairs of <dfn class="term" data-def="Thread-like structures of DNA found in the nucleus of a cell. Humans have 23 pairs (46 total).">chromosomes</dfn> (46 total) inside the nucleus of most cells. Each chromosome carries hundreds of <dfn class="term" data-def="A short section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a specific protein, which determines a particular characteristic.">genes</dfn> — short sections of DNA that code for specific proteins. These proteins determine our characteristics, from eye colour to blood type (B5.1a).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Most body cells are <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two complete sets of chromosomes (one from each parent). In humans, diploid cells have 46 chromosomes.">diploid</dfn> — they contain two copies of each chromosome (one from each parent). Sex cells (<dfn class="term" data-def="Reproductive cells (sperm and egg) that contain half the normal number of chromosomes. In humans, gametes have 23 chromosomes.">gametes</dfn>) are <strong>haploid</strong>, containing just one set of 23 chromosomes. At fertilisation, two haploid gametes fuse to restore the diploid number (B5.1b).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many chromosomes do human body cells have? How many do gametes have? Why is this important for fertilisation?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Human body cells are diploid (46 chromosomes in 23 pairs). Gametes are haploid (23 chromosomes). At fertilisation, two gametes fuse to restore the diploid number.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Alleles and Inheritance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Because chromosomes come in pairs, we have two copies of each gene — one from each parent. Different versions of a gene are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Different versions of the same gene. For example, the gene for eye colour may have a brown allele and a blue allele.">alleles</dfn>. If both alleles are the same, the individual is <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two identical alleles for a particular gene, e.g. BB or bb.">homozygous</dfn> (e.g. BB or bb). If the alleles are different, the individual is <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two different alleles for a particular gene, e.g. Bb.">heterozygous</dfn> (e.g. Bb). A <dfn class="term" data-def="An allele that is expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present (in heterozygous individuals). Shown with a capital letter.">dominant</dfn> allele is expressed whenever it is present (shown with a capital letter), while a <dfn class="term" data-def="An allele that is only expressed in the phenotype when two copies are present (homozygous recessive). Shown with a lowercase letter.">recessive</dfn> allele is only expressed when homozygous (both copies the same, shown with a lowercase letter) (B5.1c).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The combination of alleles an organism has is its <dfn class="term" data-def="The genetic makeup of an organism, described using letters (e.g. Bb, BB, bb).">genotype</dfn> (e.g. Bb). The observable characteristic that results is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The observable characteristics of an organism, resulting from the interaction of its genotype with the environment.">phenotype</dfn> (e.g. brown eyes). The phenotype depends on both the genotype and environmental factors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Some characteristics are controlled by a single gene (monogenic inheritance), such as ear lobe attachment or tongue rolling. However, most characteristics are influenced by <strong>multiple genes</strong> (polygenic inheritance), such as height, skin colour and intelligence. Polygenic traits show <strong>continuous variation</strong> — a range of phenotypes rather than distinct categories — and are strongly influenced by environmental factors alongside genetics.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, mapped the entire human genome — around 20,000–25,000 genes. This has enabled scientists to identify genes linked to inherited disorders, develop genetic tests, and research personalised medicine where treatments are tailored to a patient’s specific genetic makeup.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Genetic screening</strong> can identify carriers of conditions like cystic fibrosis before they have children, allowing informed family planning. Embryos created through IVF can be tested for genetic disorders using <strong>pre-implantation genetic diagnosis</strong> (PGD), though this raises ethical questions about selecting embryos.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Meiosis</strong> is the type of cell division that produces gametes. During meiosis, chromosomes are shuffled and randomly assorted, and sections of DNA can be exchanged between chromosomes (<strong>crossing over</strong>). This creates genetically unique gametes, which is why siblings (other than identical twins) are genetically different from each other despite having the same parents. Meiosis is essential for maintaining genetic diversity within a population.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">A <strong>Punnett square</strong> is used to predict the possible offspring from a genetic cross. Place one parent’s alleles along the top and the other parent’s alleles down the side. Each box shows a possible genotype of the offspring.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Example: crossing two heterozygous parents (Bb × Bb) for a trait where B (brown eyes) is dominant over b (blue eyes):</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The predicted ratio is 3 brown : 1 blue (genotype ratio 1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb). This gives a 75% probability of the dominant phenotype and 25% probability of the recessive phenotype. Remember, these are <strong>probabilities</strong> — actual results may differ due to chance, especially with small sample sizes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">For OCR Gateway, you should be able to construct genetic diagrams showing: parental phenotypes and genotypes, gametes, a Punnett square, and offspring ratios with probabilities (B5.1d).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and draw a Punnett square for Bb x Bb from memory. Write out the offspring genotypes, phenotype ratio and percentage probabilities.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A Punnett square predicts offspring genotypes and phenotypes. Crossing Bb × Bb gives a 3:1 phenotype ratio (75% dominant, 25% recessive). These are probabilities, not certainties.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Some genetic disorders are caused by faulty alleles. <strong>Cystic fibrosis</strong> is caused by a recessive allele (ff). A person must be homozygous recessive to have the condition; heterozygous individuals (Ff) are <strong>carriers</strong> — they carry the allele but do not show symptoms. If two carriers have children, there is a 25% chance each child will have cystic fibrosis.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Polydactyly</strong> (extra fingers or toes) is caused by a dominant allele (D). Only one copy of the allele is needed for the condition to appear, so a cross between Dd and dd gives a 50% chance of affected offspring.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Sex is determined by the 23rd chromosome pair. Females have XX, males have XY. Since the father can pass either an X or a Y chromosome, there is a 50:50 chance of a child being male or female (B5.1e).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what is the probability of cystic fibrosis if both parents are carriers (Ff x Ff)? Draw the Punnett square to check.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele (two copies needed). Polydactyly is caused by a dominant allele (one copy is enough). Sex determination: XX = female, XY = male.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes. Alleles are different versions of genes; they can be dominant or recessive.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Punnett squares predict offspring genotypes and phenotype ratios. A Bb × Bb cross gives 3:1 (dominant : recessive).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Inherited disorders include cystic fibrosis (recessive, ff) and polydactyly (dominant, D). Sex is determined by XX (female) or XY (male).</li>
</ul>
