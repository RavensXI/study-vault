--- DESCRIPTION ---
What costumes, hair, and make-up reveal about characters — colour symbolism, the COSTUMES framework, and masks.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Costume, hair, and make-up are essential design elements in Blood Brothers. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A member of the creative team responsible for designing and organising the clothes, make-up, and accessories worn by actors to reflect character, status, and time period.">costume designer</dfn> must communicate character, social class, time period, and emotional development through their choices. In the exam, you will need to describe and justify costume decisions for specific characters and moments, using drama terminology throughout.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Remember the COSTUMES framework for answering design questions: <strong>C</strong>haracter/Context, in my <strong>O</strong>pinion, <strong>S</strong>tyle, <strong>T</strong>ime Period, <strong>U</strong>ber (location), <strong>M</strong>aterials, <strong>E</strong>xamples from the text, <strong>S</strong>emiotics.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every costume choice must be <strong>justified</strong>. In the exam, never just describe what a character wears — always explain <em>why</em> they wear it and what it <dfn class="term" data-def="The study of signs and symbols and how they communicate meaning to an audience. In drama, costume colours, props, and design choices can all be semiotic.">communicates to the audience</dfn> through semiotics.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mickey’s Costume</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Mickey as a child:</strong> Grey shorts with clear darned patches, home-knitted socks, a home-knitted jumper, a white shirt that is well-washed (suggesting it has been cleaned many times), and leather shoes that are scuffed and well-worn. A red jumper could reflect the working-class background and the family’s connection to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A political party traditionally associated with the working class, whose colour is red. The Johnstone family represents Labour values.">Labour Party</dfn>. Every detail signals poverty — the patches show Mrs Johnstone mends rather than replaces, the home-knitting shows she cannot afford shop-bought clothes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Mickey as an adult:</strong> Jeans with a slight flare to reflect the 1970s, a red t-shirt or shirt, and a denim jacket. All items should appear faded to show they are well-worn and that Mickey still cannot afford new clothes. The red continues to link him to the working class. After prison, his clothing could become even more dishevelled — untucked, stained, ill-fitting — to reflect his mental decline and reliance on antidepressants.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Edward’s Costume</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Edward as a child:</strong> Grey shorts that are ironed and made from good-quality material, manufactured socks (not home-knitted), a bright white shirt, and leather shoes that are highly polished. A blue jumper could reflect his family’s connection to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A political party traditionally associated with the middle and upper classes, whose colour is blue. The Lyons family represents Conservative values.">Conservative Party</dfn>. The contrast with Mickey is immediate — same type of clothing (shorts, shirt, jumper) but the quality, condition, and care are completely different.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Edward as an adult:</strong> Formal trousers, a white shirt, and a tweed-style jacket. These choices communicate education, professionalism, and wealth. His clothing is well-maintained and fits properly, in stark contrast to Mickey’s faded, worn clothing. The tweed jacket could suggest an academic or political lifestyle.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The costume design for Mickey and Edward works on a principle of <strong>contrast</strong>. As children, they wear similar types of clothing (shorts, shirts, jumpers) but the quality reveals their class. As adults, the gap widens dramatically: Mickey in faded denim and worn red; Edward in tailored tweed and crisp white. The audience should be able to see the class divide at a glance, without a word being spoken. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="Using visual elements on stage to communicate meaning to the audience without dialogue.">visual storytelling</dfn> through costume.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Mrs Johnstone’s Costume</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Young Mrs Johnstone:</strong> A wide-skirted dress above the knee to reflect the 1950s fashion and show she is not a reserved character. No tights, which reflects poverty — she cannot afford luxuries. An apron shows she works as a cleaner. A home-knitted cardigan shows she has to make do with what she can produce herself. Flat shoes show she is practical and on her feet all day. A pink dress could reflect that she is a woman who loves easily and has a warm, romantic nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Older Mrs Johnstone:</strong> A pink flowery dress to show she still has a sense of fun despite her hardships. A home-knitted cardigan shows she still does not waste money. The dress is now below the knee to show she has become more reserved with age. She now wears tights, suggesting she can finally afford small luxuries after moving to Skelmersdale. The continuity of the pink and the cardigan shows that her core personality — warmth, love, resilience — has not changed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Mrs Lyons’ Costume</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Young Mrs Lyons:</strong> A formal grey suit, well-tailored, to show her formal nature and high social status. Tights show she can afford luxuries. High heels show she does not have to work physically for a living. Everything about her appearance is controlled, neat, and expensive — the opposite of Mrs Johnstone’s practical, make-do style.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Older Mrs Lyons:</strong> A black skirt and top to show her descent into madness. Her hair should be wild and unkempt, contrasting with her earlier neatness. Mismatched shoes show she no longer cares about her appearance — her paranoia has consumed her. A red cardigan could show her anger and mistrust. The transformation from immaculate grey to dishevelled black visually communicates her psychological deterioration to the audience.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about mrs Johnstone’s costume gets slightly better.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Johnstone’s costume gets <em>slightly better</em> over time (she gains tights, keeps her fun style). Mrs Lyons’ costume gets <em>dramatically worse</em> (from tailored grey to dishevelled black). This reversal communicates a key message: Mrs Johnstone’s honesty and love sustain her, while Mrs Lyons’ guilt and deception destroy her from within.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Other Characters’ Costumes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Mr Lyons:</strong> A white shirt, a well-tailored suit, polished shoes, and a blue tie to signal his political affiliation with the Conservative Party. His costume never changes, reflecting his stable, privileged position. The blue tie is a semiotic choice — it links him to the party whose policies created the unemployment that destroys Mickey’s life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Linda:</strong> As a child, bright yellow to show she is positive, enthusiastic, and full of life. As an adult, her colours become duller, reflecting how life has worn her down. However, she might wear a yellow cardigan when she begins her affair with Edward, suggesting a brief return of the hope and excitement she had as a child.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Sammy:</strong> As an adult, dark colours (black, dark grey) to show that he lives a criminal lifestyle and needs to keep to the shadows. His clothing should look practical and anonymous — nothing that would make him stand out. This contrasts with his mother’s bright pinks and Linda’s yellows.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>The Narrator:</strong> A formal suit to show he is someone who should be respected and has authority. The suit suggests he is above the other characters, not part of their world but controlling it. Dark colours (black or very dark grey) would reinforce his connection to fate, death, and the supernatural.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Colour Symbolism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of specific colours in costume, set, or lighting to communicate meaning. For example, red can symbolise danger, love, or the Labour Party.">Colour symbolism</dfn> is central to costume design in Blood Brothers. Every colour choice should be deliberate and justifiable in the exam.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Red:</strong> Danger, warning, blood, anger — but also the Labour Party. The Johnstone family wears red to signal both their political identity and the danger that surrounds them. <strong>Blue:</strong> Calm, cold, isolation — but also the Conservative Party. The Lyons family wears blue to signal their political affiliation and emotional distance. <strong>Pink:</strong> Warmth, love, femininity — Mrs Johnstone’s signature colour, reflecting her loving nature. <strong>Black:</strong> Mourning, mystery, sinister intent — used for Mrs Lyons in her decline and for Sammy’s criminal persona. <strong>Yellow:</strong> Happiness, warmth, enthusiasm — Linda as a child. <strong>White:</strong> Purity, cleanliness, emptiness — the Lyons’ bright white shirts signal both wealth and emotional sterility.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Hair</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Hair design supports characterisation and tracks character development across the play.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Mickey:</strong> Messy throughout, reflecting his chaotic home life and lack of parental attention to grooming. <strong>Edward:</strong> Smart with a side parting, reflecting his well-groomed, middle-class upbringing. <strong>Mrs Johnstone:</strong> Tidy in Act One, loose in Act Two — showing how the weight of her secret and years of hardship have worn her down. <strong>Mrs Lyons:</strong> Tidy in Act One, wild in Act Two — her unkempt hair is a visual sign of her mental deterioration. <strong>Linda:</strong> Loose in Act One (young, free), tidy in Act Two (reflecting Mrs Johnstone’s influence, trying to hold things together). <strong>Sammy:</strong> Longer, shoulder-length hair to reflect his rebellious nature and rejection of social norms. <strong>The Narrator:</strong> Smart, reflecting his control and authority over the story.</p>

Make-up should be naturalistic for most characters, supporting the realistic style of the production.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Masks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Only one character in Blood Brothers could justifiably wear an actual <dfn class="term" data-def="A covering worn over the face by an actor to disguise identity, represent a character type, or create a specific dramatic effect.">mask</dfn>: the Narrator. This is because he is an anonymous character who controls and manipulates the other characters. He foreshadows events and remains outside the story. A mask would reinforce his mysteriousness, his separation from the human drama, and his role as a fate figure. No other character should wear a mask because the play relies on naturalistic facial expressions to communicate emotion.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n35">Costume in Blood Brothers uses contrast (Mickey vs Edward, Mrs Johnstone vs Mrs Lyons) to visually communicate the class divide and character development without dialogue.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Colour symbolism is deliberate: red = Labour/danger (Johnstones), blue = Conservative/cold (Lyons), black = crime/madness, pink = warmth/love, yellow = hope/youth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Hair and make-up track character change across the play — Mrs Lyons goes from tidy to wild, Mrs Johnstone from tidy to loose, and Mickey remains messy throughout, reflecting his lack of opportunity.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">In costume design questions, always cover: <strong>what</strong> the character would wear (specific items), <strong>what materials and colours</strong>, <strong>why</strong> each choice is made (character, class, time period), and <strong>what it communicates to the audience</strong> (semiotics). Use the COSTUMES framework. If discussing how costume <em>changes</em>, explain what the change reveals about the character’s development. Never describe costume in isolation — always link it to context, themes, and the audience’s understanding.</p>
