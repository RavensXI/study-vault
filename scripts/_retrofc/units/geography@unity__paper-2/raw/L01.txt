--- DESCRIPTION ---
Global trends in urbanisation, why cities grow faster in LICs and NEEs, and the rise of megacities with populations over 10 million.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Urbanisation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities.">Urbanisation</dfn> is the increase in the proportion of people living in towns and cities. In 2007 the world reached a landmark moment — for the first time in history, more people lived in urban areas than in rural areas. By 2050, it is projected that around 70% of the global population will live in cities.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the population in urban areas in 2005 and the projected figure for 2050.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In 2005, roughly 3.4 billion people lived in urban areas. By 2050, this figure is expected to reach 6.5 billion — nearly double in just 45 years.</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Push and pull factors driving urbanisation" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-2/diagram_urbanisation_factors.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">How Do Rates of Urbanisation Vary?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Urbanisation is happening at different speeds around the world. The pattern depends largely on how developed a country is:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A high-income country with a well-developed economy and infrastructure, such as the UK or USA.">HICs</dfn></strong> — were the first to urbanise during the Industrial Revolution. Most of their population already lives in cities (around 80%), so urbanisation rates have slowed and levelled off.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A low-income country with a less developed economy, such as many nations in sub-Saharan Africa.">LICs</dfn></strong> — are urbanising rapidly. High rates of <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities.">rural-urban migration</dfn> are driving fast city growth, especially in Africa and South Asia.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A newly emerging economy experiencing rapid industrialisation, such as Brazil, China or India.">NEEs</dfn></strong> — are also urbanising rapidly. Many South American NEEs urbanised earlier due to colonisation and industrialisation, but growth continues through <dfn class="term" data-def="When the birth rate is higher than the death rate, causing the population to grow.">natural increase</dfn> rather than migration.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Why Do People Move to Cities?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Rural-urban migration is the main driver of urbanisation in LICs and NEEs. People move because of a combination of <strong>push factors</strong> (reasons to leave rural areas) and <strong>pull factors</strong> (reasons to move to cities).</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Push and Pull Factors of Rural-Urban Migration</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Push factors</strong> (reasons people leave rural areas):</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n10">
<li>Drought and climate hazards reduce crop yields, leading to food shortages.</li>
<li>Farming is often <dfn class="term" data-def="Farming that produces only enough food to feed the farmer’s family, with nothing left to sell.">subsistence</dfn> — families produce only enough food to survive, with nothing to sell.</li>
<li>Few doctors or hospitals, leading to poor general health.</li>
<li>Schools provide only basic education, limiting future job prospects.</li>
<li>Rural areas are isolated with poor roads, rail and internet connections.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Pull factors</strong> (reasons people move to cities):</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n12">
<li>More well-paid jobs and a higher standard of living.</li>
<li>Better education, leading to higher-paid employment.</li>
<li>Better healthcare facilities and access to clean water.</li>
<li>Improved transport and communication links.</li>
<li>More entertainment and social opportunities.</li>
<li>Friends and family who already live there.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div></div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Natural Increase in Cities</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Rural-urban migration is not the only cause of city growth. Many migrants are young adults of childbearing age, so cities experience high rates of natural increase — more births than deaths. Improvements in healthcare, sanitation and water supply in cities also reduce the death rate and infant mortality, pushing the population even higher.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">What Is a Megacity?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A city with a population of more than 10 million people.">megacity</dfn> is a city with a population of more than 10 million people. In 2020 there were 34 megacities worldwide, and this number is predicted to reach 50 by 2050.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the world's largest megacity, and estimate its population size in millions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The world’s largest megacity is Tokyo, Japan, with a population of around 37 million. Other examples include Delhi (30 million), Shanghai (27 million), São Paulo (22 million) and Mexico City (22 million).</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Where Are Megacities Found?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The distribution of megacities has changed dramatically over time. In the 1950s, most megacities were in HICs — cities like New York, London and Tokyo. Today, the pattern has shifted. The majority of megacities are now found in LICs and NEEs, particularly in Asia, Africa and South America.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n20">
<li><strong>Asia</strong> has the most megacities, including Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Beijing, Mumbai and Dhaka.</li>
<li><strong>Africa</strong> is seeing rapid growth — Lagos (Nigeria) and Kinshasa (DR Congo) are already megacities, with more expected.</li>
<li><strong>South America</strong> includes São Paulo, Buenos Aires and Rio de Janeiro.</li>
<li><strong>Europe and North America</strong> have fewer, and their growth is much slower.</li>
</ul>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Why Are Megacities Growing Fastest in LICs and NEEs?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21">Cities in LICs and NEEs are growing fastest for two main reasons. First, large-scale rural-urban migration is driven by the search for jobs, education and healthcare. Second, natural increase is high because many migrants are young and healthcare improvements reduce death rates. In contrast, HICs have already urbanised and their populations are growing much more slowly, or even declining in some cities.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three cities predicted to become megacities by 2030.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>By 2030, the UN predicts 10 new megacities will emerge, almost all in less developed regions. Cities such as Dar es Salaam (Tanzania), Luanda (Angola) and Lahore (Pakistan) are among those expected to cross the 10 million threshold.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Urbanisation is a global trend, but it is happening at very different speeds depending on a country’s level of development. Push and pull factors drive rural-urban migration, while natural increase accelerates city growth further. Megacities are increasingly concentrated in LICs and NEEs.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Urbanisation rates</strong> are fastest in LICs and NEEs, where rural-urban migration and natural increase drive rapid city growth.</li>
<li><strong>Megacities</strong> have populations over 10 million, and most are now found in Asia, Africa and South America rather than in HICs.</li>
<li><strong>Push and pull factors</strong> explain why people leave rural areas for cities — the search for jobs, education and healthcare is the main driver.</li>
</ul>
