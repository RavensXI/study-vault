--- DESCRIPTION ---
What controls how fast a reaction goes and how much product we get.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rate of Reaction</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how quickly reactants are converted into products in a chemical reaction. Calculated as the quantity of reactant used or product formed per unit time.">rate of reaction</dfn> tells you how quickly a reaction proceeds. It is calculated from:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">$$\text{rate} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used or product formed}}{\text{time taken}}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The units of rate depend on how the quantity is measured. If you measure the volume of gas produced in cm<sup>3</sup> over time in seconds, the rate has units of cm<sup>3</sup>/s. A common student error is to measure volume in cm<sup>3</sup> but record time in minutes, and then forget to convert &mdash; always check your units match.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the four factors that can change reaction rate, giving one sentence of explanation for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rate of reaction can be increased by: raising temperature, increasing concentration (or pressure for gases), increasing surface area of solid reactants, or using a catalyst. Rate = quantity of reactant or product &divide; time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Collision Theory</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that a chemical reaction can only occur when reactant particles collide with sufficient energy (at least the activation energy) and in the correct orientation.">Collision theory</dfn> explains why rate changes when conditions change. A reaction only occurs when particles: (1) collide with each other, and (2) have at least the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum amount of energy that colliding reactant particles need to react and form products.">activation energy</dfn>. If either condition is not met, particles bounce off without reacting.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Each factor affects either the frequency of collisions or the proportion that have enough energy:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Temperature</strong> &mdash; raising temperature gives particles more kinetic energy. They move faster, collide more frequently, and a greater proportion exceed the activation energy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Concentration / Pressure</strong> &mdash; more particles in a given volume means more frequent collisions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Surface area</strong> &mdash; breaking a solid into smaller pieces exposes more surface, increasing the area available for collisions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Catalyst</strong> &mdash; provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. More collisions now have sufficient energy to succeed. The catalyst is not used up and does not appear in the overall equation.</li>
</ul>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">Three common methods to measure reaction rate:</p>
    <ol>
      <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Gas collection</strong> &mdash; gas produced is collected in a gas syringe or over water in an inverted measuring cylinder. Volume is recorded at intervals.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Mass loss</strong> &mdash; the reaction vessel sits on a balance; mass decreases as gas escapes. Mass is recorded at intervals.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Colour/turbidity change</strong> &mdash; used for reactions that produce a precipitate or change colour. The &ldquo;disappearing cross&rdquo; experiment (sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid) measures the time for a cross beneath the flask to become invisible as sulfur precipitate forms. Rate = 1 &divide; time.</li>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words: why does a catalyst increase rate without changing the overall equation? Mention activation energy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Catalysts lower the activation energy by providing an alternative reaction pathway. More collisions have sufficient energy to succeed. The catalyst is not used up and does not appear in the overall equation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Yield and Atom Economy</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The mass of product actually obtained from a reaction, expressed as a percentage of the maximum theoretical yield.">Percentage yield</dfn> compares what you actually obtained to what you could theoretically obtain:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n24">Yield is rarely 100%. Reasons include: the reaction may not go to completion, some product is lost in filtering or transferring, or a reversible reaction may not fully favour the products. In industry, maximising yield while controlling costs is a key challenge.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the proportion of the reactant atoms that end up in the desired product, expressed as a percentage of the total mass of all products.">Atom economy</dfn> measures how efficiently atoms from reactants end up in the desired product:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n26">$$\text{atom economy} = \frac{\text{relative formula mass of desired product}}{\text{sum of relative formula masses of all products}} \times 100$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n27">A high atom economy means less waste. Addition reactions (where all reactants become one product) have 100% atom economy. Reactions that produce by-products have lower atom economy. Green chemistry aims to maximise atom economy to reduce waste and cost.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Atom Economy Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Consider the reaction: \(CaCO_3 \rightarrow CaO + CO_2\). The desired product is CaO (\(M_r = 56\)). The by-product is CO<sub>2</sub> (\(M_r = 44\)). Total mass of products = 56 + 44 = 100. Atom economy = (56 &divide; 100) &times; 100 = <strong>56%</strong>. This means 44% of the atom mass ends up as the waste gas CO<sub>2</sub>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Why Yield and Rate Both Matter</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n30">In industry, the goal is not simply to achieve the fastest possible rate or the highest possible yield &mdash; it is to produce the most product economically over time. A reaction that runs very quickly but has a low yield may be less profitable than a slower reaction with a high yield, depending on the cost of raw materials and energy. Conversely, a reaction with a high theoretical yield but a very slow rate may not be commercially viable without a catalyst to speed it up.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n31">Industrial chemists therefore optimise conditions to balance rate and yield together. For example, using a catalyst simultaneously increases rate (without reducing yield) while allowing lower temperatures &mdash; reducing energy costs. Recycling unreacted starting materials back into the reaction vessel can also improve the overall yield over time, even if the yield at any single pass is moderate. These economic and environmental factors make the design of industrial chemical processes genuinely complex, and they illustrate why chemistry has real-world importance beyond the laboratory.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Rate of reaction = quantity of reactant or product &divide; time. Four factors increase rate: higher temperature, higher concentration (or pressure), greater surface area, or a catalyst &mdash; all explained by collision theory and the activation energy concept.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Percentage yield = (actual yield &divide; theoretical yield) &times; 100. Yield is rarely 100% due to incomplete reactions, product loss, or reversible reactions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Atom economy = (\(M_r\) of desired product &divide; total \(M_r\) of all products) &times; 100. High atom economy means less waste; addition reactions have 100% atom economy.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">Rate calculation questions frequently involve unit errors &mdash; always check whether time is in seconds or minutes before dividing. For graph questions, the gradient at any point represents the rate: a steeper gradient means a faster rate. In collision theory questions, you must link the factor to <em>frequency</em> of collisions or <em>proportion</em> with sufficient energy &mdash; not just say &ldquo;particles collide more.&rdquo; For atom economy, remember addition reactions give 100% because all atoms end up in a single product.</p>
