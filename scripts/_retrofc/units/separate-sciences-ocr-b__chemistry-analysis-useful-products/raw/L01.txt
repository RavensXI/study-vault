Filtration, distillation and how to test if a sample is pure.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pure Substances and Mixtures</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">In chemistry, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance containing only one type of element or compound. A pure substance has a fixed, sharp melting and boiling point.">pure substance</dfn> consists of a single element or compound. A pure substance melts and boils at a precise, fixed temperature. A <dfn class="term" data-def="Two or more substances that are not chemically combined. Mixtures melt and boil over a range of temperatures rather than at a sharp point.">mixture</dfn> contains two or more substances not chemically bonded together. Mixtures melt and boil over a range of temperatures, not at a fixed point &mdash; and this difference is the basis of purity testing.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">To test whether a sample is pure, measure its melting point and compare it to the accepted value for that substance. If the melting point is lower than expected and occurs over a range of temperatures, the sample contains impurities. This technique is used routinely in pharmaceutical quality control to check that a medicine contains the correct active compound.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how does melting point evidence tell you whether a substance is pure or a mixture? Give one specific difference.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A pure substance has a sharp melting point at a fixed temperature. A mixture melts over a range of temperatures. Impurities lower the melting point and broaden the melting range.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Separation Techniques</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Because mixtures are not chemically combined, their components can be separated using physical methods. The correct technique depends on the nature of the mixture and the properties of its components.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Filtration and Crystallisation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A separation technique that separates an insoluble solid from a liquid by passing the mixture through filter paper. The solid remains on the paper as the residue; the liquid passes through as the filtrate.">Filtration</dfn> separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel; the solid remains as the <strong>residue</strong> and the liquid passes through as the <strong>filtrate</strong>. For example, sand is separated from salt solution by filtration.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for obtaining a pure solid from a solution by evaporating the solvent until crystals form. The crystals are then filtered and dried.">Crystallisation</dfn> recovers a dissolved solid from solution. The solution is heated to evaporate most of the solvent, then left to cool slowly. As the solution cools, the dissolved substance becomes less soluble and forms crystals. The crystals can be filtered and dried. This is used to purify soluble salts such as copper sulfate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Simple and Fractional Distillation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A separation technique used to separate a dissolved solid from its solvent, or to separate two liquids with very different boiling points. The mixture is heated and the vapour condensed back to a liquid.">Simple distillation</dfn> works when the substances in a mixture have very different boiling points (usually more than 25&deg;C apart). The mixture is heated; the more volatile liquid evaporates first, the vapour is cooled in a condenser, and the liquid is collected. For example, distilled water is produced from salt solution by simple distillation &mdash; water evaporates and is condensed, leaving salts behind.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A separation technique for liquids with similar boiling points. A fractionating column allows vapours to condense and re-evaporate multiple times, separating the mixture into fractions by boiling point.">Fractional distillation</dfn> is used when liquids have similar boiling points. A fractionating column is packed with glass beads or plates that provide surfaces for condensation. Liquids with lower boiling points travel further up the column and are collected first. This is the method used to separate crude oil into fractions such as petrol, kerosene, and diesel.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chromatography</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating and identifying dissolved substances in a mixture by their different solubilities in a moving solvent.">Chromatography</dfn> separates substances in a mixture based on how strongly they are attracted to the stationary phase (e.g. paper) compared to how soluble they are in the moving phase (the solvent). In paper chromatography, a pencil line is drawn near the bottom of the paper, spots of mixture are placed on the line, and the paper is placed in solvent. As the solvent moves up, it carries substances with it &mdash; those that are more soluble travel further.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The position of each spot is described by its <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the distance moved by a substance to the distance moved by the solvent in chromatography. A pure substance has a unique Rf value in a given solvent.">R<sub>f</sub> value</dfn>:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Each substance has a unique R<sub>f</sub> value in a given solvent at a given temperature. A pure substance gives a single spot; a mixture gives multiple spots at different heights. By comparing R<sub>f</sub> values with reference values, or by running known substances alongside the unknown, substances can be identified.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the formula for Rf value, then explain what it would mean if two spots on a chromatogram had the same Rf value.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>R<sub>f</sub> = distance moved by substance &divide; distance moved by solvent. In a given solvent, each substance has a unique R<sub>f</sub> value. A pure substance produces one spot; a mixture produces multiple spots.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Required Practical: Paper Chromatography</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">To carry out a paper chromatography investigation: draw a pencil baseline (not pen &mdash; ink would also travel) near the bottom of the chromatography paper. Spot the sample mixture and reference substances on the baseline. Place the paper in a small amount of appropriate solvent &mdash; the solvent level must be <strong>below</strong> the pencil line so the spots do not dissolve directly into the solvent pot. Allow the solvent to rise until it nears the top of the paper, then remove and immediately mark the solvent front with pencil. Measure distances from the pencil line to calculate R<sub>f</sub> values. Variables to control include the type of solvent and the length of time the chromatogram is run.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Instrumental Methods of Identification</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n19">As well as chromatography, modern laboratories use instrumental techniques that are faster, more sensitive, and capable of analysing very small samples. <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrumental technique that ionises a substance and measures the mass-to-charge ratios of the resulting ions to identify the substance and determine its molecular mass.">Mass spectrometry</dfn> identifies compounds by fragmenting molecules into ions and measuring their mass-to-charge ratios. The output is a mass spectrum: a graph showing the relative abundance of different fragment masses. The largest fragment (the molecular ion) gives the relative molecular mass of the compound. Mass spectrometry is used in forensic science, drug testing, and quality control of pharmaceuticals.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Flame tests use the colour of light emitted when a compound is heated in a flame to identify metal ions. Lithium gives a crimson flame; sodium gives yellow; potassium gives lilac; calcium gives red-orange; copper gives blue-green. These are qualitative tests &mdash; they tell you which metal is present but not how much.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Pure substances have sharp, fixed melting and boiling points; mixtures melt over a range. Purity testing compares the measured melting point against the accepted value.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Separation techniques (filtration, crystallisation, simple and fractional distillation) are chosen based on the properties of the components; chromatography separates and identifies dissolved substances by their R<sub>f</sub> values in a given solvent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Instrumental methods such as mass spectrometry can identify substances with greater speed and sensitivity than traditional tests, and are widely used in forensic science and pharmaceutical quality control.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">For the required practical chromatography question, always state why a <em>pencil</em> baseline is used (pen ink would travel in the solvent). When asked to calculate an R<sub>f</sub> value, make sure you measure from the <em>baseline</em> to the centre of each spot, not from the bottom of the paper. If asked about purity, always link melting point evidence to the conclusion &mdash; a melting range rather than a sharp point indicates a mixture.</p>
