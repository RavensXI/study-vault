<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Background: Bach and the Badinerie</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Johann Sebastian Bach was a German composer and musician of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A period of Western Classical music from 1600 to 1750, characterised by ornate musical decoration and the use of basso continuo.">Baroque era</dfn>. He was born on 31st March 1685 and died on 28th July 1750. Bach is known for instrumental works such as the Brandenburg Concertos and the Goldberg Variations, and vocal music such as the St Matthew Passion and the Mass in B minor. He is generally regarded as one of the greatest composers of all time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A quick, light movement in a Baroque suite, meaning a playful or teasing piece.">Badinerie</dfn> is the final movement of Bach’s Orchestral Suite No. 2 in B minor, composed in 1738–1739. The suite contains seven movements: Overture, Rondeau, Sarabande, Bourrée, Polonaise, Menuet and Badinerie. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A collection of short musical pieces, usually dances, which can be played one after another.">suite</dfn> is a collection of short pieces, usually dances, played one after another. The word “badinerie” means something playful or teasing, and the music reflects this with its fast tempo and lively flute part.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define the Badinerie in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Badinerie is scored for <strong>transverse flute</strong>, <strong>string orchestra</strong> (Violin 1, Violin 2, Viola, Cello) and <strong>harpsichord</strong> (basso continuo). The transverse flute is held horizontally, unlike a recorder which is held vertically. Basso continuo is a system of partially improvised accompaniment played above a bass line, typically by a keyboard instrument with the cello reinforcing the bass.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Form and Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Badinerie is in <dfn class="term" data-def="A musical form with two contrasting sections, A and B, each usually repeated (AABB).">binary form</dfn> (AB), with each section repeated once to give AABB. This is a standard structure for Baroque dance movements.</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n7">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Section A</div>
    <h4>Bars 0<sup>2</sup> – 16<sup>1</sup> (16 bars)</h4><p>Begins in B minor (the tonic key) and ends in F# minor (the dominant minor). Both repeat marks are present, so the whole section is played twice.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Section B</div>
    <h4>Bars 16<sup>2</sup> – 40<sup>1</sup> (24 bars)</h4><p>Begins in F# minor and modulates through E minor and D major (the relative major) before returning to the tonic key, B minor. Section B is longer than Section A and is also repeated.</p>
  </div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Notice how Section A moves <em>away</em> from the tonic (B minor → F# minor), while Section B mirrors this by moving <em>back</em> to the tonic (F# minor → B minor). This tonal journey is a hallmark of binary form in the Baroque era.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Melody: Motifs X and Y</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The entire movement is built on just two short musical ideas, called <dfn class="term" data-def="A short, recurring musical idea that forms the basis of a melody or theme.">motif</dfn> X and motif Y. Bach develops, modifies and passes these motifs between instruments throughout the piece.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Motif X — The Descending Arpeggio</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Motif X is heard at the very start (bars 0<sup>2</sup>–2<sup>1</sup>) in the flute. It is a <strong>descending B minor arpeggio/broken chord</strong> with a characteristic quaver-and-semiquaver rhythm. This motif appears many times throughout the piece in modified forms:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Bars 6<sup>2</sup>–8<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif X moves to the cellos in a modified version — the final crotchet is replaced with a quaver and two semiquavers. This shifts the tonality to A major and begins a <dfn class="term" data-def="The repetition of a motif at a higher or lower pitch in the same voice or instrument.">sequence</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Bars 8<sup>2</sup>–10<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif X continues in the cellos with its ending further modified (four semiquavers). It moves the tonality to F# minor and completes the answering phrase of the sequence.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Bars 18<sup>2</sup>–20<sup>1</sup> (Section B):</strong> Motif X is <strong>inverted</strong> so that it ascends rather than descends. It is also divided between two instruments. This moves the tonality to E minor.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Bars 32<sup>2</sup>–34<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif X becomes part of a musical conversation between the flute and the 1st violin. It is decorated by an <dfn class="term" data-def="An added note, one step above or below the main note, which takes some of the main note's time value; written as a small grace note without a line through it.">appoggiatura</dfn> and rapid demisemiquaver movement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Motif Y — The Ascending Semiquaver Figure</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Motif Y first appears in bars 2<sup>2</sup>–4<sup>1</sup> in the flute. It is an <strong>ascending semiquaver figure</strong> consisting of arpeggios/broken chords and conjunct (stepwise) movement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Bars 10<sup>2</sup>–12<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif Y returns in the flute with a modified ending — the last two quavers are replaced by four semiquavers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Bars 12<sup>2</sup>–16<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif Y is extended and developed (labelled Y1 in the analysis). Section A closes in F# minor.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Bars 22<sup>2</sup>–24<sup>1</sup> (Section B):</strong> Motif Y returns with the same modified ending as bars 10<sup>2</sup>–12<sup>1</sup>, but transposed into a new key.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Bars 34<sup>2</sup>–36<sup>1</sup>:</strong> Motif Y returns with a modified beginning — the first four semiquavers are replaced by a quaver and four demisemiquavers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Harmony and Tonality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The harmony is <dfn class="term" data-def="Music that uses only the notes belonging to the current key, without chromatic alterations.">diatonic</dfn> throughout. The piece begins in B minor and modulates to closely related keys. The key scheme is carefully structured:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Section A:</strong> B minor → A major → F# minor (dominant minor). <strong>Section B:</strong> F# minor → E minor → D major (relative major) → B minor (tonic). Both <dfn class="term" data-def="A chord progression at the end of a musical phrase; a perfect cadence moves from chord V to chord I.">perfect cadences</dfn> and imperfect cadences appear clearly throughout the movement.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about chords frequently occur in inversion.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Chords frequently occur in <strong>inversion</strong>, with occasional use of V<sup>7</sup> in third inversion. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A major chord built on the flattened supertonic (second note) of the key, most commonly found in first inversion.">Neapolitan sixth chord</dfn> appears in bar 35. Suspensions occur in bars 8<sup>1</sup>, 10<sup>1</sup> and 32<sup>1</sup>.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Rhythm, Metre and Tempo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The time signature is <strong>2/4</strong> (simple duple) throughout. The tempo is Allegro (fast), though this is not marked on the original score — this was typical of the Baroque period. The rhythms are built from simple <dfn class="term" data-def="A short, repeated rhythmic or melodic pattern that forms a continuous accompaniment.">ostinato</dfn> patterns using almost entirely quavers and semiquavers, with occasional demisemiquavers used as decoration (e.g. bars 32–34).</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Texture and Instrumentation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The texture is <dfn class="term" data-def="A texture in which one melody line is accompanied by chords or other supporting parts.">homophonic</dfn> (melody and accompaniment). The flute carries the main melody for most of the movement. The cello provides important melodic material too, especially during the sequences in bars 6<sup>2</sup>–10<sup>1</sup> and 28<sup>2</sup>–32<sup>1</sup>. The 1st violin joins in occasionally (e.g. the musical conversation in bars 32<sup>2</sup>–34<sup>1</sup>), while the 2nd violin and viola provide harmony with less busy lines.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The dynamics are mostly <strong>forte</strong>, with <dfn class="term" data-def="A feature of Baroque music where volume levels shift suddenly from quiet to loud without gradual crescendos or diminuendos.">terraced dynamics</dfn> (sudden shifts between loud and quiet rather than gradual crescendos and diminuendos). Very few dynamic markings appear on the score, which was typical of the period.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Inversion:</strong> In bars 18<sup>2</sup>–20<sup>1</sup> and 36<sup>2</sup>–38<sup>1</sup>, motif X is inverted so it ascends rather than descends.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Terraced dynamics:</strong> Volume shifts suddenly rather than gradually.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Diatonic harmony:</strong> The harmony stays within closely related keys.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39"><strong>Binary form:</strong> A standard structure for Baroque dance movements.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n40"><strong>Instrumentation:</strong> Use of transverse flute, strings and harpsichord is typical of a Baroque orchestral suite.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n41" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 2 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The flute part has a <strong>two-octave pitch range</strong>. The entire movement is built from just two motifs (X and Y) which are constantly modified, extended, inverted and passed between instruments. This motivic development is a key feature of Bach’s compositional style.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n43">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n44">The Badinerie is in <strong>binary form (AABB)</strong>, starting in B minor. Section A modulates to F# minor; Section B returns to B minor via E minor and D major.</li><li data-narration-id="n45">The whole movement is built from <strong>two motifs (X and Y)</strong> which are developed through sequences, inversion, extension and modification across the flute, cello and 1st violin.</li><li data-narration-id="n46">Key Baroque features include basso continuo (harpsichord &amp; cello), terraced dynamics, ornaments (trills and appoggiaturas), diatonic harmony with perfect and imperfect cadences, and the use of a transverse flute.</li></ul>
