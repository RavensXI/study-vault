--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Visualisation Diagrams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A hand-drawn or digitally created sketch that shows the planned layout and design of a static media product such as a magazine page, poster, DVD cover, or game interface, including annotations explaining design choices.">visualisation diagram</dfn> is a sketch that shows what a <strong>static</strong> media product will look like. It is used for products that do not involve moving images — for example, a magazine page, a poster, a CD cover, a leaflet, a game interface, or a print advertisement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The purpose of a visualisation diagram is to show the client and design team the <strong>planned layout</strong> of the product before it is created. It allows the designer to experiment with positioning text, images and graphic elements, and to get feedback before committing to the final design.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the typography terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Visualisation diagrams are used for <strong>static products</strong> (print layouts, posters, DVD covers, game interfaces). They show design ideas and possible layouts with <strong>annotations</strong> explaining choices such as colour, font and image placement.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">A good visualisation diagram includes:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Layout sketch</strong> — a drawing showing where each element (images, text, logos) will be positioned</li>
<li><strong>Annotations</strong> — labels and notes explaining design decisions, such as why a particular font or colour has been chosen</li>
<li><strong>Colour indications</strong> — colour swatches or written descriptions of the intended colour scheme</li>
<li><strong>Font/typography choices</strong> — the style, size and weight of text to be used</li>
<li><strong>Image placeholders</strong> — boxes or rough sketches showing where photographs or graphics will go</li>
<li><strong>Dimensions</strong> — the overall size and proportions of the product</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Visualisation diagrams can be created by hand (using pencil, paper and coloured pens) or digitally (using software such as Adobe Illustrator, Photoshop, or Canva). Hand-drawn versions are quick to produce and easy to sketch during meetings, while digital versions look more professional and are easier to edit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">They are used by <strong>graphic designers</strong>, <strong>art directors</strong>, <strong>marketing teams</strong>, and <strong>clients</strong>. The diagram allows stakeholders to agree on the layout and style before the final product is built, saving time and cost.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Wireframe Layouts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A simple, skeletal outline of a web page or app screen that shows the placement of elements such as navigation, headers, content areas and buttons, without colours, images or detailed styling.">wireframe</dfn> is a simplified, skeleton layout of a <strong>digital product</strong> — most commonly a website or mobile app. It focuses on the <strong>structure and positioning</strong> of elements rather than the visual design.</p>

<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/pre-production/diagram_visualisation_diagrams_and_wireframes.jpg" alt="Diagram: Visualisation Diagrams and Wireframes" loading="lazy"></figure>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the typography terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The purpose of a wireframe is to plan the <strong>structure and layout</strong> of a web page or app screen — showing where the navigation, headers, content areas, images and buttons will go — <strong>before</strong> any visual design (colours, fonts, images) is applied.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Components and Conventions of Wireframes</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">Wireframes follow specific conventions:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li><strong>Boxes and rectangles</strong> — represent content areas, image placeholders, and buttons</li>
<li><strong>Lines of text (or “lorem ipsum”)</strong> — indicate where text content will appear, without using real wording</li>
<li><strong>Crossed boxes</strong> — an “X” through a rectangle indicates an image placeholder</li>
<li><strong>Navigation elements</strong> — menus, buttons and links are shown in their planned positions</li>
<li><strong>No colour or styling</strong> — wireframes are deliberately plain (usually black, white and grey) to focus attention on layout, not aesthetics</li>
<li><strong>Labels</strong> — text labels identify each section (e.g. “Header”, “Main content”, “Sidebar”, “Footer”)</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Wireframes can be sketched by hand on paper or created using dedicated tools such as Figma, Balsamiq, or Adobe XD. They are used by <strong>web developers</strong>, <strong>UX designers</strong>, <strong>project managers</strong>, and <strong>clients</strong>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Visualisation Diagram vs Wireframe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">These two documents are easy to confuse, but they serve different purposes:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n17">
<li>A <strong>visualisation diagram</strong> is for <strong>static/print products</strong> (posters, magazine covers, DVD cases). It includes colour, font choices, and design detail.</li>
<li>A <strong>wireframe</strong> is for <strong>digital/interactive products</strong> (websites, apps). It is deliberately stripped of visual design to focus on structure and <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall experience a person has when interacting with a digital product, including how easy it is to navigate, find content and complete tasks.">user experience</dfn>.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Making These Documents Effective</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">An <strong>effective visualisation diagram</strong> clearly shows the layout with detailed annotations explaining every design choice. It includes colour swatches and font samples so the client can visualise the final product. It can be improved by adding multiple versions showing alternative layouts, including size/dimension annotations, or using digital tools for a cleaner presentation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">An <strong>effective wireframe</strong> clearly shows the position and size of all page elements, uses consistent conventions (crossed boxes for images, lorem ipsum for text), and is labelled so non-technical stakeholders can understand it. It can be improved by creating wireframes for multiple screen sizes (desktop, tablet, mobile), adding interactive prototyping links between pages, or annotating the user journey.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Visualisation diagrams</strong> show the planned layout and design of static products (posters, magazine covers, DVD cases) with colour, font and image annotations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Wireframes</strong> show the skeletal structure of digital products (websites, apps) without colour or styling, focusing purely on element placement and user navigation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Both are created in pre-production to get client and team agreement on layout before the final product is built.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">A common exam mistake is mixing up visualisation diagrams and wireframes. Remember: <strong>visualisation diagrams = static/print</strong> (with colour and design detail), <strong>wireframes = digital/interactive</strong> (plain layout only). If the scenario is about a website or app, choose a wireframe. If it is about a poster, magazine cover, or DVD case, choose a visualisation diagram.</p>
