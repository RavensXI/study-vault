<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Scripts in Media Pre-Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A written document that contains the dialogue, stage directions, camera directions and scene descriptions for a media product such as a film, TV programme, radio show or animation.">script</dfn> is one of the most important pre-production documents for any media product that involves spoken words, acting, or narration. Scripts are used for films, TV programmes, radio shows, animations, podcasts, and even some advertisements.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The purpose of a script is to provide the cast and crew with all the information they need to perform and film each scene. It tells actors what to say, how to move, and what emotions to convey. It also tells the director and camera operators how scenes should look.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the pre-production documents and what each contains.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A script contains <strong>dialogue</strong> (what characters say), <strong>stage directions</strong> (how characters move and behave), <strong>camera directions</strong> (shot types and angles), and <strong>scene descriptions</strong> (setting, time of day, location). Together, these ensure the entire team understands the creative vision.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">Professional scripts follow strict formatting conventions:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Scene heading (slug line)</strong> — written in capitals, stating whether the scene is interior (INT.) or exterior (EXT.), the location, and the time of day. Example: <em>INT. SCHOOL LIBRARY — DAY</em></li>
<li><strong>Action/description</strong> — written in present tense, describing what the audience sees and what characters are doing</li>
<li><strong>Character name</strong> — centred and in capitals above the character’s dialogue</li>
<li><strong>Dialogue</strong> — centred beneath the character name, showing what they say</li>
<li><strong>Parenthetical</strong> — brief <dfn class="term" data-def="A short instruction in brackets within a script, placed between the character name and dialogue, indicating how a line should be delivered (e.g. angrily, whispering, sarcastically).">parenthetical directions</dfn> in brackets indicating how a line should be delivered (e.g. “angrily”, “whispering”)</li>
<li><strong>Transitions</strong> — instructions for how one scene moves to the next, e.g. CUT TO:, FADE OUT, DISSOLVE TO:</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Scripts can be written using word processing software or specialist screenwriting tools such as Celtx or Final Draft. These dedicated tools automatically format the script to industry standard layout.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Scripts are used by <strong>directors</strong>, <strong>actors</strong>, <strong>camera operators</strong>, <strong>sound technicians</strong>, and <strong>editors</strong>. Each person reads the same script but focuses on the information relevant to their role.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Storyboards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A visual planning document that uses a sequence of drawn panels to show the key scenes, camera shots, angles and movements for a film, animation, TV programme or advertisement.">storyboard</dfn> is a visual plan that looks like a comic strip. It shows the key moments of a media product as a sequence of drawn panels, each representing a different shot or scene.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the pre-production documents and what each contains.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The purpose of a storyboard is to <strong>visually plan the sequence of shots</strong> in a moving-image product. It shows the production team what each shot should look like, what camera angles and movements to use, and how scenes flow from one to the next.</p>
</div>

<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Components and Conventions of Storyboards</span>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">Each panel of a storyboard typically includes:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li><strong>Image/sketch</strong> — a drawing showing what the camera will see in that shot</li>
<li><strong>Shot type</strong> — close-up, medium shot, long shot, etc.</li>
<li><strong>Camera angle</strong> — high angle, low angle, eye level, bird’s eye</li>
<li><strong>Camera movement</strong> — pan, tilt, zoom, tracking shot, crane shot</li>
<li><strong>Dialogue/sound</strong> — any dialogue or sound effects that accompany the shot</li>
<li><strong>Duration</strong> — how long the shot lasts (in seconds)</li>
<li><strong>Transitions</strong> — how the shot connects to the next (cut, fade, dissolve)</li>
<li><strong>Notes</strong> — additional instructions for lighting, props, or special effects</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Storyboards can be hand drawn on pre-printed templates or created digitally using software such as Storyboarder, Canva, or Boords. Hand drawn storyboards are quick to produce but can be harder to share. Digital versions are easier to edit and distribute.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">When to Use Scripts and Storyboards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Scripts are appropriate whenever a media product involves <strong>spoken dialogue</strong>, <strong>narration</strong>, or <strong>performed actions</strong>. A TV drama, podcast, radio advertisement, or animation would all require a script.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Storyboards are appropriate whenever a product involves <strong>moving images</strong>. Films, TV adverts, animations, music videos, and even some website animations benefit from storyboarding. A printed magazine or a podcast, however, would not need a storyboard.</p>

<span>Making Scripts and Storyboards Effective</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">An <strong>effective script</strong> uses industry standard formatting, provides clear stage and camera directions, and includes all necessary scene descriptions. It can be improved by adding more detailed parentheticals for actors, including timing notes, or adding a <dfn class="term" data-def="A detailed breakdown of each scene in a script, listing all the props, costumes, locations, cast, and equipment needed, used to organise the shooting schedule.">shooting schedule</dfn> reference for each scene.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">An <strong>effective storyboard</strong> has clear drawings (they do not need to be artistic masterpieces), accurate shot descriptions, and enough panels to cover the full sequence. It can be improved by adding colour to distinguish different locations, including timecodes, or annotating camera movements with arrows.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">How Scripts and Storyboards Work Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">In most professional productions, scripts and storyboards are used <strong>together</strong>. The script provides the dialogue and scene descriptions in written form, while the storyboard translates these into a visual sequence. The director uses both to brief the cast and crew — actors read the script for their lines, camera operators study the storyboard for shot composition.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Scripts</strong> contain dialogue, stage directions, camera directions and scene descriptions. They follow strict formatting conventions including slug lines, character names in capitals, and transitions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Storyboards</strong> are visual sequences showing shots, camera angles, movement, dialogue, duration and transitions — like a comic strip of the planned product.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Scripts are for any product with spoken words; storyboards are for any product with moving images. Professional productions typically use both together.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">If the exam gives you a scenario involving a moving-image product, think about whether a script, a storyboard, or both are needed. A film needs both. A podcast needs only a script. A photo shoot might need neither. Always name <strong>specific components</strong> in your answer — saying “it includes shot types and camera angles” is stronger than just saying “it shows the shots.”</p>
