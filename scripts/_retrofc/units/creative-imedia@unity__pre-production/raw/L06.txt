<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Camera Techniques in Pre-Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When planning a moving-image media product, the production team must decide which <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance and framing of the camera relative to the subject, determining how much of the scene is visible in the frame. Examples include close-up, medium shot and long shot.">camera shots</dfn>, angles and movements will be used. These technical decisions are recorded on storyboards and scripts during pre-production and then executed by the camera operator during filming.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding camera terminology is essential for R093. You need to know the different shot types, what they communicate to the audience, and when each would be appropriate in a given scenario.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Shot Types</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Shot types describe <strong>how much of the subject and setting</strong> the camera captures. Moving from widest to closest:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The exam may show you an image and ask you to identify the shot type, or give you a scenario and ask which shot type would be most effective. Learn all shot types and <strong>why</strong> each is used, not just their names.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Full List of Shot Types</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>Extreme Long Shot (ELS)</strong> — shows a vast area of landscape or setting. Used to establish location and make subjects appear small and insignificant.</li>
<li><strong>Long Shot (LS)</strong> — shows the full body of a character within their surroundings. Used to show the character in context.</li>
<li><strong>Medium Long Shot (MLS)</strong> — frames the character from roughly the knees up. Shows body language while giving some environmental context.</li>
<li><strong>Medium Shot (MS)</strong> — frames the character from the waist up. The standard “conversational” shot used in interviews and dialogue scenes.</li>
<li><strong>Bust Shot</strong> — frames the character from the chest/shoulders up. Common in news broadcasts and formal interviews.</li>
<li><strong>Close-Up (CU)</strong> — fills the frame with the subject’s face (or a single object). Used to show emotion, detail, or importance.</li>
<li><strong>Extreme Close-Up (ECU)</strong> — shows a very small detail — just the eyes, a hand, or a specific object. Creates intensity and draws attention to a key detail.</li>
<li><strong>Two Shot</strong> — frames two characters together, often used in dialogue or relationship scenes.</li>
<li><strong>Over-the-Shoulder Shot (OTS)</strong> — films over one character’s shoulder to show the other character’s face. Creates a sense of conversation and connection.</li>
<li><strong>Point of View (POV)</strong> — shows what a character sees from their perspective. Makes the audience feel as if they are experiencing events through that character’s eyes.</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Camera Angles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The position of the camera relative to the subject's height, such as high angle (looking down), low angle (looking up) or eye level. Camera angles affect how the audience perceives power, vulnerability and status.">Camera angles</dfn> describe the <strong>vertical position</strong> of the camera relative to the subject. The angle changes how the audience perceives a character or object.</p>

<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/pre-production/diagram_camera_shots_angles_and_movement.jpg" alt="Diagram: Camera Shots, Angles and Movement" loading="lazy"></figure>

<ul data-narration-id="n10">
<li><strong>Eye Level</strong> — the camera is at the same height as the subject. Creates a neutral, natural feeling — the audience views the subject as an equal.</li>
<li><strong>High Angle</strong> — the camera looks down on the subject. Makes the subject appear <strong>small, weak, vulnerable or inferior</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Low Angle</strong> — the camera looks up at the subject. Makes the subject appear <strong>powerful, dominant, imposing or heroic</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Bird’s Eye / Aerial</strong> — the camera is positioned directly above, looking straight down. Used for establishing shots or to show the scale of a scene.</li>
<li><strong>Worm’s Eye</strong> — the camera is positioned at ground level, looking up. Creates an extreme sense of height and power.</li>
<li><strong>Dutch Angle / Canted Angle</strong> — the camera is tilted to one side, making the horizon appear diagonal. Creates a sense of <strong>unease, tension, or disorientation</strong>.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Camera angles convey <strong>power dynamics</strong>. A low angle makes someone look powerful; a high angle makes them look vulnerable. A Dutch angle suggests something is wrong or unsettling. Always link the angle to its <strong>effect on the audience</strong> in your exam answers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Camera Movements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The physical movement of the camera during filming, such as panning (horizontal rotation), tilting (vertical rotation), tracking (moving alongside the subject), or zooming (changing focal length).">Camera movements</dfn> describe how the camera physically moves during a shot. Movement adds dynamism and can guide the audience’s attention.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Camera Movement</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<ul data-narration-id="n14">
<li><strong>Pan</strong> — the camera rotates horizontally (left to right or right to left) on a fixed point, like turning your head. Used to follow action or reveal a wide scene.</li>
<li><strong>Tilt</strong> — the camera rotates vertically (up or down) on a fixed point, like nodding your head. Used to reveal height — e.g. tilting up a tall building.</li>
<li><strong>Zoom</strong> — the camera lens changes focal length to make the subject appear closer (zoom in) or further away (zoom out). The camera itself does not move.</li>
<li><strong>Tracking / Dolly Shot</strong> — the entire camera moves alongside, towards, or away from the subject, usually on rails or a wheeled dolly. Creates smooth, cinematic movement.</li>
<li><strong>Crane Shot</strong> — the camera is mounted on a crane and moves vertically through space — rising above or descending into a scene. Used for dramatic reveals.</li>
<li><strong>Handheld</strong> — the camera is held by the operator without stabilisation, creating a shaky, raw, documentary-style feel. Conveys <strong>urgency, chaos, or realism</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Steadicam</strong> — the camera is mounted on a stabilised harness, allowing smooth movement while the operator walks. Combines the freedom of handheld with the smoothness of tracking.</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Linking Camera Techniques to Meaning</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">In the exam, you must explain <strong>why</strong> a particular camera technique would be used, not just name it. Link the technique to its <strong>effect on the audience</strong>:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n16">
<li>A <strong>close-up</strong> of a character’s face shows their emotion, creating empathy</li>
<li>A <strong>low angle</strong> on a villain makes them appear threatening and powerful</li>
<li>A <strong>handheld shot</strong> in a chase scene increases tension and realism</li>
<li>A <strong>crane shot</strong> rising above a city establishes the setting and creates a sense of scale</li>
<li>A <strong>Dutch angle</strong> during a horror scene creates a feeling of unease and disorientation</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Shot types</strong> control how much of the subject and setting is visible, from extreme long shots (landscape) to extreme close-ups (detail).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Camera angles</strong> affect how the audience perceives power and status — low angles suggest dominance, high angles suggest vulnerability.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Camera movements</strong> (pan, tilt, zoom, tracking, crane, handheld) add dynamism and guide audience attention. Always link technique to audience effect in exam answers.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When the exam asks about camera techniques, always provide three things: <strong>name</strong> the technique, <strong>describe</strong> what it looks like, and <strong>explain</strong> its effect on the audience. For example: “A low angle shot is where the camera looks up at the subject. This makes the character appear powerful and dominant, which would be effective for introducing the villain.”</p>
