<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Minimalism?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A style of music that uses small musical ideas repeated for a long time, developing through gradual changes in texture, rhythm, dynamics or instrumentation.">Minimalism</dfn> is a style of music that uses small musical ideas which are repeated for a long time. Instead of changing melody or harmony often, the music develops through gradual changes — such as adding or removing layers, changing rhythm, dynamics, or texture.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In film, minimalism is a powerful tool. It can create a hypnotic, unsettling, or suspenseful atmosphere without relying on big melodic themes. Composers like Jóhann Jóhannsson and Hans Zimmer use minimalist techniques to build mood slowly, letting small musical shifts mirror what is happening on screen.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about minimalist film music often uses repetition.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Minimalist film music often uses repetition and <dfn class="term" data-def="A short musical pattern that repeats continuously throughout a piece or section.">ostinato</dfn> patterns, has very slow or no harmonic change, builds mood and tension through texture and rhythm, and creates a hypnotic or suspenseful effect.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Eight Minimalist Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For AoS3, you need to know eight key minimalist techniques. Each one describes a different way composers develop music from simple, repeated ideas. Let’s go through them one by one.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Repetition / Ostinato & Loops / Cells</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Repetition / Ostinato</strong> — A short musical idea is repeated continuously. An ostinato might be a rhythmic pattern, a bass line, or a melodic fragment that loops over and over, forming the backbone of the piece. In Hans Zimmer’s “Mountains” from <em>Interstellar</em>, a ticking ostinato drives the entire track. The rhythm and tempo stay the same while the texture and dynamics build around it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Loops / Cells</strong> — Similar to an ostinato, but a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeating musical idea that comes and goes within a piece, rather than playing continuously.">loop or cell</dfn> is a repeating idea that comes and goes. It might appear, disappear, and return later. This gives the composer more flexibility to shape the mood of a scene.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Layering / Addition & Subtraction</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Layering / Addition</strong> — New parts or instruments are added gradually on top of existing ones. The piece starts thin and becomes thicker in <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of layers of sound in a piece and how they relate to each other.">texture</dfn>. This is one of the most common ways minimalist film music builds tension — it feels like something is growing or approaching.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Subtraction</strong> — The opposite of layering. Parts or instruments are gradually removed, thinning the texture. A piece might start with many layers which then drop out one by one, leaving only a solo instrument at the end. This can create a feeling of isolation or emptiness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Motif Development & Use of Rhythm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Motif Development</strong> — A short musical idea (a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short, distinctive musical idea that can be developed and varied throughout a piece.">motif</dfn>) is repeated and slowly changed over time. The changes are usually small — adding notes, altering the rhythm, or introducing new instruments — rather than writing a completely new melody. This creates a sense of evolution without dramatic contrast.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Use of Rhythm</strong> — Minimalist music often uses strong, repetitive rhythms to create drive and momentum. The rhythm usually stays the same for a long time, helping to build tension, energy, or a sense of movement. In “Mountains” from <em>Interstellar</em>, each tick of the rhythmic ostinato represents one day passing on Earth, directly linking the musical rhythm to the film’s narrative.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the exam, you may be asked to identify a minimalist technique and describe how it is used. A strong answer names the technique, explains what happens musically (e.g. “layers are gradually added”), and links it to the effect on the listener or the scene.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Phase Shifting & Static Harmony</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="When two parts play the same pattern but one gradually moves out of sync with the other, creating new rhythmic and melodic combinations.">Phase shifting</dfn></strong> — The same musical pattern is played by two parts, but one part slowly moves out of sync with the other. This creates a slightly unsettled or hypnotic effect, even though the music itself does not change much. The listener hears new rhythmic and melodic combinations emerge as the two parts drift apart.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="When the harmony stays on one chord or changes very little for a long time, creating a sense of stillness or suspension.">Static harmony</dfn></strong> — The harmony stays on one chord or changes very little for a long time. Instead of chord changes driving the music forward, the composer creates interest through texture, rhythm, dynamics, or instrumentation. This is especially effective in sci-fi and thriller films, where it creates an eerie sense of suspension or timelessness.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Case Study 1: “Heptapod B” — Jóhann Jóhannsson (<em>Arrival</em>)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Jóhannsson’s score for <em>Arrival</em> (2016) is a masterclass in minimalism for film. In “Heptapod B,” a short, melodic, vocal-like phrase is repeated throughout the piece. The dynamics and texture change gradually, but the harmony and tempo stay largely the same.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Several minimalist techniques overlap in this cue:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Repetition / Ostinato</strong> — The vocal-like phrase loops continuously.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Phase shifting</strong> — Layers of the phrase move subtly out of alignment, creating an alien, otherworldly texture.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Layering / Addition and Subtraction</strong> — Parts are gradually added to build intensity, then removed to create moments of vulnerability.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Static harmony</strong> — The harmonic foundation barely moves, supporting the film’s themes of mystery and the unknown.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The overall effect is hypnotic and unsettling — perfect for scenes where humans are trying to communicate with an alien species they cannot understand.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Multiple Techniques Matter in the Exam</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Film composers rarely use just one minimalist technique at a time. In “Heptapod B,” Jóhannsson combines repetition, phase shifting, layering, subtraction, and static harmony all within the same cue. In extended listening questions, a strong answer identifies multiple techniques and explains how they work together to create a mood or support the narrative.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Case Study 2: “Mountains” — Hans Zimmer (<em>Interstellar</em>)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">“Mountains” from <em>Interstellar</em> (2014) uses a relentless ticking <dfn class="term" data-def="A short musical pattern that repeats continuously throughout a piece or section.">ostinato</dfn> as its foundation. The rhythm and tempo stay constant throughout, while the texture and intensity increase dramatically. Dynamics build from quiet and sparse to loud and overwhelming.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Key techniques in this cue include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Repetition / Ostinato</strong> — The ticking pattern never stops, representing the passage of time.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Layering / Addition</strong> — Organ, strings, and other instruments are gradually added, thickening the texture.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Use of rhythm</strong> — The steady pulse creates drive and urgency, mirroring the characters’ race against time.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n33">The effect is one of mounting tension and panic. The audience feels the pressure of time passing as the texture grows thicker and the dynamics get louder, all anchored by that constant, unrelenting tick.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Texture Manipulation in Minimalist Film Scores</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35"><dfn class="term" data-def="Changing the number or type of layers in music to alter the mood, intensity, or atmosphere.">Texture manipulation</dfn> is central to how minimalism works in film. Because the melody and harmony often change very little, it is changes in texture that do the heavy lifting. Here are some common approaches:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Thin to thick</strong> — Starting with a single instrument and gradually adding layers creates a sense of something building or approaching. This is used in horror and sci-fi films to build suspense.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Thick to thin</strong> — Removing layers creates isolation or sadness. The audience feels like something is being lost.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Contrasting textures</strong> — Alternating between sparse and dense sections can mirror shifts in the narrative, such as moving between calm dialogue and intense action.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n39" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When answering exam questions about minimalist film music, always link the technique to the effect it creates. For example: “Layering is used to gradually add instruments, which thickens the texture and creates a growing sense of tension as the scene becomes more dramatic.”</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n40">Describing Minimalist Techniques in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n41">The exam may ask you to identify a minimalist technique and describe how it is used. A model answer structure looks like this:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n42">“This extract uses <strong>subtraction</strong>. The piece starts with many layers, which gradually drop out, leaving us with only the piano at the end.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n43">Notice how that answer names the technique, describes what happens in the music, and states the result. Always aim for this three-part structure: <strong>name it → describe it → explain the effect</strong>.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n45">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n46">Minimalism in film music relies on eight key techniques: repetition/ostinato, loops/cells, layering/addition, subtraction, motif development, use of rhythm, phase shifting, and static harmony.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n47">Texture manipulation — adding or removing layers — is the main way minimalist film composers build and release tension, because melody and harmony often change very little.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n48">In the exam, always use the three-part answer structure: name the technique, describe what happens musically, and explain the effect on the listener or the scene.</li>
</ul>
