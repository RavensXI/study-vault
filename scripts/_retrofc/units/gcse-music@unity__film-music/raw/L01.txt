<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Film Music?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Music composed specifically to accompany a film, supporting the story, characters and atmosphere.">Film music</dfn> is all about how music is used in films to create atmosphere, show emotion, build tension, and support the story. Composers use musical elements to match what’s happening on screen. In the Eduqas GCSE listening exam, there will be two questions based on Film Music (AoS3), so understanding the key concepts and vocabulary is essential.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Film composers think carefully about every musical choice they make. The instruments they pick, the dynamics they use, the tempo they set — all of these decisions shape how the audience feels while watching a scene. In this lesson, we’ll cover the difference between diegetic and non-diegetic sound, what an underscore is, and how to analyse a film score using the MAD T SHIRT framework.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 2 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AoS3 is assessed in the listening exam only. You will hear extracts and need to identify musical features and explain their impact on the film’s atmosphere. There are two exam questions on Film Music.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Diegetic vs Non-Diegetic Sound</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">One of the first things you need to understand is the difference between <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound that occurs within the world of the film — the characters can hear it, such as dialogue, a radio playing, or a glass smashing.">diegetic</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound added for the audience only — the characters cannot hear it, such as an underscore or a voiceover.">non-diegetic</dfn> sound. This is a key exam term.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Diegetic sounds</strong> occur within the world of the film. In other words, the characters can hear them. Examples include dialogue between characters, a radio playing in the background, footsteps, or a glass smashing on the floor.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Non-diegetic sounds</strong> are just for the audience — the characters cannot hear them. The most common example is the <dfn class="term" data-def="Background music that plays underneath the action of a film to create mood and atmosphere without the characters hearing it.">underscore</dfn> (the background music that plays under the action). A voiceover narration is also non-diegetic.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">In the exam, you may be asked whether a sound is diegetic or non-diegetic. Understanding this helps you explain how music creates atmosphere. For example, if a character is playing piano in a scene, that’s diegetic — it’s part of the story. But if dramatic orchestral music swells during a battle scene and no orchestra is visible, that’s non-diegetic — it’s added purely to heighten the audience’s emotional response. Sometimes a clever director will blur the line between the two to surprise the audience.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">What Is an Underscore?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">An underscore is non-diegetic background music that plays beneath the dialogue and action of a film. It is one of the most important tools a film composer has. The underscore supports the mood of each scene without drawing too much attention to itself. It can build tension during a chase, create sadness during a farewell, or add excitement during a victory.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A good underscore responds to changes in the on-screen action. It might grow louder and faster as danger approaches, or become quiet and sparse during an emotional moment. The composer Hans Zimmer is well known for his powerful underscores, including the music for the film <em>Inception</em>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define an underscore in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An underscore is non-diegetic — the characters in the film cannot hear it. Its purpose is to support the story and create atmosphere for the audience.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">How Music Creates Atmosphere in Different Genres</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Different film genres use music in different ways. A horror film will use very different musical techniques to a romantic comedy. Here are some typical approaches:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Horror:</strong> Minor keys, <dfn class="term" data-def="Notes that do not belong to the main key of the piece, creating tension or an unsettling sound.">chromatic</dfn> harmony, dissonant chords, sudden dynamic changes (quiet to loud), low sustained notes, tremolo strings, and silence followed by stings (sudden loud sounds).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Sci-fi:</strong> Electronic sounds, synthesisers, unusual timbres, sustained pads, wide melodic intervals, and a mix of orchestral and electronic textures to suggest the unknown.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Romantic comedy:</strong> Major keys, warm harmonies, light instrumentation (acoustic guitar, piano, strings), moderate tempo, and singable melodies to create a feel-good atmosphere.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">MAD T SHIRT is a useful acronym for remembering the musical elements you should listen for when analysing any film score:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>M</strong> — Melody | <strong>A</strong> — Articulation | <strong>D</strong> — Dynamics | <strong>T</strong> — Tempo | <strong>S</strong> — Structure | <strong>H</strong> — Harmony | <strong>I</strong> — Instrumentation | <strong>R</strong> — Rhythm | <strong>T</strong> — Texture</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">You don’t need to discuss every single element in an exam answer, but you should aim to cover several, always linking the musical feature to its impact on the atmosphere or action.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Case Study: Hedwig’s Theme — John Williams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Hedwig’s Theme from the Harry Potter films is a well-known example of how a film score creates atmosphere. Here is how Williams uses different musical elements:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Melody:</strong> The melody has a memorable, wavy <dfn class="term" data-def="The shape of a melody as it moves up and down in pitch.">melodic contour</dfn> with a narrow range at first. It uses rising and falling arpeggiated figures and a repeated motif. This makes it highly memorable (a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short, recurring musical theme associated with a particular character, place, or idea in a film.">leitmotif</dfn>) and helps the listener associate it with the wizarding world.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Dynamics:</strong> The piece starts quietly (piano) and builds to a powerful, loud (forte/tutti) climax as more instruments are added. This dynamic range follows the on-screen action, building excitement and a sense of “lift-off”.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Texture:</strong> The texture is primarily <dfn class="term" data-def="A musical texture where one main melody is supported by accompanying chords or harmony.">homophonic</dfn>. Later repetitions add complex counter-melodies and rapid string scales. The initial lightness adds an airy, floating quality that suits the magical atmosphere. Increased density in later sections builds tension and intensity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Harmony:</strong> The piece is in the key of E minor. It uses unusual, non-functional chromatic chords and progressions. The minor key establishes a mysterious, slightly dark mood. The unexpected chord choices create a sense of the strange and otherworldly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Instrumentation:</strong> It opens with a solo celesta. The melody is later passed to French horns and then the full orchestra. The celesta’s bell-like, ethereal sound creates an immediate sense of mystery and child-like wonder. The full orchestration builds grandeur and excitement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Rhythm:</strong> It is written in 3/8 time with a moderate, waltz-like tempo. It uses dotted rhythms. The triple time gives the music a feeling of lightness and buoyancy, evoking flight or elegance. This contrasts with the more common duple time heard in action film scores.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n30" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When analysing any film score in the exam, always link the musical feature to its impact. Don’t just say “it uses a minor key” — explain that the minor key creates a mysterious or dark mood.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Case Study: Time (Inception) — Hans Zimmer</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31">“Time” from <em>Inception</em> by Hans Zimmer is an excellent example of an underscore. You can analyse it using the MAD T SHIRT framework. Listen for how the piece builds gradually: it starts with a simple, quiet piano motif and slowly layers in strings and brass, growing in dynamics and texture. The slow tempo and sustained harmonies create a sense of emotional weight and reflection. The way the music swells to a huge orchestral climax and then fades away mirrors the emotional arc of the film. This piece is a great example of how an underscore can follow the on-screen action without any dialogue or lyrics.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Diegetic sound</strong> is heard by the characters (e.g. dialogue, a radio); <strong>non-diegetic sound</strong> is for the audience only (e.g. underscore, voiceover).</li><li data-narration-id="n35">An <strong>underscore</strong> is non-diegetic background music that supports the mood and action of a film scene.</li><li data-narration-id="n36">Use the <strong>MAD T SHIRT</strong> framework (Melody, Articulation, Dynamics, Tempo, Structure, Harmony, Instrumentation, Rhythm, Texture) to analyse film scores, and always link each feature to its <strong>impact on atmosphere</strong>.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">In the listening exam, always use precise musical vocabulary from MAD T SHIRT. When a question asks you to “explain” or “describe” how music creates atmosphere, you must name the musical feature (e.g. “tremolo strings”, “minor key”, “crescendo”) <strong>and</strong> explain its effect on the listener or its link to the on-screen action. Vague answers like “it sounds scary” will not earn marks. Instead, write: “The tremolo strings create a sense of unease and tension, suggesting danger is approaching.”</p>
