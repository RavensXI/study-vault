--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ancient astronomers measured the Earth, Moon and Sun using shadows and geometry.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Measuring the Earth: Eratosthenes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Around 240 BCE, the Greek scholar <dfn class="term" data-def="Greek astronomer (c. 276–194 BCE) who calculated the circumference of the Earth by comparing shadow angles at two different locations.">Eratosthenes</dfn> devised one of the most elegant measurements in the history of science. He knew that at noon on the summer solstice, sunlight shone directly down a deep well at Syene (modern Aswan, Egypt) &#8212; meaning the Sun was at the zenith there. At the same moment in Alexandria, roughly 800 km to the north, he measured the angle of a shadow cast by a vertical stick and found it to be about 7.2°.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eratosthenes reasoned that if the Sun&#8217;s rays are essentially parallel (the Sun being so distant), then the angular difference between the two shadow angles must equal the angle subtended at Earth&#8217;s centre between the two cities. Since 7.2° is approximately 1/50th of 360°, the full circumference of the Earth must be 50 times the distance between the two cities: 50 × 800 km = 40&#8239;000 km. This gives a diameter of about 40&#8239;000 ÷ π ≈ 12&#8239;700 km &#8212; remarkably close to the modern value. The geometry involved is straightforward: if you know the arc length (the north&#8211;south distance between the cities) and the central angle (the shadow angle difference), you can calculate the full circumference.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and reproduce Eratosthenes' method from scratch: state his two measurements, the geometric reasoning, and the calculation leading to Earth's circumference.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eratosthenes measured Earth&#8217;s circumference using shadow angles: 7.2° angular difference between Syene and Alexandria (~800 km apart) gives a circumference of 50 × 800 km = 40&#8239;000 km and a diameter of ~12&#8239;700 km.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Aristarchus: Moon and Sun Measurements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Greek astronomer (c. 310–230 BCE) who attempted to measure the relative distances and sizes of the Moon and Sun using geometric methods.">Aristarchus</dfn> of Samos is famous for proposing a heliocentric model of the Solar System nearly 1,800 years before Copernicus. But he also devised clever geometric methods to estimate the sizes and distances of the Moon and Sun.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Diameter of the Moon:</strong> Aristarchus used lunar eclipses. During a total lunar eclipse, Earth casts a shadow across the Moon. By timing how long the Moon took to cross Earth&#8217;s umbral shadow and comparing that to the Moon&#8217;s own travel time across its full diameter, he estimated the ratio of Earth&#8217;s shadow width (approximately equal to Earth&#8217;s diameter at that distance) to the Moon&#8217;s diameter. His value was roughly 2&#8211;3 Earth diameters for the Moon&#8217;s distance, though his estimate of the Moon&#8217;s actual diameter was less accurate than Eratosthenes&#8217; Earth measurement.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Distance to the Moon:</strong> Aristarchus used the angular diameter of the Moon (about 0.5°) and a geometry argument based on the Moon&#8217;s apparent size. Knowing or estimating Earth&#8217;s diameter, if the Moon&#8217;s angular size is 0.5°, simple trigonometry gives its distance as approximately 60 Earth radii &#8212; close to the modern value of ~60.3 Earth radii.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Distance to the Sun:</strong> Aristarchus observed the Moon at exactly half-phase (first or last quarter), when the angle Moon&#8211;Earth&#8211;Sun should be exactly 90°. He measured the angle Earth&#8211;Moon&#8211;Sun (the angular separation between the Moon and Sun at half-phase) as approximately 87°. Using trigonometry, he concluded the Sun was about 18&#8211;20 times further than the Moon. The true ratio is about 400 &#8212; Aristarchus&#8217; measurement was inaccurate because the half-phase angle is extremely sensitive to small errors &#8212; but his <em>method</em> was entirely sound.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Diameter of the Sun:</strong> Because the Sun and Moon have nearly equal angular diameters (~0.5°), the Sun&#8217;s diameter must be in the same ratio to the Sun&#8217;s distance as the Moon&#8217;s diameter is to the Moon&#8217;s distance. If the Sun is 400 times further away and has the same angular size, it must be 400 times larger in diameter.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Axial Precession: The Wobbling Earth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Earth&#8217;s axis is tilted at about 23.5° to the plane of its orbit. But this tilt does not point in a fixed direction forever. Like a spinning top that slowly wobbles, Earth&#8217;s axis slowly traces out a cone in space &#8212; a motion called <dfn class="term" data-def="The slow, conical wobble of Earth's rotational axis over a period of about 26 000 years, caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun on Earth's equatorial bulge.">axial precession</dfn>. The period of this wobble is approximately <strong>26&#8239;000 years</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">At present, Earth&#8217;s north pole points roughly toward <strong>Polaris</strong> (the Pole Star) in the constellation Ursa Minor. But this has not always been the case &#8212; and it will not always be. Around 3000 BCE, the star Thuban (in Draco) served as the pole star for ancient Egyptians. In about 12&#8239;000 years&#8217; time, the brilliant star <strong>Vega</strong> (in Lyra) will lie near the celestial north pole. The cycle then continues, and Polaris will be the pole star once more in roughly 26&#8239;000 years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Precession also shifts the date on which Earth is at any particular position in its orbit relative to the stars. The <strong>equinoxes precess</strong> (move slowly westward along the ecliptic) by about 1° every 72 years. This is why the astronomical constellations of the zodiac no longer match the Sun&#8217;s position at the dates associated with astrology&#8217;s zodiacal signs &#8212; the sky has shifted significantly since those signs were defined in antiquity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state: (a) the period of axial precession; (b) today's pole star; (c) the future pole star in ~12 000 years; (d) what drives precession.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Axial precession has a period of ~26&#8239;000 years. The current pole star is Polaris; Vega will be the pole star in ~12&#8239;000 years. Precession is caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun on Earth&#8217;s equatorial bulge.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The study of the astronomical knowledge, practices and beliefs of ancient cultures, often through the alignment of surviving monuments.">Archaeoastronomy</dfn> investigates how ancient cultures used celestial events in the design and orientation of monuments. Stonehenge, erected in Wiltshire between roughly 3000 and 1500 BCE, is one of the most-studied examples. Its Heel Stone is aligned with the rising Sun on the summer solstice as seen from the centre of the monument. This alignment still works today because solstice sunrise position depends on Earth&#8217;s axial tilt (which has changed very little &#8212; from about 24° at Stonehenge&#8217;s construction to 23.5° today) rather than on precession of the pole. Other monuments across Egypt, Mexico and Ireland also encode astronomical alignments, testifying to the sophistication of naked-eye astronomy thousands of years before the invention of the telescope.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Eratosthenes measured Earth&#8217;s circumference (~40&#8239;000 km) around 240 BCE by comparing shadow angles 7.2° apart between Syene and Alexandria, using the geometry of parallel solar rays.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Aristarchus used eclipse geometry and half-phase angles to estimate the size and distance of the Moon and Sun. His methods were geometrically correct even where his measurements were limited by observational accuracy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Axial precession is Earth&#8217;s ~26&#8239;000-year wobble caused by the Moon and Sun pulling on the equatorial bulge. It shifts the pole star over millennia (currently Polaris, formerly Thuban, eventually Vega) and is studied through archaeoastronomy sites like Stonehenge.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For Eratosthenes questions, always show the proportional reasoning: angle / 360° = arc length / circumference. If you are given numbers in the question, substitute them immediately and show your working step by step. For Aristarchus questions, focus on the method &#8212; what he observed, what geometric assumption he made, and what ratio or measurement he derived. For precession, remember the two key numbers: 26&#8239;000 years (the full cycle period) and 23.5° (the tilt angle itself, which stays roughly constant &#8212; precession changes the direction the axis points, not its angle). A common error is to confuse the precession of the axis with a change in the tilt &#8212; the tilt remains close to 23.5°; it is the direction that precesses.</p>
