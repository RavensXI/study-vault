--- DESCRIPTION ---
Government, commercial and subscription funding models, and how media operates as a global industry.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Funding Models Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Where a media organisation gets its money shapes almost everything about the content it produces: what subjects it covers, who it targets, how independent its journalism is and whether it can take creative risks. The Media Industries framework requires you to understand the main funding models operating in the UK media landscape &mdash; and to explain how each model creates different priorities and pressures for the organisations that use it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Public Service Broadcasting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A model of broadcasting funded by the government or through a licence fee, with a remit to inform, educate and entertain all sections of the public regardless of commercial value.">Public service broadcasting (PSB)</dfn> is designed to serve the public interest rather than generate profit. In the UK, the BBC is funded by the television licence fee &mdash; a flat-rate charge paid by households that receive live television or use the BBC&rsquo;s on-demand services. Because the BBC is not dependent on advertising revenue, it can invest in content that would be difficult to justify on purely commercial grounds: expensive investigative journalism, specialist arts programming, minority-interest content and regional news that reaches smaller audiences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The BBC has a <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of obligations a broadcaster must fulfil, typically to inform, educate and entertain the full range of audiences, regardless of whether doing so is commercially profitable.">public service remit</dfn> enshrined in its Royal Charter. Other UK broadcasters also carry PSB obligations despite being commercially funded: Channel 4 is commercially funded through advertising but operates under a PSB remit; Channel 5 is a commercial broadcaster with a lighter PSB obligation. The PSB framework creates a mixed ecology in which public-interest content is guaranteed to exist alongside commercial content &mdash; but it is always subject to political debate about the appropriate level of public funding.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name two UK broadcasters with public service obligations and state whether each is funded by the licence fee, advertising or both.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The BBC is funded by the television licence fee and has a full PSB remit. Channel 4 is commercially funded through advertising but also operates under a PSB remit. Neither model is purely commercial &mdash; both carry obligations to serve audiences that go beyond what advertising revenue alone would incentivise.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Commercial Funding: Advertising, Sponsorship and Product Placement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Commercial media organisations fund their content primarily through selling audiences to advertisers. <dfn class="term" data-def="Income generated by selling advertising space or time to companies wishing to reach the media organisation's audience.">Advertising revenue</dfn> depends on the size and demographic profile of the audience: a product aimed at young adults will pay premium rates to advertise on a platform or channel with a large young-adult audience. This creates a structural incentive for commercial media to maximise their audience numbers and to attract the demographics most valued by advertisers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Beyond direct advertising, commercial media can earn revenue through <dfn class="term" data-def="A commercial arrangement in which a company pays to have its brand associated with a specific programme or event, usually in exchange for on-screen credit.">sponsorship</dfn> and product placement. Sponsorship means a company funds or associates itself with a specific programme or section of content. Product placement means a brand&rsquo;s products appear within the content itself &mdash; a character drives a branded car, drinks a recognisable beverage or uses a named piece of technology. Product placement is regulated in the UK: it is permitted in some forms of television content but must be disclosed with an &ldquo;P&rdquo; logo at the start of the programme.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A funding model in which users pay a regular fee to access a product or service, providing the organisation with predictable recurring revenue independent of advertising.">Subscription models</dfn> have grown rapidly with the rise of streaming services and digital platforms. Rather than relying on advertising, subscription-funded organisations charge users a monthly or annual fee. This creates a different set of incentives: the goal is to retain subscribers rather than to attract maximum advertising revenue, which means creating content that users value highly enough to keep paying for.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Many digital media services use a <dfn class="term" data-def="A business model offering a basic version of a service for free while charging for premium features or ad-free access.">freemium</dfn> approach &mdash; offering a basic version of their service for free while charging for premium features, ad-free access or exclusive content. This model attempts to maximise total audience size while converting a proportion of free users to paying subscribers. The tension between keeping the free tier attractive and giving subscribers enough additional value is a constant challenge in freemium media businesses.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Media as a Global Commercial Industry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Media organisations do not only operate within national markets. The global media industry involves significant cross-border trade in content, formats and distribution rights. A television format created in one country may be sold and adapted in dozens of others; a streaming service commissions content to reach subscriber bases across multiple continents. This internationalisation of media commerce is part of the broader process of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which media organisations, content and business practices operate across national borders, creating a global media marketplace.">globalisation</dfn> within the media industries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Not everyone views media globalisation as neutral or beneficial. Critics have argued that the dominance of globally operating media organisations &mdash; many of which are based in a small number of wealthy countries &mdash; can produce a form of cultural influence that privileges certain values, aesthetics and narratives over others. This idea is sometimes linked to the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The concern that the global dominance of media from powerful countries displaces local and national culture in smaller or less powerful nations.">cultural imperialism</dfn>: the argument that media globalisation is not just economic but carries cultural weight, shaping what audiences worldwide see as normal, desirable or prestigious.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain in two sentences what cultural imperialism means in the context of the global media industry.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cultural imperialism is the concern that the worldwide dominance of media from powerful nations shapes what audiences in other countries see as culturally normal, potentially displacing local stories, values and production. It is a critical perspective on globalisation, not simply a neutral description of market size.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">Not all media funding fits neatly into public or commercial categories. Some media organisations are funded through the voluntary or not-for-profit sector &mdash; supported by charitable grants, membership subscriptions or reader donations rather than advertising or licence fees. Community radio stations, local community newspapers and some investigative journalism outlets operate on this basis. These organisations are often able to serve audiences and cover subjects that neither commercial pressures nor public-sector commissioning priorities would sustain &mdash; but they typically operate with limited resources and face ongoing funding challenges.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Media funding models include public service broadcasting (licence fee or government grant), commercial funding (advertising, sponsorship, product placement) and subscription models (direct user payment, freemium); each creates different incentives and content priorities.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">In the UK, the BBC is licence-fee funded with a full PSB remit; Channel 4 is commercially funded but also carries PSB obligations; these distinctions affect what content each broadcaster is incentivised to produce.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Globalisation has created a worldwide media market; critics of cultural imperialism argue that the dominance of globally powerful media organisations can displace local cultures and privilege certain values over others.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">In questions about funding models, a common error is describing the BBC as simply &ldquo;government funded&rdquo; &mdash; it is funded by the licence fee, which is a household charge, not direct government expenditure from general taxation. This distinction matters because it affects the degree of editorial independence from the government of the day. Similarly, do not confuse Channel 4 (commercially funded, PSB remit) with Channel 5 (commercial, lighter PSB obligation) &mdash; examiners can award or withhold marks on this precision.</p>
