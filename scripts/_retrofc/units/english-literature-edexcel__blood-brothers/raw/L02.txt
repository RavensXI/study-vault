Mrs Johnstone's impossible choice, Mrs Lyons' manipulation, and the superstitious pact that seals the twins' fate.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Opening: Death Before Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Blood Brothers</em> opens with the ending. The Narrator reveals that twin brothers “were born, and died, on the self same day.” Before the audience sees Mrs Johnstone, or the birth, or the pact, they already know the outcome. This is Russell’s most powerful structural choice: by revealing the <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension and emotional engagement.">dramatic irony</dfn> from the very first moment, he transforms the entire play into a tragedy of inevitability. The audience does not watch to find out <em>what</em> happens — they watch to understand <em>why</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Narrator’s opening also establishes the play’s central question: “Did you ever hear the story of the Johnstone twins?” The word “story” positions the events as a cautionary tale — something to be learned from. Russell wants the audience to leave the theatre questioning the causes of the tragedy, not simply feeling sad about it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Mrs Johnstone: Poverty and Motherhood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Mrs Johnstone is introduced through her song “Marilyn Monroe,” which establishes her as a romantic, optimistic woman whose dreams have been crushed by reality. She married young, had many children, and was then abandoned by her husband when she became pregnant again. She is left alone with seven children, struggling to feed them, behind on the rent, and facing eviction. She works as a cleaner for Mrs Lyons to survive.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what is the 'Marilyn Monroe' song's refrain, and how does Russell use the dancing motif to track Mrs Johnstone's journey?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Johnstone’s song “Marilyn Monroe” uses the refrain “we went dancing” to represent lost happiness and youthful hope. As her life deteriorates, the dancing stops. Russell uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring image, word, or phrase in a text that develops a theme or creates atmosphere.">motif</dfn> to track Mrs Johnstone’s emotional journey from hope to despair.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Mrs Johnstone’s poverty is not exaggerated for dramatic effect — it reflects the real experience of single mothers in 1980s Liverpool. With no husband, no savings, and a large family, she is trapped. When she discovers she is carrying twins, her first reaction is fear, not joy. She says she “already got seven” and cannot afford to keep two more. Russell makes clear that her decision to give up a child is driven entirely by economic desperation, not lack of love.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Mrs Lyons: Wealth, Manipulation, and Desperation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose qualities contrast with another character, highlighting their differences.">Mrs Lyons</dfn> is presented as Mrs Johnstone’s opposite. She is wealthy, educated, and married — but she cannot have children. Her husband works away frequently, leaving her isolated in a large, empty house. When she learns that Mrs Johnstone is expecting twins, she sees an opportunity: “Give one to me, Mrs Johnstone.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">This line is crucial. Mrs Lyons does not ask tentatively or sympathetically — the <dfn class="term" data-def="A verb form that gives a command or instruction, suggesting authority and power.">imperative</dfn> “Give” reveals her sense of entitlement. She is accustomed to getting what she wants because she has money. Russell shows that her wealth gives her power over Mrs Johnstone: she is the employer, and Mrs Johnstone is the employee. The class dynamic makes it almost impossible for Mrs Johnstone to refuse.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Mrs Lyons uses a series of manipulative arguments to convince Mrs Johnstone. She appeals to practical logic: the child will have a better life with her. She appeals to emotion: the child will grow up in the same street and Mrs Johnstone will see him every day. She appeals to guilt: Mrs Johnstone already has more children than she can feed. Each argument is designed to make Mrs Johnstone feel that giving up the child is the <em>responsible</em> choice.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Crucially, Mrs Lyons also exploits the power imbalance. She is Mrs Johnstone’s employer. Mrs Johnstone depends on this job to survive. The unspoken threat is clear: refuse, and you may lose your income. Russell uses this scene to show how wealth gives the middle class power over the working class, even in the most personal decisions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Pact and the Superstition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Once Mrs Johnstone agrees, Mrs Lyons makes her swear on the Bible — a binding oath in a Catholic community. But then Mrs Lyons adds a chilling condition: “If either twin was told he was one of a pair, they shall both immediately die.” This <dfn class="term" data-def="An irrational belief that certain actions or events can cause supernatural consequences.">superstition</dfn> becomes the mechanism that keeps the secret intact throughout the play. Mrs Johnstone, who is deeply superstitious, believes the curse is real.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Russell uses the superstition in two ways. On one level, it drives the plot: Mrs Johnstone never tells the twins the truth because she genuinely fears they will die. On another level, it is a tool of <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of power or influence to control someone unfairly, often by exploiting their fears or vulnerabilities.">manipulation</dfn>: Mrs Lyons invented the curse to ensure Mrs Johnstone’s silence. The audience must decide whether the curse is real (the twins <em>do</em> die when the truth is revealed) or whether it is a coincidence exploited by a manipulative woman.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall Mrs Lyons' superstition word-for-word, then explain its two functions: as plot device and as symbol.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Lyons’ superstition — “If either twin was told he was one of a pair, they shall both immediately die” — functions as both a plot device and a symbol. It represents how the powerful use fear to control the powerless. The Narrator’s closing question asks whether the tragedy was caused by this superstition or by class.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Narrator as Fate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who addresses the audience directly, commenting on the action and guiding their interpretation of events.">Narrator</dfn> appears immediately after the pact, singing “Shoes upon the table” — a superstitious omen of death. His presence at this moment reinforces the idea that the pact has set an irreversible chain of events in motion. Throughout the play, the Narrator reappears at critical moments, always reminding the audience that tragedy is approaching.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The Narrator’s role is deliberately ambiguous. Is he a character? A chorus? The voice of fate? The devil? Russell never clarifies, and this ambiguity is the point. The Narrator represents the forces — whether supernatural or social — that the characters cannot escape. His repeated refrain of “shoes upon the table” and “the devil’s got your number” creates a sense of <dfn class="term" data-def="Hints or warnings about future events in a narrative, building tension and anticipation.">foreshadowing</dfn> that hangs over every scene.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">The play opens with the ending, creating dramatic irony that transforms the entire story into a tragedy of inevitability.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Mrs Johnstone’s decision is driven by poverty, not lack of love. Mrs Lyons exploits her financial power as an employer to take the child.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">The superstition (“they shall both immediately die”) is both a plot device and a symbol of how the powerful use fear to control the powerless.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">When writing about the pact in the exam, always analyse it on two levels: the literal plot level (Mrs Johnstone gives up her child) and the thematic level (the scene dramatises how wealth gives power over the poor). Link Mrs Lyons’ manipulation to the wider context of class inequality. A top-level response will also discuss the audience’s reaction — Russell positions us to sympathise with Mrs Johnstone and feel uneasy about Mrs Lyons’ actions, even though she is not a straightforward villain.</p>
