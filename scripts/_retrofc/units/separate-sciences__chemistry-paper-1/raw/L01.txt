<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What are Atoms?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Everything around us is made up of tiny particles called <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest particle of an element that can exist.">atoms</dfn>. Atoms are incredibly small — about 0.1 nanometres across. To put this in perspective, if an atom was the size of a football, a football would be the size of Earth!</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State what all substances are made of and what cannot happen to atoms in chemical reactions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All substances are made of atoms. Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in chemical reactions.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Atoms are made up of three types of particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons. Understanding how these particles are arranged helps us understand how different elements behave and react with each other.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Elements and the Periodic Table</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance made of only one type of atom.">element</dfn> is a pure substance that contains only one type of atom. For example, oxygen contains only oxygen atoms, and carbon contains only carbon atoms. There are about 100 different elements, and they’re all organised in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A chart that organises all known elements by their atomic number.">periodic table</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Elements are Organised</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Each element has a unique <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.">atomic number</dfn>. This tells us how many protons are in each atom of that element. Hydrogen has atomic number 1 (1 proton), helium has atomic number 2 (2 protons), and so on.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Elements in the same column (group) of the periodic table have similar properties because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell. This affects how they react with other elements.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Compounds and Chemical Formulas</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">When atoms of different elements join together, they form <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded together.">compounds</dfn>. For example, water (\(H_2O\)) is a compound made from hydrogen and oxygen atoms. The chemical formula tells us exactly which atoms are present and in what ratio.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain what the subscript numbers mean in H₂O and count the atoms of each element present.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In chemical formulas, the small numbers (subscripts) tell us how many atoms of each element are present. \(H_2O\) means 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Common compounds you should know include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13">Water: \(H_2O\) (hydrogen and oxygen)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14">Carbon dioxide: \(CO_2\) (carbon and oxygen)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15">Sodium chloride: \(NaCl\) (sodium and chlorine)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">Methane: \(CH_4\) (carbon and hydrogen)</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Structure of the Atom</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Modern atomic theory tells us that atoms have a specific structure. At the centre is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central part of an atom containing protons and neutrons.">nucleus</dfn>, which contains protons and neutrons. Around the nucleus, electrons move in regions called electron shells or energy levels.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Subatomic Particles</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Protons:</strong> Positively charged particles in the nucleus. The number of protons determines what element the atom is.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Neutrons:</strong> Neutral particles (no charge) in the nucleus. They add mass to the atom but don’t affect its chemical properties.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Electrons:</strong> Negatively charged particles that move around the nucleus in shells. They’re involved in chemical bonding.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n22">The relative masses are important to remember: protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of 1, while electrons have a relative mass of almost zero (1/1836 to be precise).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">How the Atomic Model Developed</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n24">Our understanding of atomic structure has changed dramatically over time as scientists gathered new evidence. This shows how scientific knowledge develops through experimentation and discovery.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n25">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1803</div>
    <h4>Dalton’s Atomic Theory</h4>
    <p>John Dalton proposed that all matter was made of tiny, indivisible particles called atoms. He thought atoms were like tiny solid spheres.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1897</div>
    <h4>Discovery of the Electron</h4>
    <p>J.J. Thomson discovered electrons and proposed the ‘plum pudding’ model — atoms were positive spheres with negative electrons embedded in them.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1909</div>
    <h4>Rutherford’s Gold Foil Experiment</h4>
    <p>Ernest Rutherford fired alpha particles at gold foil. Most passed through, but some bounced back, proving atoms have a tiny, dense nucleus.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1913</div>
    <h4>Bohr’s Model</h4>
    <p>Niels Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in specific energy levels or shells, like planets around the sun.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n26">Each new discovery built on previous work and led to a better understanding of atomic structure. This is a great example of how scientific knowledge is provisional — it can change when new evidence is discovered.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Mass Number and Atomic Number</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n28">Every element can be described using two important numbers. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in an atom’s nucleus.">atomic number</dfn> tells us how many protons are in the nucleus. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom’s nucleus.">mass number</dfn> tells us the total number of protons and neutrons.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n29">We can use these numbers to work out the number of each type of particle in an atom:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Number of protons = atomic number</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Number of neutrons = mass number - atomic number</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Number of electrons = number of protons (in a neutral atom)</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Describe the relationship between protons and electrons in a neutral atom and why the charges balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a neutral atom, the number of protons always equals the number of electrons. This means the positive and negative charges balance out.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">All matter is made of atoms, which contain protons, neutrons, and electrons arranged with a nucleus at the centre</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Elements contain only one type of atom, while compounds contain atoms of different elements chemically bonded together</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Our model of atomic structure has evolved over time as scientists made new discoveries and gathered evidence</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n38">When drawing atomic structure diagrams, remember that the nucleus is much smaller than shown in most diagrams — if drawn to scale, it would be invisible! Also, make sure you can calculate the number of neutrons by subtracting atomic number from mass number. This type of calculation appears frequently in exam questions.</p>
