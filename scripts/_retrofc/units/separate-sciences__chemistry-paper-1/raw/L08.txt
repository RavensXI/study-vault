--- DESCRIPTION ---
How electrolysis breaks down ionic compounds using electricity, including aluminium extraction.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Electrolysis?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of using an electric current to break down an ionic compound (electrolyte) into its elements. The compound must be molten or dissolved so that the ions are free to move.">Electrolysis</dfn> is the process of using electricity to break down an ionic compound into its elements. The compound being broken down is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved because its ions are free to move. Ionic compounds are electrolytes.">electrolyte</dfn> and must be either molten or dissolved in water so that the ions are free to move.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The electrolyte is placed between two <dfn class="term" data-def="Solid electrical conductors placed in the electrolyte during electrolysis. The cathode is negative (attracts positive ions) and the anode is positive (attracts negative ions).">electrodes</dfn>: the <strong>cathode</strong> (negative electrode) attracts positive metal ions, and the <strong>anode</strong> (positive electrode) attracts negative non-metal ions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name which ions move to which electrodes, what happens at the cathode and anode, and the type of materials involved.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Electrolysis: positive ions (cations) move to the cathode (negative electrode), negative ions (anions) move to the anode (positive electrode). At the cathode, metals are deposited. At the anode, non-metals are released.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">At the cathode, positive metal ions <strong>gain electrons</strong> (reduction) to form metal atoms. At the anode, negative ions <strong>lose electrons</strong> (oxidation) to form atoms or molecules. Remember: <strong>PANIC</strong> — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Electrolysis of Molten Compounds</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n7">When a molten binary ionic compound (e.g. lead bromide, \(PbBr_2\)) is electrolysed:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cathode:</strong> \(Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb\) (lead metal is deposited — reduction).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Anode:</strong> \(2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-\) (bromine gas is released — oxidation).</li>
</ul>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">When an ionic compound is dissolved in water, there are additional \(H^+\) and \(OH^-\) ions from the water. The rules for predicting products at each electrode are:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Cathode:</strong> If the metal is more reactive than hydrogen, hydrogen gas is produced. If less reactive than hydrogen, the metal is deposited.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Anode:</strong> If a <dfn class="term" data-def="An ion formed from a halogen atom (fluoride, chloride, bromide, iodide). In electrolysis of aqueous solutions, halide ions produce the halogen at the anode.">halide</dfn> ion is present, the halogen is produced. If no halide is present, oxygen is produced (from \(OH^-\) ions).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Extraction of Aluminium</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Aluminium is too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis of molten <dfn class="term" data-def="Aluminium oxide (Al2O3), the ore from which aluminium is extracted by electrolysis. It is dissolved in cryolite to lower its melting point.">aluminium oxide</dfn> (\(Al_2O_3\)). The aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten <dfn class="term" data-def="A mineral (Na3AlF6) mixed with aluminium oxide to lower its melting point, reducing the energy cost of electrolysis.">cryolite</dfn> to lower the melting point and reduce energy costs.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">At the cathode: \(Al^{3+} + 3e^- \rightarrow Al\) (aluminium is deposited).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">At the anode: \(2O^{2-} \rightarrow O_2 + 4e^-\) (oxygen is released). The carbon anodes gradually burn away because the oxygen reacts with them at high temperature, so they need regular replacement.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the formula for aluminium oxide, name the solvent used, state what forms at each electrode and why carbon anodes need replacing.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aluminium extraction: electrolysis of \(Al_2O_3\) dissolved in cryolite. Al deposited at cathode, \(O_2\) at anode. Carbon anodes burn away and need replacing. The process uses large amounts of electricity, making it expensive.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Electrolysis uses electricity to decompose ionic compounds; the electrolyte must be molten or dissolved.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Cathode (negative): metal deposited or hydrogen released (reduction). Anode (positive): non-metal released (oxidation).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of \(Al_2O_3\) in cryolite. Carbon anodes burn away and need replacing.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Always state which electrode each product forms at and whether the process at that electrode is oxidation or reduction. Remember PANIC (Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode) and OIL RIG. For aqueous solutions, you must apply the rules for competing ions (metal vs hydrogen at cathode, halide vs hydroxide at anode).</p>
