<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Polymers?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A very large molecule made by joining many small monomer molecules together in a repeating pattern.">polymer</dfn> is a very large molecule formed when many small molecules called <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, reactive molecule that joins with others to form a polymer chain.">monomers</dfn> bond together in a repeating pattern. The word “polymer” comes from the Greek for “many parts.” Polymers can be synthetic (made by humans) or naturally occurring. Everyday synthetic polymers include poly(ethene), PVC, and PTFE, while natural polymers include proteins, starch, and DNA.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The process of joining monomers together is called <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction in which many monomer molecules join together to form a polymer.">polymerisation</dfn>. There are two main types of polymerisation you need to know for GCSE Chemistry: addition polymerisation and condensation polymerisation. Each works in a different way and produces a different type of polymer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Addition Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of polymerisation in which unsaturated monomers (alkenes) open their double bonds and join together, producing only the polymer with no other product.">addition polymerisation</dfn> occurs when many unsaturated monomer molecules (alkenes) join together. The carbon–carbon double bond (C=C) in each monomer “opens up,” allowing the monomers to link into a long chain. No other substance is produced — the only product is the polymer itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The simplest example is poly(ethene). Ethene (C₂H₄) is the monomer. Under high pressure and with a catalyst, thousands of ethene molecules open their double bonds and bond together to form a long-chain polymer. The repeating unit of poly(ethene) looks the same as the monomer but with single bonds on each side instead of a double bond.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the only product in addition polymerisation and what does the letter n represent in the equation?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In addition polymerisation, the only product is the polymer. No atoms are lost or gained — the polymer has the same empirical formula as the monomer. The letter <em>n</em> in the equation represents a very large number of monomer molecules joining together.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Condensation Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of polymerisation in which monomers with two functional groups join together, forming a polymer and releasing a small molecule (usually water) as a by-product.">condensation polymerisation</dfn> is different from addition polymerisation in two important ways. First, the monomers have two <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific group of atoms within a molecule that determines how that molecule reacts.">functional groups</dfn> — one on each end of the molecule. Second, a small molecule (usually water) is released every time two monomers join together. This by-product is the reason it is called “condensation” polymerisation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Condensation polymers include polyesters and polyamides. To form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A condensation polymer formed when dicarboxylic acid monomers react with diol monomers, releasing water.">polyester</dfn>, a dicarboxylic acid reacts with a diol. The –COOH group on the acid reacts with the –OH group on the alcohol, forming an ester link (–COO–) and releasing a molecule of water (H₂O) each time.</p>

<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/separate-sciences/chemistry-separate/diagram_polymers_addition_condensation_and_natural.jpg" alt="Diagram: Polymers: Addition, Condensation and Natural" loading="lazy"></figure>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close your notes and explain the key difference between addition and condensation polymerisation products, including the usual by-product.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The crucial difference: addition polymerisation produces only the polymer, while condensation polymerisation produces the polymer <em>and</em> a small molecule by-product (usually water). This is the most common comparison question in the exam.</p>
</div>

The –COOH group reacts with the –NH₂ group, forming an amide link (–CONH–) and releasing water. Nylon is a well-known synthetic polyamide.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Small organic molecules that contain both an amine group (–NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (–COOH); they are the monomers of proteins.">amino acids</dfn> are special monomers because each molecule contains <em>both</em> an amine group (–NH₂) and a carboxylic acid group (–COOH). This means amino acids can undergo condensation polymerisation with each other. When two amino acids join, a peptide bond forms (a type of amide link) and water is released. Chains of amino acids form polypeptides, and one or more polypeptides fold into a <dfn class="term" data-def="A biological polymer made of long chains of amino acids folded into a specific 3D shape; proteins carry out most functions in living cells.">protein</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Naturally Occurring Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Many of the most important molecules in living organisms are polymers. You need to know three key examples for the exam: proteins, starch, and DNA. Each is built from smaller monomer units through condensation polymerisation.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Proteins are natural polyamides. They are condensation polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. There are 20 different amino acids found in nature, and the order in which they join determines the protein’s shape and function. Proteins act as enzymes, hormones, antibodies, and structural materials such as collagen and keratin.</p>
  </div></div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A natural carbohydrate polymer made of many glucose monomer units joined by condensation reactions; plants use it to store energy.">starch</dfn> is a natural polymer made from glucose monomers joined together by condensation reactions. Plants produce starch to store energy. Cellulose is another glucose-based polymer, but the glucose units are arranged differently, giving cellulose rigid, structural properties. Both starch and cellulose release water molecules when their monomers join.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Deoxyribonucleic acid — a large polymer made of nucleotide monomers arranged in a double helix; it carries the genetic code in living organisms.">DNA</dfn> (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a polymer made from <dfn class="term" data-def="The monomer of DNA, consisting of a sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four bases (A, T, C, or G).">nucleotide</dfn> monomers. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base. Nucleotides join by condensation reactions to form the two strands of the DNA double helix. The sequence of bases along the strand carries the genetic instructions for building proteins.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Hide this section and name all three naturally occurring polymers that form by condensation polymerisation and their by-product.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All three naturally occurring polymers — proteins, starch, and DNA — are formed by condensation polymerisation. In every case, monomers join together and water molecules are released as by-products.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Comparing the Two Types of Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Exam questions frequently ask you to compare addition and condensation polymerisation. The table below summarises the key differences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Addition polymerisation:</strong> monomers are alkenes with C=C double bonds; only one product (the polymer); no by-product; examples include poly(ethene), poly(propene), PVC. <strong>Condensation polymerisation:</strong> monomers have two functional groups (e.g. –OH and –COOH); two products (the polymer and a small molecule, usually water); examples include polyesters, nylon, proteins, starch, DNA.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Question:</strong> A polymer is formed from monomers with two different functional groups. Water is also produced. What type of polymerisation is this?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Answer:</strong> This is condensation polymerisation. The clues are: (1) the monomers have two functional groups, not a C=C double bond, and (2) a small molecule (water) is produced as a by-product. In addition polymerisation, there is no by-product — only the polymer is formed.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n29">Addition polymerisation joins alkene monomers by opening C=C double bonds, producing only the polymer with no by-product.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">Condensation polymerisation joins monomers with two functional groups, producing the polymer and a small molecule (usually water).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Proteins (from amino acids), starch (from glucose), and DNA (from nucleotides) are all naturally occurring condensation polymers.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">The most common exam question on this topic asks you to <strong>compare</strong> addition and condensation polymerisation. Always state three differences: (1) the type of monomer used, (2) whether a by-product is formed, and (3) give a named example of each. For 6-mark extended response questions, link your answer to naturally occurring polymers such as proteins and DNA to show breadth of knowledge. Remember — if water is produced, it is condensation polymerisation.</p>
