--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ecosystems are organised, the factors that affect communities, and why biodiversity matters.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Levels of Organisation in an Ecosystem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A community of living organisms interacting with each other and with the non-living (abiotic) components of their environment.">ecosystem</dfn> is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with the non-living parts of their environment. Ecosystems can be as small as a pond or as large as an ocean. To understand how they work, we organise living things into levels:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Individual</strong> → <strong>Population</strong> → <strong>Community</strong> → <strong>Ecosystem</strong>. An individual is a single organism. A <dfn class="term" data-def="All the organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time.">population</dfn> is all the organisms of one species living in the same area. A <dfn class="term" data-def="All the populations of different species living and interacting in the same area.">community</dfn> is all the different populations living and interacting together. An ecosystem includes the community <strong>plus</strong> the non-living (abiotic) environment they depend on.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the four levels of organisation in order, with a definition of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The levels of organisation: <strong>Individual → Population</strong> (one species, same area) <strong>→ Community</strong> (all species in an area) <strong>→ Ecosystem</strong> (community + abiotic environment).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Abiotic and Biotic Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Communities are affected by both living and non-living factors. <dfn class="term" data-def="Non-living factors that affect organisms in an ecosystem, such as temperature, light intensity, water availability, and pollutants.">Abiotic factors</dfn> are non-living conditions that influence where organisms can survive. Key abiotic factors include:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Temperature</strong> — affects the rate of enzyme-controlled reactions and therefore metabolic processes. Most organisms have an optimum range. <strong>Light</strong> — essential for photosynthesis in plants; affects where plants grow and the animals that depend on them. <strong>Water</strong> — all organisms need water; availability limits where organisms can live, especially in deserts. <strong>Pollutants</strong> — chemicals released into the environment can poison organisms or disrupt ecosystems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Living factors that affect organisms in an ecosystem, such as competition for resources and predation.">Biotic factors</dfn> are living influences on an ecosystem. The two most important are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Competition</strong> — organisms compete for limited resources. Animals compete for food, water, territory, and mates. Plants compete for light, water, minerals, and space. <strong>Predation</strong> — the relationship between <dfn class="term" data-def="An animal that hunts and kills other animals for food.">predators</dfn> and their prey affects population sizes. If a predator population increases, more prey are eaten, so prey numbers fall. With less food available, predator numbers then decline too, creating cyclical patterns.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Species in a community depend on each other — this is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The way in which organisms in a community rely on each other for survival, meaning a change in one species affects others.">interdependence</dfn>. If one species is removed, it can have a knock-on effect throughout the food web. For example, if a pollinating insect declines, the plants it pollinates cannot reproduce, which then affects the animals that eat those plants.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Some organisms have particularly close relationships. In <dfn class="term" data-def="A relationship where one organism (the parasite) lives on or inside another organism (the host), benefiting at the host's expense.">parasitism</dfn>, one organism (the parasite) benefits at the expense of another (the host). Fleas on a dog and tapeworms in a human gut are examples — the parasite gains food or shelter, while the host is harmed. In <dfn class="term" data-def="A relationship between two organisms where both benefit from the interaction.">mutualism</dfn>, both organisms benefit. For example, mycorrhizal fungi grow on plant roots — the fungi help the plant absorb mineral ions from the soil, and the plant provides the fungi with sugars from photosynthesis.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sampling Organisms — Quadrats and Belt Transects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">To study the distribution and abundance of organisms in an ecosystem, scientists use sampling techniques. It is not practical to count every individual, so we sample and then estimate the total.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A square frame (usually 0.25 m² or 1 m²) placed on the ground to count the organisms within it, used for random or systematic sampling.">quadrat</dfn> is a square frame placed on the ground. You count the organisms inside it. For a reliable estimate, place quadrats <strong>randomly</strong> (using random number coordinates) and repeat many times. The more quadrats you use, the more reliable your estimate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A line laid across an area along which quadrats are placed at regular intervals to study how the distribution of organisms changes.">belt transect</dfn> uses a line (a tape measure) laid across an area. Quadrats are placed at regular intervals along the line. This shows how the distribution of organisms changes across a habitat — for example, from a pond edge into woodland.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how you would use quadrats and a belt transect to investigate how plant species change from a footpath into a field.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>To estimate the number of organisms in an area: count the organisms in several quadrats, calculate the <strong>mean per quadrat</strong>, then multiply by the total area ÷ quadrat area. Formula: $$\text{Estimated population} = \frac{\text{mean count per quadrat} \times \text{total area}}{\text{area of quadrat}}$$</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Human Impacts on Biodiversity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of different species of organisms living in an ecosystem or on Earth as a whole.">Biodiversity</dfn> is the variety of different species in an ecosystem. High biodiversity makes ecosystems more stable and resilient. However, human activities can both increase and decrease biodiversity.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Negative Human Impacts on Ecosystems</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Fish farming</strong> can have negative effects on wild ecosystems. Farmed fish are kept in high densities, which can lead to disease spreading to wild populations. Waste from fish farms pollutes surrounding water, and escaped farmed fish can outcompete native species or interbreed with them, reducing genetic diversity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Introduction of non-indigenous species</strong> (species not native to an area) can devastate local ecosystems. The introduced species may outcompete native organisms for food and habitat, prey on native species, or introduce diseases. For example, grey squirrels introduced to the UK outcompeted the native red squirrel for food and habitat.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process where excess nutrients (often from fertilisers) enter waterways, causing algal blooms that block light and deplete oxygen, killing aquatic organisms.">Eutrophication</dfn> occurs when excess nutrients (usually from agricultural fertilisers) wash into rivers and lakes. The nutrients cause rapid growth of algae (an algal bloom). The algae block light to aquatic plants, which die. Bacteria decompose the dead organisms, using up dissolved oxygen. Fish and other aquatic organisms suffocate due to the lack of oxygen.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Maintaining Biodiversity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">It is important to maintain biodiversity at both <strong>local</strong> and <strong>global</strong> levels. Locally, conservation programmes protect habitats and endangered species. This includes creating nature reserves, protecting hedgerows, and managing woodlands. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The protection, preservation, and management of natural habitats and species to prevent extinction and maintain biodiversity.">conservation</dfn> of animal species involves breeding programmes, habitat restoration, and legal protection.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="The planting of trees in areas that were previously forested, helping to restore habitats, absorb carbon dioxide, and increase biodiversity.">Reforestation</dfn> — replanting trees in deforested areas — has multiple benefits. It restores habitats for wildlife, absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere (reducing the greenhouse effect), prevents soil erosion, and increases biodiversity. Globally, international agreements and conservation organisations work to protect endangered species and critical habitats such as rainforests, coral reefs, and wetlands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three negative human impacts and three ways to maintain biodiversity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Humans reduce biodiversity through fish farming, introducing non-indigenous species, and eutrophication. We can maintain biodiversity through <strong>conservation</strong> (protecting species and habitats) and <strong>reforestation</strong> (planting trees to restore ecosystems and absorb CO₂).</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n26">
  <p>Fieldwork questions often ask you to <strong>describe a method</strong> and <strong>explain why certain steps improve reliability</strong>. Remember: random placement of quadrats avoids bias, repeating measurements improves reliability, and using a belt transect shows how distribution changes across a habitat. Always state the specific measurements you would take.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
  <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n29">Ecosystems are organised into individuals, populations, communities, and the whole ecosystem. Abiotic factors (temperature, light, water, pollutants) and biotic factors (competition, predation) affect communities.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n30">Quadrats and belt transects are used to sample organisms and estimate population size. Random sampling and large sample sizes improve reliability.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n31">Human activities (fish farming, non-indigenous species, eutrophication) can reduce biodiversity. Conservation and reforestation help maintain it.</li>
  </ul>
</div>
