--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Endocrine System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of glands that produce and secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to regulate body functions.">endocrine system</dfn> is a network of glands that produce chemical messengers called <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical substance produced by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood to a target organ where it produces an effect.">hormone</dfn>s. Unlike the nervous system, which uses fast electrical impulses, hormones travel in the blood and produce slower, longer-lasting effects. Hormones are secreted directly into the bloodstream and carried to specific <dfn class="term" data-def="The organ or tissue where a hormone has its effect.">target organ</dfn>s, where they trigger a response.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six main endocrine glands and the hormone each produces.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The main endocrine glands are: the pituitary gland (the "master gland"), thyroid gland, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries (in females) and testes (in males). Each gland produces specific hormones that are transported in the blood to target organs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Key Endocrine Glands and Their Hormones</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A small gland at the base of the brain that secretes several hormones controlling other glands; often called the master gland.">pituitary gland</dfn>, located at the base of the brain, is often called the "master gland" because it secretes hormones that control other endocrine glands. For example, it releases <dfn class="term" data-def="Thyroid-stimulating hormone; a hormone released by the pituitary gland that stimulates the thyroid to produce thyroxine.">TSH</dfn> (thyroid-stimulating hormone) to control the thyroid.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A gland in the neck that produces thyroxine, which controls the rate of metabolism.">thyroid gland</dfn> produces <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the thyroid gland that regulates the basal metabolic rate. Controlled by negative feedback.">thyroxine</dfn>, which controls the body's metabolic rate. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Two glands located above the kidneys that produce adrenaline.">adrenal glands</dfn>, sitting on top of the kidneys, produce adrenaline. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A gland behind the stomach that produces insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose levels.">pancreas</dfn> produces insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose. The ovaries produce oestrogen and progesterone, while the testes produce testosterone.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Negative Feedback and Thyroxine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A control mechanism where a change in a condition triggers a response that reverses the change, bringing the condition back to its normal level.">Negative feedback</dfn> is the mechanism by which the body reverses a change to bring conditions back to normal. Thyroxine regulation is a key example you need to understand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">When thyroxine levels drop, the hypothalamus releases <dfn class="term" data-def="Thyrotropin-releasing hormone; released by the hypothalamus to stimulate the pituitary gland to release TSH.">TRH</dfn> (thyrotropin-releasing hormone). TRH stimulates the pituitary gland to release TSH. TSH then acts on the thyroid gland to produce more thyroxine. Once thyroxine levels return to normal, thyroxine inhibits the release of both TRH from the hypothalamus and TSH from the pituitary — this is the "negative feedback" loop that prevents overproduction.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Draw the thyroxine negative feedback loop from memory: hypothalamus → TRH → pituitary → TSH → thyroid → thyroxine, then show the inhibition arrows.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thyroxine is controlled by negative feedback: low thyroxine → hypothalamus releases TRH → pituitary releases TSH → thyroid produces thyroxine. When levels are normal, thyroxine inhibits TRH and TSH release.</p>
</div>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Blood Glucose Regulation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Blood glucose concentration must be kept within a narrow range. After a meal, blood glucose rises. The pancreas detects this and secretes <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the pancreas that lowers blood glucose levels by stimulating cells to take up glucose and the liver to convert glucose into glycogen.">insulin</dfn>. Insulin causes cells (especially liver and muscle cells) to absorb glucose from the blood. Excess glucose is converted to glycogen and stored in the liver. This brings blood glucose back down to normal.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">When blood glucose falls too low (for example, during exercise), the pancreas releases <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the pancreas that raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the liver to convert glycogen back into glucose.">glucagon</dfn>. Glucagon stimulates the liver to break down glycogen into glucose and release it into the blood. This is another example of negative feedback — insulin and glucagon work in opposition to keep blood glucose stable.</p>
</div>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the pancreas produces little or no insulin, usually due to the immune system destroying the insulin-producing cells. It is controlled by insulin injections.">Type 1 diabetes</dfn> is an autoimmune condition where the pancreas produces little or no insulin. The body's immune system destroys the insulin-producing cells. It is treated with regular insulin injections and careful monitoring of blood glucose and diet.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the body's cells become resistant to insulin, or the pancreas does not produce enough. Often linked to obesity and lifestyle factors.">Type 2 diabetes</dfn> occurs when the body's cells no longer respond properly to insulin (insulin resistance) or the pancreas does not produce enough. It is strongly linked to obesity, poor diet and lack of exercise. It can often be controlled through lifestyle changes — a balanced diet, regular exercise and weight loss — though some patients may also need medication.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Doctors use <dfn class="term" data-def="Body Mass Index; calculated as mass in kg divided by height in metres squared. Used to classify underweight, healthy, overweight and obese categories.">BMI</dfn> (Body Mass Index) and waist-to-hip ratio to assess the risk of type 2 diabetes. BMI is calculated using: \(\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{mass (kg)}}{\text{height (m)}^2}\). A higher BMI is correlated with a greater risk of developing type 2 diabetes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the key differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes: cause, treatment, and link to lifestyle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Type 1 diabetes — the pancreas produces no insulin (autoimmune); treated with insulin injections. Type 2 diabetes — cells become resistant to insulin; linked to obesity and treated with diet and exercise.</p>
</div><p data-narration-id="n20">When asked to explain negative feedback, always describe both directions: what happens when the level is too low AND what stops production when the level returns to normal. Many students only describe one direction and lose marks. For blood glucose, make sure you name both hormones (insulin and glucagon) and state they have opposite effects.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood to produce slower, longer-lasting responses than the nervous system.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Thyroxine regulation is a key example of negative feedback involving the hypothalamus, pituitary gland and thyroid gland.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Blood glucose is controlled by insulin (lowers glucose) and glucagon (raises glucose) from the pancreas. Type 1 diabetes is treated with insulin injections; type 2 is linked to lifestyle and treated with diet and exercise.</li>
</ul>
