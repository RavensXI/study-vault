--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Trophic Levels and Food Chains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In any ecosystem, energy flows from the Sun through living organisms. The position an organism occupies in a food chain is called its <dfn class="term" data-def="The level at which an organism feeds in a food chain. Level 1 = producers, Level 2 = primary consumers, Level 3 = secondary consumers, and so on.">trophic level</dfn>. Every food chain begins with a <dfn class="term" data-def="An organism that makes its own food using energy from the Sun through photosynthesis (e.g. green plants and algae).">producer</dfn> — an organism (usually a green plant or alga) that converts light energy into chemical energy through <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which green plants and algae use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.">photosynthesis</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Organisms that eat producers are called <dfn class="term" data-def="An animal that eats producers (plants). Also called a herbivore. Occupies trophic level 2.">primary consumers</dfn> (trophic level 2). Those that eat primary consumers are <dfn class="term" data-def="An animal that eats primary consumers. Occupies trophic level 3.">secondary consumers</dfn> (trophic level 3). <dfn class="term" data-def="An animal at the top of the food chain with no natural predators. Occupies trophic level 4 or above.">Tertiary consumers</dfn> eat secondary consumers and sit at trophic level 4 or above.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and draw a food chain with four trophic levels, labelling the producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, and tertiary consumer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Trophic levels:</strong> Level 1 = Producers (plants) → Level 2 = Primary consumers (herbivores) → Level 3 = Secondary consumers (carnivores) → Level 4 = Tertiary consumers (top predators). Energy is transferred along the chain but decreases at each level.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Pyramids of Biomass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that shows the total mass of living material (biomass) at each trophic level of a food chain.">pyramid of biomass</dfn> represents the total mass of living material at each trophic level. The producer level always forms the widest bar at the bottom, and each successive level is narrower. This is because energy is lost at every trophic level, so less <dfn class="term" data-def="The total dry mass of all the organisms at a particular trophic level.">biomass</dfn> can be supported higher up the food chain.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Energy is lost between trophic levels through several processes: organisms use energy for <strong>movement</strong> and other life processes; energy is lost as <strong>heat</strong> during respiration; and not all parts of an organism are eaten (bones, shells, roots). As a result, only about <strong>10%</strong> of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Pyramids of Biomass Are Always Pyramid-Shaped</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Unlike pyramids of numbers (which can sometimes be inverted, for example when a single large tree supports many insects), pyramids of biomass are almost always a true pyramid shape. This is because they show the total mass of organisms, not just how many there are. Since energy is lost at each transfer, the total biomass decreases at each trophic level, regardless of the number or size of individual organisms.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This also explains why food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels — by the fifth level, so little energy remains that it cannot support a viable population of top predators.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Material Cycles — Why Recycling Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The materials that living organisms are made from are constantly recycled between the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts of an ecosystem. When organisms die, <dfn class="term" data-def="Organisms (mainly bacteria and fungi) that break down dead organic matter, releasing nutrients back into the soil and gases back into the atmosphere.">decomposers</dfn> (mainly bacteria and fungi) break down the dead material. This releases nutrients back into the soil and gases back into the atmosphere, where they can be used again by other organisms. Without decomposition, essential elements would remain locked up in dead organisms and would eventually run out.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Carbon Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The biogeochemical cycle by which carbon moves between the atmosphere, living organisms, the ocean, and the Earth's crust.">carbon cycle</dfn> describes how carbon moves between the atmosphere, living organisms, and the environment. It is one of the most important material cycles because carbon is the basis of all organic molecules.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The key processes in the carbon cycle are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Photosynthesis</strong> — producers remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and convert it into glucose (organic compounds). This is the main way carbon enters living systems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Respiration</strong> — all living organisms (plants, animals, and microorganisms) carry out <dfn class="term" data-def="The chemical process in living cells that releases energy from glucose. It occurs in all living organisms and produces carbon dioxide as a waste product.">respiration</dfn>, releasing carbon dioxide back into the atmosphere. The equation: $$\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Feeding</strong> — carbon passes along food chains when animals eat plants or other animals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Decomposition</strong> — when organisms die, decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the organic matter. The decomposers respire, returning carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. This is a critical role of <strong>microorganisms</strong> in the carbon cycle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Combustion</strong> — burning <dfn class="term" data-def="Non-renewable energy sources formed from the remains of ancient organisms over millions of years, including coal, oil, and natural gas.">fossil fuels</dfn> (coal, oil, natural gas) releases carbon dioxide that was locked away millions of years ago, increasing atmospheric CO₂ levels.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Draw a simplified carbon cycle from memory, labelling photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, and feeding.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Carbon enters living systems through <strong>photosynthesis</strong> and returns to the atmosphere through <strong>respiration</strong>, <strong>decomposition</strong>, and <strong>combustion</strong>. Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) play a vital role by breaking down dead matter and respiring, returning carbon to the cycle.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">The Water Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous movement of water between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere through evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection.">water cycle</dfn> describes how water moves continuously between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere. The main processes are: <strong>Evaporation</strong> — water from oceans, lakes, and rivers is heated by the Sun and turns into water vapour. <strong>Transpiration</strong> — plants release water vapour from their leaves. <strong>Condensation</strong> — water vapour cools as it rises and condenses into tiny droplets, forming clouds. <strong>Precipitation</strong> — water falls as rain, snow, or hail. The water then collects in rivers, lakes, and oceans, or soaks into the ground.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">In areas of drought, <dfn class="term" data-def="Water that is safe to drink, containing low levels of dissolved salts and microorganisms.">potable water</dfn> (safe drinking water) can be produced by <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing salt from seawater to produce fresh drinking water, usually by distillation or reverse osmosis.">desalination</dfn> — removing salt from seawater. This can be done by distillation (boiling and condensing) or reverse osmosis (forcing water through a membrane). Desalination is effective but requires a lot of energy, making it expensive.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Nitrogen Cycle</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Plants need <dfn class="term" data-def="Mineral ions containing nitrogen (NO₃⁻) that plants absorb from the soil and use to make amino acids and proteins.">nitrates</dfn> from the soil to make amino acids and proteins. But plants cannot use nitrogen gas (N₂) directly from the atmosphere. Nitrates are made available to plants in three main ways:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>1. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria</strong> — these live freely in the soil or in root nodules of leguminous plants (peas, beans, clover). They convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, which is then converted to nitrates by other bacteria. <strong>2. Decomposition and nitrifying bacteria</strong> — when organisms die, decomposers break down proteins into ammonia. <dfn class="term" data-def="Bacteria in the soil that convert ammonia into nitrites and then into nitrates, making nitrogen available for plant uptake.">Nitrifying bacteria</dfn> then convert ammonia into nitrites and then into nitrates that plants can absorb. <strong>3. Fertilisers</strong> — farmers add artificial fertilisers containing nitrates directly to the soil. <strong>Crop rotation</strong> with leguminous plants also helps — the nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules enrich the soil with nitrates for the next crop.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="Bacteria that convert nitrates in the soil back into nitrogen gas, which is released into the atmosphere. This completes the nitrogen cycle.">Denitrifying bacteria</dfn> do the reverse — they convert nitrates back into nitrogen gas, which is released into the atmosphere. This completes the cycle but can reduce soil fertility.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three ways nitrates are made available for plant uptake.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nitrates are made available for plants through: <strong>1.</strong> Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (in soil or legume root nodules). <strong>2.</strong> Decomposers + nitrifying bacteria converting dead matter → ammonia → nitrates. <strong>3.</strong> Adding fertilisers or using crop rotation with legumes.</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n28">
  <p>When describing the carbon cycle, always mention the <strong>role of microorganisms as decomposers</strong>. Decomposers don't just break things down; they also <strong>respire</strong>, which is what actually returns carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Many students forget this second step. Also remember that combustion of fossil fuels is a human addition to the cycle that increases atmospheric CO₂.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
  <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n31">Trophic levels show an organism’s position in a food chain. Energy is lost at each level (as heat, movement, and waste), so biomass decreases — giving pyramids their characteristic shape.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n32">The carbon cycle: photosynthesis removes CO₂, while respiration, decomposition, and combustion return it. Microorganisms are essential decomposers.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n33">Nitrates reach plants via nitrogen-fixing bacteria, nitrifying bacteria acting on decomposed matter, and artificial fertilisers or crop rotation.</li>
  </ul>
</div>
