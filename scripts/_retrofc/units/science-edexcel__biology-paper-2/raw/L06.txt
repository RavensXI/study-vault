--- DESCRIPTION ---
Classification systems for organisms, the five kingdoms to three domains shift, and genetic engineering processes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Classification — Organising the Living World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">With millions of species on Earth, scientists need a system to organise and name them. <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of grouping organisms based on shared characteristics, from broad groups (domains) down to individual species.">Classification</dfn> places organisms into groups based on their shared characteristics. Traditionally, organisms were classified into <strong>five kingdoms</strong>: animals, plants, fungi, protists, and prokaryotes. This system was based mainly on observable features such as body structure and how organisms obtain food.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three domains and the five kingdoms, explaining why the system changed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Modern genetic analysis has led scientists to reclassify life into <strong>three domains</strong>: <strong>Archaea</strong> (ancient prokaryotes found in extreme environments), <strong>Bacteria</strong> (true bacteria), and <strong>Eukarya</strong> (all organisms with cells containing a nucleus — animals, plants, fungi, and protists). DNA analysis revealed that Archaea are more different from Bacteria than previously thought, so they were separated.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">From Five Kingdoms to Three Domains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The five-kingdom system grouped all prokaryotes together. However, when scientists analysed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The complete set of genetic material (DNA) in an organism.">genome</dfn> of different prokaryotes, they discovered that <dfn class="term" data-def="A domain of single-celled prokaryotic organisms that often live in extreme environments such as hot springs and salt lakes.">Archaea</dfn> are fundamentally different from <dfn class="term" data-def="A domain of single-celled prokaryotic organisms, including all true bacteria.">Bacteria</dfn> at the genetic level. Their DNA sequences, cell membrane chemistry, and metabolic processes are distinct. This genetic evidence led to the three-domain system proposed by Carl Woese, which better reflects the evolutionary relationships between organisms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The third domain, <dfn class="term" data-def="The domain containing all organisms whose cells have a nucleus, including animals, plants, fungi, and protists.">Eukarya</dfn>, contains all organisms with eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus). This domain includes the animal, plant, fungal, and protist kingdoms.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Genetic Analysis Changed Classification</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Before DNA technology, classification relied on physical features — what organisms looked like. This sometimes led to mistakes. For example, two organisms might look similar because they adapted to the same environment (<dfn class="term" data-def="When unrelated species evolve similar features because they live in similar environments, not because they share a recent ancestor.">convergent evolution</dfn>), not because they are closely related. Dolphins and sharks look similar but are very different genetically — one is a mammal, the other a fish.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Genetic analysis compares DNA sequences directly. The more similar the DNA of two organisms, the more closely related they are and the more recently they shared a common ancestor. This approach gives a much more accurate picture of evolutionary relationships than physical features alone.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Genetic Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of modifying an organism's genome by inserting a gene from another organism to introduce a desirable characteristic.">Genetic engineering</dfn> is the process of modifying the genome of an organism to introduce a desirable characteristic. Unlike selective breeding, which takes many generations, genetic engineering can transfer a specific gene from one organism to another in a single step — even between completely different species.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Main Stages of Genetic Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The process involves four key stages:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>1.</strong> The desired gene is identified and cut out of the donor organism's DNA using <dfn class="term" data-def="Enzymes that cut DNA at specific base sequences, leaving exposed unpaired bases called sticky ends.">restriction enzymes</dfn>. These enzymes cut at specific DNA sequences, leaving short, unpaired sections of bases called <dfn class="term" data-def="Short, single-stranded overhangs of DNA left after cutting with a restriction enzyme. They allow complementary pieces of DNA to join together.">sticky ends</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>2.</strong> A <dfn class="term" data-def="A carrier used to transfer a gene into a target organism, such as a bacterial plasmid or a virus.">vector</dfn> (such as a bacterial plasmid or a virus) is cut open with the same restriction enzyme, creating matching sticky ends.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>3.</strong> The desired gene is inserted into the vector. The sticky ends of the gene and the vector are complementary, so they pair up. <dfn class="term" data-def="An enzyme that joins two pieces of DNA together by forming bonds between the sugar-phosphate backbones.">Ligase</dfn> enzyme then seals the DNA backbone, joining the gene permanently into the vector.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>4.</strong> The vector is introduced into the target organism's cells. If successful, the target organism will now produce the protein coded for by the inserted gene, giving it the desired characteristic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and draw a flow diagram showing the four stages of genetic engineering, naming all the enzymes and structures involved.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Genetic engineering steps: <strong>Cut</strong> the gene out with restriction enzymes → <strong>Cut</strong> the vector with the same enzyme → <strong>Insert</strong> the gene (sticky ends pair up) → <strong>Seal</strong> with ligase → <strong>Transfer</strong> vector to target organism.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Examples and Ethical Issues of Genetic Engineering</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Medicine:</strong> Human insulin is produced by genetically engineered bacteria. The human insulin gene is inserted into bacterial plasmids, and the bacteria produce large quantities of insulin for people with diabetes. This is more reliable and ethical than extracting insulin from pig or cow pancreases.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Agriculture:</strong> Crop plants can be engineered to resist herbicides, pests, or drought. For example, Bt corn contains a gene from a bacterium that produces a natural insecticide, reducing the need for chemical pesticides.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Benefits:</strong> Genetic engineering can produce organisms with very specific, useful characteristics quickly. It can improve crop yields, reduce reliance on pesticides, and produce medicines cheaply. <strong>Risks and concerns:</strong> Genes could transfer to wild populations (gene flow), potentially creating "superweeds". There are ethical concerns about "playing God" and modifying organisms. Long-term effects on ecosystems are not fully understood. Some people are concerned about eating genetically modified (GM) food, although scientific evidence has not shown it to be harmful.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n21">
  <p>When asked to <strong>evaluate</strong> genetic engineering, the examiner wants you to give <strong>both sides</strong> — benefits and risks — and then reach a conclusion. Use specific examples (e.g. insulin production, GM crops) rather than vague statements. Always consider practical, ethical, and environmental implications.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
  <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n24">Genetic analysis has replaced the five-kingdom system with three domains: Archaea, Bacteria, and Eukarya, because DNA evidence better reflects evolutionary relationships.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n25">Genetic engineering uses restriction enzymes to cut a gene, inserts it into a vector using sticky ends and ligase, then transfers it to a target organism.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n26">Genetic engineering has benefits (medicine, agriculture) and risks (gene flow, ethical concerns, unknown long-term effects) that must be evaluated together.</li>
  </ul>
</div>
