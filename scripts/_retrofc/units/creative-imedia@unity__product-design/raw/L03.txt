--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Audience Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Understanding the <dfn class="term" data-def="The group of people that a media product is designed for and aimed at, defined by characteristics such as age, gender, interests, and lifestyle.">target audience</dfn> is essential when designing any media product. If a product does not appeal to its intended audience, it will fail — no matter how well it is made. The OCR specification requires you to know how audiences are categorised, why segmentation is used, and how audience characteristics influence design decisions.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Audience Segmentation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of dividing a broad audience into smaller, more specific groups based on shared characteristics such as age, gender, income, or interests.">Audience segmentation</dfn> is the process of dividing a broad audience into smaller groups based on shared characteristics. This allows media producers to create products that are specifically tailored to the needs and preferences of each group, rather than trying to appeal to everyone.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The specification lists eight categories of audience segmentation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Age</strong> — different age groups have different preferences, reading levels, and media consumption habits. A product for children aged 5–10 looks very different from one for adults aged 30–50. <strong>Gender</strong> — some products are designed with a specific gender in mind, though many modern products aim to be gender-neutral. <strong>Occupation</strong> — a person’s job can influence their interests and the type of media they consume (e.g. trade publications for specific professions). <strong>Income</strong> — income level affects what products people can afford and what they are willing to spend on entertainment or services.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What audience factors are described? List them from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The eight categories of audience segmentation: <strong>age, gender, occupation, income, education, location, interests, lifestyle</strong>. You must know all eight and be able to explain how each influences media product design.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Education</strong> — a person’s education level affects the complexity of language and content they are comfortable with. <strong>Location</strong> — where the audience lives can affect their access to media, language preferences, and cultural references. <strong>Interests</strong> — hobbies and passions influence what media products people seek out (e.g. gaming, sports, fashion, cooking). <strong>Lifestyle</strong> — a person’s daily habits, values, and social behaviour shape their media preferences. This is sometimes called <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of audience segmentation based on people's values, attitudes, interests, and lifestyles rather than just demographic data.">psychographic segmentation</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Reasons for segmentation:</strong> it helps producers understand exactly who they are creating for, so the product can be tailored to their specific needs. It makes marketing more effective because messages can be targeted at the right group. It improves engagement because the product feels relevant to the audience. It reduces waste because resources are focused on the people most likely to use the product. <strong>Benefits:</strong> higher audience satisfaction, better sales/engagement, more efficient use of budget, clearer creative direction.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">How Audience Characteristics Influence Design</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Audience characteristics directly influence every aspect of a media product’s design. The colour palette, language style, visual representation, audio choices, and overall layout must all be appropriate for the target audience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For example, a website for elderly users should use larger fonts, clear navigation, high contrast colours, and simple language. A social media campaign for teenagers should use informal language, vibrant colours, trending references, and mobile-friendly formats. A professional training video for corporate employees should use formal language, clean design, and a factual tone.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Age:</strong> children — bright colours, simple language, cartoon visuals; teenagers — trendy design, informal language, social media integration; adults — professional layouts, formal language, detailed content. <strong>Gender:</strong> traditionally some products use gendered colour schemes and themes, though modern design increasingly avoids stereotypes. <strong>Income:</strong> luxury audiences expect premium design (gold, black, elegant typography); budget audiences respond to bold offers and value messaging. <strong>Location:</strong> language and cultural references should be appropriate for the audience’s region.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Regulation: BBFC and PEGI</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Audience segmentation by age is formalised through classification systems. You need to know two key systems: <dfn class="term" data-def="The British Board of Film Classification — the organisation responsible for rating films, DVDs, and some video content in the UK using age categories (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18).">BBFC</dfn> (British Board of Film Classification) rates films and video content using categories: U (universal), PG (parental guidance), 12A/12, 15, and 18. <dfn class="term" data-def="Pan European Game Information — the age rating system used for digital games across Europe, with ratings of 3, 7, 12, 16, and 18.">PEGI</dfn> (Pan European Game Information) rates digital games using age ratings: 3, 7, 12, 16, and 18. These systems protect audiences, particularly children, from content that may be unsuitable for their age group.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What audience factors are described? List them from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>BBFC</strong> classifies films and video content (U, PG, 12A, 12, 15, 18). <strong>PEGI</strong> classifies digital games (3, 7, 12, 16, 18). Both systems help audiences and parents make informed choices about age-appropriate content.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Audiences are segmented by eight categories: age, gender, occupation, income, education, location, interests, and lifestyle. Segmentation helps producers create targeted, relevant products.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Audience characteristics directly influence design decisions including colour, language, visual style, audio, and layout — every element must be appropriate for the target group.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">BBFC classifies films and video content; PEGI classifies digital games. Both use age-based ratings to protect audiences from unsuitable content.</li></ul>
