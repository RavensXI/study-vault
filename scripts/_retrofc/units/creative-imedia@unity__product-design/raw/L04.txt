--- DESCRIPTION ---
Primary and secondary research methods, qualitative vs quantitative data, and their uses.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Research Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before creating any media product, the production team must conduct <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of gathering information to understand the audience, market, competition, and context before designing a media product.">research</dfn>. Research helps producers understand their audience, identify what competitors are doing, gather inspiration, and make informed decisions about content and design. The OCR specification divides research into two types: <strong>primary research</strong> and <strong>secondary research</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the key facts about primary research = new.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Primary research</strong> = new, original data collected directly by the researcher (first-hand). <strong>Secondary research</strong> = existing data collected by someone else and used for a new purpose (second-hand).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Primary Research Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Original research where data is collected first-hand by the researcher using methods such as surveys, interviews, and focus groups.">Primary research</dfn> involves collecting new, original data directly. The specification lists four primary research methods you must know.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Focus groups</strong> — a small group of people from the target audience are brought together to discuss and give opinions on a product, concept, or idea. A moderator leads the discussion and records responses. Focus groups provide detailed, qualitative feedback but are time-consuming and may be influenced by dominant voices in the group.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Interviews</strong> — one-on-one conversations between the researcher and a participant, where specific questions are asked. Interviews allow in-depth exploration of views and opinions. They can be structured (set questions), semi-structured (some set questions with flexibility), or unstructured (open conversation). Interviews provide rich qualitative data but are time-consuming to conduct and analyse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Online surveys</strong> — questionnaires distributed digitally through websites, email, or social media. They can reach a large number of people quickly and cheaply. However, response rates can be low, and it is difficult to ask follow-up questions. <strong>Questionnaires</strong> — similar to surveys but can be distributed in print or online. They use a structured set of questions with closed (multiple choice, yes/no) and open (written response) questions. Questionnaires are good for collecting quantitative data from large groups.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Advantages:</strong> data is specific to your project and audience, data is current and up-to-date, you control the questions asked, and the data is unique to your research. <strong>Disadvantages:</strong> can be time-consuming and expensive to collect, may have a small sample size, respondents may give biased or socially desirable answers, and designing good questions requires skill.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Secondary Research Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Research that uses existing data collected by others, found in sources such as books, journals, internet sites, magazines, newspapers, and television.">Secondary research</dfn> involves using data that has already been collected and published by someone else. The specification lists four secondary research sources.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/product-design/diagram_research_methods_primary_and_secondary.jpg" alt="Diagram: Research Methods: Primary and Secondary" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Books and journals</strong> — published, often peer-reviewed sources that provide reliable, in-depth information. Academic journals are particularly authoritative. <strong>Internet sites/research</strong> — websites, databases, and online articles provide a vast range of information quickly. However, online sources vary widely in reliability and must be evaluated critically. <strong>Magazines and newspapers</strong> — provide current affairs, trends, and industry-specific information. Trade magazines can be especially useful for media industry research. <strong>Television</strong> — documentaries, news programmes, and industry-related content can provide valuable research material.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Advantages and Disadvantages of Secondary Research</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Advantages:</strong> quick and easy to access, cheaper than primary research, provides a broad range of information, can be used to support and validate primary findings. <strong>Disadvantages:</strong> data may be outdated, may not be specific to your project, sources may be unreliable or biased, and data was collected for a different purpose so may not directly answer your questions.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Qualitative vs Quantitative Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Research produces two types of data. <dfn class="term" data-def="Descriptive data based on opinions, feelings, and experiences, usually gathered through interviews, focus groups, and open-ended questions.">Qualitative data</dfn> is descriptive — it captures opinions, feelings, experiences, and reasons. It answers the question “why?” and is typically gathered through interviews, focus groups, and open-ended survey questions. Qualitative data is rich and detailed but harder to analyse statistically.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Numerical data that can be measured and counted, usually gathered through surveys, questionnaires, and experiments with closed questions.">Quantitative data</dfn> is numerical — it can be measured, counted, and expressed as statistics. It answers the questions “how many?” and “how much?” and is typically gathered through surveys, questionnaires with closed questions, and analytics tools. Quantitative data is easier to analyse statistically and present as charts and graphs, but it does not explain the reasons behind the numbers.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the pre-production documents and what each contains.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Qualitative</strong> = opinions, feelings, and descriptions (words). <strong>Quantitative</strong> = numbers, statistics, and measurable facts. The best research uses <strong>both</strong> types to get a complete picture.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Primary research collects new, first-hand data through focus groups, interviews, online surveys, and questionnaires. It is specific but time-consuming.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Secondary research uses existing data from books, journals, internet, magazines/newspapers, and television. It is quick and cheap but may be outdated or unreliable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Qualitative data describes opinions and feelings (words); quantitative data measures facts and figures (numbers). Effective research uses both.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">A common exam question is: “Identify one secondary research source a media producer could use.” Make sure you name the <em>source</em> (books, journals, internet sites, magazines, newspapers, television) — not a primary <em>method</em> (surveys, interviews, focus groups). Similarly, if asked about primary research, give the <em>method</em>, not the source. Also, know the difference between qualitative and quantitative data and be able to give examples of each.</p>
