--- DESCRIPTION ---
How purpose, style, content, and layout are linked in media products to meet their goals.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Purpose of Media Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every media product is created for a specific <dfn class="term" data-def="The reason a media product is created, such as to advertise, educate, entertain, inform, or influence the audience.">purpose</dfn>. Understanding the purpose is the starting point for all design decisions. The OCR specification identifies five purposes of media products that you must know.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Advertise/promote</strong> — to persuade the audience to buy a product, use a service, or attend an event. Advertisements use bold colours, persuasive language, and eye-catching layouts. <strong>Educate</strong> — to teach the audience something new, such as a how-to video or an educational website. Educational products use clear language, structured layouts, and informative visuals. <strong>Entertain</strong> — to provide enjoyment, such as films, games, music, or comedy. Entertainment products use engaging visuals, dramatic audio, and immersive design. <strong>Inform</strong> — to provide factual information, such as a news report, documentary, or information leaflet. Informative products use formal language, clear layouts, and evidence-based content. <strong>Influence</strong> — to change the audience’s opinion or behaviour, such as a political broadcast or public health campaign. Products designed to influence use persuasive language, emotional imagery, and compelling arguments.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five purposes of media products: <strong>advertise/promote, educate, entertain, inform, influence</strong>. A single product can have more than one purpose — for example, an educational game both educates and entertains.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Style, Content, and Layout</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The style, content, and layout of a media product must be adapted to match its purpose. The specification identifies seven elements of style, content, and layout that you need to understand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Colour</strong> — colour choices create mood and meaning. Horror products use dark colours; children’s products use bright colours; luxury brands use gold, black, and white. <strong>Conventions of <dfn class="term" data-def="A category of media products that share similar characteristics, themes, and styles, such as horror, comedy, or documentary.">genre</dfn></strong> — different genres have established visual and audio conventions that audiences expect. A horror film uses dark lighting and eerie music; a comedy uses bright settings and upbeat audio. <strong>Formal/informal language</strong> — informative products tend to use more formal language, while products aimed at younger audiences or entertainment use informal, conversational language.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/product-design/diagram_generated_107d9f48.jpg" alt="Generated diagram for Purpose, Style, Content and Layout"><figcaption>Generated diagram for Purpose, Style, Content and Layout</figcaption></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Positioning of elements</strong> — where images, text, and other elements are placed on a page or screen affects how the audience reads and understands the product. In film promotions, the main characters are often positioned centrally. <strong>Style of audio representation</strong> — the type of audio used (music genre, voiceover style, sound effects) must match the product’s purpose and audience. <strong>Style of visual representation</strong> — the visual style (photographic, illustrated, animated) must suit the product. <strong>Tone of language</strong> — the overall attitude conveyed through word choice. Persuasive products use an encouraging, confident tone; informative products use a neutral, factual tone.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Media products from the horror genre tend to have <strong>darker colours</strong>. Informative media products can have more <strong>formal language</strong>. Music can be used to create tension in <strong>dramatic</strong> media products. Media products designed to influence often use <strong>persuasive language</strong>. In film promotions, the main characters are often positioned <strong>centrally</strong> in the layout. When making animated media products for children, designers often use <strong>brighter colours</strong>.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Typography in Media Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The style, arrangement, and appearance of text in a media product, including font type, size, and emphasis.">Typography</dfn> is a key element of style in media products. There are two main categories of fonts: <strong>serif fonts</strong> have decorative strokes at the ends of letters (e.g. Times New Roman) and are often associated with tradition and formality. <strong>Sans serif fonts</strong> do not have these decorative strokes (e.g. Arial) and are considered more modern and easier to read on screens. The choice of font, size, and emphasis affects how the audience perceives the product.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">When analysing or designing a media product, always start with the purpose. Ask: <em>What is this product trying to achieve?</em> Then consider: <em>How do the colour, genre conventions, language, positioning, audio, visuals, and tone of language work together to achieve that purpose?</em> For example, a children’s educational app would use bright colours, informal language, a friendly tone, cartoon-style visuals, upbeat audio, and large, clear sans serif fonts — all designed to engage and educate young learners.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the typography terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Serif fonts</strong> have decorative strokes and feel traditional/formal. <strong>Sans serif fonts</strong> have no decorative strokes and feel modern/clean. Sans serif is generally easier to read on digital screens.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Media products are created for five purposes: advertise/promote, educate, entertain, inform, or influence. A product can have more than one purpose.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Style, content, and layout (colour, genre conventions, language, positioning, audio, visuals, tone) must be adapted to match the product’s purpose and target audience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Typography choices (serif vs sans serif, font size, emphasis) affect how audiences perceive the product — sans serif fonts are more modern and screen-friendly.</li></ul>

Always identify the <strong>purpose</strong> first, then explain how each element of style supports that purpose. Use the correct terminology from the specification (e.g. “conventions of genre”, “tone of language”, “positioning of elements”) to show your knowledge.</p>
