<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Mise-en-Scène?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A French term meaning 'placing on stage' — it refers to everything the audience can see in a frame, including setting, lighting, costume, props, colour, and the arrangement of characters.">Mise-en-scène</dfn> is a French term meaning “placing on stage.” In media studies, it refers to everything the audience can see in a frame or scene. This includes the setting, lighting, costume, props, colour, and how characters are arranged. Every element of mise-en-scène is a deliberate choice made by the director or designer to convey meaning, create atmosphere, and engage the audience.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the typography terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Mise-en-scène is part of the <strong>symbolic codes</strong> category of media codes. It works alongside technical codes (camera techniques, transitions) and written codes (typography, language) to create the overall meaning of a media product.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Setting and Location</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical location or environment where a scene takes place, which establishes the time, place, and atmosphere of the media product.">setting</dfn> establishes where and when a scene takes place. It creates the foundation for the audience’s understanding of the story or message. A dark, abandoned warehouse creates a sense of danger and mystery. A bright, modern office suggests professionalism and productivity. A sunlit beach conveys relaxation and freedom.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Settings can be real locations or constructed sets. The choice of setting communicates information about the characters, the genre, and the mood of the product without any dialogue needing to be spoken. In advertising, the setting helps position the product — a luxury perfume advert might use a grand Parisian apartment, while a sports brand might use an urban street or gym.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Lighting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of light in a scene to create mood, direct attention, and convey meaning through its intensity, direction, colour, and contrast.">Lighting</dfn> is one of the most powerful elements of mise-en-scène. It creates mood, directs the audience’s attention, and reveals or hides details. The specification identifies two key aspects of lighting: <strong>intensity/levels</strong> (how bright or dark the lighting is) and <strong>position</strong> (where the light comes from).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>High-key lighting</strong> uses bright, even illumination with minimal shadows. It creates a cheerful, open, and safe atmosphere — common in comedies, children’s TV, and advertisements. <strong>Low-key lighting</strong> uses strong contrast between light and dark areas, creating dramatic shadows. It builds tension, mystery, and danger — common in horror, thriller, and film noir genres. <strong>Back lighting</strong> places the light source behind the subject, creating a silhouette or halo effect. <strong>Side lighting</strong> illuminates one side of the subject, creating shadows on the other side to suggest duality or conflict.</p>

Flickering or unreliable light sources increase unease. <strong>Comedy:</strong> high-key lighting that is bright and even, making everything feel light-hearted and safe. <strong>Documentary:</strong> natural lighting to create an authentic, realistic feel. <strong>Advertising:</strong> carefully controlled lighting to make products look their best — soft, flattering light for beauty products; dramatic lighting for cars and technology.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Costume and Props</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Costume</strong> — what characters wear communicates information about their personality, status, occupation, and time period. A character in a business suit suggests professionalism and authority. A character in torn, dirty clothing suggests poverty or hardship. Historical costumes establish the time period of a drama.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Props</strong> — objects placed within a scene that characters interact with or that add detail to the setting. Props can be symbolic — a clock might represent time running out, a mirror might represent vanity or self-reflection, a weapon might foreshadow danger. In advertising, the product itself is the most important prop, and everything else in the scene is designed to support it.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Colour and Semiotics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Colour is used throughout media products to convey meaning and emotion. This links to <dfn class="term" data-def="The study of signs and symbols and how they create meaning — in media, this includes how colours, objects, and visual elements represent ideas and concepts.">semiotics</dfn> — the study of signs and symbols and how they create meaning. In semiotics, there are two key concepts: <dfn class="term" data-def="The literal, surface-level meaning of what is seen — for example, a red rose is literally a flower.">denotation</dfn> (what something literally is) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The deeper, symbolic meaning associated with something — for example, a red rose connotes love, romance, or passion.">connotation</dfn> (what it represents or suggests).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Denotation</strong> = what you literally see (a red rose is a flower). <strong>Connotation</strong> = what it symbolically represents (a red rose connotes love, passion, or romance). The exam will ask you to identify both.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Colour connotations are used deliberately in media products. Red connotes danger, passion, love, or urgency. Blue connotes calm, trust, professionalism, or sadness. Green connotes nature, growth, health, or envy. Black connotes power, elegance, mystery, or death. White connotes purity, innocence, cleanliness, or simplicity. Gold connotes luxury, wealth, and prestige.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">A charity poster shows a child standing alone in the rain. <strong>Denotation:</strong> a child in wet weather. <strong>Connotation:</strong> vulnerability, loneliness, neglect — designed to evoke sympathy and encourage donations. A luxury car advert shows the vehicle on a dark road at night with city lights reflected in the paint. <strong>Denotation:</strong> a car on a road. <strong>Connotation:</strong> sophistication, power, exclusivity. Every visual element in mise-en-scène carries both denotative and connotative meaning.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Audio Codes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Audio is also a key media code used to convey meaning. The specification lists five types of audio: <strong>dialogue</strong> (spoken words between characters), <strong>music genre</strong> (the type of music used to set mood and tone), <strong>silence</strong> (the deliberate absence of sound to create tension or emphasis), <strong>sound effects</strong> (added sounds that enhance the scene, such as footsteps, doors creaking, or explosions), and <strong>vocal intonation</strong> (how a voice is delivered — tone, pitch, pace, and emotion). Each type of audio contributes to the overall meaning and atmosphere of the product.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21">Fast-paced music creates excitement and urgency — used in action sequences and sports advertising. Slow, minor-key music creates sadness or tension — used in dramatic scenes and charity appeals. Silence can be more powerful than any sound — a sudden silence after an explosion makes the audience feel the shock. Sound effects make scenes feel realistic and immersive. Vocal intonation affects how dialogue is perceived — a whisper creates intimacy, a shout conveys anger or urgency.</p>
</div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Mise-en-sc&egrave;ne covers everything the audience sees in a frame: setting, lighting, costume, props, colour, and character arrangement. Every element is a deliberate choice to convey meaning.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Semiotics is the study of signs and symbols. Denotation is the literal meaning; connotation is the symbolic meaning. Colours, objects, and settings all carry connotations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Audio codes (dialogue, music genre, silence, sound effects, vocal intonation) work alongside visual codes to create the overall meaning, mood, and impact of a media product.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">When analysing mise-en-scène in the exam, always give both the <strong>denotation</strong> (what you literally see) and the <strong>connotation</strong> (what it represents). For example: “The scene uses low-key lighting (denotation: dark with strong shadows), which connotes danger and mystery, creating tension for the audience.” This two-step analysis — identify then interpret — is what earns the highest marks on extended response questions.</p>
