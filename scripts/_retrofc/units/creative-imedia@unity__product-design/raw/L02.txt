--- DESCRIPTION ---
How client briefs work, what they contain, and the different formats used to communicate them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Understanding Client Requirements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before any media product is created, the producer needs to understand exactly what the <dfn class="term" data-def="The person or organisation commissioning a media product, who defines what they want and approves the final result.">client</dfn> wants. Client requirements define the boundaries of a project — they tell the production team what to create, who it is for, and when it needs to be finished. The OCR specification requires you to understand how client requirements are defined and communicated.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What audience factors are described? List them from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Client requirements include: <strong>type of product, purpose, audience, client ethos, content, genre, style, theme, and timescales</strong>. These requirements inform all planning and production decisions.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">What Client Briefs Contain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A document or communication that outlines what a client wants from a media product, including the type, purpose, audience, content, style, and deadlines.">client brief</dfn> is a document or communication that sets out what the client wants. It is the starting point for any media project. You need to know what information is typically included in a brief and how to interpret it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Type of product</strong> — what format the product will take (e.g. website, poster, animation, video). <strong>Purpose</strong> — why the product is being created (advertise, educate, entertain, inform, or influence). <strong>Audience</strong> — who the product is aimed at (age group, demographics, interests). <strong>Client ethos</strong> — the values, beliefs, and identity of the client’s brand or organisation. <strong>Content</strong> — what information or material should be included. <strong>Genre</strong> — the style category the product falls into. <strong>Style</strong> — the visual and tonal approach (e.g. modern, vintage, minimalist). <strong>Theme</strong> — the overarching subject or concept. <strong>Timescales</strong> — deadlines and milestones for delivery.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">When reading a client brief, you should: (1) identify the <strong>keywords</strong> that tell you what the client wants (product type, purpose, audience); (2) recognise the <strong>requirements</strong> that will inform your planning (content, style, genre, theme); (3) note the <strong>constraints</strong> that will limit your options (timescales, budget, brand guidelines); (4) use the brief to <strong>generate ideas</strong> and begin planning the product. In the exam, you will be given a scenario based on a client brief and asked to apply your knowledge to it.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">How Client Briefs Constrain Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Client requirements do not just inform planning — they also <strong>constrain</strong> it. A constraint is a limitation that restricts what the production team can do. <dfn class="term" data-def="Limitations set by the client brief that restrict the production team's options, such as budget, timescales, brand guidelines, and content requirements.">Constraints</dfn> from a client brief include: tight timescales that limit how long can be spent on each phase; the client’s ethos may restrict the types of imagery, language, or content that can be used; specific content requirements mean the team cannot choose to focus on different topics; and genre or style requirements limit the creative direction.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/product-design/diagram_client_requirements_and_communication.jpg" alt="Diagram: Client Requirements and Communication" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Understanding constraints is important because in the exam, you may be asked why a particular design choice was made or how a requirement limits the options available. For example, if a children’s charity wants a video that is “fun and educational” with a “family-friendly” style, the production team cannot use dark themes, complex language, or inappropriate content — even if those choices might be more visually striking.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Client Brief Formats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Client briefs can be communicated in several different ways. The specification lists six formats you need to know.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Commission</strong> — a formal request from a client to a media company to produce a specific product, often following a competitive tender process. <strong>Formal brief</strong> — a detailed, structured written document that clearly sets out all requirements, often used for large or complex projects. <strong>Informal brief</strong> — a less structured communication, perhaps a quick email or conversation, used for smaller or ongoing projects. <strong>Meeting/discussion</strong> — requirements communicated verbally in a face-to-face or video meeting, which may then be written up as minutes or a follow-up document. <strong>Negotiated brief</strong> — a brief that is developed collaboratively between the client and the production team, with both parties contributing ideas and agreeing on requirements. <strong>Written brief</strong> — any brief provided in written form, which could be formal or informal.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What client requirements or constraints are described here?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Six client brief formats: <strong>commission, formal, informal, meeting/discussion, negotiated, written</strong>. A negotiated brief means both the client and the production team contribute to defining the requirements together.</p>
</div>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <strong>formal brief</strong> provides the most detail and clarity, reducing the risk of misunderstandings, but takes longer to prepare. An <strong>informal brief</strong> is quicker but may miss important details. A <strong>meeting/discussion</strong> allows for immediate clarification of questions but details may be forgotten if not recorded. A <strong>negotiated brief</strong> ensures both parties are satisfied but takes more time to agree. A <strong>commission</strong> is the most structured and professional format. The best format depends on the size of the project, the relationship between client and producer, and the complexity of the requirements.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Feedback Loops</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Communication between the client and production team does not end when the brief is agreed. Throughout the production process, the team seeks <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of sharing work-in-progress with the client to get their opinions and suggestions, then making adjustments based on their response.">feedback</dfn> from the client to ensure the product meets their expectations. Feedback loops allow the client to review progress at key stages (e.g. after concept designs, after a first draft, before final delivery) and request changes. This iterative process reduces the risk of producing a final product that the client is unhappy with.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Client briefs define what a media product should be: type, purpose, audience, ethos, content, genre, style, theme, and timescales. These requirements inform and constrain all production decisions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Briefs are communicated in six formats: commission, formal, informal, meeting/discussion, negotiated, and written. Each has advantages depending on the project size and complexity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Feedback loops throughout production ensure the product meets client expectations and allow for adjustments at key stages.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the R093 exam, Section B scenarios are essentially client briefs. Read them like a brief: identify the product type, purpose, audience, content, style, and constraints. When answering questions about the scenario, always refer back to these specific requirements rather than giving generic answers. If asked why a design choice is appropriate, link it to a specific requirement from the brief.</p>
