<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Media Codes: An Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The techniques and conventions used in media products to convey meaning, create impact, and engage audiences, divided into technical, symbolic, and written codes.">Media codes</dfn> are the techniques and conventions used in media products to convey meaning, create impact, and engage audiences. The OCR specification divides media codes into three categories: <strong>technical codes</strong> (how something is filmed, edited, or produced), <strong>symbolic codes</strong> (what objects, colours, and settings represent), and <strong>written codes</strong> (how text and language communicate meaning). Camera techniques are a key part of <strong>technical codes</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the typography terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Media codes are divided into three categories: <strong>technical codes</strong> (camera techniques, lighting, transitions, audio), <strong>symbolic codes</strong> (mise-en-scène, colour, props, costume), and <strong>written codes</strong> (typography, font, language). These categories overlap and work together.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Camera Shot Types</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Camera <dfn class="term" data-def="The different framings used by a camera operator to show varying amounts of the subject and surroundings, from extreme close-up to extreme long shot.">shot types</dfn> refer to how much of the subject and surroundings are visible in the frame. Each shot type creates a different effect and is chosen for a specific purpose.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Extreme close-up (ECU)</strong> — shows a very small detail, such as an eye or a hand on a door handle. Used to emphasise emotion, tension, or an important detail. <strong>Close-up (CU)</strong> — frames the subject’s face or a specific object. Shows emotions clearly and creates intimacy between the subject and the audience. <strong>Medium close-up (MCU)</strong> — shows the subject from the chest up. Common in interviews and dialogue scenes, balancing facial expression with body language. <strong>Medium shot (MS)</strong> — shows the subject from the waist up. Gives a sense of the character’s body language while still showing facial expression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Medium long shot (MLS)</strong> — shows the subject from the knees up, providing context about their position and clothing. <strong>Long shot (LS)</strong> — shows the full body of the subject with some surrounding environment. Used to establish a character’s position in a scene. <strong>Extreme long shot (ELS) / Establishing shot</strong> — shows a wide view of the location or setting. Used at the beginning of scenes to establish where the action takes place.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>ECU/CU:</strong> to show emotion, create tension, or draw attention to a detail (horror films use these to build suspense). <strong>MCU/MS:</strong> for dialogue, interviews, and conversational scenes (most common shot type in TV programmes). <strong>MLS/LS:</strong> to show action, movement, and the relationship between characters and their environment. <strong>ELS:</strong> to establish the setting at the start of a scene or to show the scale of a location (common in travel documentaries and action films). Choosing the right shot type is a deliberate creative decision that affects how the audience experiences the content.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Camera Angles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Camera <dfn class="term" data-def="The position of the camera relative to the subject, which affects how the audience perceives the subject's power, vulnerability, or relationship to the scene.">angles</dfn> affect how the audience perceives the subject. Changing the angle can make a character appear powerful, vulnerable, or neutral.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/product-design/diagram_media_codes_camera_techniques.jpg" alt="Diagram: Media Codes: Camera Techniques" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Eye-level angle</strong> — the camera is positioned at the subject’s eye level. This feels natural and neutral, creating a sense of equality between the subject and the audience. <strong>High angle</strong> — the camera looks down on the subject. This makes the subject appear smaller, weaker, vulnerable, or less significant. <strong>Low angle</strong> — the camera looks up at the subject. This makes the subject appear larger, more powerful, dominant, or threatening. <strong>Bird’s eye view</strong> — the camera is positioned directly above, looking straight down. This shows the layout of a scene and can make subjects appear insignificant or ant-like. <strong>Dutch angle / canted angle</strong> — the camera is tilted to one side. This creates a sense of unease, disorientation, or instability — common in thriller and horror genres.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Low angle</strong> = subject looks powerful/dominant. <strong>High angle</strong> = subject looks vulnerable/weak. <strong>Eye level</strong> = neutral/natural. These are the three most commonly examined angle types.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Camera Movement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Camera <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical movement of the camera during filming, including panning, tilting, tracking, and zooming, each creating different visual effects.">movement</dfn> adds dynamism and guides the audience’s attention. The specification requires you to know four types of camera movement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Pan</strong> — the camera rotates horizontally (left to right or right to left) on a fixed point. Used to follow action, reveal a setting, or show the relationship between objects. <strong>Tilt</strong> — the camera rotates vertically (up or down) on a fixed point. Used to reveal the height of a building, show a character from head to toe, or create dramatic effect. <strong>Track</strong> — the camera physically moves alongside, towards, or away from the subject (often on rails or a dolly). Tracking shots create a sense of following a character or moving through a space. <strong>Zoom</strong> — the camera lens adjusts to make the subject appear closer or further away without the camera physically moving. A zoom in draws attention to a detail; a zoom out reveals more of the scene.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Transitions</strong> are the techniques used to move from one shot or scene to another. <strong>Cut</strong> — an instant switch from one shot to the next (the most common transition). <strong>Dissolve/crossfade</strong> — one shot gradually fades out while the next fades in, creating a smooth transition often used to show the passage of time. <strong>Fade to black</strong> — the screen gradually goes black, used to signal the end of a scene or a significant time jump. <strong>Wipe</strong> — one shot is pushed off screen by the next, sometimes used for a stylised or dramatic effect.</p>
</div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Camera shot types range from extreme close-up to extreme long shot, each conveying different amounts of detail and creating different effects for the audience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Camera angles (eye-level, high, low, bird’s eye, Dutch) affect how the audience perceives the subject — low angles suggest power, high angles suggest vulnerability.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Camera movements (pan, tilt, track, zoom) and transitions (cut, dissolve, fade, wipe) add dynamism and guide the audience’s experience of the product.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When answering camera technique questions, always explain the <strong>effect on the audience</strong>, not just what the technique is. For example, don’t just write “a low angle is when the camera looks up at the subject” — add “this makes the subject appear more powerful and dominant, which could be used to show a villain’s authority.” Linking the technique to its purpose and audience impact is what earns higher marks.</p>
