Fall of Saigon, détente and how Cold War historians have debated who was responsible.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Fall of Saigon 1975</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Following the withdrawal of US combat troops in March 1973, the Paris Peace Accords left South Vietnam to defend itself against a continuing North Vietnamese military campaign. The South Vietnamese government received continued US financial support but no military intervention. In early 1975, North Vietnamese forces launched a major offensive. On 30 April 1975, <dfn class="term" data-def="The capital of South Vietnam, which fell to North Vietnamese forces on 30 April 1975, ending the Vietnam War and unifying Vietnam under communist rule.">Saigon</dfn> fell. Dramatic footage of US helicopters evacuating the last Americans from the roof of the embassy transmitted a powerful image of defeat around the world. Vietnam was reunified under communist rule as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The legacy of the war for the USA was profound. Approximately 58,000 American soldiers had died; South Vietnam had lost far more. The conflict exposed the limits of American military power, damaged public trust in government, and produced a generation of veterans dealing with physical and psychological injuries. Congress passed the War Powers Act in 1973, requiring the President to notify Congress within 48 hours of deploying forces and limiting deployments to 60 days without congressional approval.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when did Saigon fall, what treaty had ended direct US involvement, and what did the US Congress do in 1973 to limit future presidential war powers?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. The USA had lost approximately 58,000 soldiers in a war that ended in the victory of the side it had fought against. The War Powers Act (1973) was Congress&rsquo;s direct response to the way the President had bypassed congressional approval throughout the conflict.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">D&eacute;tente and the Relaxation of Tensions 1969&ndash;1979</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Alongside the Vietnam War, a broader shift in superpower relations was underway. <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of relaxed superpower tensions from roughly 1969 to 1979, characterised by arms control negotiations, diplomatic exchanges and reduced Cold War confrontation.">D&eacute;tente</dfn> &mdash; the French word for &ldquo;relaxation&rdquo; &mdash; described a policy of managing rather than winning the Cold War through negotiation. Nixon and his National Security Adviser <dfn class="term" data-def="US National Security Adviser and Secretary of State who, with Nixon, pursued détente: the policy of managing superpower competition through negotiation rather than confrontation.">Henry Kissinger</dfn> led this approach. In February 1972 Nixon made a landmark visit to communist China, opening diplomatic relations after more than two decades of hostility. This triangular diplomacy played the USSR against China, exploiting the Sino-Soviet split.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In May 1972, Nixon and Soviet leader <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet leader 1964–1982 who negotiated the SALT I agreement with Nixon and the Helsinki Accords of 1975 as part of the détente era.">Leonid Brezhnev</dfn> signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks treaty signed in May 1972, freezing the number of intercontinental ballistic missiles and submarine-launched ballistic missiles held by each superpower.">SALT I</dfn> agreement, the first treaty to limit the growth of nuclear weapons. It froze the number of intercontinental ballistic missiles and submarine-launched ballistic missiles. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Agreements signed in 1975 by 35 nations including the USA and USSR, recognising existing European borders and including a human rights commitment that dissidents later used to challenge Soviet repression.">Helsinki Accords</dfn> of 1975, signed by 35 nations, recognised the existing European borders established after 1945 and included a commitment to human rights that Soviet dissidents later used to challenge the Kremlin.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">D&eacute;tente ended abruptly in December 1979 when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan to prop up a communist government. The USA boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympics and Carter withdrew the SALT II treaty from Senate ratification. The invasion marked the return of intense superpower competition, sometimes called the &ldquo;Second Cold War&rdquo;. The episode showed how the structure of d&eacute;tente &mdash; based on negotiation rather than shared values &mdash; was vulnerable to a single decisive military action.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Cold War Historiography: Orthodox, Revisionist and Post-Revisionist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Historians have debated responsibility for the Cold War across three broad schools of thought. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Cold War historical school, dominant in the 1950s, that blamed Soviet expansionism and Stalin's aggression for causing the Cold War.">orthodox school</dfn>, dominant in the 1950s and 1960s, blamed Soviet expansionism: Stalin&rsquo;s takeover of Eastern Europe and his hostile ideology made the Cold War inevitable. Early Cold War historians such as Arthur Schlesinger Jr. held this view.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">From the 1960s, <dfn class="term" data-def="The Cold War historical school that shifted blame toward the United States, arguing that US economic imperialism and atomic diplomacy provoked Soviet defensiveness.">revisionist historians</dfn> such as <dfn class="term" data-def="American revisionist historian who argued in 'The Tragedy of American Diplomacy' (1959) that US economic expansionism and the Open Door policy drove Cold War confrontation.">William Appleman Williams</dfn> shifted emphasis toward American responsibility, arguing that US economic expansionism &mdash; the &ldquo;Open Door&rdquo; policy &mdash; and atomic diplomacy provoked Soviet defensiveness rather than Soviet aggression causing the conflict. The Vietnam War and revelations about CIA activities deepened public receptiveness to this reading.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the three Cold War historical schools, one key argument for each, and the name of one historian associated with each school.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The post-revisionist school, developed by <dfn class="term" data-def="American post-revisionist historian who synthesised orthodox and revisionist readings, arguing both superpowers pursued their interests and made the Cold War through interaction rather than one side causing it.">John Lewis Gaddis</dfn> from the 1970s, rejected simple blame narratives. Gaddis argued that both superpowers pursued their own interests and that the Cold War arose from their interaction, not from one side&rsquo;s aggression alone. Post-Cold War access to Soviet archives from the 1990s gave historians new evidence that largely supported post-revisionist conclusions.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The interpretation of appeasement has also shifted across three schools. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The critique of British appeasement policy, originating in Michael Foot's 1940 pamphlet 'Guilty Men', arguing that Chamberlain and others were culpably naive in appeasing Hitler.">Guilty Men</dfn> reading, originating in Michael Foot&rsquo;s 1940 pamphlet, condemned Chamberlain and other appeasers as culpably naive men who handed Hitler advantage after advantage. A.J.P. Taylor&rsquo;s revisionist approach in &ldquo;The Origins of the Second World War&rdquo; (1961) argued that appeasement reflected the genuine popular desire to avoid another catastrophic war &mdash; the &ldquo;Popular Majority&rdquo; view. More recent revisionist work by R.A.C. Parker argued that Chamberlain had genuine strategic alternatives that he refused to explore, making appeasement a deliberate choice rather than an inevitable response to public pressure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Examiners reward students who can name these schools and anchor each to specific evidence: for Guilty Men, the Munich Agreement and Chamberlain&rsquo;s claim of &ldquo;peace for our time&rdquo;; for the Popular Majority reading, public opinion polls of 1938; for the Parker revisionist reading, the rejected Soviet alliance proposals of 1939. Quoting a phrase from a given interpretation and linking it to one of these schools is the key technique for the highest band.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Saigon fell on 30 April 1975, ending the Vietnam War; the defeat damaged US credibility and led to the 1973 War Powers Act limiting presidential authority to deploy forces without Congress.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">D&eacute;tente (1969&ndash;1979) brought SALT I (1972), Nixon&rsquo;s opening of China, and the Helsinki Accords (1975), but ended when the USSR invaded Afghanistan in December 1979.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Cold War historiography has three main schools: orthodox (Soviet blame, 1950s), revisionist (US blame, Williams 1960s), and post-revisionist (mutual interaction, Gaddis 1970s onwards) &mdash; post-Cold War Soviet archives broadly supported the post-revisionist reading.</li>
</ul>
