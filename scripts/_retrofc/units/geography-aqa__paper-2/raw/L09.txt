--- DESCRIPTION ---
Economic and social indicators, their limitations and how the DTM links to development.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Measure Development?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The progress of a country in terms of wealth, use of technology and quality of life for its citizens.">Development</dfn> is uneven across the planet — some countries are very rich, with long lives and good schools, while others struggle with poverty and disease. Geographers measure development so that aid can be targeted, governments can compare progress, and patterns of inequality become visible. The challenge is that no single number captures everything that matters about a country’s quality of life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which indicator combines income, education and life expectancy into one score?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Development is measured using two types of indicator: economic measures (GNI per head, employment) and social measures (life expectancy, literacy, infant mortality). HDI combines both into one score from 0 to 1.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Economic Indicators</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The most common economic measure is <dfn class="term" data-def="The total value of all goods, services and income earned by a country (including overseas earnings) divided by population, usually expressed in US dollars.">Gross National Income (GNI) per head</dfn>. It adds up everything a country’s citizens and businesses earn, including overseas income, and divides by population. Norway’s GNI per head is around US$95,000; Burundi’s is around US$240. The gap is enormous. Economists also track unemployment rates and the share of workers in primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary industries — richer countries tend to have far more service-sector jobs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Social Indicators</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Social indicators measure quality of life. <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of babies who die before their first birthday, expressed per 1,000 live births.">Infant mortality</dfn> is one of the most sensitive: in the UK around 4 babies in 1,000 die before their first birthday, while in Sierra Leone the figure is over 80. Life expectancy ranges from 84 years in Japan to under 55 in some sub-Saharan African countries. Literacy rates measure education access, and the number of people per doctor shows healthcare provision — the UK has roughly 3 doctors per 1,000 people; Malawi has fewer than 0.05. Access to safe water completes the picture: where less than 60% of people have clean water, child deaths from diarrhoea soar.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Limitations of Single Indicators</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Every single indicator has weaknesses. GNI per head is an average — it hides huge inequality. A country can have an oil-rich elite and a poor majority, yet still record high GNI. Data may be old, missing or unreliable, especially in poorer countries. Some economic activity goes unrecorded in the <dfn class="term" data-def="Economic activity (such as street selling, subsistence farming or cash work) that is not officially recorded or taxed.">informal economy</dfn> — an estimated 70% of African workers earn this way. Social indicators have their own issues: literacy may be self-reported, and life expectancy averages mask whether men or women, rich or poor, live longer.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Human Development Index</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">To overcome the weaknesses of single measures, the United Nations created the <dfn class="term" data-def="A composite index that combines GNI per head, life expectancy and years of schooling into a single score between 0 (low) and 1 (very high).">Human Development Index (HDI)</dfn>. It blends three dimensions: a long, healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (GNI per head). Each country gets a score between 0 and 1. Norway and Switzerland sit above 0.95, the UK around 0.93, India around 0.64, and Niger near 0.40. HDI is more useful than GNI alone because it shows whether wealth is being turned into education and health.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three dimensions HDI combines and write down the typical UK and Niger scores.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>HDI combines life expectancy, education (years of schooling) and GNI per head. Scores run from 0 to 1: UK around 0.93, India around 0.64, Niger around 0.40.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Demographic Transition Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A five-stage model showing how a country's birth rate and death rate change as it develops; the gap between the two determines population growth.">Demographic Transition Model (DTM)</dfn> links population change to development. As a country develops, both birth and death rates fall, but at different times. The gap between them shapes population growth.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Five Stages of the DTM</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Stage 1 — High fluctuating:</strong> birth and death rates both high, population stable. Now found only in remote tribal communities.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Stage 2 — Early expanding:</strong> death rate falls due to better food, water and medicine, but birth rate stays high. Population grows fast. Examples: Afghanistan, parts of sub-Saharan Africa.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Stage 3 — Late expanding:</strong> birth rate falls as women gain education, contraception spreads and child labour becomes less needed. Population still grows but more slowly. Examples: India, Brazil.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Stage 4 — Low fluctuating:</strong> both rates low, population steady. Examples: UK, USA, Australia.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Stage 5 — Decline:</strong> birth rate falls below death rate, so population shrinks and ages. Examples: Japan, Germany, Italy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Linking the DTM to Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The DTM lines up neatly with the development indicators. Stage 2 countries usually have low GNI, high infant mortality and HDI below 0.55. Stage 3 countries are the classic NEEs — rapid growth, falling birth rates, HDI rising past 0.65. Stage 4 countries are HICs with high GNI and HDI above 0.85. Stage 5 introduces a new problem: an ageing population means fewer workers supporting many pensioners. The DTM is not perfect — it was based on European history and assumes every country follows the same path — but it is a powerful way of linking population change to development.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Economic measures (GNI per head) and social measures (infant mortality, life expectancy, literacy, doctors per 1,000) each show one face of development; HDI combines income, education and health into one score.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">Every indicator has limitations: GNI hides inequality, data may be unreliable, the informal economy is uncounted, and averages mask gaps between rich and poor.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">The Demographic Transition Model links birth and death rates to development — from Stage 1 (high fluctuating) through Stage 4 (HIC) to Stage 5 (declining ageing populations).</li></ul>
