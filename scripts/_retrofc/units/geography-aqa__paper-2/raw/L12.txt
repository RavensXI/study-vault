--- DESCRIPTION ---
How political, social and environmental factors set the stage for Nigeria's emerging economy.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Study Nigeria?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Nigeria is the obvious case study for an emerging economy. It is Africa’s most populous country (around 225 million people in 2024 and rising fast), Africa’s largest economy by GDP for most of the last decade, and one of the world’s top ten oil producers. Yet around 40% of Nigerians still live below the national poverty line. It is a textbook example of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A country with rapidly growing wealth and industry whose economy has not yet reached the level of a high-income country; abbreviated NEE.">Newly Emerging Economy (NEE)</dfn> — growing fast, but with deep inequalities still to address.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Nigeria's population, the share of GDP from oil and the share of people in poverty.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria has around 225 million people, the largest economy in Africa, but ~40% of the population lives below the poverty line. Oil and gas account for roughly 7% of GDP but over 80% of export earnings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Location and Regional Importance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Nigeria sits in West Africa, on the Gulf of Guinea coast. It borders Benin to the west, Niger to the north, Chad to the north-east and Cameroon to the east. The Atlantic coastline gives it major ports at Lagos and Port Harcourt, opening trade with Europe, the Americas and increasingly Asia. The country lies entirely within the tropics, between roughly 4°N and 14°N, so it has a hot climate but a sharp north-south rainfall divide. Nigeria is a founder member of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Economic Community of West African States; a regional bloc of 15 countries that promotes trade, free movement and political cooperation.">ECOWAS</dfn>, the African Union, OPEC and the Commonwealth, giving it diplomatic weight far beyond its borders.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Global Importance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Globally, Nigeria matters for three reasons. First, oil: it is the largest oil producer in Africa and a major supplier to Europe and the USA. Second, culture: Nigerian Afrobeats music (Burna Boy, Wizkid, Tems) and Nollywood films are exported across the world — Nollywood produces around 2,500 films a year, second only to India in volume. Third, demographics: Nigeria is projected to overtake the USA as the world’s third most populous country before 2050. Where Nigeria goes, Africa often follows.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Nigeria gained independence from Britain in 1960. It was ruled by a sequence of military governments from the 1960s through the 1990s, with a brutal civil war (the Biafran War, 1967–70) early on. Stable democratic elections have been held every four years since 1999, and peaceful handovers of power between parties (notably 2015 and 2023) have boosted investor confidence. Corruption remains a serious challenge: Nigeria scored 25/100 in the Transparency International Corruption Perceptions Index in 2023, ranking among the bottom third globally.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Social and Cultural Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Nigeria is one of the most diverse countries in the world, with over 250 ethnic groups and 500 languages. Three groups dominate: the Hausa-Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the south-west and the Igbo in the south-east. The country is roughly 53% Muslim (mostly in the north) and 45% Christian (mostly in the south). This diversity is a source of vibrant culture but also of tension — the Islamist <dfn class="term" data-def="An Islamist insurgent group that has carried out attacks in north-east Nigeria since 2009, including the 2014 kidnapping of 276 schoolgirls in Chibok.">Boko Haram</dfn> insurgency in the north-east has displaced over two million people since 2009. The 2014 kidnapping of 276 Chibok schoolgirls drew global condemnation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name Nigeria's three largest ethnic groups and the religion that dominates each region.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria has over 250 ethnic groups, dominated by the Hausa-Fulani (north), Yoruba (south-west) and Igbo (south-east). The country is roughly 53% Muslim (north) and 45% Christian (south).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Environmental Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Nigeria’s landscape varies dramatically from north to south. The far north is part of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A semi-arid belt of grassland and acacia trees stretching across northern Africa, prone to drought and the spread of the Sahara.">Sahel</dfn>, with under 500 mm of rain a year and creeping desertification. The Middle Belt has wooded savanna grassland used for cattle grazing and grain crops. The south-east holds the remains of West African tropical rainforest, while the Niger Delta is a vast wetland of mangroves, creeks and oil pipelines. Climate change is making the north drier and the rainfall in the south heavier and less predictable, hitting farmers hardest.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Nigeria’s mix of features shapes its development. Oil reserves in the Niger Delta have made the country wealthy on paper, but pollution from spills and gas flaring has damaged fisheries and farmland for local communities. Coastal Lagos is one of Africa’s biggest ports and home to Africa’s largest stock exchange, but it is also the world’s fastest-growing megacity. Northern droughts and southern flooding both push migration to the cities, fuelling the urbanisation studied in earlier lessons.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Nigeria Is an NEE</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Nigeria ticks every box of an emerging economy. GDP grew at an average of 5–7% a year between 2000 and 2014, lifting tens of millions out of extreme poverty. The economy has diversified beyond oil into telecoms, banking and Nollywood. A young population (median age just 18) provides a growing workforce. Yet challenges remain: 40% poverty, regional inequality (the south is much richer than the north), corruption and Boko Haram. Understanding the political, social and environmental setting is the first step to understanding everything else about Nigeria’s development — and the next two lessons build on it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Nigeria is West Africa’s largest economy and most populous country (~225 million), with global importance through oil exports, Nollywood films and Afrobeats music.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Politically Nigeria has held democratic elections since 1999 but still faces corruption and the Boko Haram insurgency in the north-east; socially it has over 250 ethnic groups split between Muslim north and Christian south.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Environmentally, Nigeria runs from semi-arid Sahel in the north to tropical rainforest and the Niger Delta in the south — geography that both helps (oil, ports, fertile farmland) and hinders (pollution, droughts, floods) development.</li></ul>
