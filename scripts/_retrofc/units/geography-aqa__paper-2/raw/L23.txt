--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why water demand is rising, what affects availability, and the consequences of water insecurity.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Water: The Resource You Cannot Live Without</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Of all Earth's water, only around 3% is fresh, and two thirds of that is locked in glaciers and ice caps. The tiny remainder — less than 1% of all water — fills rivers, lakes and aquifers on which humanity depends. Yet that 1% is unevenly distributed, and as global demand rises, the mismatch between where water is and where people need it is growing into one of the defining crises of the 21st century. Geographers call this <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation in which demand for fresh water exceeds reliable supply, or where quality is so poor that supply is effectively reduced.">water insecurity</dfn> — a state in which demand either exceeds supply or where pollution and poor infrastructure make water unreliable even where it exists.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what percentage of Earth's water is fresh, and what does water insecurity mean?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Only around 3% of Earth's water is fresh, and less than 1% is accessible in rivers, lakes and shallow aquifers. Around 2 billion people currently live in countries experiencing high water stress, and the UN projects that by 2025 two thirds of the world's population could face water shortages.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Global Patterns of Water Surplus and Deficit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Water availability is measured as the volume of freshwater available per person per year, adjusted for seasonal variability. <strong>Water surplus</strong> regions — tropical Central Africa (Congo basin), equatorial South America (Amazon), much of South-East Asia and Scandinavia — receive heavy, reliable rainfall and have relatively small populations. <dfn class="term" data-def="A country or region where water availability per person per year falls below 1,700 cubic metres, making water shortage a periodic or permanent constraint on development.">Water scarcity</dfn> is most severe in the Middle East and North Africa (Libya, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait), where rainfall averages below 100 mm a year, as well as parts of Central Asia, the Sahel and northern India where demand from rapidly growing populations outstrips supply.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Two main forces push global water demand <strong>upward</strong>:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Rising population:</strong> more people drink more, cook more and produce more wastewater. The global population passed 8 billion in 2022 and is growing fastest in already water-scarce regions of Africa and the Middle East.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Economic development:</strong> as countries industrialise and living standards rise, water use increases sharply. Industry (manufacturing, power generation) and commercial agriculture consume vast volumes. A single litre of bottled water requires several litres in production; a car requires around 400,000 litres to manufacture. Higher incomes also mean longer showers, washing machines and garden irrigation.</li>
</ul>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Agriculture accounts for roughly 70% of all freshwater withdrawals globally — most of it for irrigation. Industry takes around 20%, used for cooling power stations, processing food and manufacturing. Domestic use (drinking, cooking, sanitation, hygiene) accounts for only about 10%. This balance matters for policy: cutting domestic use has a modest impact; improving the efficiency of irrigated agriculture is the single biggest lever available for reducing global water demand.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Factors Affecting Water Availability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Several factors determine whether a country or region can access enough safe water:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Climate:</strong> rainfall, evaporation rates and seasonality set the natural water budget. Areas with monsoon climates receive most of their rainfall in a few intense months, creating seasonal extremes of flood and drought. Climate change is intensifying these extremes — wetter wet seasons, drier dry seasons, and shifting monsoon timings — making water planning harder.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Geology:</strong> permeable rock types such as limestone and chalk store water in underground layers called <dfn class="term" data-def="An underground layer of permeable rock or sediment that holds water and allows it to flow, serving as a source for wells and springs.">aquifers</dfn>, which can supply water even in dry seasons. Impermeable basement rocks (much of sub-Saharan Africa) hold little groundwater, so surface rainfall is all that is available — and that is seasonal and unreliable.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Pollution of supply:</strong> industrial discharge, agricultural run-off (fertilisers and pesticides), untreated sewage and mining waste contaminate rivers, lakes and groundwater. In Bangladesh, naturally occurring arsenic in shallow tubewells poisoned millions of people who drilled for clean water. In China, rapid industrialisation has severely polluted many major rivers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Over-abstraction:</strong> pumping groundwater faster than it recharges permanently lowers aquifer levels. In northern India the water table beneath the Punjab farmland is falling by around 1 metre a year as tube wells extract water for irrigation. Saudi Arabia has largely depleted its 'fossil' aquifers — ancient water that accumulated over thousands of years and will not refill in any human timescale.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Limited infrastructure:</strong> even where water exists, it cannot be used without treatment plants, pipe networks and pumping stations. Around 2.2 billion people lack access to safely managed drinking water, mostly because governments lack the money to build and maintain the necessary infrastructure — not because water is physically absent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Poverty:</strong> poor households often pay more for water (from water trucks or vendors) than rich households do on tap, because they cannot afford the connection fees or live beyond the pipe network. In countries without municipal supply, collecting water from rivers or ponds takes hours each day, a burden that falls disproportionately on women and girls.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Impacts of Water Insecurity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">When water supply is unreliable or inadequate, the consequences affect health, food, economy and politics:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Waterborne disease and water pollution:</strong> when people drink from contaminated sources, diseases such as cholera, typhoid, dysentery and diarrhoea spread rapidly. Diarrhoeal disease kills around 1.5 million people a year globally, including around 495,000 children under the age of nine, mostly in LICs. In 2010, an earthquake in Haiti damaged water infrastructure, triggering a cholera outbreak that killed nearly 10,000 people.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Reduced food production:</strong> agriculture needs reliable water to function. As aquifers fall and rivers run low, irrigated farmland goes out of production. In California's Central Valley, a decade of drought forced farmers to fallow millions of acres and draw down groundwater reserves. Across the Sahel, reduced river flow has shrunk irrigable land, pushing millions toward food insecurity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Industrial output:</strong> factories, power stations and mines need water for cooling, processing and transport. In India during a 2019 water crisis, several cities reported that industries were shutting down because municipal water supply had failed. Thermal power stations, which need water for cooling, cut output when rivers run low during droughts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Potential for conflict:</strong> where rivers cross national boundaries, upstream users who divert or dam water reduce supply for downstream countries. The Nile is shared by 11 countries; Ethiopia's Grand Renaissance Dam (completed in 2020) triggered a prolonged diplomatic and strategic crisis with Egypt and Sudan, who depend on the Nile for over 90% of their fresh water. The UN has identified over 150 potential future water conflict zones globally.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: name four impacts of water insecurity, giving one specific example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Water insecurity causes waterborne disease (diarrhoeal disease kills around 1.5 million people a year, including around 495,000 children), reduces food production (drought fallowing in California's Central Valley), cuts industrial output and power generation, and drives geopolitical conflict — most starkly over the Nile, shared by 11 countries.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Egypt receives virtually no rainfall and depends on the Nile for about 97% of its fresh water. Ethiopia, upstream, began filling the reservoir behind its Grand Renaissance Dam in 2020. Egypt and Sudan argued this would cut Nile flow during filling, damaging their agriculture. Ethiopia, one of Africa's poorest and most populous nations, argued it had the right to develop its own hydroelectric resource. No final legal agreement has been reached, illustrating how water scarcity can turn resource development in one country into an existential threat for another.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Water insecurity is not an abstract environmental issue — it kills people, destroys harvests, closes factories and starts conflicts. As climate change alters rainfall patterns and as population and development drive demand ever higher, the gap between supply and need will widen in many parts of the world. The next lesson explores the strategies — from massive transfer projects to simple rainwater harvesting — that can close that gap sustainably.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Water surplus is concentrated in tropical and equatorial regions with reliable rainfall; water scarcity is worst in the Middle East, North Africa and parts of Central Asia, driven upward by rising population and economic development.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Availability is reduced by climate variability, impermeable geology, pollution, over-abstraction of aquifers, limited infrastructure and poverty — often acting together in the same country.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Water insecurity causes waterborne diseases (diarrhoeal disease kills around 1.5 million people a year), reduces food production and industrial output, and drives geopolitical tension — most acutely over shared rivers such as the Nile.</li>
</ul>
