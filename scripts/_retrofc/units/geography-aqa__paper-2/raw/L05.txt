--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why population clusters where it does and the global importance of one UK city case study.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Do People Live in the UK?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK’s population is just over 67 million, but it is far from evenly spread. Some areas are densely populated — <strong>Greater London</strong> alone holds over 9 million people on a tiny patch of land — while large parts of <strong>Scotland</strong> and <strong>Wales</strong> have fewer than 50 people per square kilometre. Geographers call this pattern a <dfn class="term" data-def="The way in which people are spread out across an area, often described as dense (crowded) or sparse (few people).">population distribution</dfn>, and it is shaped by physical features and human history acting together.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Where the UK Is Crowded</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A line drawn from the Bristol Channel in the south-west to the Wash in the east neatly separates the most densely populated half of England from the more sparsely populated north and west. Within that crowded south-east lies London and the south-east region. A second cluster runs across the <strong>industrial north</strong> — Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds, Sheffield — and a third in the <strong>West Midlands</strong> around Birmingham. <strong>Glasgow</strong> and <strong>Edinburgh</strong> are the largest population centres in Scotland, and <strong>Cardiff</strong> in Wales. People cluster on the lowlands, near rivers and coasts, and around old industrial cities.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name two regions of the UK with high population density and two with low density, with one reason for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK has roughly 67 million people. Density is highest in London and the south-east, the industrial north (Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds) and the West Midlands. The Scottish Highlands, mid-Wales and the Lake District are the most sparsely populated.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why the UK Is Sparse in Some Places</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Three factors push people away from sparse areas. First, <strong>relief</strong>: mountainous land in the Scottish Highlands, Snowdonia and the Lake District is steep and hard to build on. Second, <strong>climate</strong>: upland areas are colder, wetter and windier, with shorter growing seasons. Third, <strong>poor soils</strong>: thin moorland and peat bogs cannot support large-scale farming. Together these mean fewer jobs, fewer services and fewer reasons to settle. Many former mining and rural communities have shrunk over the past century as people moved south or to the cities for work.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">London’s pull is a story of physical advantage and historic accident in equal measure. The Thames was a navigable river, the south-east lowlands were warm and fertile, and the position made London a natural gateway to mainland Europe. Once a financial centre had grown there, jobs, transport links and universities followed — each one drawing more people in. Today around one in seven UK residents lives in Greater London, and the wider south-east is the most densely populated part of the country. Geographers call this self-reinforcing process <em>cumulative causation</em>: success in one place pulls in more success.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">A Major UK City: Manchester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Manchester</strong> is the chosen case study city. It sits in the north-west of England, at the confluence of the rivers Irwell, Irk and Medlock, around 50 km east of Liverpool and the Atlantic coast. The city of Manchester itself has around 560,000 residents; the wider <strong>Greater Manchester</strong> metropolitan region has around 2.9 million. It is the second-largest urban area in the UK after London and the heart of the country’s ‘Northern Powerhouse’ economic strategy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Manchester’s Importance in the UK</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Manchester is the leading city of north-west England. It is a major financial centre — <em>Spinningfields</em> is sometimes called the ‘Canary Wharf of the North’ and hosts banks, law firms and consultancies. The city is home to four universities, including the <strong>University of Manchester</strong>, one of the largest in the UK and a world leader in research. <strong>Manchester Airport</strong> is the third-busiest in the UK after Heathrow and Gatwick, with direct flights to over 200 destinations. Manchester is the BBC’s second hub at <em>MediaCityUK</em> in Salford, broadcasting to millions of viewers daily.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Manchester became Cottonopolis</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Manchester was the world’s first industrial city. Wet climate suited cotton spinning, fast-flowing Pennine streams powered early mills, and coal from Lancashire fed the steam engines. By 1850 it was nicknamed <em>Cottonopolis</em>, processing raw cotton from the United States, India and Egypt and shipping cloth around the world. The Manchester Ship Canal, opened in 1894, allowed ocean-going ships to reach the city directly — bypassing Liverpool. The cotton industry has long collapsed, but the infrastructure, the universities and the global connections it created still shape Manchester today.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Manchester’s Importance in the Wider World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Manchester punches above its weight internationally. It is home to two of the most globally followed football clubs — <strong>Manchester United</strong> and <strong>Manchester City</strong> — whose Premier League matches reach hundreds of millions of viewers worldwide. The city is a top-twenty destination for international students and a major host for global business events. The Sir Henry Royce Institute and the Graphene Engineering Innovation Centre, both based in Manchester, lead world research in advanced materials. Through MediaCityUK, the BBC reaches international audiences from Salford. Manchester is, in short, a globally-recognised brand.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: where is Manchester, what is its population, and three reasons it is important to the UK or the wider world.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Manchester sits in north-west England, about 50 km from the coast. The city has around 560,000 residents and Greater Manchester around 2.9 million. It is the UK’s second-largest urban region and a global city for sport, media, research and finance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why Manchester Matters as a Case Study</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Manchester is the perfect case study for UK urban issues because every theme is visible in its streets. It is reshaped by both <dfn class="term" data-def="Movement of people within the same country, often from one region to another for jobs, study or quality of life.">national migration</dfn> — students arriving from across the UK, families moving from the south-east for cheaper housing — and <dfn class="term" data-def="Movement of people across national borders, into and out of a country.">international migration</dfn>. Its inner suburbs are a patchwork of regeneration and deprivation. Brownfield sites and urban greening projects sit beside areas struggling with poverty. The next two lessons examine the social, economic and environmental opportunities and challenges that come with being a major UK city.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">UK population is unevenly distributed — dense in London and the south-east, the industrial north and the West Midlands; sparse in the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District and mid-Wales because of relief, climate and poor soils.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Manchester is the major UK city case study — in north-west England, around 560,000 in the city and 2.9 million in Greater Manchester, the UK’s second-largest urban region.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Manchester is important nationally (Spinningfields finance, four universities, Manchester Airport) and internationally (Manchester United and City, MediaCityUK, the Royce Institute).</li>
</ul>
