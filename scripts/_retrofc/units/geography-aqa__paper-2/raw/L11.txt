--- DESCRIPTION ---
How investment, fair trade, microfinance and tourism reduce global development inequalities.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Strategies to Close the Gap</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Reducing the development gap means lifting incomes, life expectancy and education in poorer countries faster than they rise in richer ones. Geographers identify seven main strategies: investment, industrial development, tourism, aid, intermediate technology, fair trade and microfinance loans, plus debt relief. None works in isolation; the best results come from combining them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list five different strategies used to reduce the development gap.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The seven main strategies for reducing the global development gap are: investment, industrial development, tourism, international aid, intermediate technology, fair trade, and microfinance loans / debt relief.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Investment, Industry and Aid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Foreign direct <dfn class="term" data-def="Money put into a country by overseas firms or governments to build factories, infrastructure or services.">investment</dfn> by transnational corporations builds factories that bring jobs and skills. Industrial development — especially manufacturing — can lift millions out of poverty, as it has in Bangladesh’s textile sector. <dfn class="term" data-def="Money, goods, food, equipment or expertise given by one country to another, usually to support development or after a disaster.">International aid</dfn> can be short-term humanitarian relief after a disaster or long-term development funding for schools, clinics and infrastructure. Aid sometimes attracts criticism: it can create dependency or be misused if governments are corrupt, so charities such as Oxfam favour aid that builds local capacity rather than handouts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Intermediate Technology and Microfinance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Simple, affordable, locally repairable technology suited to the skills and resources available in a developing country (e.g. hand-drilled wells, treadle pumps, solar lanterns).">Intermediate technology</dfn> uses simple, affordable, locally repairable tools rather than expensive imported machinery. Examples include hand-drilled wells in Malawi, treadle pumps in Bangladesh and clay pot fridges in Sudan. <dfn class="term" data-def="Small loans (often under £100) given to people in poorer countries who cannot access normal banking, used to start small businesses.">Microfinance loans</dfn> — pioneered by the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh — give people small amounts of capital to start businesses such as a sewing workshop or vegetable stall. Repayment rates are over 95%, and women run most of the loans, helping their families and communities together.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A system of trading that guarantees producers in poorer countries a minimum price plus a community premium, in exchange for meeting environmental and labour standards.">Fair trade</dfn> guarantees farmers a minimum price for crops like coffee, cocoa and bananas, plus a community premium spent on schools or clean water. The Fairtrade Foundation works with around 1.9 million farmers worldwide. <dfn class="term" data-def="When richer countries or international banks cancel some or all of the debt owed by a poorer country, freeing up money for spending on health and education.">Debt relief</dfn> — for example through the IMF’s Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative — has cancelled over US$100 billion of poor-country debt since 1996, freeing money for schools and healthcare.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Tourism: Why It Works</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Tourism is one of the fastest ways to inject money into a poorer country’s economy. It creates jobs in hotels, restaurants, transport and crafts, and earns valuable foreign currency. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The way money spent by tourists circulates through the local economy as it is re-spent on goods, services and wages.">multiplier effect</dfn> means that money spent by tourists is re-spent locally on food, building materials and education. Tourism also drives investment in airports, roads and electricity that benefit everyone.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Case Study: Tourism in The Gambia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Gambia is the smallest country on the African mainland, with a population of around 2.6 million and a GNI per head of just under US$800. It has used tourism since the 1960s as a development strategy, attracting around 600,000 visitors a year before the pandemic, mostly from the UK, Netherlands and Belgium escaping the European winter for sandy Atlantic beaches and the warm dry season from November to April.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Tourism Reduces the Development Gap in The Gambia</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Tourism contributes roughly 20% of The Gambia’s GDP and provides around 42,000 direct jobs — one in six adults. Hotels at Kotu, Kololi and Senegambia employ chefs, drivers, guides, cleaners and gardeners. The Gambia Tourism Board’s ‘Roots’ festival celebrates the country’s links with the African diaspora, attracting visitors from the USA. Foreign currency earned by tourism pays for medicines and equipment that the government could not otherwise afford.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Local craft markets and the ‘Tourism Concern’ initiative make sure money reaches small businesses, not only big resorts. New schools and clinics have been built using a tourism levy. Improved infrastructure — the upgraded Banjul airport and a coastal road — benefits Gambians as well as tourists.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Costs and Limits of Tourism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Tourism is not perfect. Around 60% of money spent at all-inclusive resorts can leak straight back to overseas tour operators, an effect known as <dfn class="term" data-def="When money paid by tourists leaves the host country and ends up in foreign-owned airlines, hotels or food suppliers rather than the local economy.">economic leakage</dfn>. Most tourism jobs are seasonal and low-paid, and many close completely in the wet summer months. Tourism is fragile: visitor numbers in The Gambia collapsed during COVID-19 and during periods of political unrest. There can be cultural and environmental costs too, from pressure on freshwater to local resentment of wealthier visitors.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two benefits and two limits of tourism as a development strategy in The Gambia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tourism brings around 20% of GDP and one in six adult jobs in The Gambia. Limits include economic leakage of up to 60% to overseas firms, seasonal work, and vulnerability to crises like COVID-19.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Bringing the Strategies Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">No single strategy closes the development gap by itself. The Gambia uses tourism alongside fair trade groundnut exports, microfinance loans for women’s cooperatives, and aid from the UK, EU and World Bank. The clearest progress comes when investment in jobs, fairer trade rules and aid for health and education are pursued together — and when local communities, not only foreign companies, share in the gains.</p>

<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Strategies for reducing the development gap include investment, industrial development, tourism, aid, intermediate technology, fair trade and microfinance loans / debt relief.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Tourism in The Gambia provides around 20% of GDP and 42,000 jobs, builds schools and clinics through a tourism levy, and improves infrastructure that locals also use.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">Tourism has limits — up to 60% economic leakage, seasonal jobs, vulnerability to COVID-19 and political unrest — so it works best alongside fair trade, microfinance and aid.</li></ul>
