--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Is Lagos and Why Does It Matter?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Lagos sits on the south-western coast of Nigeria, on the Atlantic Ocean and either side of a chain of lagoons. It is the largest city in Africa, with an estimated population of around 16 million in the official metropolitan area — some estimates push the wider urban region above 20 million. It is the financial and cultural capital of Nigeria, even though the political capital was moved to Abuja in 1991. Lagos is the heart of an <dfn class="term" data-def="A country whose economy is rapidly industrialising and growing in size, sitting between low-income and high-income status.">NEE</dfn> — a newly emerging economy — and its growth has been described as one of the most extraordinary urban stories of the twenty-first century.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where is Lagos, what is its rough population, and why does it matter regionally and internationally?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Lagos is on Nigeria’s south-west Atlantic coast and is Africa’s largest city, with around 16 million people. It produces roughly 30% of Nigeria’s GDP and is West Africa’s leading port and financial hub.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Importance: Regional, National, International</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Regionally</strong> Lagos dominates West Africa — its port handles around 70% of Nigeria’s sea trade and serves landlocked countries inland such as Niger and Chad. <strong>Nationally</strong> it generates roughly 30% of Nigeria’s GDP from less than 1% of its land area, hosting the country’s biggest banks, telecoms firms and oil-services companies. <strong>Internationally</strong> Lagos is home to Nollywood, the second-largest film industry in the world by output, exporting Nigerian film and music across Africa and the diaspora. The city is a regional headquarters for transnational corporations such as Shell and Unilever, and Murtala Muhammed International Airport is West Africa’s busiest hub.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why Lagos Is Growing So Fast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Two engines drive Lagos’s growth, and both are turning at full speed. The first is <strong>migration</strong>. Around 2,000 people arrive in Lagos every day from rural Nigeria and from neighbouring countries. They are pushed by drought in the Sahel, by conflict in the north-east where the militant group Boko Haram has displaced communities, and by collapsing harvests on small family farms. They are pulled by the obvious wealth of Lagos — the construction sites, the port, the markets — even though most arrivals will not find a steady wage straight away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The second engine is <dfn class="term" data-def="Population growth caused by birth rate being higher than death rate.">natural increase</dfn>. Migrants are mostly young adults of working age. They have children, and city clinics mean those children are far more likely to survive than in a remote village. Birth rates in Lagos are still high by global standards, and the death rate has fallen with better access to healthcare and clean water. Even if migration stopped tomorrow, Lagos would keep growing from inside.</p>

Add natural increase and Lagos has been gaining roughly a million extra residents a year. That is why even huge construction projects struggle to keep pace: by the time a new road or housing block is finished, another 100,000 people have already arrived.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7b">Economic Opportunities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Urban industrial areas in Lagos are a powerful stimulus for economic development. The <strong>port of Apapa</strong> handles container traffic for half of West Africa, employing tens of thousands of dock workers, lorry drivers and freight clerks. Industrial estates in <strong>Ikeja</strong> manufacture electronics, pharmaceuticals and processed food — multinational firms such as Nestlé and Unilever have factories there. The <strong>Eko Atlantic City</strong> project, built on land reclaimed from the Atlantic, is positioning Lagos as a regional financial centre to rival Dubai. The result is a wage premium: an industrial worker in Lagos can earn three to four times what they would on a family farm in rural Nigeria.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two named economic opportunities in Lagos and the workers each one supports.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Apapa port handles around 70% of Nigeria’s sea trade. Industrial estates in Ikeja host multinationals such as Nestlé and Unilever. Eko Atlantic, built on reclaimed land, is being developed as a financial hub.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Social Opportunities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Cities concentrate services in a way villages cannot. In Lagos, residents have access to <strong>healthcare</strong> at facilities like Lagos University Teaching Hospital, where specialists treat conditions that no rural clinic could handle. <strong>Education</strong> stretches from primary school to several major universities, including the University of Lagos and Lagos State University — opening careers in medicine, law and engineering that simply do not exist in the villages migrants leave behind. <strong>Resources</strong> arrive through formal infrastructure: a piped water system serves much of central Lagos, the national grid supplies electricity (when supply allows), and waste collection covers the planned districts. None of these are perfect — but compared with a rural household carrying water from a stream, the difference is significant.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Roughly 60% of Lagos’s workforce earns its living in the <dfn class="term" data-def="Work that is unregulated and untaxed, such as street trading and small workshops, often providing the only income for the urban poor.">informal economy</dfn> — street selling, motorbike taxis (called <em>okadas</em>), small workshops repairing phones and TVs, and casual building work. It is irregular and unprotected by employment law, but it gets people earning quickly. Sociologists studying Lagos describe the informal economy as the city’s ‘real economy’, because for most new arrivals it is the first — and often only — way to put food on the table.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Lagos Is Worth Studying</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For students of geography, Lagos is the perfect case study because every theme of urban issues plays out in extreme form. Migration, natural increase, opportunities, slums, sanitation, transport, planning and inequality all sit side by side. The next lesson tackles the other half of the story: the squatter settlements, polluted lagoons and traffic gridlock that come with growing this fast.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Lagos is on Nigeria’s south-west Atlantic coast, has around 16 million people and produces roughly 30% of Nigeria’s GDP, making it West Africa’s most important economic centre.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">It grows by around a million people a year through rural-to-urban migration (around 2,000 daily arrivals) and natural increase, with young migrants raising children in the city.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Urban growth has created social opportunities (healthcare, education, services) and economic opportunities (Apapa port, Ikeja industry, Eko Atlantic) that pay several times rural wages.</li>
</ul>
