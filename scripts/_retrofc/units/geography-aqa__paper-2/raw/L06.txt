--- DESCRIPTION ---
How national and international migration reshape UK cities and why deprivation persists.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A City Made by Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Manchester’s population fell during the late twentieth century as factories closed, but it is now growing fast again — both from <strong>national migration</strong> within the UK and from <strong>international migration</strong> from outside it. Migration changes the size of a city, the mix of its people and the kinds of opportunities and challenges it faces. To understand modern Manchester you have to understand who has been moving there, and why.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">National Migration into Manchester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Tens of thousands of UK residents move into Greater Manchester each year. The single biggest group is <strong>students</strong>: the four universities have around 100,000 students between them, with the University of Manchester alone hosting roughly 45,000. Many graduates stay on, attracted by lower house prices than London, growing employers in finance, media and digital, and the city’s cultural scene. <strong>Families and professionals</strong> also move from the south-east to escape London rents. The 2017 opening of the BBC at MediaCityUK pulled hundreds of broadcasters out of London — a clear example of how government policy can redirect national migration.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two largest groups of UK residents moving into Manchester and one reason each is drawn to the city.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Manchester’s four universities host around 100,000 students — the city’s biggest national-migration group. Many graduates stay; families and professionals also move from the south-east for cheaper housing and growing employers in finance, media and tech.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">International Migration into Manchester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Manchester has been an international city for centuries. Irish migrants arrived in the nineteenth century to work in the mills; Jewish refugees settled in Cheetham Hill in the late 1800s; Caribbean migrants came in the 1950s and 60s; South Asian families — especially from Pakistan, Bangladesh and India — settled across Rusholme and Longsight from the 1960s. More recent waves include <strong>EU migrants</strong> after eastern European countries joined the EU in 2004, and <strong>refugees and asylum seekers</strong> from Syria, Afghanistan and Ukraine. The 2021 census found that around 31.4% of Manchester city residents were born outside the UK — one of the highest shares of any English city outside London.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Geographers describe a city’s <em>character</em> as the mix of cultures, languages, food, religion and history that you can feel walking around. Manchester’s <strong>Curry Mile</strong> on Wilmslow Road in Rusholme grew out of South Asian migration; <strong>Chinatown</strong> on Faulkner Street is one of Britain’s oldest. The Caribbean Carnival has been part of Manchester’s summer since 1971. Schools teach over 200 languages between them. None of these features would exist without migration — and they now feed back into the economy by drawing tourists and global talent.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Opportunities Created by Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Migration has made Manchester a culturally vibrant city — the <em>cultural mix</em> includes festivals like the Caribbean Carnival, Eid celebrations in Platt Fields and Chinese New Year in Chinatown. Migrant communities have driven entrepreneurship: many of the restaurants, takeaways and small businesses in Rusholme, Cheetham Hill and Longsight were founded by first- or second-generation migrants. Migration also fills key jobs — the NHS in Greater Manchester depends heavily on overseas-trained doctors and nurses, and EU migrants have long staffed the construction, hospitality and food-processing sectors.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Urban Challenges: Deprivation and Inequality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For all its growth and energy, Manchester has some of the most deprived neighbourhoods in England. <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where people lack basic resources and opportunities such as good housing, education, health care and well-paid work.">Urban deprivation</dfn> is measured by the <strong>Index of Multiple Deprivation</strong>, and Manchester consistently ranks among the most deprived local authorities in England. Wards such as <strong>Harpurhey</strong>, <strong>Gorton</strong> and parts of <strong>Ardwick</strong> have unemployment, child poverty and ill-health rates well above the national average. Life expectancy varies by years across the city — a man in Manchester city centre lives roughly seven years less on average than a man in the wealthier Greater Manchester suburbs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: name two deprived Manchester wards and two specific inequalities that affect residents’ lives there.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Manchester’s most deprived wards include Harpurhey, Gorton and Ardwick. Life expectancy is around seven years lower in central Manchester than in the wealthier suburbs. Inequalities cut across housing, education, health and employment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Inequalities in Housing, Education and Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In <strong>housing</strong>, much of Manchester’s Victorian terraced stock is poor quality — damp, cold and expensive to heat. Council housing waiting lists are long, and rough sleeping is rising in the city centre. In <strong>education</strong>, GCSE attainment in deprived wards is well below the national average, and progression to university is far lower than in suburbs like Trafford. In <strong>health</strong>, deprived wards have higher rates of smoking, obesity, diabetes and heart disease — partly because lower incomes mean unhealthier diets and higher chronic stress, partly because GP services are stretched. <strong>Employment</strong> follows the same pattern: even when jobs are growing, those jobs increasingly need degrees.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">When the cotton mills closed in the second half of the twentieth century, Manchester lost tens of thousands of skilled jobs. The replacement economy — finance, media, digital, universities — needs different skills, and the workers laid off in the 1970s and 80s did not always get the chance to retrain. Their children inherited the housing, the schools and the health profile of communities that had been built around the old industries. Geographers call this a <em>cycle of deprivation</em>: low incomes lead to poor housing and poor health, which lead to poor educational outcomes, which lead back to low incomes. Breaking the cycle takes long-term, joined-up investment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Urban Sprawl and the Rural-Urban Fringe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">As Manchester has grown again, the built-up area has spread outwards into the countryside — <dfn class="term" data-def="The unplanned outward spread of a city's built-up area into surrounding rural land, often consuming farmland and green space.">urban sprawl</dfn>. New estates have appeared in Trafford, Wigan and Stockport on what used to be farmland, and <em>commuter villages</em> like Hale and Knutsford have grown rapidly as families seek green space within reach of city jobs. This puts pressure on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The transition zone on the edge of a city where urban land use meets rural land use, often under pressure from new housing and roads.">rural-urban fringe</dfn>: traffic and air pollution rise, hedgerows and woodland are lost, and longer commutes deepen environmental impacts. The conflict between protecting <em>green belt</em> land and building enough new homes is one of the central planning debates in modern Manchester.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Why It Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Migration brings the energy, talent and culture that have remade Manchester — but the same growth has deepened housing pressure and exposed the inequalities that linger from the city’s industrial past. The next lesson looks at how Manchester is responding through urban regeneration, brownfield development and urban greening.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">National migration brings around 100,000 students plus families and professionals into Manchester; international migration has shaped the city for centuries, with around 33% of central residents born outside the UK at the 2021 census.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Migration has created opportunities — cultural mix in Chinatown and the Curry Mile, NHS staffing, vibrant entrepreneurship — but Manchester also has some of England’s most deprived wards (Harpurhey, Gorton, Ardwick).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Inequalities persist in housing, education, health and employment, with a roughly seven-year life-expectancy gap. Urban sprawl into the rural-urban fringe is reshaping commuter villages around Manchester.</li>
</ul>
