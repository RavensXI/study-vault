--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">After the Factories: A Service Economy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">As traditional industries shrank, the UK economy did not collapse — it changed shape. The country became a <dfn class="term" data-def="An economy in which most workers and most output come from service industries rather than manufacturing.">post-industrial economy</dfn>, where most jobs are in services and high-tech research rather than mining, smelting or shipbuilding. Three areas have grown especially fast: information technology, service industries and finance, and research clustered around science and business parks. Together they have created millions of new jobs — though they are not always located where the old jobs disappeared.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three main growth areas of the post-industrial UK economy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK’s post-industrial economy is built on three pillars: information and communication technology (ICT), financial and business services, and research clustered in science and business parks. Together they account for the majority of UK economic output today.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Information Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The ICT industry covers software design, mobile networks, cloud computing, gaming and digital media. The UK has more than 1.7 million ICT jobs and the sector contributes over £150 billion a year to the economy. Major hubs include the M4 corridor west of London (often called ‘Silicon Roundabout’s sibling’), Cambridge and Manchester. ICT is attractive because it requires only modest physical infrastructure compared with heavy industry — offices, fast broadband and skilled workers, rather than coal supplies and railway lines.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Service Industries and Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Banking, insurance, retail, healthcare, education and tourism dominate UK employment. London is one of the world’s top financial centres alongside New York and Tokyo — the City and Canary Wharf alone employ over half a million people in finance. The growth of universities has turned cities like Oxford, Edinburgh and Manchester into major exporters of higher education. The NHS is one of the world’s largest employers with over 1.4 million staff.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Science Parks and Business Parks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A purpose-built area, often linked to a university, where high-tech research and quaternary industries cluster together to share knowledge and skilled workers.">science park</dfn> is a planned cluster of research-led firms, often attached to a university. The Cambridge Science Park, founded by Trinity College in 1970, now hosts over 130 companies in biotechnology, pharmaceuticals and software. Co-locating businesses lets staff share ideas, recruits and equipment — what economists call ‘agglomeration’. Business parks are similar but for tertiary firms (offices, headquarters), usually on city outskirts with good motorway access.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Modern industry can be more environmentally sustainable than the factories of the 1970s. Nissan’s plant in Sunderland is the UK’s largest car factory and produces electric Leaf and Qashqai vehicles. The site is partly powered by an on-site wind farm of ten turbines, captures rainwater for use in production, and reuses solvent from paint shops. Newer manufacturing demonstrates that industry and environmental responsibility can coexist — an idea now spreading to steelworks (electric arc furnaces) and chemical plants (carbon capture).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Rural Areas of Population Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Economic change reshapes the countryside as well as the cities. Some rural areas are gaining population through <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of people from urban to rural areas, often for a quieter lifestyle, while sometimes still working in the city.">counter-urbanisation</dfn>. <strong>South Cambridgeshire</strong> is a good example. Its population has grown by over 20% since 2001 because:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Commuter access:</strong> the area is within 30 minutes of Cambridge and around an hour of London by train, so professionals can live rural while working in the city.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Quality of life:</strong> villages offer cheaper houses than central Cambridge, good schools, less traffic and more open space.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Remote work:</strong> faster broadband and the rise of home-working since 2020 mean fewer journeys are required at all.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The downsides include rising house prices that price out younger locals, traffic on small village roads and pressure on services such as doctors’ surgeries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Rural Areas of Population Decline</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Other rural areas are losing people. The <strong>Outer Hebrides</strong> off the north-west coast of Scotland have seen population fall by over 50% since 1900 — though the recent decline has slowed. Causes include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Lack of jobs:</strong> the local economy depends on fishing, crofting (small-scale farming) and tourism — all seasonal or low-paid.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Out-migration of young people:</strong> teenagers leave for university and rarely return, leaving an ageing population.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Declining services:</strong> as population falls, schools, post offices and bus routes close, making the area less attractive still.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Local authorities in declining rural areas use several strategies. The Outer Hebrides council has invested in faster broadband to attract remote workers, supported small craft and food businesses through grants, and worked with the Scottish government to subsidise ferry routes that keep the islands connected. Affordable housing schemes try to keep young families on the islands rather than losing them to the mainland. Progress is slow, but population decline has flattened compared with earlier decades.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: contrast one growth and one decline rural area, naming each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>South Cambridgeshire (growth, near Cambridge) and the Outer Hebrides (decline, remote north-west Scotland) show how access to jobs, services and city links determines whether a rural area gains or loses people. Rural change is not one trend — it depends on location.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The shift to a post-industrial economy is uneven. Some places — Cambridge, the M4 corridor, central London — have flourished. Others — remote uplands, former mining towns, isolated coastal communities — have not. The next lesson asks why this divide between north and south, and between thriving and struggling regions, is one of the biggest challenges facing the UK economy.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The post-industrial UK economy rests on ICT, financial and service industries, and research clustered in science parks like Cambridge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Modern industry can be more sustainable: Nissan Sunderland uses on-site wind power and water recycling alongside producing electric vehicles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Rural change is uneven — counter-urbanisation has grown places like South Cambridgeshire, while remote areas such as the Outer Hebrides have lost population through job shortages and out-migration.</li>
</ul>
