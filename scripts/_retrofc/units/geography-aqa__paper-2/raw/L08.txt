--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Does ‘Sustainable’ Mean in a City?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Living in a way that meets the needs of the present without damaging the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.">Sustainable urban living</dfn> is about running a city so it works for the people in it today and still works for their grandchildren. UK cities use about three-quarters of the country’s energy and produce most of its waste, so getting urban life right matters for the whole planet. Sustainable cities cut pollution, manage resources carefully and let people move around without choking the air.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four main features of sustainable urban living from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main features of sustainable urban living are: water conservation, energy conservation, waste recycling and creating green space. All four reduce a city’s ecological footprint.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Water Conservation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">UK households use roughly 140 litres of water per person each day. Sustainable cities cut that figure in three ways. Homes are fitted with <dfn class="term" data-def="A meter that records how much water a household uses, so they pay only for what they consume.">water meters</dfn> so families pay for what they use — meters typically cut consumption by around 15%. Low-flush toilets, aerated taps and dual-flush systems lower demand inside the home. Outside, rainwater is harvested from roofs into water butts to water gardens, and grey water from showers is recycled to flush toilets in newer developments.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Energy Conservation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Heating and lighting buildings is the biggest single use of energy in UK cities. Sustainable building reduces this through thick insulation, double or triple glazing, LED lighting and smart meters that show householders what they are spending. New flagship districts such as <dfn class="term" data-def="A sustainable district in south-east London opened in 2002, designed to be carbon-neutral with timber-frame homes, solar panels and shared green spaces.">BedZED</dfn> in south-east London were designed from the start to be carbon-neutral, combining solar panels with super-insulated homes. Across the wider city, solar panels on rooftops and small-scale wind turbines feed renewable electricity into the grid.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">UK cities now recycle just under half of household waste. Kerbside collections separate paper, glass, metals, plastics and food waste so they can be reprocessed instead of buried in <dfn class="term" data-def="A site where rubbish is buried in the ground; landfill produces methane, a powerful greenhouse gas.">landfill</dfn>. Food waste can be sent to anaerobic digesters, which produce biogas to generate electricity. Bristol’s ‘closed-loop’ scheme turns food waste from city schools into compost used on city allotments — the same nutrients re-enter the local food system.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Creating Green Space</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Parks, street trees and roof gardens cool a city by 2–4°C in summer, soak up rainwater to cut flood risk, and absorb carbon dioxide and air pollution. They also boost mental health: studies by the University of Exeter found that people living within sight of green space report lower stress. London’s ‘National Park City’ status, declared in 2019, set a target of making 50% of the capital green by 2050 through new pocket parks, green roofs and tree planting.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three benefits of urban green space without looking back at this section.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Urban green space cools the city, reduces surface flooding, absorbs CO₂ and improves mental health. London aims to become a 50% green city by 2050.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why Traffic Congestion Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="When too many vehicles try to use the same road space, slowing traffic to walking pace.">Traffic congestion</dfn> wastes time, fuel and money — the consultancy INRIX estimated that the average London driver lost roughly 156 hours stuck in jams in 2022. It also causes serious air-quality problems: emissions of nitrogen dioxide and particulates from petrol and diesel engines are linked to thousands of early deaths in UK cities each year. Sustainable transport strategies aim to shift people out of single-occupancy cars and onto cleaner alternatives.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A network where buses, trams, trains and bikes are linked by a single ticketing and timetable system, making it easy to move between modes.">integrated transport system</dfn> joins buses, trains, trams and cycle hire under one ticket, like Manchester’s Bee Network. Park-and-ride sites at the edge of the city let drivers leave cars and ride a frequent shuttle bus into the centre. Congestion charging — London charges £15 a day to enter the central zone — pushes drivers onto public transport. Pedestrianised streets, cycle highways and Low Traffic Neighbourhoods reclaim space for walkers and cyclists.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">London’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A zone where vehicles must meet strict emissions standards or pay a daily charge to drive in; designed to cut roadside air pollution.">Ultra-Low Emission Zone (ULEZ)</dfn> charges £12.50 a day for older, more polluting vehicles. Within two years of expansion, roadside nitrogen-dioxide levels in inner London fell by around 40%. Electric buses and trams produce no roadside pollution at all.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Putting It Together: Curitiba and Freiburg</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Two cities are often used as global models. Curitiba in Brazil pioneered the bus rapid-transit system — long ‘bendy’ buses run in dedicated lanes with raised stations, so 70% of commuters use public transport. Freiburg in Germany combines a dense tram network, car-free residential districts (Vauban) and powerful incentives for solar power; over 25% of the city’s electricity comes from renewables. Both show that joining transport, energy and green space planning together creates a measurably more sustainable city.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The four features of sustainable urban living are water conservation, energy conservation, waste recycling and creating green space — together they cut a city’s ecological footprint.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Smart transport strategies — integrated networks, congestion charging, ULEZ-style emissions zones and electric buses — reduce both traffic congestion and air pollution.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Real schemes prove it works: BedZED (UK), ULEZ in London, Curitiba’s bus rapid-transit and Freiburg’s solar-tram model.</li></ul>
