--- DESCRIPTION ---
Regional inequality, transport investment and the UK's links through trade and culture.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country of Two Halves?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">If you draw a line from the Severn Estuary to the Wash, you separate the UK into a richer south-east and a poorer north and west. Geographers call this the <dfn class="term" data-def="The economic and social divide between the wealthier south-east and the poorer north and west of the United Kingdom.">north–south divide</dfn>. The pattern is not absolute — there are wealthy towns in the north (Harrogate, Cheshire) and pockets of poverty in the south (parts of east London, Plymouth) — but on most measures the gap is real.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three measurable differences that show the north–south divide.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wages in London are around 30% higher than the UK average, while parts of the north-east sit roughly 15% below it. Life expectancy in Glasgow is around 73 for men, compared with 82 in parts of West Sussex — a gap of nine years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why the Divide Exists</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The divide grew partly out of the previous lesson’s story. Deindustrialisation hit the north, the Midlands, south Wales and central Scotland hardest, because that is where coal, steel and shipbuilding were concentrated. The new growth sectors — finance, ICT, biotech — are clustered in London, Cambridge, Oxford and the M4 corridor. London also benefits from being the political and financial capital, attracting head offices, embassies and the highest-paid jobs. Connectivity makes it worse: a train from London to Brighton takes an hour, but Manchester to Leeds (less than half the distance) can take just as long.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">The north–south description is a useful shortcut but hides smaller divides. Some London boroughs (Tower Hamlets, Newham) have child poverty rates above 40%, similar to deprived parts of Liverpool or Middlesbrough. Equally, the ‘Golden Triangle’ of Cambridge, Oxford and London is even richer than the rest of the south. The divide is partly a regional one, partly a city-versus-coalfield one, and partly a global-economy-versus-old-economy one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Strategies to Resolve the Divide</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">UK governments have tried for decades to rebalance the economy. Some of the main strategies:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Northern Powerhouse:</strong> a strategy launched in 2014 to link Manchester, Leeds, Liverpool, Sheffield and Newcastle into a single ‘mega-city region’ with shared transport, science investment and devolved powers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of certain powers from the UK Parliament to lower levels of government, such as Scottish Parliament, Welsh Senedd, or city-region mayors.">Devolution</dfn> deals:</strong> giving city-region mayors (Greater Manchester, West Yorkshire, Liverpool City Region) control over local transport, housing and skills budgets.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Enterprise zones:</strong> areas with tax breaks for new businesses, designed to attract investment to former industrial sites such as Tees Valley.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Levelling Up Fund:</strong> a multi-billion-pound programme of grants for transport, regeneration and cultural projects in towns that have fallen behind.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Transport Investment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Transport projects are central to closing the divide because better connectivity attracts business. Major examples include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Rail upgrades:</strong> the Elizabeth Line (Crossrail) opened in 2022 to speed up east–west journeys across London. The Trans-Pennine Route Upgrade is electrifying the Manchester–Leeds–York line. <strong>HS2</strong> Phase 1 will eventually link London and Birmingham, though northern sections have been scaled back.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Roads:</strong> the A14 Cambridge to Huntingdon improvement (opened 2020) eased a major freight bottleneck. Smart-motorway upgrades have added capacity on parts of the M1, M6 and M62.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Airports and ports:</strong> Heathrow handles around 80 million passengers a year. Manchester is the third-busiest UK airport. Felixstowe handles roughly 40% of UK container traffic, linking the country to Asian and European supply chains.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The UK in the Wider World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The UK is one of the most connected countries on Earth. Its position straddling the Atlantic and Europe, English-speaking workforce and historic global links shape its economy and culture.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Trade:</strong> the UK exports around £700 billion of goods and services a year. The European Union remains its largest trading partner; the United States is the largest single-country market. Other big partners include China, Japan and India.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Culture:</strong> UK music, film, TV (BBC, Netflix UK productions) and football (the Premier League) are watched worldwide. The English language itself is a major export, with overseas students learning it bringing £30 billion+ a year into UK universities and language schools.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Transport links:</strong> Heathrow, the Channel Tunnel and the Port of Felixstowe make the UK a global gateway. Submarine cables across the Atlantic carry the majority of internet traffic between Europe and North America.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Political links:</strong> through the <dfn class="term" data-def="A voluntary association of 56 mostly former British Empire countries, with a combined population of around 2.5 billion.">Commonwealth</dfn>, the United Nations, NATO and trading agreements with the European Union, the UK shapes and is shaped by global decisions.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: name three ways the UK is connected to the wider world.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK’s global links run through trade (EU and US lead), culture (Premier League, BBC, English language teaching), transport (Heathrow, Felixstowe, the Channel Tunnel) and politics (Commonwealth, NATO, UN).</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Even after leaving the European Union in 2020, the UK still does roughly half its trade with EU member states. Geography matters — the EU is the closest large market — and supply chains built up over forty years cannot easily be redirected. The Trade and Cooperation Agreement signed at the end of 2020 keeps tariff-free trade for most goods, though customs checks and paperwork have added cost and delay.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Understanding the divide and the UK’s global links matters because both shape opportunities for ordinary people. A child born in Glasgow has different prospects from one born in Surrey, and a UK exporter relies on Heathrow, the Channel Tunnel and the Port of Felixstowe to reach customers. Closing the divide while staying globally competitive is the central challenge of UK economic policy — and a topic the next two lessons return to from the resource side.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">The north–south divide is a real economic and social gap — wages, life expectancy and opportunities differ — rooted in deindustrialisation in the north and growth of finance and tech in the south-east.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">UK strategies to narrow the gap include the Northern Powerhouse, devolution deals, enterprise zones, the Levelling Up Fund and major transport projects like HS2 and the Trans-Pennine Route Upgrade.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">The UK’s global links span trade (EU and US dominant), culture (Premier League, BBC, English-language education), transport hubs (Heathrow, Felixstowe) and political networks (Commonwealth, NATO, UN).</li>
</ul>
