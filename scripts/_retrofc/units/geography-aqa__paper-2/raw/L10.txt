--- DESCRIPTION ---
Physical, economic and historical drivers — and why disparities cause migration and conflict.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A World That Is Not Flat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Wealth and quality of life are not spread evenly. The richest 10% of the global population owns more than half of all wealth, while billions live on under US$2.15 a day — the World Bank’s 2022 extreme poverty line. <dfn class="term" data-def="The differences in levels of wealth and quality of life between countries and within countries.">Uneven development</dfn> is the result of physical, economic and historical forces working together over decades and centuries, and it has powerful consequences for the people caught on the wrong side of the gap.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three main groups of causes of uneven development.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three main groups of causes of uneven development are physical (climate, location, hazards), economic (trade and debt) and historical (colonialism, conflict). The main consequences are wealth and health gaps and large-scale migration.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Physical Causes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Geography matters. Landlocked countries such as Chad, Mali and Burkina Faso lack ports, so importing and exporting goods is expensive and slow. A harsh climate — whether the heat and aridity of the Sahel or the seasonal rains of Bangladesh — makes farming risky and disease, like malaria, more common. Tropical regions lose roughly 1% of GDP a year to malaria alone. Frequent <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural event such as an earthquake, hurricane, flood or drought that causes loss of life, damage and economic disruption.">natural hazards</dfn> compound the problem: Haiti has been hit by earthquakes, hurricanes and cholera outbreaks, each of which destroys infrastructure faster than it can be rebuilt. Poor soils, deserts and mountains all add to the difficulty.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Economic Causes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Many low-income countries depend on exporting <dfn class="term" data-def="Raw materials such as crops, minerals or oil that are sold without being processed; their prices are usually low and unstable.">primary commodities</dfn> — cocoa, coffee, cotton, copper, oil. World market prices for these goods swing wildly: cocoa farmers receive about 6–10% of the price of a chocolate bar, while the chocolate company keeps most of the rest. Trade rules favour rich countries: they pay low tariffs on raw beans, but high tariffs on processed chocolate, locking poorer countries into low-value exports. Heavy national <dfn class="term" data-def="Money owed by a country, often to richer governments, banks or international institutions; servicing the interest can absorb a large slice of public spending.">debt</dfn> — some HIPCs have spent more on interest payments than on schools and hospitals combined — further drains money out of poorer economies.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">From the 16th century, European powers conquered much of Africa, Asia and the Americas. Under <dfn class="term" data-def="The policy by which one country takes control of another, exploiting its land, people and resources for the benefit of the colonising country.">colonialism</dfn>, raw materials were extracted to feed European factories while local industries were suppressed. Borders were drawn by colonial powers without regard for ethnic groups — in much of Africa this still fuels tension today. After independence, many countries inherited weak institutions, single-crop economies and external debt. Civil wars in places such as the Democratic Republic of Congo and Sierra Leone destroyed decades of progress.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Consequences: Wealth and Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The first consequence of uneven development is dramatic gaps in wealth and health. The average UK citizen lives 26 years longer than a citizen of Sierra Leone. A baby born in Norway has roughly 20 times the lifetime income of a baby born in Burundi. <dfn class="term" data-def="Lack of access to enough nutritious food to lead a healthy life; chronic malnutrition stunts growth and weakens immunity.">Malnutrition</dfn> still affects around 750 million people worldwide. Within countries, the gap can be just as wide: in Brazil, the richest 10% earn 38% of national income, leaving the poorest with very little. These disparities are not just numbers — they shape every part of daily life.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Consequences: International Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When the gap is large enough, people move. <dfn class="term" data-def="Movement of people from one country to another, usually for work, family or to escape danger.">International migration</dfn> is driven by a mix of <dfn class="term" data-def="Reasons that drive people away from a place (e.g. poverty, conflict, drought) and reasons that attract them to a destination (e.g. jobs, safety, family).">push and pull factors</dfn>. Push factors include unemployment, low wages, conflict, drought and poor public services. Pull factors include higher incomes, safer cities, better schools and hospitals, and family already living abroad. In 2022 the UN estimated that 281 million people lived outside their country of birth. Mexico to USA, North Africa to southern Europe, Syria to Germany, and South Asia to the Gulf states are some of the world’s biggest migration streams.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list three push factors and three pull factors that drive international migration.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Push factors driving migration include conflict, drought, low wages and lack of jobs. Pull factors include safer cities, better wages, better schools and existing family abroad.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Source countries often gain from <dfn class="term" data-def="Money sent home by migrants working overseas; remittances are a major source of income in many low-income countries.">remittances</dfn> — in Tajikistan and South Sudan they account for over 30% of GDP. But they also lose skilled workers in a phenomenon called ‘brain drain’ — the Philippines trains nurses who then move to the UK or USA. Host countries gain a younger, working-age population that pays taxes, but face pressure on housing, schools and political tensions over identity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why It Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Uneven development is not just a moral concern — it shapes the global economy, drives mass migration and sustains conflict. Closing the gap means tackling all three causes together: investment in physical infrastructure, fairer trade rules to share the benefits of globalisation, and political stability after a long history of colonialism. The next lesson looks at the strategies designed to do exactly that.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Causes of uneven development fall into three groups: physical (climate, location, hazards), economic (trade, primary commodity dependence, debt) and historical (colonialism, conflict).</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The main consequences are wealth and health disparities — people in richer countries live decades longer — and large-scale international migration.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Migration is shaped by push factors (poverty, conflict) and pull factors (jobs, safety, family); remittances help source countries while host countries gain workers but face pressure on housing and services.</li></ul>
