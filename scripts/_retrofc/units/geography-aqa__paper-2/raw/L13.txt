--- DESCRIPTION ---
How manufacturing, transnational corporations and changing trade links drive Nigerian growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Economy on the Move</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Nigeria’s economy looks completely different to the one that gained independence in 1960. Then, around 70% of workers grew crops on small farms, oil had only just been discovered, and the country exported almost no manufactured goods. Today the share of agriculture has fallen to 22% of GDP, while services account for 53% and industry 25%. Behind that shift lie three forces studied in this lesson: manufacturing, transnational corporations and changing trade links.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the rough share of Nigerian GDP from agriculture, industry and services today.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria’s economy has shifted from mainly farming to a mixed economy. Agriculture is now around 22% of GDP, industry 25% and services 53%. Oil and gas alone provide over 80% of export earnings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Changing Industrial Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Geographers describe an economy by its <dfn class="term" data-def="The mix of primary (raw materials), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary (services) and quaternary (research) industries that make up an economy.">industrial structure</dfn>. As Nigeria has developed, workers have moved out of farming and into manufacturing and services. <dfn class="term" data-def="The making of finished goods from raw materials, usually in factories; also called secondary industry.">Manufacturing</dfn> is critical because it adds value: cocoa beans sold raw earn one price, chocolate bars made in Nigeria earn far more. Factories also create skilled jobs, train workers and pay taxes that fund infrastructure. The Nigerian government has set up Free Trade Zones at Lekki, Calabar and Lagos to attract factories with low taxes and reliable electricity. Major industries now include cement (Dangote Cement is one of Africa’s largest companies), food and drinks, textiles, vehicle assembly and growing pharmaceutical production.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Each new factory creates jobs directly — chefs, drivers, engineers, line workers — but also stimulates the local economy through the <dfn class="term" data-def="When jobs and income created in one industry generate further jobs and spending in suppliers, services and shops nearby.">multiplier effect</dfn>. A cement plant near Ibadan, for example, creates demand for transport firms, food sellers, mechanics and small shops. Tax revenue funds schools and hospitals. Skilled workers can move on to other industries, raising the country’s overall productivity. This is the engine of NEE growth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Transnational Corporations (TNCs)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A company with operations in more than one country, often headquartered in a HIC and producing or selling goods in many others; abbreviated TNC.">transnational corporation (TNC)</dfn> is a firm with operations in more than one country. Nigeria hosts dozens, including Shell, ExxonMobil, Chevron and TotalEnergies in oil; MTN (a South African TNC) in telecoms; Unilever in soaps and food; and Cadbury in confectionery. They are attracted by oil reserves, a vast consumer market and English as the language of business.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Advantages and Disadvantages of TNCs to Nigeria</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Advantages:</strong> TNCs bring foreign investment that the government cannot raise alone. Shell’s operations in the Niger Delta employ around 6,000 Nigerians directly and many more through contractors. TNCs train local engineers and managers, build schools and clinics under corporate social responsibility programmes, and pay taxes that fund the federal budget. Telecoms TNCs like MTN have brought mobile phones to over 200 million Nigerians, transforming banking and small business.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Profits often leave Nigeria for the TNC’s home country. TNCs may impose poor working conditions or pay low wages. Worst is the environmental damage: Shell, Chevron and other oil companies have spilled millions of litres of crude oil into the Niger Delta over decades, killing fisheries. Tax avoidance through transfer pricing is common. Decisions about Nigerian jobs are often made in London, Houston or Johannesburg.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Changing Political and Trading Relationships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For most of the 20th century Nigeria’s biggest trading partners were the UK and other former colonial powers. That has changed. Today the largest single buyer of Nigerian crude oil is India. China has become the largest source of imports, providing machinery, vehicles and electronics, and is funding major infrastructure such as the Lagos–Ibadan railway through <dfn class="term" data-def="Investment that one country makes in another by building factories or buying companies, rather than just lending money or trading.">foreign direct investment</dfn>. The European Union remains an important market for Nigerian agricultural products. Nigeria’s membership of <dfn class="term" data-def="The African Continental Free Trade Area; a 2021 agreement to remove tariffs on most goods between African countries, creating the world's largest free-trade zone by membership.">AfCFTA</dfn> from 2021 means tariffs are being removed on most goods traded between African countries.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name Nigeria's largest trading partners today and one project funded by Chinese investment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria’s top trading partner for imports is China; its biggest oil customer is India. China has funded major projects like the Lagos–Ibadan railway. AfCFTA (since 2021) is opening up trade with the rest of Africa.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Putting the Pieces Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Nigeria’s economy works because manufacturing, TNCs and trade are now reinforcing each other. Dangote Cement, a Nigerian-owned firm, has expanded across Africa using export earnings. International TNCs supply technology and capital. Free Trade Zones attract both. New trade links with China and India have replaced reliance on former colonial markets. The challenges — oil dependence, environmental damage in the Delta, regional inequality — are exactly what the next lesson on aid and quality of life looks at.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Nigeria’s industrial structure has shifted from agriculture (now ~22% of GDP) towards manufacturing and services; manufacturing adds value, creates jobs and triggers a multiplier effect.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">TNCs like Shell, MTN and Unilever bring investment, jobs and technology, but profits often leave the country and oil firms have caused major pollution in the Niger Delta.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Trading partnerships have shifted from former colonial powers to China (largest source of imports), India (biggest oil buyer) and other African states through AfCFTA from 2021.</li></ul>
