--- DESCRIPTION ---
How urban planning and community-led projects raise quality of life for the urban poor.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Approaches to Improving Lagos</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Improving life for the urban poor in a city growing this fast needs more than one strategy. Lagos uses two main approaches. <dfn class="term" data-def="Government-led action to manage and improve city growth, including transport, housing and infrastructure projects.">Top-down planning</dfn> is led by the government and can deliver large schemes such as roads and railways. <dfn class="term" data-def="Community-led projects in which residents organise to improve their own neighbourhoods, often with NGO support.">Self-help schemes</dfn> are bottom-up — residents and NGOs work together to build, fix and run things themselves. The strongest improvements usually come where the two work side by side.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Makoko Floating School</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In a slum like Makoko, where every spare metre of dry land is built on, a school for local children seemed almost impossible. In 2013 Nigerian architect <dfn class="term" data-def="The Nigerian architect who designed the Makoko Floating School, a triangular wooden classroom block floating on plastic barrels.">Kunlé Adeyemi</dfn> proposed a radical answer: a school that floated on the lagoon itself. The Makoko Floating School was a triangular three-storey wooden building set on 256 empty plastic barrels, with a green roof, solar panels and a rainwater collection system. It cost around £5,000 to build and could host 100 children.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Crucially, it was a project <em>with</em> the Makoko community rather than imposed on it. Local fishermen helped tow it into place, residents helped run it, and the school used materials that had been thrown away — the barrels were salvaged. It became a global symbol of self-help architecture for slum communities.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three sustainable features of the Makoko Floating School and the architect who designed it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Makoko Floating School was designed by Nigerian architect Kunlé Adeyemi in 2013. It used 256 plastic barrels for buoyancy, solar panels for power and rainwater harvesting for water — and was built with the Makoko community.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">In 2016 a heavy storm caused the original school to collapse. Critics said it showed self-help architecture had limits; supporters argued the design had always been a prototype. Adeyemi’s firm went on to develop a stronger second-generation version called <em>MFS II</em>, which was exhibited internationally. The lesson is honest: small, low-cost projects can deliver huge symbolic and practical wins, but they need long-term funding and engineering support to last. Self-help is powerful, but it works best alongside well-resourced top-down planning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Top-Down Transport: BRT and the Lagos Rail</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Lagos State Government has invested heavily in transport to ease the ‘go-slow’. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A bus-based public transport system using dedicated lanes to bypass traffic, faster and cheaper to build than a metro.">Bus Rapid Transit (BRT)</dfn> system opened in 2008 and now runs along several main corridors. BRT buses use lanes separated from general traffic, so they keep moving while cars sit in jams. Fares are low enough for low-income workers, and journey times have been cut by up to half on some routes. The Lagos Rail Mass Transit project — the city’s first mass-transit railway — opened its first line in 2023, designed to move tens of thousands more commuters off the roads each day.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Improving Water and Sanitation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Lagos Water Corporation has expanded the mains network and partnered with NGOs to install community boreholes in slum districts — tens of thousands of residents now have safer water than they did a decade ago. NGOs such as <strong>WaterAid</strong> have built communal toilet blocks in slum areas, with female-only sections to improve safety. Public-health teams run free vaccination campaigns and educate parents about oral rehydration to limit cholera outbreaks. None of these are total solutions, but each one improves life for thousands.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Eko Atlantic and Resilience to Flooding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A second top-down scheme is <strong>Eko Atlantic City</strong>. It is a new district built on land reclaimed from the Atlantic next to Victoria Island. The project includes a 8-kilometre sea wall called the <em>Great Wall of Lagos</em>, designed to protect the city from rising sea levels and the storm surges that already flood low-lying neighbourhoods every rainy season. Eko Atlantic adds high-end housing, offices and public space — but critics argue it serves the wealthy first, with little direct benefit for the slum residents who suffer most when the city floods.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what is the Great Wall of Lagos, and what is its purpose for the city as a whole?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eko Atlantic City is built on land reclaimed from the Atlantic. Its 8 km sea wall, the Great Wall of Lagos, is designed to protect Victoria Island and central Lagos from sea-level rise and storm surges.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Self-Help: Energy and Recycling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Bottom-up energy projects are spreading across slum districts. NGOs such as <strong>Solar Sister</strong> train local women to sell low-cost solar lanterns; households that once relied on smoky kerosene now have clean light for evening homework. Community recycling co-operatives buy plastic bottles and aluminium cans from street pickers, then sell them on to industrial buyers — cleaning up streets and creating informal incomes at the same time. These schemes do not solve the city’s waste crisis on their own, but they demonstrate something important: when residents are paid to recycle, the streets get cleaner.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Government planners often want quick, dramatic results — a flyover, a new sea wall, a tower block. Residents want practical day-to-day fixes — cleaner water, a safer toilet, a school close to home. The most successful Lagos projects combine both. Eko Atlantic protects central Lagos from flooding, but only the BRT, community boreholes and projects like the Makoko Floating School reach families in places like Makoko itself. Top-down delivers the scale; self-help delivers the relevance.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why It Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Improving the quality of life for the urban poor is the central challenge of every fast-growing megacity. Lagos shows that progress is possible — one community borehole, one BRT lane, one floating school at a time — but also that the scale of the problem demands sustained government investment alongside community-led solutions. The next lesson shifts the lens to a UK city, where the challenges look very different.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Lagos uses both top-down planning (BRT, Lagos Rail, Eko Atlantic City and the Great Wall of Lagos) and bottom-up self-help (Makoko Floating School, Solar Sister, community recycling).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Makoko Floating School (2013, Kunlé Adeyemi) used 256 plastic barrels, solar panels and rainwater harvesting to give Makoko children a classroom on the lagoon — built with the community.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The most effective improvements combine large infrastructure with community-led action; top-down delivers scale, self-help delivers relevance to slum residents.</li>
</ul>
