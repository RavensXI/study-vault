--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Ghost of Christmas Past shows Scrooge his lost happiness with Fezziwig and Belle.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Ghost of Christmas Past</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">In Stave 2, Dickens introduces the first of the three Christmas spirits who will guide Scrooge through his redemption. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A supernatural being representing memories and the innocence of childhood.">Ghost of Christmas Past</dfn> appears as a figure that shifts between youth and age, symbolising how memories can make the past feel both distant and immediate.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what springs from the Ghost's head and what this light represents about memories and hidden truths."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The Ghost appears with “a bright clear jet of light” springing from its head, representing how memories can illuminate truths about ourselves that we prefer to keep hidden.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n4">Dickens deliberately makes the spirit’s appearance ambiguous — it has the face of a child yet white hair, and fluctuates between light and shadow. This reflects how our memories of the past can be both clear and uncertain, joyful and painful.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n5">Scrooge’s Childhood: Lost Innocence</h2><p data-narration-id="n6">The ghost first takes Scrooge to his childhood school, where we see him as a lonely boy left behind during Christmas holidays. Dickens writes: “The school is not quite deserted... A solitary child, neglected by his friends, is left there still.”</p><p data-narration-id="n7">This scene reveals the origins of Scrooge’s isolation. His abandonment as a child planted the seeds of his adult misanthropy. However, Dickens also shows young Scrooge’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to use imagination to escape difficult realities.">escapism</dfn> through books, mentioning characters like Ali Baba and Robinson Crusoe.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>The Significance of Literary Characters</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n8">Dickens’ choice of Ali Baba and Robinson Crusoe is significant. Both are stories of isolation and eventual rescue or triumph. This suggests that even as a lonely child, Scrooge had hope and imagination — qualities he has since buried under years of bitterness.</p><p data-narration-id="n9">The fact that Scrooge weeps when seeing his younger self shows these qualities still exist within him, waiting to be reawakened.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n10">Fan: The Power of Love and Loss</h2><p data-narration-id="n11">The ghost then shows Scrooge his sister Fan arriving to take him home for Christmas. Fan represents pure, unconditional love in Scrooge’s life. Dickens describes her as having “a large heart” and being “quite a woman” despite her small stature.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain how Fan died, who she gave birth to, and why this makes Scrooge resent his nephew Fred."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Fan died young, leaving Fred motherless. Dickens does not reveal the cause of her death, but her loss shapes Scrooge’s complicated feelings towards Fred — he pushes away the last living connection to the sister he loved.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n13">The scene with Fan shows Scrooge’s capacity for joy and love. When she arrives, young Scrooge is transformed: “his face began to wear the signs of care and avarice” disappear. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic change in character or circumstances.">transformation</dfn> proves that Scrooge’s miserly nature is learned, not innate.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n14">Fezziwig’s Party: The Value of Human Connection</h2><p data-narration-id="n15">The ghost next shows Scrooge as a young apprentice at Fezziwig’s warehouse. Old Fezziwig throws a Christmas party that costs little money but brings immense joy to his employees and their families.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Fezziwig as Scrooge’s Foil</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n16">Fezziwig represents everything Scrooge has become the opposite of. Where Scrooge hoards money, Fezziwig shares joy. Where Scrooge isolates himself, Fezziwig brings people together. Where Scrooge sees Christmas as a burden, Fezziwig sees it as an opportunity for generosity.</p><p data-narration-id="n17">Importantly, Fezziwig is also a businessman, proving that commercial success and human kindness can coexist — a lesson Scrooge has forgotten.</p></div></div></div><p data-narration-id="n18">During the party, young Scrooge dances and laughs, showing his natural capacity for happiness. Dickens emphasises that “his heart and soul were in the scene.” This memory forces present-day Scrooge to confront what he has lost through his obsession with wealth.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n19">Belle: The Price of Greed</h2><p data-narration-id="n20">The most painful memory the ghost reveals is Scrooge’s broken engagement to Belle. She releases him from their promise because “another idol has displaced me... a golden one.” This scene shows the exact moment when Scrooge chose money over love.</p><p data-narration-id="n21">Belle’s words are prophetic: “May you be happy in the life you have chosen.” The irony is clear — Scrooge’s pursuit of wealth has brought him nothing but misery. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of words to suggest the opposite of their literal meaning.">irony</dfn> is emphasised when the ghost shows Scrooge Belle’s happy family life with another man.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Belle’s Family: What Scrooge Lost</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n22">The vision of Belle’s family is deliberately painful for Scrooge. Dickens shows her surrounded by children, warmth, and love — everything Scrooge sacrificed for money. Her husband mentions Scrooge, noting he is “quite alone in the world,” which forces Scrooge to see himself as others see him.</p><p data-narration-id="n23">This scene demonstrates the consequences of Scrooge’s choices. While Belle found happiness through love and family, Scrooge found only isolation through greed.</p></div></div></div><p data-narration-id="n24">Unable to bear any more painful memories, Scrooge extinguishes the ghost’s light by pressing its cap down upon its head. This action symbolises his attempt to suppress painful truths about himself, but Dickens makes clear that the light cannot be completely extinguished — it continues to stream from beneath the cap.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n27">Stave 2 reveals that Scrooge’s miserly nature developed gradually through childhood abandonment and the loss of loved ones</li><li data-narration-id="n28">Dickens uses memory to show Scrooge’s capacity for joy, love, and redemption still exists beneath his bitter exterior</li><li data-narration-id="n29">The contrast between characters like Fezziwig and Belle versus present-day Scrooge highlights the moral choices that shaped his isolation</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---

Look for moments where Scrooge shows emotion (crying, anger, regret) as evidence that his redemption is possible. Remember to quote specific phrases like “golden idol” and “quite alone in the world” to support points about how greed has isolated him.</p>
