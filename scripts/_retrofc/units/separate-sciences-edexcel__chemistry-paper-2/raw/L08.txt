--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Natural Resources and Sustainability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Humans use the Earth&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="Materials from the Earth used by humans, including water, minerals, fossil fuels and the atmosphere; some are finite, others are renewable.">natural resources</dfn> to meet needs for warmth, shelter, food, and transport. Some resources are <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources used faster than they can be replaced, which will eventually run out, such as fossil fuels and metal ores.">finite</dfn> &mdash; they are being consumed faster than nature can replace them. Others are <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources replaced at the same rate as they are used, so they will not run out, such as timber, cotton, and solar energy.">renewable</dfn> &mdash; they replenish naturally and will not run out if managed carefully.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Development that meets the needs of today&rsquo;s people without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.">Sustainable development</dfn> means meeting present needs without preventing future generations from meeting theirs. Chemistry contributes by finding alternatives to finite resources (biofuels, bioplastics), improving recycling, and designing more efficient processes that generate less waste.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall two examples each of finite and renewable resources, then define sustainable development in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Finite resources (e.g. fossil fuels, metal ores) will eventually run out. Renewable resources (e.g. timber, cotton, solar energy) replenish naturally. Sustainable development balances today&rsquo;s needs with preserving resources for future generations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Potable Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Water safe to drink; not the same as pure water, as it still contains low levels of dissolved substances, but free from harmful microorganisms.">Potable water</dfn> is safe to drink. It is not pure water (which contains only H<sub>2</sub>O); it may contain low levels of dissolved minerals but must be free from harmful microorganisms and toxic substances. In the United Kingdom, most potable water comes from fresh water sources &mdash; rivers and reservoirs &mdash; and is treated before distribution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The treatment of fresh water involves three main steps:</p>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sedimentation:</strong> the water is allowed to stand in settlement tanks. Larger particles and fine debris sink to the bottom as sediment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Filtration:</strong> the water passes through filter beds of sand and gravel to remove smaller suspended particles, producing clear water.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Chlorination:</strong> a controlled amount of chlorine is added to kill bacteria and other pathogens, making the water safe to drink.</li>
</ol>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the three stages of UK potable water treatment in order, describing what each stage removes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK potable water treatment: (1) sedimentation &mdash; particles settle; (2) filtration through sand/gravel &mdash; removes suspended particles; (3) chlorination &mdash; kills bacteria. The water is safe to drink but is not chemically pure.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Desalination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In regions where fresh water is scarce, <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing dissolved salts from sea water or salty water to produce potable water, using distillation or reverse osmosis.">desalination</dfn> is used to convert sea water into potable water. Two main methods are used:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Distillation:</strong> sea water is boiled; the steam (water vapour) condenses and is collected as pure water, leaving the salts behind. This requires large amounts of energy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Reverse osmosis:</strong> sea water is forced under high pressure through a membrane that blocks salt ions but allows water molecules through. This also requires significant energy input.</li>
</ul>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">Desalination removes dissolved salts from sea water &mdash; a much more energy-intensive process. However, for countries with limited rainfall and no major rivers (e.g. parts of the Middle East), desalination is essential. The high energy cost also means higher carbon emissions unless renewable energy sources are used.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Waste Water Treatment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Used water from homes, hospitals, and industry is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Water that has been used and contaminated by domestic, industrial, or agricultural activity and must be treated before release.">waste water</dfn> (sewage). It must be treated before being returned to rivers or the sea. The main stages of sewage treatment are: (1) <strong>screening</strong> &mdash; large solids are removed by metal screens; (2) <strong>sedimentation</strong> &mdash; fine suspended solids settle as sludge; (3) <strong>biological treatment</strong> &mdash; aerobic bacteria break down organic matter in the liquid effluent; (4) <strong>anaerobic digestion of sludge</strong> &mdash; the separated sludge is digested by bacteria in the absence of oxygen, releasing biogas (mainly methane), which can be used as a fuel.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Recycling and Waste Reduction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="Collecting and reprocessing waste materials to produce new products, conserving finite resources and reducing landfill.">Recycling</dfn> conserves finite resources by recovering materials from waste products rather than extracting new raw materials. It generally requires less energy than extracting and processing virgin materials, and it reduces the volume of waste sent to landfill.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">However, recycling is not without cost. Collection, sorting, transport, and reprocessing all require energy and produce some waste. Some materials (such as mixed plastics or composite materials) are difficult to recycle cost-effectively. Despite these challenges, recycling plays a vital role in sustainable development &mdash; particularly for metals such as aluminium, where the energy saving over primary production is very large compared to extracting fresh aluminium from its ore by electrolysis.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Life-Cycle Assessment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An assessment of the environmental impact of a product across its entire lifetime: raw material extraction, manufacture, use, and disposal or recycling.">life-cycle assessment</dfn> (LCA) evaluates the environmental impact of a product at every stage of its life:</p>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Extracting and processing raw materials</strong> &mdash; energy used, land disturbed, waste generated.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Manufacturing and packaging</strong> &mdash; energy and chemicals consumed in production.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Using the product</strong> &mdash; energy consumed during use; repairs and maintenance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>End-of-life disposal</strong> &mdash; whether the product goes to landfill, is incinerated (releasing CO<sub>2</sub>), or is recycled.</li>
</ol>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>LCA: Limitations and Subjectivity</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29">LCAs involve value judgements, making them potentially subjective. Different companies or researchers may choose to include different stages, use different data sources, or weight environmental impacts differently. For example, a manufacturer might minimise the extraction stage in their own product&rsquo;s LCA but scrutinise it heavily for a competitor.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">LCA results are most reliable when conducted by independent bodies using agreed methodologies. Students should note that LCA data can be presented selectively and should evaluate the source of any LCA before accepting its conclusions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four stages of a life-cycle assessment in order, with one example of an environmental impact at each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An LCA covers four stages: (1) raw material extraction; (2) manufacturing and packaging; (3) use of the product; (4) disposal (landfill, incineration, or recycling). LCAs can be subjective depending on what data is included and how impacts are weighted.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Finite resources (fossil fuels, metal ores) will run out; renewable resources replenish naturally. Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">UK potable water: sedimentation &rarr; filtration &rarr; chlorination. Desalination (distillation or reverse osmosis) converts sea water to potable water but requires significantly more energy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">A life-cycle assessment evaluates environmental impact across extraction, manufacture, use, and disposal. Recycling reduces use of finite resources but LCAs can be subjective; evaluate the source before accepting conclusions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">Questions on potable water treatment often ask you to &lsquo;describe&rsquo; or &lsquo;explain&rsquo; the stages. Make sure you give them in order (sedimentation &rarr; filtration &rarr; chlorination) and state what each stage <em>does</em>, not just what it is called. For LCA evaluation questions (6 marks), the top band requires you to comment on the <em>limitations</em> of LCAs (subjectivity, selective data) as well as their usefulness &mdash; a one-sided answer capping the mark at Secure. For &lsquo;compare desalination with UK fresh water treatment&rsquo; questions, always mention energy cost as the key distinguishing factor.</p>
