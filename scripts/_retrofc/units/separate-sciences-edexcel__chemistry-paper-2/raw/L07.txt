<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Nanoparticles?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Particles with dimensions in the range 1&ndash;100 nanometres; a nanometre is one billionth of a metre (10⁻⁹ m).">Nanoparticles</dfn> are structures that measure between 1 and 100 nanometres (nm) in at least one dimension. One nanometre is 10<sup>-9</sup> m &mdash; far smaller than a human hair (~80 000 nm wide). By comparison, fine particles (used in older applications such as talcum powder) are typically 100 nm&ndash;2500 nm, and bulk materials are larger still.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Nanoparticles are not a new phenomenon: soot from candles and carbon black in ancient ink contain nanoparticles. What is new is the ability to engineer them precisely and exploit their unusual properties.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the size range of nanoparticles in nm, then compare with the width of a human hair and a fine particle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles: 1&ndash;100 nm. Fine particles: 100&ndash;2500 nm. Bulk materials: larger. A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. The extremely small size of nanoparticles gives them unique properties not seen in the same material in bulk form.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Surface Area to Volume Ratio</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The key reason nanoparticles behave differently from bulk materials is their very high <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of a particle&rsquo;s surface area to its volume; increases dramatically as particle size decreases.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn>. As a particle is made smaller, the proportion of atoms on the surface compared to those in the interior increases dramatically. Surface atoms are more reactive because they have unsatisfied bonds &mdash; so a higher surface area to volume ratio means greater chemical reactivity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Imagine cutting a large cube into tiny cubes: the total surface area increases while the total volume stays the same. This principle is why nanoparticles of gold, silver, and titanium dioxide behave very differently from the same substances in their visible (bulk) forms.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">A cube of side 4 cm: surface area = 6 &times; 4<sup>2</sup> = 96 cm<sup>2</sup>; volume = 4<sup>3</sup> = 64 cm<sup>3</sup>; ratio = 96/64 = 1.5 cm<sup>-1</sup></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Split into 64 cubes of side 1 cm each: total surface area = 64 &times; 6 = 384 cm<sup>2</sup>; total volume = 64 cm<sup>3</sup>; ratio = 384/64 = 6 cm<sup>-1</sup> &mdash; four times larger. Reducing particle size dramatically increases surface area to volume ratio, explaining why nanoparticles are much more reactive than bulk material.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Applications of Nanoparticles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Nanoparticles are exploited across many fields because of their enhanced properties:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Sunscreen:</strong> titanium dioxide and zinc oxide nanoparticles absorb and scatter ultraviolet radiation more effectively than bulk particles of the same materials. Their tiny size also makes them transparent on skin rather than leaving a white film.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Medicine:</strong> nanoparticles can be engineered to carry drugs directly to cancer cells, reducing side effects compared with conventional chemotherapy. Silver nanoparticles have antimicrobial properties and are incorporated into wound dressings and clothing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Electronics:</strong> nanoparticles enable smaller, faster components. Carbon nanotubes have very high tensile strength and electrical conductivity, making them valuable in advanced electronics and structural materials.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Catalysis:</strong> nanoparticle catalysts (e.g. platinum nanoparticles in catalytic converters) provide a much larger surface area for reactions to occur, improving efficiency.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Risks and the Precautionary Principle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The same properties that make nanoparticles useful also raise concerns. Their tiny size means they can penetrate biological barriers &mdash; entering cells, crossing the blood&ndash;brain barrier, or accumulating in organs &mdash; in ways that larger particles of the same material cannot. The long-term health effects are still being researched, and some nanoparticles have been shown to cause inflammation or cellular damage in laboratory studies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Because the full risks are not yet understood, scientists and regulators apply the <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach to risk management: where evidence of harm is uncertain, action should be taken to protect people and the environment.">precautionary principle</dfn>: taking protective measures before harms are proven, rather than waiting for certainty. This means using nanoparticles only where benefits clearly outweigh the risks, and continuing active research.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why nanoparticles may be harmful in ways that bulk particles of the same substance are not, using the idea of surface area.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles can cross biological membranes due to their tiny size, potentially causing harm. The precautionary principle says: act cautiously when risks are uncertain. Their high surface area also makes them more chemically reactive than bulk particles of the same substance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Properties of Glass and Ceramics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="An amorphous (non-crystalline) solid made by melting silicon dioxide (sand) with other metal oxides; transparent and hard but brittle.">Soda glass</dfn> is made by melting silicon dioxide (sand) with sodium carbonate and limestone. It is transparent, hard, and chemically resistant, but brittle and has a relatively low melting point. <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of glass with added boron trioxide that has a higher melting point and greater thermal shock resistance than soda glass.">Borosilicate glass</dfn> contains boron trioxide, giving it a much higher melting point and resistance to thermal shock &mdash; it is used in laboratory glassware and ovenproof dishes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="Hard, brittle materials made by heating clay or other minerals to high temperatures; examples include porcelain and bricks.">Clay ceramics</dfn> such as pottery and bricks are made by shaping clay and firing it at high temperatures. They are very hard, brittle, and chemically inert. Their crystalline structure makes them good thermal and electrical insulators.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Metals, Alloys and Composites</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Pure metals have regular, closely packed crystal structures, allowing layers of atoms to slide over each other &mdash; making them malleable. Adding another element creates an <dfn class="term" data-def="A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, designed to have improved properties such as greater hardness or corrosion resistance.">alloy</dfn>, where the different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers, making it harder for them to slide. This is why alloys are generally stronger and harder than pure metals. Steel (iron + carbon), brass (copper + zinc), and bronze (copper + tin) are common examples.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A material made of two or more constituent materials that together have better properties than either alone; e.g. fibreglass (glass fibres in a polymer matrix).">composite material</dfn> combines materials so that each contributes its best property. Glass-fibre-reinforced polymer (GFRP, or fibreglass) combines glass fibres (stiff and strong) embedded in a polymer resin (lightweight). Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) uses carbon fibres, giving even higher stiffness and strength at low mass. Both are used in sports equipment, aircraft bodies, and boat hulls where a high strength-to-weight ratio is needed.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing GFRP and CFRP</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">GFRP (fibreglass): glass fibres in a polymer matrix. Strong, lightweight, corrosion-resistant, and relatively cheap. Used in boat hulls, car body panels, and insulation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">CFRP (carbon fibre): carbon fibres in a polymer matrix. Stronger and stiffer than GFRP, with a lower density, but significantly more expensive to produce. Used in racing car chassis, aerospace components, and high-performance sports equipment (bicycles, golf clubs).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">The key principle for both: the composite exploits the stiffness of the fibres and the toughness of the polymer matrix, achieving properties neither material has on its own.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Nanoparticles are 1&ndash;100 nm in size. Their very high surface area to volume ratio makes them more reactive than bulk materials, enabling uses in sunscreen, medicine, electronics, and catalysis &mdash; but also raising health concerns addressed by the precautionary principle.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Glass (soda and borosilicate) and clay ceramics are hard, brittle, and chemically resistant. Alloys are harder than pure metals because different-sized atoms disrupt the regular crystal layers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Composite materials like GFRP and CFRP combine fibres and polymer matrices to produce materials with superior strength-to-weight ratios, suitable for aerospace, marine, and sports applications.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">For questions about nanoparticles, always link their properties to surface area to volume ratio &mdash; this is the examiner&rsquo;s expected mechanism. If asked to &lsquo;explain why nanoparticles are more reactive,&rsquo; you must say &lsquo;higher surface area to volume ratio, so more atoms are exposed on the surface.&rsquo; For material selection questions (4&ndash;6 marks), compare two materials explicitly: name a property of each, explain why that property matters for the application, and state which is better suited. Avoid vague answers like &lsquo;stronger&rsquo; &mdash; say <em>what</em> it is stronger than and why that matters.</p>
