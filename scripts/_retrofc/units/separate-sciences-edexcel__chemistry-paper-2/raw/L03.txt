Fractional distillation, alkanes and alkenes, combustion, cracking and atmospheric pollutants.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Crude Oil and Hydrocarbons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A finite natural resource formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms. It is a mixture of many different hydrocarbon compounds.">Crude oil</dfn> is a finite resource that took millions of years to form from the compressed remains of ancient marine organisms. It is a <strong>mixture</strong> of many different <dfn class="term" data-def="Organic molecules made up of hydrogen and carbon atoms only.">hydrocarbons</dfn> &mdash; molecules containing only hydrogen and carbon. Because crude oil is a mixture rather than a pure compound, its components can be separated without a chemical reaction.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Fractional Distillation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Crude oil is separated into useful <strong>fractions</strong> (groups of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points) by <dfn class="term" data-def="A physical separation process in which crude oil is heated until it vaporises, and different fractions condense at different levels of a tall column based on their boiling points.">fractional distillation</dfn>. The crude oil is heated in a furnace until most of it vaporises. The vapour enters a tall fractionating column that is hot at the bottom (&gt;350&deg;C) and cool at the top (&lt;25&deg;C). Hydrocarbons condense when they reach their boiling point:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Top of column:</strong> petrol (gasoline) and gases &mdash; small molecules, low boiling point.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Middle:</strong> kerosene (aviation fuel), diesel.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Bottom:</strong> heavy fuel oil and bitumen &mdash; large molecules, very high boiling point.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: do smaller hydrocarbons have higher or lower boiling points, and are they collected at the top or bottom of the column?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In fractional distillation, smaller hydrocarbons have lower boiling points and are collected near the top of the column. They are also less viscous (flow more easily) and more flammable. Larger hydrocarbons have higher boiling points, greater viscosity, and are less flammable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Alkanes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Most hydrocarbons in crude oil are <dfn class="term" data-def="A family of hydrocarbons with only single C–C bonds. Their general formula is CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. They are described as saturated because they contain no C=C double bonds.">alkanes</dfn>. They have the general formula \(C_nH_{2n+2}\) and are described as <strong>saturated</strong> because they contain only single C&mdash;C bonds. The first four alkanes are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n11">Methane: \(CH_4\) (natural gas)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12">Ethane: \(C_2H_6\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13">Propane: \(C_3H_8\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14">Butane: \(C_4H_{10}\)</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n15">As the carbon chain length increases, boiling point and viscosity <strong>increase</strong>, while flammability <strong>decreases</strong>. These trends are caused by stronger <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between molecules. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, giving them higher boiling points and greater viscosity.">intermolecular forces</dfn> between larger molecules.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Combustion of Hydrocarbons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">When hydrocarbons burn in a plentiful supply of oxygen, <dfn class="term" data-def="The complete burning of a hydrocarbon in excess oxygen, producing only carbon dioxide and water.">complete combustion</dfn> occurs, producing only carbon dioxide and water:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">$$C_nH_{2n+2} + \frac{3n+1}{2}O_2 \rightarrow nCO_2 + (n+1)H_2O$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">For example, for methane: \(CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O\). This releases large amounts of energy, making hydrocarbons valuable fuels.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">When oxygen supply is limited, <dfn class="term" data-def="The burning of a hydrocarbon in a limited oxygen supply, producing carbon monoxide and/or soot (carbon particles) instead of carbon dioxide.">incomplete combustion</dfn> occurs, producing toxic <strong>carbon monoxide</strong> (CO) and/or <strong>carbon particles</strong> (soot). Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless but dangerously toxic &mdash; it binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, preventing oxygen transport.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Burning hydrocarbon fuels also releases other pollutants:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Sulfur dioxide (SO<sub>2</sub>):</strong> produced when sulfur impurities in fuels burn. Dissolves in rainwater to form <em>acid rain</em>, which damages buildings, soils, and aquatic ecosystems.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ):</strong> formed when nitrogen and oxygen in the air react at the high temperatures inside engines. Also contribute to acid rain and the formation of photochemical smog.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Carbon dioxide:</strong> a greenhouse gas &mdash; its increasing concentration in the atmosphere contributes to global warming.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three pollutants from burning hydrocarbon fuels, naming one health effect and one environmental effect for any two.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide (toxic) and soot. Sulfur impurities produce SO<sub>2</sub> (acid rain). High-temperature combustion produces nitrogen oxides (acid rain, smog). Carbon dioxide causes global warming.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Cracking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="A process in which large hydrocarbon molecules are broken down into smaller, more useful molecules by passing them over a hot catalyst or by thermal cracking at very high temperatures.">Cracking</dfn> is used to break long-chain hydrocarbons (plentiful but less useful) into shorter-chain hydrocarbons that are in higher demand. Cracking always produces at least one <dfn class="term" data-def="A hydrocarbon with one or more C=C double bonds. Described as unsaturated. General formula CₙH₂ₙ. Alkenes are more reactive than alkanes and are used to make polymers.">alkene</dfn> &mdash; a hydrocarbon with a C=C double bond. Alkenes have the general formula \(C_nH_{2n}\).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Catalytic Cracking vs Thermal Cracking</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Catalytic cracking:</strong> long-chain hydrocarbons are vaporised and passed over a heated aluminium oxide (or zeolite) catalyst at around 500&deg;C. The catalyst lowers the activation energy and allows cracking at a lower temperature. Produces mainly branched-chain alkanes and aromatic compounds, useful for petrol.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Thermal cracking (steam cracking):</strong> carried out at very high temperatures (around 850&deg;C) and high pressure using steam. Produces a higher proportion of alkenes (especially ethene, \(C_2H_4\)), which are used as monomers to make plastics and polymers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">A simple example of cracking: a long alkane such as decane can be cracked into octane (a petrol component) and ethene: \(C_{10}H_{22} \rightarrow C_8H_{18} + C_2H_4\).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Testing for Alkenes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Alkenes can be distinguished from alkanes using bromine water. Alkenes <strong>decolourise</strong> bromine water (orange to colourless) because the C=C double bond reacts with bromine in an addition reaction. Alkanes do not react with bromine water under normal conditions, so the orange colour remains.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n38">Exam Focus: Fractions and Cracking</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n39">When answering questions on fractional distillation, remember that shorter hydrocarbon chains have lower boiling points, are more volatile, flow more easily, and ignite more readily. Cracking serves the dual purpose of increasing the supply of shorter, more useful fractions and producing alkenes as raw materials for polymer manufacture. Thermal cracking uses very high temperatures and pressures; catalytic cracking uses a zeolite catalyst at lower temperatures — be precise about which conditions apply to each method in your exam answers.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons separated by fractional distillation. Smaller fractions (top of column) have lower boiling points and higher flammability; larger fractions (bottom) have higher boiling points and greater viscosity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Complete combustion produces CO<sub>2</sub> and water. Incomplete combustion produces CO (toxic) and soot. Sulfur impurities give SO<sub>2</sub> (acid rain); high temperatures produce NOₓ (acid rain, smog).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Cracking breaks large alkanes into smaller alkanes and alkenes. Alkenes are unsaturated (C=C bond), decolourise bromine water, and are used to make polymers.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">When writing combustion equations, check that your equation is balanced and that you specify whether oxygen supply is limited (incomplete) or excess (complete). For pollutant questions, name the specific gas AND give its environmental impact &mdash; &lsquo;it pollutes the air&rsquo; earns no marks. For cracking questions, remember: every cracking reaction produces at least one alkene. Bromine water test: decolourises with alkene, stays orange with alkane.</p>
