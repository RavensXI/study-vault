Reversible reactions, dynamic equilibrium, Le Chatelier's principle and the Haber process.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reversible Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Some chemical reactions can proceed in <strong>both directions</strong>. In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that can proceed in both the forward and reverse directions, shown by the symbol ⇌. Products can react to re-form the original reactants.">reversible reaction</dfn>, the products can react together to re-form the original reactants. These reactions are represented using a double arrow (\(\rightleftharpoons\)) rather than a single arrow. A straightforward example is the thermal decomposition of ammonium chloride:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$NH_4Cl \rightleftharpoons NH_3 + HCl$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Heating drives the forward reaction; cooling drives the reverse. The energy is conserved: if the forward reaction is exothermic, the reverse reaction is endothermic by exactly the same amount.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall: if the forward reaction releases 100 kJ/mol, how much energy is absorbed in the reverse reaction?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a reversible reaction, if the forward reaction is exothermic, the reverse reaction is endothermic by the same amount of energy &mdash; and vice versa.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Dynamic Equilibrium</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n7">When a reversible reaction takes place in a <strong>closed system</strong> (nothing enters or leaves), it eventually reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="The state reached in a closed system when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Concentrations of reactants and products remain constant but both reactions are still occurring.">dynamic equilibrium</dfn>. At equilibrium:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">The rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (they are not necessarily equal).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">Both reactions are still happening &mdash; equilibrium is <em>dynamic</em>, not static. The reaction has not stopped.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why a system at equilibrium is described as dynamic rather than static.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At dynamic equilibrium, both forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate. Concentrations stay constant because the rate of product formation equals the rate of product being converted back to reactants. The reaction has NOT stopped.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Le Chatelier&rsquo;s Principle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of equilibrium shifts in the direction that partially opposes the change.">Le Chatelier&rsquo;s principle</dfn> states: if conditions are changed in a system at equilibrium, the equilibrium <em>shifts</em> in the direction that opposes the change.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Effects of Changing Temperature, Concentration and Pressure</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Temperature:</strong> increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction (absorbs the extra heat). Decreasing temperature shifts it in the exothermic direction (releases heat to compensate). Temperature is the only factor that changes the <em>value</em> of the equilibrium constant.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Concentration:</strong> increasing the concentration of a reactant shifts equilibrium to the right (forward reaction) to use up the extra reactant. Increasing the concentration of a product shifts equilibrium to the left.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Pressure (gases only):</strong> increasing pressure shifts equilibrium towards the side with <em>fewer</em> moles of gas, to reduce the pressure. Decreasing pressure shifts it towards the side with more moles of gas.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Haber Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Haber process manufactures <dfn class="term" data-def="A compound of nitrogen and hydrogen (NH₃) made industrially via the Haber process. It is a key raw material for fertilisers.">ammonia</dfn> from nitrogen and hydrogen:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">$$N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3 \quad (\Delta H = -92 \text{ kJ/mol})$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The forward reaction is exothermic. The nitrogen comes from the air; the hydrogen is obtained mainly from natural gas (methane). The reaction is carried out at approximately <strong>450&deg;C</strong> and <strong>200 atmospheres</strong> pressure, using a finely divided <strong>iron catalyst</strong>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why 450&deg;C and 200 Atmospheres? The Compromise</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Temperature &mdash; rate vs yield:</strong> the forward reaction is exothermic. Lowering temperature would favour the forward reaction (better yield, around 98% at room temperature), but the rate would be too slow to be economical. Higher temperatures speed up the reaction but reduce yield. 450&deg;C is a <em>compromise</em> &mdash; an acceptable yield (around 15&ndash;25%) at a commercially viable rate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Pressure &mdash; more moles on the left:</strong> on the left there are 4 moles of gas (1 N<sub>2</sub> + 3 H<sub>2</sub>); on the right there are 2 moles (2 NH<sub>3</sub>). By Le Chatelier&rsquo;s principle, high pressure shifts equilibrium to the right (fewer moles of gas). Very high pressures give better yields but are extremely expensive and dangerous to maintain. 200 atmospheres is again a compromise: a high enough pressure to be practical without prohibitive engineering costs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Iron catalyst:</strong> lowers the activation energy of both the forward and reverse reactions equally, so the system reaches equilibrium faster without changing the position of equilibrium. It does not improve yield, only rate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Recycling Unused Gases and the Industrial Process</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Because the yield per pass is only around 15&ndash;25%, the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are separated from the ammonia and recycled back into the reactor. The ammonia is cooled and liquefied (it has a higher boiling point than N<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>), allowing the unreacted gases to remain as gas and be pumped back. Overall efficiency is very high despite the low single-pass yield.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Ammonia and Fertilisers (Triple-only)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Ammonia manufactured by the Haber process is used to make nitrogen-based fertilisers. Ammonia can be oxidised to form <dfn class="term" data-def="HNO₃ — a strong acid made by the catalytic oxidation of ammonia, used in the production of fertilisers.">nitric acid</dfn>, which then reacts with more ammonia to make <dfn class="term" data-def="NH₄NO₃ — a compound used as a nitrogen-rich fertiliser because it contains nitrogen in both the ammonium (NH₄⁺) and nitrate (NO₃⁻) forms.">ammonium nitrate</dfn>, a widely used fertiliser. <dfn class="term" data-def="A fertiliser that contains three key nutrients: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), formulated to match the needs of specific crops.">NPK fertilisers</dfn> contain nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in ratios designed to optimise crop growth. They are formulated by combining ammonium salts with phosphate and potassium compounds.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">In a reversible reaction at dynamic equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, concentrations are constant, and both reactions continue. Equilibrium is dynamic, not static.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Le Chatelier&rsquo;s principle: any change in conditions shifts equilibrium to partially oppose that change. Temperature changes affect yield; catalysts only affect rate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The Haber process makes ammonia from N<sub>2</sub> and H<sub>2</sub> at 450&deg;C and 200 atm with an iron catalyst &mdash; a compromise between yield and rate. Unreacted gases are recycled.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">The Haber process is a classic exam context for Le Chatelier&rsquo;s principle. When explaining why 450&deg;C is chosen, you must address both rate <em>and</em> yield &mdash; stating only one will lose marks. Remember: the catalyst does NOT change the position of equilibrium or the yield; it only speeds up attainment of equilibrium. For equilibrium questions, always state which direction the equilibrium shifts AND why, linking clearly to Le Chatelier&rsquo;s principle.</p>
