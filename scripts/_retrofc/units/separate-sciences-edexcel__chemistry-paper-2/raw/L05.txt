--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Earth's atmosphere evolved, modern composition, greenhouse gases and climate change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Early Atmosphere</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For the first billion years of Earth&rsquo;s history, the atmosphere was very different from today. Scientists believe it was dominated by <dfn class="term" data-def="A gas with the formula CO₂, produced by volcanic activity and respiration. It made up most of the early atmosphere and is now a key greenhouse gas.">carbon dioxide</dfn>, with very little or no oxygen. It also contained water vapour, nitrogen, and small amounts of methane and ammonia. This early atmosphere was produced by intense <strong>volcanic activity</strong>, which released gases trapped inside the planet.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">As the Earth cooled, the water vapour in the atmosphere condensed to form the early oceans. This removed a large amount of water vapour from the atmosphere. Scientists draw comparisons with the present-day atmospheres of Venus and Mars, which are still dominated by carbon dioxide &mdash; supporting the model of what Earth&rsquo;s early atmosphere may have looked like.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name three gases present in the early atmosphere and explain how oceans formed from one of them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The early atmosphere: mainly CO<sub>2</sub>, plus water vapour, nitrogen, and traces of methane and ammonia. As the planet cooled, water vapour condensed to form the oceans. Volcanic activity was the source of most of these gases.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">How the Atmosphere Changed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Over billions of years, the composition of the atmosphere changed dramatically, primarily due to the evolution of life and the formation of the oceans:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Three Key Processes That Changed the Atmosphere</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>1. Photosynthesis by early organisms:</strong> the first photosynthetic organisms (simple algae and cyanobacteria) evolved in the oceans. They used carbon dioxide from the water and atmosphere, releasing oxygen as a waste product. Over hundreds of millions of years, oxygen levels in the atmosphere slowly increased.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>2. Oceans absorbing CO<sub>2</sub>:</strong> carbon dioxide dissolved in the oceans, where it reacted to form carbonates. These were deposited as carbonate rocks such as limestone, and became part of the shells and skeletons of marine organisms. This locked up vast quantities of carbon dioxide in solid rock &mdash; removing it from the atmosphere.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>3. Formation of the ozone layer:</strong> as oxygen levels rose, some oxygen molecules were converted to ozone (O<sub>3</sub>) in the upper atmosphere. The ozone layer absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun. Its formation allowed more complex life to evolve on land without being damaged by UV radiation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Modern Atmosphere</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Today&rsquo;s atmosphere has been roughly stable for around 200 million years. Its approximate composition by volume is:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Nitrogen (N<sub>2</sub>):</strong> approximately 78%</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>):</strong> approximately 21%</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Other gases</strong> (mainly argon, plus very small amounts of carbon dioxide and water vapour): approximately 1%</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: the approximate percentages of nitrogen, oxygen, and other gases in the modern atmosphere.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Modern atmosphere: approximately 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and about 1% other gases (mainly argon). Carbon dioxide makes up approximately 0.04% &mdash; a very small proportion, but it has a disproportionately large effect on climate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Greenhouse Effect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Sun emits energy as short-wavelength radiation (mainly visible light), which passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth&rsquo;s surface. The warm surface emits longer-wavelength <dfn class="term" data-def="Heat radiation emitted by the Earth's warm surface. It has a longer wavelength than visible light and is absorbed by greenhouse gases in the atmosphere rather than passing straight out into space.">infrared radiation</dfn>. This infrared radiation is absorbed by <dfn class="term" data-def="Gases in the atmosphere that absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth and re-emit it in all directions, trapping heat and keeping the planet warm. Key examples: carbon dioxide, methane, water vapour.">greenhouse gases</dfn> in the atmosphere, which then re-emit the energy in all directions &mdash; including back towards Earth. This natural <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which greenhouse gases trap infrared radiation in the lower atmosphere, keeping the Earth's surface warmer than it would otherwise be.">greenhouse effect</dfn> is essential for life &mdash; without it, the average surface temperature would be far below zero.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Greenhouse Gases and Their Sources</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>):</strong> released by burning fossil fuels, deforestation (removing trees that absorb CO<sub>2</sub>), and cement production. Its concentration in the atmosphere has been rising significantly since the Industrial Revolution.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Methane (CH<sub>4</sub>):</strong> released from livestock (digestive processes), landfill sites (decomposition of organic waste), and rice paddy fields. Methane is a more potent greenhouse gas than CO<sub>2</sub> over a short timescale, though it breaks down in the atmosphere more quickly.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Water vapour (H<sub>2</sub>O):</strong> a naturally occurring greenhouse gas and the most abundant one, though its concentration is largely governed by temperature rather than direct human activity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Human Impact and Climate Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Human activities have significantly increased the concentration of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, particularly carbon dioxide and methane. The scientific consensus, supported by many independent lines of evidence, is that this enhanced <dfn class="term" data-def="The observed long-term rise in the Earth's average surface temperature, attributed to increasing concentrations of greenhouse gases released by human activities.">global warming</dfn> is causing <dfn class="term" data-def="Long-term shifts in global temperatures and weather patterns. While some climate change is natural, human activities have been the dominant cause since the mid-20th century.">climate change</dfn>. Evidence includes rising average global temperatures, retreating glaciers and ice caps, rising sea levels, and shifts in the timing of seasons and species distribution.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Consequences and Responses to Climate Change</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Predicted consequences of continued climate change include: more frequent and severe extreme weather events (droughts, floods, storms); rising sea levels threatening low-lying coastal areas; disruption to food and water supplies; loss of biodiversity as ecosystems shift or collapse.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Responses include: reducing fossil fuel use and transitioning to renewable energy sources; increasing energy efficiency; carbon capture and storage; international agreements to limit emissions. The challenge is balancing economic development with environmental protection &mdash; particularly for developing nations.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences: what the greenhouse effect is and how human activity enhances it beyond natural levels.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm; without it, life could not exist. Human activities (burning fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture) are increasing greenhouse gas concentrations, enhancing the greenhouse effect and causing measurable global warming and climate change.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The early atmosphere was mainly CO<sub>2</sub>. Photosynthesis by early organisms increased O<sub>2</sub>. Ocean absorption and carbonate formation removed CO<sub>2</sub>. Today&rsquo;s atmosphere is approximately 78% N<sub>2</sub>, 21% O<sub>2</sub>, 1% other gases.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">The greenhouse effect: greenhouse gases (CO<sub>2</sub>, CH<sub>4</sub>, water vapour) absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth&rsquo;s surface and re-emit it, keeping the planet warm. This process is natural and essential for life.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Human activities are increasing greenhouse gas concentrations, enhancing the greenhouse effect and causing global warming and climate change, supported by multiple independent lines of scientific evidence.</li>
</ul>
