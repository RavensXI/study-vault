--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Alkanes and Alkenes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Hydrocarbons are compounds containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms. <dfn class="term" data-def="Saturated hydrocarbons where all carbon-carbon bonds are single bonds; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.">Alkanes</dfn> are saturated &mdash; every carbon atom is bonded to as many hydrogen atoms as possible. <dfn class="term" data-def="Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond; general formula CₙH₂ₙ.">Alkenes</dfn> are unsaturated, containing at least one carbon&mdash;carbon double bond.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The distinction matters because the double bond in alkenes is chemically reactive. You can test for an alkene using <dfn class="term" data-def="An orange solution of bromine used to test for unsaturation: it decolourises in the presence of an alkene.">bromine water</dfn>: alkenes decolourise the orange solution to colourless because bromine adds across the double bond. Alkanes do not react, so the bromine water stays orange.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the bromine water test result for both an alkane and an alkene, explaining why the results differ.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alkanes are saturated (single C&mdash;C bonds) and do not decolourise bromine water. Alkenes are unsaturated (contain a C=C double bond) and <strong>do</strong> decolourise bromine water from orange to colourless.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Addition Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The joining of many small monomer molecules, each containing a double bond, to form a long polymer chain; no other product is made.">Addition polymerisation</dfn> occurs when alkene monomers react together to form a polymer. The double bond in each monomer opens up to join the chain. No other product is formed. A simple example is ethene (\(\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2\)) polymerising to form poly(ethene):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$n\, \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2 \rightarrow \text{---}(\text{CH}_2\text{---}\text{CH}_2)_n\text{---}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The monomer is written inside brackets with a double bond; the polymer is shown with the repeat unit inside brackets and the subscript <em>n</em> indicating many thousands of units. Other addition polymers include poly(propene) and poly(chloroethene) (PVC).</p>

To identify the monomer from a polymer: reverse the process &mdash; add the double bond back between the two carbons in the repeat unit.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Condensation Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Polymerisation in which monomers join together and release small molecules (usually water) at each step.">Condensation polymerisation</dfn> links monomers that each have two functional groups. Each time two monomers join, a small molecule &mdash; usually water &mdash; is eliminated. Unlike addition polymerisation, the monomers do <em>not</em> need a double bond.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Two important condensation polymers are <dfn class="term" data-def="A condensation polymer formed from a diol and a dicarboxylic acid, releasing water at each join. Used in clothing fibres and plastic bottles.">polyesters</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A condensation polymer formed from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid, releasing water. Nylon is an example.">polyamides</dfn>. Polyesters form ester links (&mdash;COO&mdash;) between a diol and a dicarboxylic acid. Polyamides form amide links (&mdash;CONH&mdash;) between a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid. Nylon is a well-known polyamide.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state two differences between addition and condensation polymerisation, including what type of monomers each requires.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Addition polymerisation: monomers have C=C double bonds; no other product. Condensation polymerisation: monomers have two functional groups; small molecule (usually water) released at each link. Polyesters and nylon are condensation polymers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Alcohols</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A homologous series of compounds containing the &mdash;OH functional group; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH.">Alcohols</dfn> form a homologous series. All alcohols contain the hydroxyl (&mdash;OH) functional group. The first four members are methanol, ethanol, propanol, and butanol. <dfn class="term" data-def="A simple alcohol with formula CH₃CH₂OH, found in alcoholic drinks and used as a fuel and solvent.">Ethanol</dfn> (C₂H₅OH) is the most important for GCSE: it is the alcohol in drinks, and it is widely used as a fuel and solvent.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Ethanol can be made in two ways: by reacting ethene with steam in the presence of an acid catalyst (hydration &mdash; industrial process, uses a finite resource), or by <dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of sugars by yeast in the absence of oxygen to produce ethanol and carbon dioxide.">fermentation</dfn> of sugars using yeast.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fermentation in Detail</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">In fermentation, yeast converts glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide under anaerobic (oxygen-free) conditions and at an optimum temperature of around 25&ndash;35 &deg;C:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">$$\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide}$$
    $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The ethanol concentration cannot rise above about 12&ndash;15% because higher concentrations kill the yeast. To obtain pure ethanol, the mixture is distilled. Fermentation uses a renewable resource (sugar) and happens at room temperature, making it lower energy than industrial hydration.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Carboxylic Acids</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="A homologous series of compounds containing the &mdash;COOH functional group; they are weak acids.">Carboxylic acids</dfn> contain the &mdash;COOH functional group. They are weak acids &mdash; they partially ionise in water. The first members are methanoic acid, <dfn class="term" data-def="A carboxylic acid with formula CH₃COOH; the acid in vinegar, with a sharp smell.">ethanoic acid</dfn> (CH₃COOH), propanoic acid, and butanoic acid. Ethanoic acid is found in vinegar.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Carboxylic acids react with carbonates to produce a salt, water, and carbon dioxide. They also react with alcohols in the presence of an acid catalyst to form esters and water. Esters have fruity smells and are used as food flavourings and perfumes.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">In this core practical, a spirit burner containing a known mass of alcohol is used to heat a measured volume of water in a calorimeter. The temperature rise of the water is recorded. Key variables to control: mass of water, draught conditions, distance between flame and calorimeter. The energy released is calculated using \(Q = mc\Delta T\), where \(m\) is the mass of water, \(c\) is the specific heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g&deg;C), and \(\Delta T\) is the temperature change. The mass of alcohol burned (from the change in mass of the spirit burner) gives the energy per gram.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Limitations include heat loss to surroundings, incomplete combustion, and evaporation of alcohol. These mean the experimental value is lower than the standard enthalpy of combustion. Repeats and averaging reduce random error.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Recycling Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Most addition polymers are non-biodegradable, meaning they persist in the environment for centuries. This creates significant waste problems. Polymers can be <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of collecting and reprocessing waste materials to produce new usable products, reducing landfill and conserving finite resources.">recycled</dfn> mechanically (sorting, melting, re-moulding) or by chemical methods such as cracking back into monomers. Some condensation polymers (e.g. polyesters) can be chemically recycled more easily than addition polymers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Alternatively, some polymers can be used as a fuel by incineration, recovering some energy. However, this releases carbon dioxide and may release harmful gases if chlorine-containing plastics like PVC are burned. <dfn class="term" data-def="Plastics made from biological sources such as plant starch or sugar, which are biodegradable.">Biodegradable plastics</dfn> (bioplastics made from plant materials) offer a more sustainable future but currently have higher production costs and limited mechanical strength.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain two environmental problems with non-biodegradable polymers and two solutions, with one advantage and one disadvantage each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Non-biodegradable polymers persist in landfill and oceans for centuries. Solutions include mechanical recycling, chemical recycling (cracking), incineration (energy recovery but CO₂ released), and biodegradable bioplastics (sustainable but costly).</p>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Alkenes have a C=C double bond and decolourise bromine water; alkanes do not. Addition polymerisation joins alkene monomers; condensation polymerisation joins bifunctional monomers, releasing water, producing polyesters (ester links) and polyamides (amide links).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Ethanol is made by fermentation of sugars by yeast (renewable, room temperature) or by hydration of ethene (industrial, finite resource). Carboxylic acids contain &mdash;COOH, are weak acids, and react with alcohols to form esters.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Non-biodegradable polymers cause environmental problems; solutions include mechanical recycling, chemical recycling, incineration, and bioplastics &mdash; each with advantages and disadvantages.</li>
</ul>
