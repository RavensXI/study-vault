<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Identifying Substances in Chemistry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chemists use a range of analytical techniques to identify unknown substances. These range from simple laboratory tests &mdash; such as flame tests and gas tests &mdash; to instrumental methods such as the flame photometer. This lesson covers the key tests you need to know, many of which are Triple-only content that goes beyond the Combined Science course.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Flame Tests</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A qualitative test used to identify the presence of specific metal ions by observing the colour of the flame they produce when heated.">flame test</dfn> is used to detect the presence of certain metal ions. A clean nichrome wire loop is dipped in the sample and held in a Bunsen flame. Different metal ions produce characteristic flame colours:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Lithium (Li&sup+;):</strong> crimson / red</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Sodium (Na&sup+;):</strong> bright yellow</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Potassium (K&sup+;):</strong> lilac</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Calcium (Ca&sup2+;):</strong> orange-red</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Copper (Cu&sup2+;):</strong> green</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the five metal ions and their flame colours from memory, starting with lithium.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Flame test colours: lithium = crimson, sodium = yellow, potassium = lilac, calcium = orange-red, copper = green. Sodium&rsquo;s yellow is so intense it can mask other colours &mdash; the wire must be thoroughly cleaned between tests.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Tests for Metal Ions Using Sodium Hydroxide</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n12">Many metal ions form insoluble <dfn class="term" data-def="Compounds formed when metal ions react with hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in solution, producing a solid precipitate of the metal hydroxide that is insoluble in water.">hydroxide precipitates</dfn> when sodium hydroxide solution is added. The colour of the precipitate identifies the metal ion:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Copper (Cu&sup2+;):</strong> blue precipitate</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Iron(II) (Fe&sup2+;):</strong> green precipitate</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Iron(III) (Fe&sup3+;):</strong> brown (rust-coloured) precipitate</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Calcium (Ca&sup2+;):</strong> white precipitate</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Aluminium (Al&sup3+;):</strong> white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Testing for Ammonium Ions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Adding sodium hydroxide solution to an ammonium salt and gently warming releases <strong>ammonia gas</strong> (NH<sub>3</sub>). Ammonia can be detected by holding damp red litmus paper near the mouth of the tube &mdash; it turns <strong>blue</strong> because ammonia is an alkaline gas. This is the definitive test for the ammonium ion (NH<sub>4</sub>&sup+;).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Gas Tests</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Four gases are commonly tested in GCSE chemistry. Each has a specific test and result:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Gas Tests: Hydrogen, Oxygen, Carbon Dioxide, Chlorine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Hydrogen (H<sub>2</sub>):</strong> hold a lit splint to the mouth of the test tube &mdash; a <em>squeaky pop</em> confirms hydrogen.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Oxygen (O<sub>2</sub>):</strong> hold a <em>glowing</em> splint to the mouth of the tube &mdash; oxygen relights (re-ignites) the splint.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>):</strong> bubble the gas through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution) &mdash; it turns <em>milky white</em> (cloudy) as calcium carbonate precipitates.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Chlorine (Cl<sub>2</sub>):</strong> hold damp litmus paper in the gas &mdash; it is first turned red (acid) then bleached white. Chlorine is a pale yellow-green gas with a pungent smell.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: the test and result for each of the four gases, starting with hydrogen and ending with chlorine.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gas tests: hydrogen &mdash; squeaky pop with lit splint. Oxygen &mdash; relights glowing splint. Carbon dioxide &mdash; turns limewater milky. Chlorine &mdash; bleaches damp litmus paper.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Chromatography</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating and identifying the components of a mixture based on how they distribute between a stationary phase and a moving solvent.">Chromatography</dfn> separates mixtures based on the relative solubility of each component in a solvent. In paper chromatography, a spot of mixture is placed on a pencil baseline on chromatography paper. The paper is placed in a small volume of solvent (below the baseline), which travels up the paper by capillary action, carrying dissolved components with it. Components that are more soluble in the solvent travel further.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Calculating Rf Values</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the distance a substance travels to the distance the solvent travels in a chromatography experiment. Each substance has a unique Rf value in a given solvent.">R<sub>f</sub> value</dfn> (retention factor) is calculated as:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">R<sub>f</sub> values are always between 0 and 1. Each substance has a characteristic R<sub>f</sub> value in a specific solvent at a specific temperature. A pure substance produces a single spot; a mixture produces multiple spots. Unknown substances can be identified by comparing their R<sub>f</sub> values with known reference values run on the same chromatogram.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Calculating an Rf Value</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n32">The solvent front travels 8.0 cm. A dye spot travels 6.4 cm. Calculate the R<sub>f</sub> value.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33">$$R_f = \frac{6.4}{8.0} = 0.80$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n34">The dye has an R<sub>f</sub> value of 0.80 in this solvent. Always measure to the centre of the spot from the pencil baseline, not from the bottom of the paper.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n35">Instrumental Analysis: The Flame Photometer</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n36">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument that identifies metal ions by measuring the characteristic wavelengths of light emitted when a sample is burned in a flame. It is faster and more sensitive than manual flame tests.">flame photometer</dfn> is an instrumental technique that identifies metal ions by analysing the specific wavelengths of light they emit when placed in a flame. It is far more sensitive than a manual flame test and can detect and measure the concentration of metal ions in very dilute solutions. Flame photometry is used in hospitals to measure sodium and potassium ion concentrations in blood and urine samples, and in agriculture to analyse soil mineral content.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n42">For the exam, learn flame test colours precisely: lithium gives crimson, sodium gives yellow, potassium gives lilac, calcium gives orange-red, and copper gives blue-green. A common error is confusing the white precipitate from aluminium or calcium ions with that from magnesium: adding excess sodium hydroxide dissolves the aluminium hydroxide precipitate but not the magnesium hydroxide, which lets you distinguish them reliably in an exam question.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n38">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Flame tests identify metal ions by characteristic colours: lithium (crimson), sodium (yellow), potassium (lilac), calcium (orange-red), copper (green). Sodium hydroxide precipitates can also distinguish metal ions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40">Gas tests: hydrogen = squeaky pop; oxygen = relights glowing splint; CO<sub>2</sub> = milky limewater; chlorine = bleaches damp litmus. Ammonia turns damp red litmus blue.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n41">Chromatography separates mixtures; R<sub>f</sub> = distance moved by substance &divide; distance moved by solvent. The flame photometer provides sensitive, quantitative identification of metal ions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n37">Flame test and gas test questions require precise language &mdash; &lsquo;it goes white&rsquo; for limewater is incomplete; state &lsquo;turns milky/cloudy white&rsquo;. For the chlorine test, mention both actions: turns red <em>then</em> bleaches. When calculating R<sub>f</sub> values, always measure from the baseline (pencil line) to the centre of the spot, not from the bottom of the paper. R<sub>f</sub> values must be between 0 and 1 &mdash; if yours is above 1, you&rsquo;ve divided incorrectly.</p>
