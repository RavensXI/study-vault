--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">1916: The Year of Attrition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the end of 1915 the Western Front had become a 450-mile line of trenches stretching from the Belgian coast to the Swiss border. Neither side had broken the deadlock through manoeuvre, so both turned to <dfn class="term" data-def="A military strategy that aims to win by gradually wearing down the enemy through continuous losses in personnel and material.">attrition</dfn> &mdash; trying to grind the other side into surrender by inflicting heavier losses than they could replace. 1916 produced the two most infamous battles of attrition in modern history: Verdun, fought by the French and Germans from February to December, and the Somme, fought largely by the British from July to November.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who designed the Verdun plan, what was its aim, and on what date did the German bombardment open?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Battle of Verdun opened on 21 February 1916. German Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn aimed to &lsquo;bleed France white&rsquo; by attacking a fortress city the French would have to defend at any cost. The battle ran for ten months and produced roughly 700,000 combined casualties.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Verdun: To Bleed France White</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Falkenhayn picked Verdun for symbolic, not strategic, reasons. The city was ringed by French forts, sat on the River Meuse, and represented a century of French military pride. He believed that France would defend it whatever the cost, and that this stubbornness would allow German artillery to chew up French divisions one by one. The German bombardment on 21 February 1916 fired around one million shells in eight hours along an eight-mile front. Fort Douaumont, the largest of the surrounding forts, fell within four days.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">General Philippe P&eacute;tain took command of the French defence. He rotated divisions through Verdun on a system known as the <em>noria</em>, so that no unit was destroyed completely &mdash; eventually around three quarters of the entire French army served at Verdun. He kept the city supplied via a single road, the <em>Voie Sacr&eacute;e</em> (&lsquo;Sacred Way&rsquo;), along which 6,000 lorries a day moved men, food and shells. The defiant order &lsquo;Ils ne passeront pas&rsquo; (&lsquo;They shall not pass&rsquo;) became a French national slogan.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The bleeding plan assumed German losses would stay low while French losses climbed. In practice the maths went both ways. By December 1916 the French had suffered around 377,000 casualties and the Germans 337,000 &mdash; the ratio was nearly even. The launch of the Somme offensive in July forced Germany to thin out the Verdun assault to send divisions north. French counter-attacks in October and December retook Forts Douaumont and Vaux. Falkenhayn was sacked in August 1916 and replaced by Hindenburg and Ludendorff.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Somme: Britain&rsquo;s First Mass Offensive</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Somme was originally planned as a joint Anglo-French offensive on a quiet sector to break through the German line. After Verdun began, the French contribution shrank from 40 divisions to 16 and the burden fell on Britain. The new British army &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Battalions of First World War volunteers from the same town, workplace or club, raised under Kitchener&rsquo;s 1914 recruitment drive.">Pals battalions</dfn> raised by Lord Kitchener and largely untested in major battle &mdash; would carry the attack.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A week-long preliminary bombardment fired 1.5 million shells along the German line, intended to destroy the wire and bury the defenders. It did neither effectively. Many British shells were duds, the wire was uncut in many places, and the German front line was protected by deep concrete dugouts up to 12 metres underground.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the date of the first day of the Somme, the British casualty figure, and one reason it was so high.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 1 July 1916 &mdash; the first day of the Somme &mdash; the British army suffered around 57,470 casualties, including 19,240 dead. It remains the bloodiest day in British military history. The battle ran on for another 140 days and finally ended in mid-November.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why the First Day Went So Wrong</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Several errors compounded one another. The bombardment failed to cut the wire or destroy the German dugouts. The infantry were ordered to advance at walking pace in extended lines, weighed down with around 30 kilograms of equipment, on the assumption that resistance would be minimal. As the bombardment lifted, German machine-gunners climbed out of their dugouts and opened fire across no man&rsquo;s land. The principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="An artillery technique in which a curtain of shells advances slowly just ahead of attacking infantry to suppress defenders until the moment of contact.">creeping barrage</dfn>, where shells lift forward in time with the infantry, was used in places but inconsistently coordinated.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">On 15 September 1916 at Flers-Courcelette, Haig deployed the new British Mark I <dfn class="term" data-def="Armoured tracked vehicle developed by Britain in 1915&ndash;16 to cross trenches, smash wire and shield infantry from machine-gun fire.">tank</dfn> for the first time. Of the 49 sent into action only 32 made it to the start line, and most broke down or got stuck in shell craters. Even so, the few that worked drove infantry forward in places and signalled a tactical answer to the trench problem. Haig was criticised for committing the weapon early in small numbers; revisionist historians argue it was a sensible learning exercise.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Significance for 1916</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By the end of November the British and French had advanced about six miles at the Somme. Total casualties were enormous: around 420,000 British, 200,000 French and 465,000 German. Yet the picture is not one of pure futility. The Somme drew German divisions away from Verdun and helped force Falkenhayn&rsquo;s removal. It taught the British army hard lessons about artillery coordination, infantry tactics and air-ground cooperation that would matter in 1917 and 1918. For Germany, it accelerated the shift to a defensive strategy: in early 1917 they fell back to the heavily-fortified Hindenburg Line. The combined experience of Verdun and the Somme convinced both sides that breakthrough by infantry alone was impossible &mdash; future progress would have to come from technology, tactics and combined arms.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Verdun (Feb&ndash;Dec 1916) was Falkenhayn&rsquo;s attempt to &lsquo;bleed France white&rsquo; at a fortress city the French would defend at any cost &mdash; it failed because German losses kept pace with French losses and the Somme forced him to divert troops.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The Somme (July&ndash;Nov 1916) opened with the bloodiest day in British military history because the bombardment did not cut the wire or destroy the German dugouts, and infantry walked into prepared machine-gun fire.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">1916 ended the idea of breakthrough by infantry alone &mdash; both sides moved towards combined arms (tanks, creeping barrage, air-ground cooperation) and Germany withdrew to the Hindenburg Line for 1917.</li></ul>
