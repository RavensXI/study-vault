--- DESCRIPTION ---
Triple Alliance, Entente Cordiale and Triple Entente — how Europe split into two camps by 1907.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Blocs Take Shape</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1907 Europe was divided into two armed groupings. On one side stood the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1882 alliance between Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, committing each to mutual support if attacked.">Triple Alliance</dfn> of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy. On the other stood the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1907 informal grouping of Britain, France and Russia, formed from a series of bilateral agreements rather than a single treaty.">Triple Entente</dfn> of Britain, France and Russia. Neither alliance was identical to the other. The Triple Alliance was a formal defensive treaty with clear obligations; the Triple Entente was a looser network of agreements settling colonial disputes and aligning policy. But the diplomatic effect was the same: a crisis between any two states risked dragging in their partners, and statesmen on both sides had to plan for a coalition war.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three states in each alliance and the year each grouping was completed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1907 Europe was split into two camps: the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy &mdash; finalised 1882) and the Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia &mdash; completed 1907). The first was a formal defensive treaty; the second was a network of bilateral agreements.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Triple Alliance, 1882</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Triple Alliance grew out of Bismarck&rsquo;s diplomacy. Germany and Austria-Hungary had signed the Dual Alliance in 1879, pledging mutual defence against Russia. In 1882 Italy joined them. Italy was a relative newcomer to European diplomacy, unified only in 1871, and resented French ambitions in North Africa &mdash; especially the French occupation of Tunisia in 1881, which Italy had hoped to claim. The treaty bound each state to come to the others&rsquo; aid if attacked by two or more Great Powers, and obliged them to remain neutral in other conflicts. It was renewed every few years; in 1902, however, Italy quietly signed a separate agreement promising France neutrality if attacked by Germany &mdash; a hint that Italian commitment was not as deep as the treaty implied.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Italy&rsquo;s commitment to the Triple Alliance was always shallow. Many Italians regarded Austria-Hungary as the &lsquo;hereditary enemy&rsquo; because it controlled Italian-speaking territories such as Trento and Trieste &mdash; the unredeemed lands or <em>Italia irredenta</em>. When war came in 1914, Italy declared its neutrality on the technical ground that the war was offensive, not defensive. In 1915 Italy switched sides, joining the Entente in the secret Treaty of London in return for promised territorial gains. The Triple Alliance was always a marriage of convenience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Britain Abandons Splendid Isolation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By 1900 Britain&rsquo;s policy of <dfn class="term" data-def="Britain's late nineteenth-century policy of avoiding permanent peacetime alliances, relying on naval supremacy and empire.">Splendid Isolation</dfn> looked increasingly risky. The Boer War (1899-1902) had shown how unpopular Britain was on the continent &mdash; cartoons in French and German papers mocked British soldiers and sympathy ran with the Boers. The Royal Navy was being challenged by a German naval programme. Britain&rsquo;s commitments stretched from Canada to India to Africa, and isolation no longer guaranteed security. The first step away was the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, easing Britain&rsquo;s position in the Far East. Then came the bigger shift: a settlement with France.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Entente Cordiale of 1904</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In April 1904 Britain and France signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1904 friendly understanding between Britain and France, settling colonial disputes — particularly over Egypt and Morocco — without committing either to military support.">Entente Cordiale</dfn>. It was not a military alliance. The agreement settled colonial quarrels: Britain recognised French interests in Morocco, France recognised British control of Egypt, and the two powers tidied up disputes in West Africa, Madagascar and Newfoundland. King Edward VII&rsquo;s state visit to Paris in 1903 had warmed the public mood. The signed agreement looked technical, but its political meaning was huge. Two states that had been on the brink of war over the Sudan as recently as 1898 (the Fashoda Incident) were now settling differences and aligning their diplomacy. Germany found this profoundly worrying.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Anglo-Russian Convention and the Triple Entente of 1907</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Britain and Russia were also rivals &mdash; the &lsquo;Great Game&rsquo; over Persia, Afghanistan and the approaches to India had run for decades. After Russia&rsquo;s humiliation in the Russo-Japanese War (1905) it was looking for European support, while Britain was looking to settle its imperial frontiers. The Anglo-Russian Convention of August 1907 carved up Persia into spheres of influence, recognised Afghanistan as a British zone and settled disputes over Tibet. Combined with the existing Franco-Russian Alliance and the Entente Cordiale, this completed the triangle. Historians from 1907 onwards spoke of the Triple Entente, even though the three states had no single treaty &mdash; just three bilateral agreements that pointed the same way.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Alliances are usually intended to deter war &mdash; if a state knows attacking one country means fighting two, it should think twice. But the European system of 1907 had a darker logic. Once both blocs were locked in, a small incident between any two states could trigger a chain reaction. Military planners on both sides built mobilisation timetables that assumed coalition war from day one. By 1914 these plans &mdash; especially Germany&rsquo;s Schlieffen Plan and Russia&rsquo;s mobilisation against Austria and Germany simultaneously &mdash; would mean that diplomacy had only days to defuse a crisis before military timetables took over. Christopher Clark&rsquo;s study <em>The Sleepwalkers</em> argues that this is not the same as alliances &lsquo;causing&rsquo; the war, but the rigidity they imposed left far less room for compromise.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down each Entente agreement and what it settled, in one sentence each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Triple Entente was built from three bilateral agreements: the Franco-Russian Alliance (1894), the Entente Cordiale (1904, settling colonial disputes between Britain and France), and the Anglo-Russian Convention (1907, settling disputes over Persia, Afghanistan and Tibet). It was a network, not a single treaty.</p>
</div>

The alliances did not cause war directly, but they created the rigid coalition logic that limited diplomacy when crisis struck.</li></ul>
