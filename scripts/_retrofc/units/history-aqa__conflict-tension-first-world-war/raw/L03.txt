--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Weltpolitik: A New German World Policy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Bismarck was forced out in 1890, Germany&rsquo;s new chancellors and the Kaiser made a decisive break with his cautious, continental focus. The new doctrine was <dfn class="term" data-def="Wilhelm II's policy from the late 1890s pursuing colonies, naval power and global influence to make Germany a world power.">Weltpolitik</dfn> &mdash; world policy. Wilhelm II declared that Germany must take its &lsquo;place in the sun&rsquo;: a global empire to match Britain&rsquo;s, a battle fleet to project power across oceans, and a louder voice in every international quarrel. The phrase came from a 1897 Reichstag speech by foreign minister Bernhard von Bülow: &lsquo;We do not want to put anyone in the shade, but we too demand a place in the sun.&rsquo; Where Bismarck had been content with a satisfied Germany at the heart of Europe, Wilhelm wanted Germany to be a global power on the model of Britain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: define Weltpolitik in one sentence, name the chancellor who articulated it, and give one practical aim.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Weltpolitik was the post-Bismarck German policy of seeking world power status &mdash; through colonies, a battle fleet and a global voice. Articulated by Bülow in his 1897 &lsquo;place in the sun&rsquo; speech, it broke decisively with Bismarck&rsquo;s European caution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Colonial Friction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Germany was a latecomer to imperialism. By 1900 the world&rsquo;s best colonial real estate was already taken by Britain, France, Russia and the smaller European powers. The German empire that did exist &mdash; in Cameroon, German East Africa (now Tanzania), South-West Africa (now Namibia), Togo and Pacific islands &mdash; was modest and often unprofitable. Germans demanding more colonies clashed repeatedly with the established empires. The Kruger Telegram of January 1896, in which Wilhelm congratulated the South African Boer leader Paul Kruger on repelling a British raid, infuriated London. The Boxer Rebellion of 1900 brought German troops into China, but again exposed how far Germany trailed Britain and France in global reach. Each colonial irritation deepened British distrust.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">Wilhelm II was not a stable diplomatic actor. He gave provocative speeches without warning his ministers, sent intemperate telegrams to foreign leaders, and once gave an interview to Britain&rsquo;s <em>Daily Telegraph</em> in 1908 that simultaneously offended Britain, France and Russia. Historians disagree about how much weight to put on personality. Christopher Clark&rsquo;s <em>The Sleepwalkers</em> argues structural rivalry mattered more than the Kaiser&rsquo;s outbursts; older accounts emphasise his erratic temperament. The truth is probably both: Wilhelm&rsquo;s instincts pointed Germany down a confrontational path that the country&rsquo;s industrial weight then made dangerous.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Tirpitz and the Naval Laws</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The most consequential strand of Weltpolitik was naval. Admiral Alfred von <dfn class="term" data-def="Admiral and German naval secretary from 1897, architect of the German battle fleet and author of successive Naval Laws building it up.">Tirpitz</dfn>, appointed Naval Secretary in 1897, believed Germany needed a battle fleet large enough to threaten Britain&rsquo;s. His &lsquo;risk theory&rsquo; argued that even a fleet smaller than the Royal Navy could deter British action: Britain would not dare risk losing so many capital ships even in victory. The First German Naval Law of 1898 set out a programme of nineteen battleships. The Second Naval Law of 1900 doubled the planned fleet. By 1914 Germany would build sixteen of the biggest new warships &mdash; the dreadnoughts &mdash; against Britain&rsquo;s twenty-nine.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">HMS Dreadnought, 1906</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In February 1906 Britain launched <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1906 Royal Navy battleship that, with its all-big-gun design and turbine engines, made every existing battleship in the world obsolete.">HMS Dreadnought</dfn>. The ship was a revolution: ten 12-inch guns where older battleships had four, steam turbine engines for unprecedented speed, and heavier armour than anything afloat. The launch made every other battleship in the world &mdash; including the Royal Navy&rsquo;s &mdash; obsolete in a single afternoon. Britain hoped to use its industrial lead to outbuild Germany; Germany saw an opening to start the race more or less even. Both sides accelerated. The Anglo-German naval race had begun.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Public opinion in both countries inflamed the rivalry. In Britain, the navalist slogan &lsquo;We want eight, and we won&rsquo;t wait!&rsquo; (a demand for eight new dreadnoughts in 1909) became a genuine political force. In Germany, the Navy League agitated for ever-larger budgets and presented the fleet as proof of German modernity. By 1912 the cost of the race forced Britain to negotiate. The Haldane Mission, a British attempt to slow construction in exchange for political concessions, came back empty-handed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">Historians disagree on this. Some, including Paul Kennedy in <em>The Rise of Anglo-German Antagonism</em>, argue the naval race was the central irritant that pushed Britain into the Entente camp. Others, including Christopher Clark, treat it as one factor among many. What is clear is that the race did not directly cause the war &mdash; in 1914 Britain went to war over Belgium, not battleships. But the race had soured Anglo-German relations to the point where, when crisis struck, Britain had no inclination to give Germany the benefit of the doubt. Naval rivalry built the political climate, even if it did not pull the trigger.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Personality versus Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A useful exam question is whether Wilhelm II personally caused this rivalry, or whether structural forces &mdash; the rise of German industry, the desire for empire among the German middle class, the strategic logic of a powerful state on the European continent &mdash; would have produced something similar under any leader. Both sides have evidence. The Kruger Telegram and the <em>Daily Telegraph</em> interview are clearly individual blunders. But the Tirpitz programme had broad political support and would have continued under most plausible chancellors. Strong essays acknowledge both: Wilhelm gave the policy its tone and damage, but the structural rivalry was real and would have caused friction even with a different emperor on the throne.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the year HMS Dreadnought launched and one reason it changed the naval race.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>HMS Dreadnought launched in February 1906. With its all-big-gun layout and turbine engines, it made every existing battleship obsolete &mdash; resetting the naval race and giving Germany a chance to compete from a more even starting line.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Weltpolitik replaced Bismarck&rsquo;s continental caution with global ambition &mdash; colonies, a battle fleet and a louder German voice in international affairs.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Tirpitz Naval Laws (1898, 1900 and later) and the launch of HMS Dreadnought in 1906 ignited an Anglo-German naval race that ran without compromise until the war.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Wilhelm II&rsquo;s personality made the policy more provocative, but the structural rivalry between a rising industrial Germany and a global Britain was the deeper cause.</li></ul>
