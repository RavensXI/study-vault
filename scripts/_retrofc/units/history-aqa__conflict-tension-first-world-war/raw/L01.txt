<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Europe in 1894: Five Great Powers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1894 the European continent was dominated by five so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="Major states with the diplomatic, military and industrial weight to shape European affairs.">Great Powers</dfn>: Britain, France, Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia. Each had a different mix of strengths and worries. Britain had the largest empire and the world&rsquo;s strongest navy, but a small regular army. France had a powerful army and overseas empire, but had not forgotten losing Alsace-Lorraine to Germany in 1871. Germany was the youngest of the powers, unified only in 1871, but its industry was the fastest-growing in Europe. Austria-Hungary was a multi-ethnic empire of Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles and Slavs &mdash; held together with difficulty. Russia was vast, populous and rich in resources, but technologically behind and politically unstable.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the five Great Powers of 1894 and one core interest of each in a single phrase.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1894 the five European Great Powers &mdash; Britain, France, Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia &mdash; each pursued a distinct interest: empire and naval supremacy, recovery of Alsace-Lorraine, industrial expansion, holding the multi-ethnic empire together, and access to the Mediterranean.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Bismarck&rsquo;s System: Keep France Isolated</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71, Germany&rsquo;s first chancellor Otto von Bismarck built his foreign policy around one fixed idea: France must never find an ally. A bitter France would always want Alsace-Lorraine back, so Bismarck made sure France stood alone in Europe. He achieved this through a web of treaties. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1873 alliance between Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia, designed by Bismarck to keep all three monarchies on the same side.">Three Emperors&rsquo; League</dfn> of 1873 tied Germany, Austria-Hungary and Russia together. The Dual Alliance of 1879 then bound Germany and Austria-Hungary in a defensive pact. In 1882 Italy joined to form the Triple Alliance. Even when Russia drifted away from the Three Emperors&rsquo; League over Balkan rivalries with Austria-Hungary, Bismarck kept Russia close with the secret Reinsurance Treaty of 1887.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Bismarck believed Germany should be a satisfied, status-quo power. Having defeated France and unified Germany, he wanted no more wars. He worried about a &lsquo;nightmare of coalitions&rsquo; &mdash; a future where France found powerful allies and Germany faced a war on two fronts. To prevent that, he kept Germany&rsquo;s ambitions in Europe modest and avoided major colonial adventures, even though many Germans wanted an empire to match Britain&rsquo;s. His message to the Reichstag in 1888 was blunt: &lsquo;We Germans fear God, but nothing else in the world.&rsquo; Yet his diplomacy showed plenty of caution underneath the bravado.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Wilhelm II Dismisses Bismarck</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Bismarck&rsquo;s system depended on him personally managing it. In 1888 the new emperor Kaiser Wilhelm II came to the throne &mdash; young, impulsive and determined to be his own chancellor. In March 1890 he forced Bismarck to resign. The dismissal was famously caricatured in the British magazine Punch as &lsquo;Dropping the Pilot&rsquo;: an anxious old seaman climbing down the ship&rsquo;s ladder while the young Kaiser leaned over the rail. Within months Wilhelm allowed the Reinsurance Treaty with Russia to lapse, judging it incompatible with Germany&rsquo;s alliance with Austria-Hungary. He underestimated the consequences. Russia, suddenly without a German guarantee, looked elsewhere for security.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">France saw its opportunity. The Third Republic was a parliamentary democracy; Russia was an autocratic monarchy under Tsar Alexander III &mdash; an unlikely match. Yet shared interest in containing Germany pulled them together. From 1891 French capital began funding Russian railway and industrial projects. In August 1892 secret military talks produced a draft military convention; in January 1894 it was formally ratified by the Tsar and the French president. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1894 military pact between France and Russia, requiring each to mobilise if the other was attacked by Germany or its allies.">Franco-Russian Alliance</dfn> bound each side to mobilise if the other was attacked by Germany, or by Austria-Hungary or Italy if those were backed by Germany.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For France, this was the diplomatic breakthrough Bismarck had spent two decades preventing. France was no longer alone. For Germany, the nightmare of a two-front war became a real strategic problem. German planners now had to imagine a war fought simultaneously in the west against France and in the east against Russia &mdash; the puzzle the Schlieffen Plan would later try to solve.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">In 1894 Britain stood deliberately apart from European alliances. This policy was nicknamed <dfn class="term" data-def="Britain's late nineteenth-century policy of avoiding permanent peacetime alliances with European powers, relying instead on naval supremacy and empire.">Splendid Isolation</dfn>. The phrase was coined by Canadian politician George Foster in 1896 and quickly adopted in London. Britain relied on the Royal Navy to protect its empire and trade routes, and saw little need to be tied to European quarrels. Yet by 1894 the policy was beginning to look strained: rivalries with France over Africa and with Russia over Persia and Afghanistan made permanent isolation harder to sustain.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Long Fuse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Historians often call 1894 the start of the slow collapse of Bismarck&rsquo;s European balance. The Franco-Russian Alliance did not cause war &mdash; it was a defensive pact and twenty years of peace would still follow. But it changed the geometry of Europe. Germany now faced a two-front problem; France had a powerful eastern partner; Britain&rsquo;s isolation began to feel less splendid. Christopher Clark&rsquo;s study <em>The Sleepwalkers</em> argues that none of the European powers wanted a continental war, but the alliance commitments they accumulated in this period made one increasingly possible. The fuse was now in place; the spark would come twenty years later, in Sarajevo.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the date the Franco-Russian Alliance was ratified, and one consequence for German strategic planning.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Franco-Russian Alliance was formally ratified in January 1894. It ended Bismarck&rsquo;s policy of French isolation and forced German planners to prepare for a two-front war &mdash; the strategic problem the Schlieffen Plan would later try to solve.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Europe in 1894 was dominated by five Great Powers, each with distinct interests &mdash; Britain&rsquo;s navy and empire, France&rsquo;s recovery of Alsace-Lorraine, Germany&rsquo;s industrial rise, Austria-Hungary&rsquo;s ethnic strains and Russia&rsquo;s vast resources.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Bismarck&rsquo;s diplomatic system kept France isolated through the Triple Alliance and the Reinsurance Treaty &mdash; a system Wilhelm II dismantled by sacking Bismarck and dropping Russia in 1890.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The Franco-Russian Alliance of January 1894 ended French isolation and gave Germany the two-front strategic problem that would shape every subsequent crisis.</li></ul>
