--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Germany’s six-week plan to defeat France ran into Belgium, the BEF and Joffre’s counter-attack.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Strategic Problem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Germany&rsquo;s strategic problem after 1894 was simple to state and almost impossible to solve. The Franco-Russian Alliance meant that any war with one would mean war with both. Germany would have to fight on two fronts &mdash; against France in the west and Russia in the east &mdash; with one army. Russia&rsquo;s vast territory and slow railways meant it would take roughly six weeks to fully mobilise. France, sitting next door, could mobilise within days. From these facts, Chief of the General Staff Alfred von <dfn class="term" data-def="German Chief of the General Staff (1891-1906) who designed the war plan to defeat France in six weeks via Belgium before turning east against Russia.">Schlieffen</dfn> drew a single conclusion: knock France out fast, then turn east before Russia is ready.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: state the central goal of the Schlieffen Plan in one sentence and the time it gave Germany to defeat France.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Schlieffen Plan, drafted by Alfred von Schlieffen between 1891 and 1906, aimed to defeat France within six weeks by invading through neutral Belgium and encircling Paris from the west &mdash; before turning east to face the slower-mobilising Russian army.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Plan in Outline</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Schlieffen&rsquo;s 1905 design called for the bulk of the German army &mdash; roughly seven-eighths of available strength &mdash; to mass on the right wing in the north, sweep through Belgium and the Netherlands, pivot south through northern France, and encircle Paris from the west. A small left wing would hold the French along the German border in Alsace-Lorraine, even pulling back to draw the French into a trap. The whole operation was timetabled to within days. Russia would still be mobilising; France would have collapsed; Germany would then transfer troops east on its excellent railways. The plan was an act of strategic boldness: it required the violation of Belgian and Dutch neutrality, the assumption that France would attack into the trap on the German border, and the gamble that Russia would not surprise everyone by mobilising faster than expected.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Schlieffen retired in 1906 and died in 1913. His successor as Chief of the General Staff, Helmuth von <dfn class="term" data-def="Schlieffen's successor as German Chief of Staff who modified and executed the Schlieffen Plan in 1914 and was sacked after the Marne.">Moltke (the Younger)</dfn>, modified the plan in important ways. He dropped the violation of Dutch neutrality (concentrating the invasion on Belgium alone), strengthened the left wing in Alsace-Lorraine and weakened the critical right wing. He also redirected some forces east in case of early Russian pressure. Schlieffen on his deathbed reportedly urged: &lsquo;Keep the right wing strong.&rsquo; Moltke&rsquo;s changes have been blamed by some historians (including Gerhard Ritter) for the plan&rsquo;s failure; others argue Moltke was responding sensibly to changed circumstances. Either way, the German army that marched in August 1914 was not exactly Schlieffen&rsquo;s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">August 1914: Belgium and the BEF</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">German troops crossed into Belgium on 4 August 1914. Belgian neutrality had been guaranteed by the 1839 Treaty of London, signed by all the Great Powers including Prussia. Britain had warned that violating Belgian neutrality would mean war; Bethmann-Hollweg dismissed the treaty as a &lsquo;scrap of paper&rsquo;. Belgium was small but its forts at Li&egrave;ge held out longer than expected, slowing the German advance by perhaps four critical days. Meanwhile the British Expeditionary Force &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="Britain's small professional army of around 100,000 men sent to France in August 1914 to support the French left flank.">BEF</dfn>, around 100,000 well-trained regulars &mdash; crossed the Channel and met the German advance at Mons on 23 August. The BEF inflicted heavy casualties before being forced to retreat. The professional accuracy of British rifle fire so impressed the Germans that some assumed they were facing machine guns.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Battle of the Marne, 5-12 September 1914</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">By early September German armies were within 50 km of Paris. The French government had fled to Bordeaux. But the German advance had over-extended itself. The right wing was weakened, troops were exhausted from forced marches, and supply lines were stretched. On 5 September French commander General <dfn class="term" data-def="French commander whose counter-attack at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914 saved Paris and ended the Schlieffen Plan timetable.">Joseph Joffre</dfn> launched a massive counter-attack along the Marne river. The Germans were exposed at a critical gap between their First and Second Armies. French and British forces drove into the gap. Famously, around 6,000 reinforcements were rushed to the front from Paris in a fleet of around 600 commandeered Parisian taxis &mdash; a small contribution but a powerful symbol of national mobilisation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">By 12 September the German army had been forced to retreat north to the Aisne river. Moltke suffered a nervous collapse and was sacked. The German government&rsquo;s spokesman admitted: &lsquo;the war is now lost&rsquo;, though the fighting would in fact continue for another four years. The retreat ended the Schlieffen timetable. France had not collapsed in six weeks. Russia was already attacking in East Prussia. The Germans now faced exactly the two-front war the plan had been designed to avoid.</p>

    <span>The Race to the Sea and Stalemate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">From mid-September each side tried to outflank the other to the north &mdash; the so-called &lsquo;race to the sea&rsquo;. By the First Battle of Ypres in late October 1914 both armies had reached the English Channel. There was nowhere left to manoeuvre. Both sides dug in. By late November the Western Front had become a continuous line of trenches running roughly 700 km from the Channel coast to the Swiss border. Movement gave way to siege. The war that was supposed to be over by Christmas would last another four years. The failure of the Schlieffen Plan therefore did not just cost Germany the campaign of 1914 &mdash; it produced the strategic deadlock that defined the rest of the war.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three reasons the Schlieffen Plan failed: one about Belgium, one about Russian timing, one about the Marne.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Schlieffen Plan failed for three connected reasons: Belgian resistance at Li&egrave;ge cost critical days; Russia mobilised faster than expected, forcing Germany to divert troops east; and Joffre&rsquo;s counter-attack at the Marne (5-12 September 1914) drove the over-extended German right wing back to the Aisne.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">The Schlieffen Plan was Germany&rsquo;s answer to the two-front problem &mdash; defeat France in six weeks via Belgium, then turn east against Russia.</li><li data-narration-id="n17">Belgian resistance, BEF intervention at Mons, faster-than-expected Russian mobilisation and German over-extension all combined to slow the advance.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">Joffre&rsquo;s counter-attack at the Marne (5-12 September 1914) ended the Schlieffen timetable; the resulting race to the sea produced the trench stalemate that defined the rest of the war.</li></ul>
