--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to research, plan, take and reflect on a citizenship action that aims to make a difference.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Citizenship Action Investigation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The citizenship action investigation is a core part of the GCSE qualification. You are required to plan and carry out a <dfn class="term" data-def="A planned course of informed action to address a citizenship issue or question of concern, aimed at delivering a benefit or change for a particular community or wider society.">citizenship action</dfn> &mdash; a real, out-of-classroom activity that applies the knowledge and skills you have developed during the course. The investigation is then assessed through questions in the active citizenship section, where you describe what you did, what happened, and how effective your action was. It accounts for 15 per cent of your total GCSE marks.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">A citizenship action can be local, national or international in focus. It might address a community issue (improving a local park, tackling litter on a school road), a rights-based question (raising awareness of disability access, campaigning for fair treatment of a group), a political question (engaging with local government on a public matter), or a broader social concern (food waste, youth mental health, veterans&rsquo; housing). What matters is that the issue arises from the specification content and that the action is aimed at delivering a benefit or change for others &mdash; not just for yourself.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Stage 1: Choosing the Issue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The first stage is identifying the issue you want to address. The issue must arise from the specification content, so it must connect to citizenship themes such as democracy, rights, equality, community or public services. It should be specific enough that you can take a focused action &mdash; examiners consistently reward actions that are tightly defined and locally grounded because these make evaluation easier and more meaningful.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">A common mistake at this stage is choosing an issue that is too broad or too vague. &ldquo;Save the environment&rdquo; or &ldquo;stop all crime&rdquo; are not workable citizenship action issues. A better framing would be: &ldquo;Reduce the amount of single-use plastic packaging sold in our school canteen&rdquo;, or &ldquo;Improve lighting on the path between school and the local bus stop to improve safety for students&rdquo;. Specific, focused issues allow you to take concrete action and evaluate your impact clearly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Stage 2: Research Using Primary and Secondary Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Before taking action, you need to understand the issue thoroughly. This means gathering evidence using both <dfn class="term" data-def="Information collected first-hand by the researcher, such as surveys, interviews or observations.">primary sources</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Information gathered from existing sources such as reports, articles, statistics or books produced by others.">secondary sources</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Primary research might include: conducting a survey of students or local residents about the issue; interviewing a local councillor, MP, headteacher or relevant expert; attending a public meeting or council session; or gathering your own observations and data (e.g. counting the number of cars that ignore a pedestrian crossing). Secondary research might include: reading local newspaper reports, ONS statistics, charity reports, parliamentary debate records, or academic studies on the issue.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe the difference between a primary and a secondary source, giving one example of each that a student could use in a citizenship action investigation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Primary sources are gathered first-hand (surveys you conduct, interviews you carry out, meetings you attend). Secondary sources are produced by others (reports, statistics, newspaper articles, government publications). Strong investigations use both: primary sources show what those directly affected think and experience; secondary sources provide context, background and comparison with other places or times.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Stage 3: Planning the Action</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Once you understand the issue, you plan what action to take. This should flow directly from your research: the evidence you have gathered should show why the action is needed, who the action is aimed at, and how the action addresses the issue. A good plan will include:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
  <li>A clear aim: what change do you want to bring about?</li>
  <li>The method: what will you actually do? (e.g. present a petition to the school council, write to your local MP, run a public awareness campaign, volunteer with a relevant organisation, establish a group)</li>
  <li>Who you will work with: most effective citizenship actions involve collaboration with others</li>
  <li>A realistic timeline and any resources or permissions needed</li>
  <li>How you will measure whether the action has worked</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Stages 4 and 5: Taking the Action and Assessing Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Stage 4 is taking the action itself. This is the practical phase: delivering your petition, sending your letters, running your campaign, attending the meeting, carrying out your volunteering. It is important to keep a record of what you actually did, including any evidence (photographs, copies of letters, notes from meetings, survey data) and any responses you received.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Stage 5 is assessing impact: did your action make a difference? <dfn class="term" data-def="A careful judgement of the value, effectiveness and outcomes of a citizenship action, considering what was achieved, what was not, and what could have been done differently.">Evaluation</dfn> requires honesty &mdash; not all actions achieve their intended aim, and recognising this (and explaining why) is itself good citizenship thinking. Did the council respond to your petition? Did the school change its policy? Did awareness of the issue increase? How do you know?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the six stages of a citizenship action investigation in the correct order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six stages: (1) Decide the question or issue; (2) Carry out initial research using primary and secondary sources; (3) Plan the action; (4) Take the action; (5) Assess the impact; (6) Evaluate the whole process. In the assessment, you will be asked about your own investigation &mdash; examiners reward answers where you name the issue, method and impact specifically, not vaguely.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Stage 6: Evaluating the Whole Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The final stage is reflecting on the whole investigation. This is different from assessing impact (stage 5): evaluation asks the deeper question &mdash; if you did this again, what would you do differently, and why? Was your initial research thorough enough? Did you choose the most effective method? Were there obstacles you did not anticipate? What have you learned about how change happens in your community or society?</p>

The more specific your action and aim, the stronger your evaluation can be.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">A citizenship action investigation follows six stages: choose an issue, research it using primary and secondary sources, plan the action, take the action, assess the impact, and evaluate the whole process.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Tightly focused, specific issues make for much stronger evaluations than broad or vague ones &mdash; examiners reward answers that name the issue, method and outcome precisely.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Evaluation means honest reflection on what worked, what did not, and what you would do differently &mdash; it is not the same as just describing what you did.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">In the active citizenship section, a very common error is describing an action in vague, general terms without naming the specific issue, method and outcome. Examiners reward answers where you state: what the issue was (specifically); what action you took (what you actually did, not just &ldquo;we campaigned&rdquo;); what happened as a result; and your honest evaluation of whether it worked and why. For an 8-mark &ldquo;Discuss&rdquo; question, present contrasting views on the effectiveness of citizenship action using your own investigation as evidence alongside any wider knowledge. For the 12-mark &ldquo;Evaluate&rdquo;, your conclusion must add evaluative thinking about what makes citizenship action effective &mdash; not just a summary of what you did. Phrases like &ldquo;we didn&rsquo;t quite achieve our aim but we learned that ...&rdquo; show genuine evaluation and can earn marks in the top band.</p>
