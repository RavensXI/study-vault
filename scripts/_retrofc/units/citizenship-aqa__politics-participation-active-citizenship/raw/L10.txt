--- DESCRIPTION ---
How public money is raised through taxation and spent on welfare, health, education and care.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Does Government Collect Tax?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Governments need money to run public services &mdash; things such as schools, hospitals, roads, the police and welfare benefits. This money is raised primarily through <dfn class="term" data-def="Compulsory payments made by individuals and businesses to the government, used to fund public services.">taxation</dfn>. In the UK, central government collects most taxes, and some funding is also raised by local councils through the council tax. Without taxation, the government would be unable to provide the services that citizens rely on throughout their lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The UK government collects tax in several different ways. <dfn class="term" data-def="A tax levied on the income a person earns from employment or self-employment.">Income tax</dfn> is paid by people who earn above a certain threshold &mdash; in 2024&ndash;25 the personal allowance (the amount you can earn before paying income tax) was &pound;12,570. National Insurance is another tax on earnings that funds state benefits and the NHS. <dfn class="term" data-def="A tax added to the price of most goods and services when they are sold, currently set at 20% on standard-rated items in the UK.">Value Added Tax (VAT)</dfn> is charged at 20 per cent on most goods and services and is paid indirectly by consumers when they make purchases. Corporation tax is paid by businesses on their profits.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two types of tax collected in the UK and what each one is charged on.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main forms of UK taxation are: income tax (on earnings), National Insurance (on earnings, funds benefits and the NHS), VAT (on most goods and services at 20%), and corporation tax (on company profits). Local government also raises money through council tax on residential properties.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">How Is Public Money Spent?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The UK government sets out its spending plans in the annual Budget, presented to Parliament by the Chancellor of the Exchequer. The largest areas of public spending are typically health (principally the National Health Service), social protection (welfare benefits and pensions), and education. Together these three areas account for the majority of government expenditure. Other significant areas include defence, transport infrastructure, and debt interest payments on money the government has borrowed in previous years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Local government also spends public money, funded partly by central government grants and partly by council tax. Local councils provide services including waste collection, local roads, social care for the elderly and disabled, planning, and early-years education. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which government allocates public funding across different services, balancing competing demands within a finite budget.">budgeting</dfn> process at both national and local level involves difficult trade-offs: increasing spending in one area often means cutting or holding back spending in another.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Debating Welfare Spending: Two Contrasting Views</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Supporters of generous welfare spending</strong> argue that a comprehensive welfare state ensures that no citizen is left without support during illness, unemployment, or old age. They contend that investment in welfare reduces poverty and inequality, generates social cohesion, and prevents the long-term costs associated with untreated mental and physical illness. They often point to the post-war settlement that created the NHS and the modern benefits system as evidence of what collective provision can achieve.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Critics of high welfare spending</strong> argue that an overly generous benefits system can reduce incentives to work and places a heavy burden on taxpayers and public debt. They suggest that welfare programmes must be means-tested and targeted at those most in need, rather than provided universally, in order to keep costs sustainable. They argue that a growing national debt creates long-term risks for the economy and future generations.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Both viewpoints reflect genuine political values about the role of the state, individual responsibility, and the balance between rights and responsibilities. There is no single &ldquo;correct&rdquo; answer to these debates &mdash; they form part of the ongoing democratic conversation about how public money should be used.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Health, Education and Elderly Care</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The NHS provides healthcare free at the point of use to all UK residents, funded by taxation and National Insurance. It is the largest single item of day-to-day public spending. Supporters of this model argue that universal free healthcare prevents a two-tier system where only the wealthy can afford good treatment. Critics argue that the NHS faces growing demand from an ageing population and that funding pressures require either higher taxes or a different funding model.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Education is funded largely through central government grants to schools, with additional funding channelled through local authorities. Early years, primary, secondary, and further education are all publicly funded, though there is ongoing debate about how university tuition should be funded &mdash; whether primarily through student loans, general taxation, or a combination of the two.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Support and assistance provided by local councils and health services to help elderly and disabled people live independently.">social care</dfn> system supports elderly and disabled people who need help with daily living. Unlike the NHS, social care is not entirely free at the point of use &mdash; individuals may need to contribute to the costs of residential or home care depending on their savings. This gap between NHS funding and social care funding has been a source of significant political debate, with different parties proposing different solutions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three largest areas of UK government spending. Write them from memory, then check.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three largest areas of UK government spending are health (the NHS), social protection (benefits and pensions), and education. Together they typically account for more than half of all public expenditure. Defence is a significant but smaller budget item.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Managing Risk and Budgetary Trade-offs</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Governments face the challenge of managing risk when making spending decisions. A sudden economic downturn, a public health emergency, or a natural disaster may require extra spending that was not planned in the annual Budget. To manage this risk, governments may hold reserves, borrow money by issuing government bonds (known as gilts), or adjust tax rates. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between what a government spends and what it raises in tax in a given year.">budget deficit</dfn> occurs when spending exceeds tax revenues. When deficits accumulate over time they add to the national debt, which must eventually be repaid through future taxation or spending cuts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Local councils face similar challenges but with much less room to manoeuvre. Unlike the central government, councils cannot borrow freely or print money. When central government grants are reduced, councils must choose which services to cut or reduce &mdash; decisions that directly affect communities and are sometimes highly controversial locally.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Taxation &mdash; including income tax, National Insurance, VAT and corporation tax &mdash; is how government raises the money needed to fund public services. Local government also raises money through council tax.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The three largest areas of UK public spending are health (the NHS), social protection (welfare and pensions), and education. Budgeting involves difficult trade-offs because increasing one area of spending often requires cuts elsewhere.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">There are genuine political debates about how much governments should spend on welfare, health and education, with supporters arguing for collective provision and critics raising concerns about cost, incentives and long-term debt. Extended-response questions expect you to present both sides fairly.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For an 8-mark &ldquo;Discuss&rdquo; or 12-mark &ldquo;Evaluate&rdquo; question on government spending, the most common mistake is writing a one-sided answer that either supports high public spending or criticises it without presenting the opposing view. Examiners reward answers that use connective language (&ldquo;however&rdquo;, &ldquo;on the other hand&rdquo;) and name a specific real-world example &mdash; for instance, the creation of the NHS, debates about welfare reform, or the distinction between health and social care funding. For a 4-mark &ldquo;Describe&rdquo; question, avoid drifting into evaluation; give two clearly described features and develop each one. For source-interpretation questions, do not simply list what the source says &mdash; reach a judgement about what it reveals and link it to your own knowledge of taxation or spending priorities.</p>
