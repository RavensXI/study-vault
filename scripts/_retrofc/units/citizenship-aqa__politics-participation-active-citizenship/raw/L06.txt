<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Political Party?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An organisation of people who share similar political beliefs and seek to win elections in order to form a government and put their policies into practice.">political party</dfn> is an organisation that contests elections with the aim of forming a government. Parties have a shared set of values and policies, and they field <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who stands for election in a constituency, seeking to be chosen as the local representative in Parliament.">candidates</dfn> in parliamentary constituencies across the country. The party with the most seats in the House of Commons after a general election normally forms the government. This is fundamentally different from a pressure group, which tries to influence policy without fielding candidates or seeking to govern.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Political parties perform several important functions in a democracy: they give voters a clear choice between different visions for the country; they recruit and train politicians; they organise debate in Parliament; and they provide an official Opposition to hold the government to account. The Leader of the Opposition leads the largest party not in government and challenges the government at PMQs and in debates.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name four functions political parties carry out in a democracy, without looking at the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Political parties contest elections to form governments, give voters clear policy choices, train politicians, and provide an organised Opposition to scrutinise the government.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Major UK Parties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Several parties contest UK general elections. Each has a distinct philosophical tradition, though all modern parties combine ideas from more than one tradition in practice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>The Conservative Party</strong> traces its roots to the belief that society is best served by gradual change, respect for tradition, and private enterprise. Supporters argue that lower taxes encourage investment and economic growth, that individuals and families should have freedom to make their own choices, and that proven institutions (the monarchy, Parliament, the judiciary) should be respected and reformed carefully rather than radically overhauled. Critics argue that Conservative policies can widen inequality and that traditional institutions sometimes protect vested interests rather than ordinary citizens.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>The Labour Party</strong> emerged from the trade union movement and socialist tradition. Supporters argue that collective action &mdash; through the state, trade unions and public services &mdash; reduces poverty and inequality, and that strong public services like the NHS give everyone a fair start regardless of wealth. They point to the creation of the NHS in 1948 as an example of transformative collective action. Critics argue that high public spending requires high taxation, which can reduce incentives for work and investment, and that centralised state provision is less efficient than market competition.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>The Liberal Democrats</strong> stand in the liberal tradition, emphasising individual rights and freedoms, an open economy and strong pro-European foreign policy. Supporters argue that individual liberty, civil rights and internationalism make society more prosperous and fair. Critics argue that centrist positioning means the party lacks a clear distinct identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>The Scottish National Party (SNP)</strong> and <strong>Plaid Cymru</strong> advocate for Scottish and Welsh independence or greater autonomy respectively, combining left-of-centre social policies with a nationalist identity. The <strong>Green Party</strong> places environmental and social justice concerns at the centre of its programme. <strong>Reform UK</strong> has campaigned on lower taxes, stricter immigration controls and scepticism of international institutions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">The terms <dfn class="term" data-def="A political position broadly associated with greater equality, collective action and state intervention to redistribute wealth and protect public services.">left-wing</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A political position broadly associated with tradition, individual liberty, free markets and limited government intervention in the economy.">right-wing</dfn> come from the seating arrangement in the French revolutionary assembly of 1789, where supporters of the king sat to the right and reformers to the left. Today these terms describe broad tendencies: left-wing parties tend to favour greater state intervention, redistribution of wealth and strong public services; right-wing parties tend to favour free markets, lower taxes and personal responsibility.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">In practice, modern parties blend ideas from across the spectrum. Most UK parties occupy a centre ground on many issues, with differences of degree rather than principle. Students should be careful not to treat these as fixed labels &mdash; party positions shift over time and parties can move left or right on different issues at different moments. These are analytical tools, not exact descriptions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Key Philosophical Differences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The most consistent philosophical debate in UK politics is between those who believe the state should play a large role in reducing inequality and providing services, and those who believe the individual and the market are better placed to make choices than central government. On economic policy, this plays out as debates over tax and spending, public ownership, and the role of trade unions. On social policy, it plays out as debates over freedom of choice versus collective provision.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A second important divide concerns constitutional and political reform. Some parties support proportional representation (to give smaller parties fairer representation) and an elected House of Lords; others defend First Past the Post and the existing constitutional settlement. Devolution and the future of the Union also divide parties &mdash; the SNP and Plaid Cymru seek independence or greater autonomy, while the Conservatives and Labour both support the Union but differ on the degree of devolution they would grant.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down one philosophical difference between the Conservative and Labour parties on economic policy, and one difference on constitutional reform.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The central philosophical debate in UK politics is between greater state intervention (Labour tradition) and greater individual freedom and market economics (Conservative tradition). Both parties have shifted positions across decades.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">This is one of the most common errors in Citizenship exams. Both parties and pressure groups campaign on issues and try to shape public policy &mdash; but there is a fundamental difference. Political parties seek to win elections and form a government. They field candidates in constituencies, compete for seats and, if successful, take executive power and responsibility for running the country.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Pressure groups (such as a trade union, a housing charity or an environmental campaign) seek to <em>influence</em> the government&rsquo;s decisions without seeking governmental power themselves. They lobby, petition, campaign and demonstrate &mdash; but they do not stand for election or take responsibility for governing. This distinction is crucial: confusing the two in an exam answer will cost marks.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Political parties contest elections to form governments, giving voters a choice between different policies and values &mdash; this is distinct from pressure groups, which seek to influence policy without governing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The major UK parties differ philosophically on the role of the state: Labour&rsquo;s roots lie in collectivism and public provision; the Conservatives emphasise free markets and individual responsibility; the Liberal Democrats stress civil liberties and internationalism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Key ongoing debates divide the parties on economic policy, constitutional reform (electoral systems, the Lords), and the future of devolution and the Union.</li>
</ul>

Examiners reward answers that name the philosophical tradition (e.g. collectivism, liberalism, conservatism) and link it to a specific policy example (e.g. NHS creation as collective action, or low-tax policy as belief in market freedom). Your conclusion must make a substantiated judgement &mdash; for example, weighing which approach better delivers democratic choice for voters. Phrases like &ldquo;both have good and bad points&rdquo; without further reasoning will not access the top band.</p>
