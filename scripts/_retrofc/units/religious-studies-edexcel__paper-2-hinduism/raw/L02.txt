--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Atman</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">One of the most important concepts in Hinduism is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The eternal, individual self or soul; the divine spark within every living being that is ultimately identical with or related to Brahman.">atman</dfn>. Unlike the Buddhist teaching of anatta (no fixed self), Hinduism teaches that each individual has an eternal self &mdash; the atman &mdash; which is divine in nature. The atman is not the ego or personality (which changes), but the deepest, unchanging core of what a person truly is.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="One of the oldest and largest Upanishads, containing philosophical discussions on the nature of the self, Brahman and rebirth; 4.4 discusses kamma and rebirth.">Brihadaranyaka Upanishad</dfn> 4.4 teaches that the atman continues after death, taking a new body determined by a person&rsquo;s actions (karma) in the current life. The atman is what transmigrates &mdash; it is not destroyed at death but moves from body to body through the cycle of rebirth.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n4">Hinduism&rsquo;s unique concept of atman is compared with both Buddhism and Christianity in wider RS discussions. Key contrast with Buddhism: Hinduism affirms a permanent, transmigrating self (atman); Buddhism denies a permanent self (anatta) &mdash; what transmigrates is not a fixed self but a causal stream of consciousness and karma. For the Christian comparison: Christianity teaches bodily resurrection and a personal soul created by God; Hinduism teaches an eternal atman cycling through many lives until liberation (moksha). <h2 data-narration-id="n5">Karma and Samsara</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="In Hinduism, the moral law of cause and effect: every action (physical, verbal or mental) produces consequences that shape future lives.">Karma</dfn> is the law of moral causation. Every intentional action produces a consequence &mdash; good actions create positive karma that leads to better future lives; harmful actions create negative karma leading to lower rebirths. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4 describes how at death a person&rsquo;s karma shapes what body the atman takes next: &ldquo;A man becomes good through good deeds and evil through evil deeds.&rdquo;</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Karma drives <dfn class="term" data-def="The cycle of death and rebirth that the atman passes through, driven by karma, until liberation (moksha) is achieved.">samsara</dfn> &mdash; the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth. As long as a person is driven by desires, attachments and ignorance, they continue to generate karma and remain in samsara. Samsara can span countless lives across different forms &mdash; human, animal, divine or hellish realms &mdash; depending on accumulated karma.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: what is the relationship between karma, samsara and moksha? Write three connected sentences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="Liberation from the cycle of samsara; the ultimate spiritual goal in Hinduism, achieved when the atman is freed from the bondage of karma and desire.">Moksha</dfn> is liberation from samsara. It is the ultimate goal of Hindu spiritual practice. When all karma is exhausted and the atman recognises its true nature (as identical with Brahman, in Advaita Vedanta, or as eternally related to Bhagavan, in Vaishnavism), the cycle of rebirth ends. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4 is the named source linking karma, the atman and the process of rebirth and liberation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Four Aims of Life (Purusharthas)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Hindu teaching identifies four legitimate aims for human life, known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The four aims or purposes of human life in Hinduism: dharma (duty/righteousness), artha (wealth/prosperity), kama (pleasure/desire) and moksha (liberation).">Purusharthas</dfn>:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Duty, righteousness, and moral order; the first of the four aims of life and the foundation of Hindu ethics.">Dharma</dfn> (duty/righteousness) is the first and foundational aim &mdash; living according to one&rsquo;s moral obligations, social responsibilities and cosmic order. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The eternal law or divine moral order that underlies the universe; Hinduism understood as the eternal truth.">anatana dharma</dfn> (eternal law/truth) is the broader cosmic principle that dharma reflects.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Wealth, prosperity and material success; the second of the four aims of life, legitimately pursued within the bounds of dharma.">Artha</dfn> (wealth/prosperity) is the pursuit of material success, legitimately earned within the bounds of dharma. <dfn class="term" data-def="Pleasure, desire and enjoyment; the third of the four aims of life, considered legitimate when pursued ethically within dharma.">Kama</dfn> (pleasure/desire) is the enjoyment of life&rsquo;s pleasures &mdash; including love, beauty and sensory experience &mdash; when pursued ethically. Moksha remains the ultimate aim.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The sacred scripture of Hinduism, presented as a dialogue between the warrior Arjuna and his charioteer Krishna (an avatar of Vishnu); central text for Hindu ethics and spirituality.">Bhagavad Gita</dfn> 3.35 teaches the importance of following one&rsquo;s own dharma rather than imitating another&rsquo;s: &ldquo;Better is one&rsquo;s own dharma, though imperfectly performed, than the dharma of another well performed.&rdquo; This verse underpins the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of duties appropriate to one's social position (varna) and stage of life (ashrama) in Hindu society.">varnashrama dharma</dfn> &mdash; the duties specific to each person&rsquo;s social position and stage of life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name all four Purusharthas and give one reason why each is important. Then name the scripture that teaches varnashrama dharma.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Bhagavad Gita 3.35 is the named source for dharma and varnashrama dharma. The Purusharthas (dharma, artha, kama, moksha) map out a complete life: ethical duty, material success, pleasure and ultimately spiritual liberation. Varnashrama dharma specifies duties according to social position (varna) and life stage (ashrama: student, householder, forest-dweller, renunciant).</p>
</div>

    <span>Misconception: is varnashrama dharma the same as the caste system?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Students often conflate varnashrama dharma with the hereditary caste system. The distinction matters for the exam. Varnashrama dharma in classical Hindu texts refers to duties appropriate to one&rsquo;s social role (varna) and stage of life (ashrama). The Bhagavad Gita&rsquo;s teaching focuses on carrying out one&rsquo;s duties faithfully as an act of worship. However, in practice the varna system became the hereditary caste system, which Hindu reformers such as Gandhi challenged. Many modern Hindus distinguish between the ideal varnashrama dharma in scripture and the social inequalities of the caste system. The 12-mark Discuss question may ask whether following dharma is always morally right &mdash; this divergence is relevant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The atman is the eternal individual self that transmigrates through samsara, shaped by karma. The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4 is the named source. Moksha is liberation from samsara &mdash; the ultimate Hindu goal.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The four Purusharthas are dharma (duty), artha (wealth), kama (pleasure) and moksha (liberation) &mdash; a map of the complete human life. Anatana dharma is the eternal moral order underlying all.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Bhagavad Gita 3.35 teaches each person to follow their own dharma; varnashrama dharma specifies duties according to social role (varna) and life stage (ashrama).</li>
</ul>
