--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Hindu Responses to Suffering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Hinduism offers several teachings about why suffering exists and how to respond to it. Key virtues include <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle of non-violence; the commitment to avoiding harm to any living being, central to Hindu ethics and spirituality.">ahimsa</dfn> (non-violence), respect, empathy, mind/sense control, humility and love. The <dfn class="term" data-def="One of the two great Sanskrit epics of ancient India; contains the Bhagavad Gita and many key Hindu teachings on ethics and dharma.">Mahabharata</dfn> 5.39 states that the highest dharma is not to harm any living being &mdash; this links ahimsa directly to dharma as an ethical foundation for responding to suffering.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Suffering in Hindu thought is ultimately explained by karma &mdash; actions in previous lives shape present experience. This does not mean Hindus accept suffering passively: dharmic living, devotion (bhakti) and spiritual practice (yoga) are all active responses to suffering that work to purify karma and move the atman toward liberation. Knowledge and wisdom (jnana) help Hindus understand the real from the unreal, reducing the delusion that generates fresh suffering.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Hindu Cosmology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Hindu cosmology is vast in scale and deeply philosophical. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The oldest and largest of the four Vedas, containing hymns, cosmological speculations and descriptions of Vedic deities.">Rig Veda</dfn> 10.129 (the &lsquo;Hymn of Creation&rsquo; or Nasadiya Sukta) reflects on the mystery of creation itself: before anything existed, there was neither being nor non-being; the universe arose from a primordial impulse of desire. This cosmological humility &mdash; acknowledging that even the gods may not know how creation began &mdash; is distinctive in Hindu scripture.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Time in Hindu cosmology is cyclical and vast. The universe passes through four ages called <dfn class="term" data-def="The four ages or cycles of the universe in Hindu cosmology: Satya Yuga (golden age), Treta Yuga, Dvapara Yuga and Kali Yuga (current age of decline).">yugas</dfn>: Satya Yuga (the golden age of truth and righteousness), Treta Yuga, Dvapara Yuga and <dfn class="term" data-def="The current age in Hindu cosmology: an age of moral decline, conflict and spiritual degeneration, described in the Mahabharata.">Kali Yuga</dfn> (the present age, characterised by moral decline and spiritual degeneration, described in the Mahabharata). Together these four yugas form a Mahayuga, and thousands of Mahayugas form an even larger cycle.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three key Sanskrit concepts of Hindu cosmology from this lesson, and which scripture anchors the creation hymn?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three cosmological concepts: <dfn class="term" data-def="The material world or primordial matter in Hindu philosophy; one of the two fundamental realities (alongside Purusha/consciousness) in Samkhya philosophy.">Prakriti</dfn> (matter/nature &mdash; the material aspect of the universe), <dfn class="term" data-def="The three qualities or modes that make up all of material nature: sattva (purity/harmony), rajas (activity/passion) and tamas (inertia/darkness).">tri-guna</dfn> (the three qualities of matter: sattva &mdash; purity/harmony, rajas &mdash; activity/passion, tamas &mdash; inertia/darkness) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Illusion; the power that causes beings to mistake the phenomenal world for ultimate reality, concealing the true nature of Brahman.">maya</dfn> (illusion &mdash; the power that causes beings to mistake the world of appearance for the ultimate reality of Brahman). The Rig Veda 10.129 is the named source for Hindu cosmological reflection.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Forms of Hindu Yoga</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Literally 'union': in Hinduism, a spiritual discipline or path that unites the practitioner with Brahman. The major forms are karma yoga, jnana yoga, raja/astanga yoga and bhakti yoga.">Yoga</dfn> means &lsquo;union&rsquo; in Sanskrit. In Hinduism it refers to the spiritual disciplines that unite the practitioner with Brahman. There are four main paths:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The yoga of action: performing one's duties selflessly, without attachment to results, as an offering to God.">Karma yoga</dfn> is the path of selfless action. The Bhagavad Gita 6.44&ndash;47 describes the value of persistent practice. Earlier in the Gita, Krishna teaches performing one&rsquo;s duty without attachment to results &mdash; every action becomes an offering to God rather than a source of new karma-bondage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The yoga of knowledge: using discrimination between the real (Brahman) and the unreal (the phenomenal world) to dissolve ignorance and achieve liberation.">Jnana yoga</dfn> is the path of wisdom and knowledge &mdash; using intellectual discrimination to see through maya and perceive Brahman directly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The yoga of meditation: eight-limbed royal path of Patanjali combining ethical conduct, physical practice, breath control and meditation to still the mind.">Raja yoga</dfn> (astanga yoga) is the path of meditation. The Bhagavad Gita 6.20&ndash;31 describes the state of a yogi whose mind is completely still and sees the self in all beings and all beings in the self.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The yoga of devotion: the path of loving worship directed toward a personal deity (especially Vishnu/Krishna or Shiva), regarded by many as the most accessible path to liberation.">Bhakti yoga</dfn> is the path of loving devotion to a personal deity. The Bhagavad Gita 9.13&ndash;27 describes how even a leaf, a flower, fruit or water offered with devotion is accepted by Krishna &mdash; making bhakti accessible to everyone regardless of social position. This is the most widely practised path in popular Hinduism.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Forms of Hindu Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hindu worship takes many forms. The Bhagavad Gita 9.13&ndash;27 specifically describes worship through offering and devotion:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The central act of Hindu worship: a ritual of offering (flowers, food, water, incense, light) to a deity, performed at home or in the temple.">Puja</dfn> is the ritual offering to the deity &mdash; flowers, food (prasad), water, incense and light. <dfn class="term" data-def="A Hindu fire sacrifice in which offerings are made into a sacred fire as an act of worship and purification.">Havan</dfn> is the sacred fire ceremony. <dfn class="term" data-def="Auspicious sight of the deity; the act of seeing and being seen by God in the temple, considered a blessing in itself.">Darshan</dfn> is the auspicious sight of the deity &mdash; Hindus come to the temple not primarily to hear a sermon but to see the deity and receive their gaze in return.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list five forms of Hindu worship with one word to describe each. Then identify which Bhagavad Gita verse describes bhakti worship through simple offerings.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key worship forms: <strong>puja</strong> (ritual offering), <strong>havan</strong> (fire sacrifice), <strong>darshan</strong> (auspicious sight of deity), <dfn class="term" data-def="Devotional songs sung in praise of a deity; a form of bhakti worship performed individually or communally.">bhajan</dfn> (devotional song), <dfn class="term" data-def="Communal devotional singing, often call-and-response, as a form of bhakti worship.">kirtan</dfn> (communal chanting/singing) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The repetition of God's name or a sacred mantra as a form of meditation and devotion.">japa</dfn> (repetition of God&rsquo;s name). All reflect the Bhagavad Gita 9.13&ndash;27 principle that sincere devotion in any form is accepted by Krishna.</p>
</div>

It is performed morning and evening in temples and homes. The flame represents the light of divine knowledge and devotion. After arti, worshippers cup their hands over the flame and bring the warmth to their faces, symbolising receiving the deity&rsquo;s blessing. Arti is the most universally practised Hindu devotional act &mdash; it is both deeply personal and communally unifying.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Hindu responses to suffering include ahimsa, respect and empathy (Mahabharata 5.39). Suffering is ultimately explained by karma, with yoga and devotion as the response. Hindu cosmology features the yugas (Kali Yuga is the current age) and the concepts of prakriti, tri-guna and maya; Rig Veda 10.129 is the cosmological anchor.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Four paths of yoga: karma yoga (selfless action), jnana yoga (wisdom), raja yoga (meditation &mdash; Bhagavad Gita 6.20&ndash;31) and bhakti yoga (devotion &mdash; Bhagavad Gita 9.13&ndash;27). All lead to Brahman by different routes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Forms of Hindu worship: puja, havan, darshan, arti, bhajan, kirtan and japa. Bhagavad Gita 9.13&ndash;27 teaches that sincere devotion in any form &mdash; even a simple leaf or flower &mdash; is accepted by Krishna.</li>
</ul>
