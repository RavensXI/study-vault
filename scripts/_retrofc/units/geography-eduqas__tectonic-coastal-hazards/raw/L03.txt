--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why erosion and flooding threaten some coastal communities more than others.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Some Coasts Are at Greater Risk</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Around 600 million people worldwide live within 10 metres of sea level. Coastal communities face two main hazards: <dfn class="term" data-def="The wearing away of cliffs and beaches by waves, weather and rivers, removing material and reshaping the coastline.">coastal erosion</dfn> — the gradual loss of cliffs and beaches — and <dfn class="term" data-def="The temporary submerging of coastal land when seawater is driven inland by storms, surges or rising sea levels.">coastal flooding</dfn>, when the sea overtops or breaches defences. Some places suffer far more than others because of physical, social and economic factors that combine to raise vulnerability.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Physical Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Geology.</strong> Soft rock cliffs (clay, sandstone) erode many times faster than hard rock cliffs (granite, limestone). The Holderness coast in East Yorkshire is the most rapidly eroding coastline in Europe — its glacial-till cliffs retreat at an average of 1.8 m per year, and over 30 villages have been lost since Roman times.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Coastal shape.</strong> Bays focus wave energy on headlands. Estuaries channel storm surges inland. Low-lying flat coasts are exposed to flooding because there is no high ground for the sea to break against. Around 30% of the Netherlands sits below sea level.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Storms and climate.</strong> Severe weather raises wave height and storm surges. The 1953 North Sea flood was caused by a storm surge that pushed sea levels up to 5.6 m above normal in places, killing 326 people in eastern England and over 1,800 in the Netherlands.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Climate change</strong> magnifies all of the above. Sea level around the UK has risen around 16 cm since 1900 and is projected to rise a further 30–115 cm by 2100. Warmer seas fuel stronger storms; melting polar ice adds to global volume; expanding warm water adds further to sea level. <dfn class="term" data-def="The long-term increase in average global sea level caused by melting ice and the thermal expansion of warming oceans.">Sea level rise</dfn> turns rare flood events into common ones.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three physical factors that make a coastline vulnerable to erosion or flooding.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Physical vulnerability rises where geology is soft, the coast is low-lying, and storms are frequent. Holderness, East Yorkshire, retreats around 1.8 m per year — the fastest-eroding coast in Europe — while the Netherlands sits up to 7 m below sea level in places.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">The Holderness cliffs are made of glacial till deposited around 18,000 years ago — a soft mix of clay, sand and stones with no real strength against waves. Long fetches across the North Sea allow waves to build up power before striking. Around 30 villages including Auburn, Hartburn and Ravenser Odd have been lost to the sea since Roman times.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">At Mappleton, sea defences (rock groynes and an offshore reef) built in 1991 have protected the village. But the trapped sediment no longer travels south by longshore drift, so the cliffs at Great Cowden, just down the coast, have eroded faster than ever — by up to 10 m a year. Defending one settlement worsens vulnerability further along the coast.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Social and Economic Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Population density.</strong> A high concentration of people on a low-lying coast turns a flood into a disaster. Bangladesh has 170 million people in a delta only metres above sea level — Cyclone Sidr (2007) killed around 3,400 there.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Income.</strong> Wealthier countries can afford taller sea walls, better warnings and rapid recovery. The Netherlands invests over €1 billion a year in its Delta Works programme. Bangladesh has fewer resources to spread among many vulnerable districts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Infrastructure.</strong> Roads, embankments and emergency shelters reduce flood deaths. Bangladesh has built around 12,000 km of embankments and over 14,000 cyclone shelters since the 1991 cyclone (140,000 deaths). The 2020 Cyclone Amphan, despite being more powerful, killed around 100 people in Bangladesh because evacuation worked.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Government and education.</strong> Long-term coastal planning, warning systems and community drills cut deaths. Local responses to UK Met Office warnings during 2013 and 2014 winter storms saw around 800,000 people protected by improved Environment Agency forecasts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A Tale of Two Vulnerabilities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Maldives and the Netherlands are both extremely low-lying. The Maldives sits at an average elevation of just 1.5 m above sea level — with no high ground to retreat to and limited income (US$13,000 GNI per capita), the country is one of the world’s most vulnerable. The Netherlands is even lower in places but has used dykes, surge barriers and storm-water pumps for centuries to keep its 17 million people safe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The same physical hazard — sea level rise — produces very different risks because of human factors. The Netherlands plans for a metre of sea level rise by 2100 with confidence; the Maldives is investing in artificial islands such as Hulhumalé (built up to 2 m above sea level) and lobbying internationally for cuts in greenhouse-gas emissions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three social or economic factors that make a coastline more vulnerable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vulnerability rises with population density, low income, weak infrastructure and limited education or warning systems. The Netherlands and the Maldives both lie low — but only the Netherlands can spend €1 billion a year on defences.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Three climate change processes drive sea level higher. <strong>Thermal expansion</strong> — warm seawater takes up more space; this alone has caused around half of recent sea level rise. <strong>Melting glaciers</strong> — ice in mountain ranges and on Greenland and Antarctica adds new water to the oceans. <strong>Stronger storms</strong> — warmer seas fuel taller storm surges that breach defences set for older sea levels.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">A 1-in-100-year flood today may become a 1-in-10-year flood by 2100. Engineers in the Thames Estuary already plan a Thames Barrier replacement for the 2070s. Communities that simply rebuild after each flood — rather than adapt — are increasingly likely to be flooded again within years rather than decades.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Why Vulnerability Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Coastal vulnerability is not destiny. The same length of low-lying coast can support a thriving city like Rotterdam or be a slow-motion disaster like the Holderness coast. Geographers map vulnerability so that planners can target money where it saves most lives, and so that international agreements (such as the UN Loss and Damage Fund agreed in 2022) can support countries that have done least to cause climate change but face the steepest cost.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Coastal vulnerability comes from physical factors (soft geology, low-lying land, storms, climate change) and human factors (population, income, infrastructure, education).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Holderness in East Yorkshire is the fastest-eroding coast in Europe at around 1.8 m/yr; over 30 villages have been lost since Roman times.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The same hazard — sea level rise — threatens the Maldives (low income, no retreat ground) and the Netherlands (low-lying but rich), with very different death-toll outcomes.</li>
</ul>
