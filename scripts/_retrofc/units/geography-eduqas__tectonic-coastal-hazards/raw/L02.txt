--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why some communities suffer more from quakes and eruptions, and how risk is reduced.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Hazard, Risk and Vulnerability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A volcanic eruption on an empty island is a hazard but not a disaster. The same eruption next to a city becomes catastrophic. Geographers separate three ideas: a <strong>hazard</strong> is the natural event itself, <dfn class="term" data-def="The likelihood of harm to people, property or the economy when a hazard meets a community that is exposed to it.">risk</dfn> is the chance that hazard does damage, and <dfn class="term" data-def="How likely a community is to suffer harm from a hazard, shaped by physical, social and economic factors.">vulnerability</dfn> is how exposed and unprotected a community is when the hazard strikes. Reducing risk means lowering vulnerability — you cannot stop the planet from moving.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Impacts of Tectonic Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Earthquakes, tsunamis and volcanic eruptions damage three things: health, infrastructure and economy. The 2010 Haiti earthquake (M7.0) killed an estimated 230,000 people, injured 300,000 and damaged or destroyed around 250,000 homes — impacts on health and infrastructure that triggered an economic collapse. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan (M9.0) was over 1,000 times more powerful but killed fewer people (around 18,500) because of stronger buildings and a tsunami warning system — though damage to the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant cost an estimated US$235 billion.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many people died in the 2010 Haiti earthquake versus the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, and why did the difference happen?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Haiti 2010 (M7.0) killed around 230,000 people. Tōhoku 2011 (M9.0) was vastly more powerful but killed about 18,500. The death-toll gap reflects vulnerability — weak buildings and limited emergency services in Haiti, against earthquake-resistant design and warning systems in Japan.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Physical Factors That Increase Vulnerability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Magnitude.</strong> Larger earthquakes (measured on the moment magnitude scale) and bigger volcanic eruptions (measured on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, VEI) release more energy and threaten more people.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Volcanic features.</strong> Some volcanoes produce uniquely deadly hazards. <dfn class="term" data-def="A fast-moving current of hot gas, ash and rock that flows down the side of an erupting volcano, capable of destroying everything in its path.">Pyroclastic flows</dfn> can move at over 100 km/h and reach 700°C — they wiped out 28,000 people in Saint-Pierre, Martinique in 1902. <strong>Lava flows</strong> burn buildings but rarely kill because they move slowly. <strong>Lahars</strong> are mudflows of melted snow, ash and rock; the 1985 Nevado del Ruiz lahar destroyed Armero, Colombia, killing 23,000 in minutes. <strong>Ash clouds</strong> can collapse roofs, ruin crops and ground aircraft — the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull eruption shut European airspace for six days.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>One Volcanic Hazard Example: Mount Pinatubo, 1991</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Mount Pinatubo erupted in the Philippines on 15 June 1991 (VEI 6 — the second-largest eruption of the twentieth century). Pyroclastic flows reached 16 km from the summit; ash fell across Luzon and contributed to roof collapses; lahars triggered by typhoon rains continued for years afterwards. Around 800 people died in total.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The death toll was kept low — despite the size of the eruption — because the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, working with the US Geological Survey, monitored the build-up for two months and persuaded around 60,000 residents and 14,500 US Air Force personnel at Clark Air Base to evacuate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Social and Economic Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Two countries can experience identical hazards and very different disasters. Vulnerability rises when:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Population density</strong> is high — Port-au-Prince in Haiti held 2.5 million people in poorly built housing concentrated near the fault. <strong>Income</strong> is low — rebuilding takes longer and lacks insurance. <strong>Building quality</strong> is poor — unreinforced concrete and masonry collapse before timber-framed or steel-framed buildings. <strong>Emergency services</strong> are limited — Haiti had no organised national fire service in 2010 and lost much of its police force to building collapse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Education</strong> matters too: Japanese schools run earthquake drills monthly, and tsunami evacuation routes are signposted along the coast. Haiti had run no such drills. <strong>Government</strong> and <strong>infrastructure</strong> shape recovery: Tōhoku rebuilt within a decade; parts of Port-au-Prince are still in temporary housing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down four social or economic factors that change how badly a community suffers in a tectonic disaster.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vulnerability is shaped by population density, average income, building quality, emergency-service capacity, education and government effectiveness. Two countries can face the same hazard with very different death tolls.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Reducing Risk — Monitoring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of scientific instruments to detect signs that an earthquake or eruption may happen, allowing early warnings to be issued.">Monitoring</dfn> uses seismometers, GPS sensors that track ground bulge, gas-emission detectors and satellite thermal imagery. The Iceland Met Office watched gas and ground deformation around the Reykjanes Peninsula throughout 2023, allowing the town of Grindavík to evacuate before the November and December 2023 eruptions cut off the town.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For earthquakes, monitoring buys seconds rather than days. Japan’s Earthquake Early Warning system uses primary waves from a fault rupture to broadcast a warning before slower, damaging surface waves arrive — trains can brake automatically and surgeons can pause operations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Reducing Risk — Hazard Mapping, Building, Planning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Hazard maps</strong> show areas at risk of pyroclastic flows, lahars or earthquake shaking. Planning authorities use them to ban housing on the highest-risk land and to design evacuation routes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Building technology</strong> reduces damage during the event. Earthquake-resistant designs include steel cross-bracing, deep foundations, base isolators (rubber pads that let a building rock without breaking) and counterweights at the top to dampen sway. The Tokyo Skytree (634 m) uses central mass-damper technology to survive the largest earthquakes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Emergency planning</strong> covers evacuation, shelter and supplies. Japan’s annual Disaster Prevention Day on 1 September rehearses tsunami evacuation across the whole country. In tsunami-prone coastal towns, vertical-evacuation buildings are constructed inland of risk zones.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">The 11 March 2011 Tōhoku earthquake (M9.0) shook for six minutes and triggered a tsunami over 14 m high in places. Around 18,500 people died — over 90% from the tsunami rather than the shaking. Building codes meant that even tall structures in Tokyo (370 km from the epicentre) survived without collapse.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Lessons learned: tsunami sea walls had been built to 5–10 m heights and were overtopped. Reconstruction has raised some walls to 14 m, relocated towns onto higher ground, and added vertical-evacuation buildings. Japan’s J-Alert warning system now sends earthquake and tsunami alerts to all phones within seconds.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Risk = hazard × vulnerability. The same magnitude earthquake can kill 230,000 in Haiti and 18,500 in Japan because of building quality, income, emergency services and education.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Volcanic hazards include pyroclastic flows, lahars, lava flows and ash clouds. Pyroclastic flows are the most lethal — over 700°C and 100 km/h.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Risk is reduced through monitoring (seismometers, gas sensors), hazard mapping, earthquake-resistant building design and emergency planning — Pinatubo’s 60,000-person evacuation in 1991 saved tens of thousands of lives.</li>
</ul>
