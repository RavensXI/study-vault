--- DESCRIPTION ---
Hard and soft engineering, ICZM and sustainable responses to rising seas in the UK and beyond.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Coastlines Need Managing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Around the UK, roughly 17 million people live within 5 km of the coast. Storms, rising sea levels and the erosion of soft cliffs put homes, farmland, businesses and ecosystems at risk. Coastal managers have to balance the cost of protecting the coast against the value of what is being lost — and they have to do this with the climate changing faster than the cliffs themselves. <dfn class="term" data-def="A reasoned comparison of the costs and the benefits of a course of action, used to decide whether to invest in coastal defences.">Cost-benefit analysis</dfn> is at the heart of every decision: spend a million pounds to save a road, or accept that the road will fall into the sea?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four hard engineering strategies and one drawback of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Coastal management uses two main approaches — hard engineering (working against the sea) and soft engineering (working with natural processes). Most modern Shoreline Management Plans combine both.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Hard Engineering Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Coastal defences that build large, artificial structures to resist the sea.">Hard engineering</dfn> uses solid, engineered structures to absorb or block wave energy. The four main strategies are sea walls, rock armour (rip-rap), groynes and gabions. A sea wall reflects waves back out to sea and protects the land behind it; a typical concrete sea wall costs around £5,000 per metre but has a lifespan of fifty years or more. Rock armour is large boulders dumped at the foot of cliffs to break up wave energy. Groynes are wooden or rock fences built across the beach to trap sediment moving by longshore drift, widening the beach so it absorbs wave energy before it reaches the cliff. Gabions are wire cages of stones used as a cheaper, shorter-life option in lower-risk locations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Hard engineering works quickly and gives communities visible reassurance, but the structures are expensive, can scar the landscape and may starve neighbouring stretches of coast of sediment. The classic example is Mappleton on the Holderness coast: rock groynes built in the 1990s now protect the village, but the cliffs to the south are eroding faster because longshore drift can no longer replenish their beaches.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Soft Engineering Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Coastal defences that work with natural processes such as longshore drift, dunes and salt marsh to reduce erosion.">Soft engineering</dfn> works with the natural system instead of against it. Beach nourishment imports sand from offshore to widen a beach so it absorbs more wave energy — the sand at Bournemouth has been topped up several times since 1970. Dune regeneration plants marram grass and uses fences to rebuild sand dunes that act as a natural sea wall. Salt-marsh creation deliberately allows low-value land to flood so that mud, plants and tidal channels can develop — the marsh then absorbs storm surges before they reach the sea wall behind. Soft methods are usually cheaper to maintain and look more natural, but they need topping up and may not protect against extreme storms.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Hard engineering is high-cost, high-impact and gives a quick result — useful where the value of property is very high (a town centre, a port, a nuclear power station). Soft engineering is lower-cost in the long term and protects ecosystems, but is best suited to lower-value coastlines where there is space for the system to flex. The trick is choosing the right tool for the right place: a sea wall in front of Blackpool Tower makes sense; a sea wall in front of an empty saltmarsh does not.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Hold the Line vs Managed Retreat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Decision-makers do not just choose between hard and soft methods — they also choose how much of the coast to defend at all. The four classic Shoreline Management Plan options are <em>hold the line</em>, <em>advance the line</em>, <em>no active intervention</em> and <em>managed realignment</em>. <dfn class="term" data-def="A coastal management policy of deliberately allowing the sea to flood low-lying land so a new coastline forms further inland.">Managed retreat</dfn> — sometimes called managed realignment — is increasingly used on the East coast of England, where defending soft, low-lying land is no longer cost-effective. At Medmerry in West Sussex (2013), the Environment Agency breached an existing sea wall and let 183 hectares of farmland flood. A new defence was built 2 km inland, and the flooded zone became a salt marsh that absorbs storm surges and protects 348 properties in the village behind.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Case Study: North Norfolk</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The North Norfolk coast is one of the fastest-eroding shorelines in Europe. At Happisburgh, the cliffs have retreated by more than 250 m since 1600, and homes have fallen into the sea since 1990. The Shoreline Management Plan recommends ‘no active intervention’ here: the cost of defending such a low-population area outweighs the cost of buying out residents. Compare this with Cromer further along the coast, where a sea wall, groynes and beach nourishment hold the line because the town’s tourism economy is worth defending. The same coastline gets two opposite policies based on cost-benefit reasoning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Integrated Coastal Zone Management</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Modern coasts are not managed metre by metre — they are managed as whole stretches of shoreline. <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach that co-ordinates coastal management across a whole region or country, balancing economic, social and environmental needs.">Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM)</dfn> brings together councils, the Environment Agency, conservation groups and local people to make co-ordinated decisions for a sediment cell — the natural stretch of coast within which sand and shingle move. <dfn class="term" data-def="A long-term plan that decides whether each stretch of coast should be defended, allowed to retreat or left alone.">Shoreline Management Plans</dfn> divide England’s coastline into about 22 sediment cells and set a policy for each in 0–20, 20–50 and 50–100 year time horizons. This stops one council building a sea wall that wrecks the beach next door.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Small Island States & Sea Level Rise</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For some countries the question is not how to defend the coast but whether the country itself will survive. The Maldives sits an average of 1.5 m above sea level, and the IPCC projects 0.4–0.8 m of global sea level rise by 2100. The government has already built a 3 m sea wall around the capital Malé (paid for largely by Japanese aid) and is constructing the artificial island of Hulhumalé at 2 m above sea level. The Republic of Kiribati has bought 20 km² of land in Fiji as future relocation insurance. These responses cost hundreds of millions of dollars in countries that did not cause climate change, raising sharp questions about climate justice and the future status of <strong>environmental refugees</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: Medmerry's location, year, area flooded and number of properties protected.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Medmerry managed retreat scheme (West Sussex, 2013) deliberately flooded 183 hectares of farmland behind a new inland defence. The resulting salt marsh now protects 348 properties from storm surges and is the largest realignment of an open coast ever attempted in the UK.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Hard engineering (sea walls, rock armour, groynes, gabions) blocks the sea; soft engineering (beach nourishment, dune regeneration, salt marsh) works with natural processes — modern plans combine both.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Shoreline Management Plans use cost-benefit analysis to choose between hold the line, no active intervention and managed retreat for each sediment cell.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Rising seas threaten Small Island States like the Maldives and Kiribati, raising questions about climate justice and environmental refugees that go beyond engineering.</li></ul>
