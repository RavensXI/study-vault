--- DESCRIPTION ---
Plate boundaries, hotspots and the volcanic features they produce.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Restless Crust</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Earth’s outer shell is broken into a jigsaw of about fifteen large pieces called <dfn class="term" data-def="Large rigid sections of Earth's outer shell that move slowly across the surface, driven by convection in the mantle below.">tectonic plates</dfn>. They float on the slow-flowing mantle below and grind past, dive beneath or pull apart from one another. Most of the world’s earthquakes and volcanoes line up along their edges — producing the famous Pacific ‘Ring of Fire’ that contains around 75% of the planet’s volcanoes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What Drives the Plates?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The slow circulating movement of hot rock in the mantle, driven by heat from Earth's core, that drags tectonic plates along.">Convection currents</dfn> in the mantle drag plates along. Hot rock rises near the core, spreads out near the crust, cools, and sinks again. Where rising convection currents diverge, two plates pull apart. Where they descend, plates collide or one slides beneath another.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Three Types of Plate Boundary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">At a <strong>destructive (convergent) boundary</strong>, two plates move towards each other. Where an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate is pushed down through <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which one tectonic plate is forced beneath another at a destructive boundary, melting in the mantle below.">subduction</dfn>. The descending plate melts, and the rising magma builds large explosive volcanoes such as Mount St Helens (USA) and Mount Pinatubo (Philippines). Where two oceanic plates meet, a chain of volcanic islands forms — Japan is a classic example. Where two continental plates collide, neither sinks; the rocks crumple upwards into mountains like the Himalayas.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">At a <strong>constructive (divergent) boundary</strong>, two plates move apart. Magma rises through the gap and cools, creating new ocean floor. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the longest such feature on Earth and slowly widens by about 2.5 cm a year. Where divergence happens on land — in Iceland or the East African Rift Valley — we see <strong>rift valleys</strong>, deep linear lowlands flanked by steep cliffs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">At a <strong>conservative (transform) boundary</strong>, plates slide past each other. No magma reaches the surface, so volcanoes are absent — but friction along the fault produces earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is the classic example.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four plate boundary types and one named landform produced by each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four plate boundary types are destructive (subduction zones — ocean trenches, explosive volcanoes), constructive (mid-ocean ridges, rift valleys), conservative (transform faults — earthquakes only) and continental collision (fold mountains like the Himalayas).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Ocean Trenches and Rift Valleys</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Subduction zones produce the deepest places on Earth. The Mariana Trench, where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the smaller Mariana Plate, reaches almost 11 km below sea level — deeper than Mount Everest is high. Rift valleys are the inverse landscape. The East African Rift Valley stretches over 6,000 km from the Red Sea to Mozambique, marking the slow split of the African Plate into two new plates.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Volcanic Hotspots</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Not every volcano sits on a plate boundary. <dfn class="term" data-def="A localised plume of hot rock rising from deep in the mantle that punches through a tectonic plate, creating volcanoes far from plate boundaries.">Volcanic hotspots</dfn> are stationary plumes of hot mantle that punch through a moving plate from below. As the plate slides over the hotspot, a chain of volcanic islands forms — the youngest sits over the plume, the older islands have drifted away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hawaii is the textbook example. The Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain stretches over 5,800 km across the Pacific. Its youngest volcano, Kīlauea on the Big Island, is one of the most active on Earth and erupted again in late 2024. The next island in the chain, Lō’ihi, is still 1 km below sea level — but in tens of thousands of years it will rise to become Hawaii’s newest landmass.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Volcanic Landforms — Larger Scale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The shape of a volcano depends on the kind of magma it produces. <strong>Shield volcanoes</strong> form where runny basaltic lava flows fast and far before it cools, building wide, gently sloping mounds. Mauna Loa in Hawaii covers an area larger than the whole island of Sicily and is the largest active volcano on Earth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Stratovolcanoes</strong> (composite volcanoes) form at destructive boundaries where sticky andesitic magma traps gases. Eruptions are explosive, alternating ash falls and lava flows that create steep symmetrical cones. Mount Fuji and Mount Rainier are classic examples.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A <strong>caldera</strong> is what is left behind when a stratovolcano’s magma chamber empties so suddenly that the summit collapses. Crater Lake (Oregon) sits in a 9 km wide caldera left by the eruption of Mount Mazama around 7,700 years ago. Yellowstone’s caldera is over 70 km across.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Cinder cones</strong> are small steep volcanoes built from blocks of ash and pumice ejected during a single eruption. Parícutin in Mexico grew to 424 m within a single year (1943). <strong>Lava tubes</strong> are tunnels formed when the surface of a fast lava flow cools and solidifies while liquid lava continues to flow underneath. Some, like the Thúrston tube on Hawaii, can be walked through.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Geysers</strong> form where groundwater seeps down to hot rock and is forced back up as a jet of steam and water. Old Faithful in Yellowstone erupts roughly every 90 minutes. Iceland’s Geysir gave the feature its English name.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words how a shield volcano differs from a stratovolcano.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shield volcanoes are wide and gentle (runny basaltic lava, e.g. Mauna Loa). Stratovolcanoes are steep and explosive (sticky andesitic magma at subduction zones, e.g. Mount Fuji). Calderas form where a stratovolcano’s summit collapses, e.g. Crater Lake at 9 km across.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">The Pacific Plate moves north-westwards at about 7 cm a year. As it slides over the stationary Hawaiian hotspot, magma punches through to form a new island. Older islands — Kauai, Oahu, Maui — have already drifted past the plume and are slowly eroding. The Big Island, with Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, sits directly above the plume now and is therefore the most active.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Hotspots remind us that not all volcanic activity is plate-boundary driven. Yellowstone in Wyoming is another famous land-based hotspot, sitting under the North American Plate. Both produce shield-style flows when basaltic, and explosive caldera-forming eruptions when conditions change.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Plates are dragged by mantle convection. Destructive boundaries subduct ocean crust and build explosive stratovolcanoes; constructive boundaries form mid-ocean ridges and rift valleys; conservative boundaries produce earthquakes only.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Volcanic hotspots like Hawaii sit far from plate boundaries; the Pacific Plate slides over a stationary mantle plume to create a 5,800 km island chain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Larger volcanic landforms include shield volcanoes (Mauna Loa), stratovolcanoes (Mount Fuji) and calderas (Crater Lake at 9 km wide). Smaller features include cinder cones, lava tubes and geysers.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">The Eduqas examiner reports flag two common errors. First, students treat ‘destructive’ and ‘convergent’ as identical — remember that a continental-continental collision (the Himalayas) is convergent but does not subduct, so it is not the same hazard profile as the Andes. Second, students forget that not all volcanoes sit on plate boundaries (Hawaii is a hotspot). For 6-mark ‘Explain with Example’, name the place and lock in numbers (5,800 km Hawaiian chain, 9 km Crater Lake caldera, Pacific Plate at 7 cm/yr).</p>
