--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why UK climate is so varied and what shapes the local weather around schools, parks and cities.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Weather vs Climate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Geographers draw a sharp line between weather and climate. <dfn class="term" data-def="The state of the atmosphere over a short period — minutes, hours or days — including temperature, rainfall and wind.">Weather</dfn> is what is happening today: the rain on your school run, the wind blowing your hood off. <dfn class="term" data-def="The average pattern of weather measured over thirty years or more for a particular place.">Climate</dfn> is the long-term pattern, normally averaged over thirty years. The UK has a temperate maritime climate — mild winters, cool summers, rain spread throughout the year — but inside that single label sits enormous local variation. The west is wet, the east is dry; the south is warm, the north is cool; cities sit warmer than the countryside they surround. This lesson explains why.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four factors that affect UK climate, with one example of where each is most obvious.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four factors shape UK climate at the regional scale: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea and ocean currents (especially the North Atlantic Drift). Together they create a clear west-east and north-south split in temperature and rainfall.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Latitude and Altitude</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The UK stretches from about 50°N (Land’s End) to nearly 60°N (Shetland). The further north you go, the lower the angle of the sun, so each square metre of ground receives less heat. Average July temperatures fall from around 20°C in southern England to 13°C in the north-west of Scotland. <dfn class="term" data-def="How high a place is above sea level; air cools by about 1°C for every 100 m of rise.">Altitude</dfn> reinforces this: temperature drops by roughly 1°C for every 100 m of rise. The summit of Ben Nevis (1,345 m) is 13°C cooler than the floor of Glen Nevis below it — which is why the Scottish Highlands get snowfall when London is frost-free.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Maritime and Continental Influences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land. Places close to the sea therefore have <dfn class="term" data-def="A climate strongly influenced by the sea: cool summers, mild winters and rain throughout the year.">maritime climates</dfn> — cool summers, mild winters and a small annual range. Plymouth, on the south coast, has an annual temperature range of just 11°C. Inland Britain has a more <dfn class="term" data-def="A climate where the influence of the sea is weak: warm summers, cold winters and a large annual range.">continental climate</dfn>: London’s annual range is 14°C, and parts of Eastern Europe see swings of more than 30°C. The bigger the landmass behind a place, the more continental its weather will feel. This is why a January day in Norfolk can be five degrees colder than the same day in Cornwall.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The <strong>North Atlantic Drift</strong> is a warm ocean current that flows past the west of the UK. It is an extension of the Gulf Stream, carrying warm tropical water from the Caribbean across the Atlantic. The current keeps western coasts about 5–9°C warmer in winter than they would otherwise be at this latitude. Compare Plymouth (50°N, January average 6°C) with Newfoundland on the same latitude across the Atlantic (January average −5°C). Without the Drift, the UK’s climate would feel much more like Canada’s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Prevailing Wind and the West-East Split</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The UK’s prevailing wind is the <strong>south-westerly</strong>. Air arriving from over the Atlantic is moisture-laden by the time it hits western Britain. Forced upwards over Snowdonia, the Lake District and the Highlands, it cools, condenses and dumps rain — this is <dfn class="term" data-def="Rainfall caused by air being forced to rise over high ground; it cools as it rises and water vapour condenses.">orographic rainfall</dfn>. By the time the air reaches the eastern side of the country it is descending, warming and drying, casting a <strong>rain shadow</strong> over places like Norfolk and Cambridgeshire. Seathwaite in Cumbria averages over 3,300 mm of rain a year; Cambridge averages just 600 mm. Same country, more than five times the rain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Micro-climates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Climate also varies on a much smaller scale. A <dfn class="term" data-def="The local climate of a small area, such as a school playground, a city street or a wood, that differs from the climate around it.">micro-climate</dfn> is the climate of a small area — a school field, a city street, the lee side of a hedge. It can differ from the regional climate by several degrees in temperature and several seconds in wind speed. Buildings funnel wind down narrow streets, glass walls reflect heat onto pavements, and trees create cool, shaded pockets. South-facing slopes warm faster than north-facing ones — one reason why vineyards and orchards in the UK tend to face south.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Urban Heat Island</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The most important micro-climate students need to know is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a city is warmer than the rural area around it because of buildings, traffic, dark surfaces and waste heat.">urban heat island</dfn>. London is on average 2°C warmer than the surrounding countryside, rising to 7°C warmer on still summer nights. The reasons stack up: tarmac and brick absorb solar energy by day and release it slowly at night; vehicles, buildings and air conditioning add waste heat; tall buildings reduce the cooling effect of wind; and there is much less vegetation to evaporate water and cool the air. Heat islands worsen heatwaves and raise mortality — one reason London needs more parks, green roofs and street trees as the climate warms.</p>

Differences of 2–4°C between sun-baked tarmac and shaded grass are common. The fieldwork shows pupils that micro-climate is something they live in every break-time, not just an exam term.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: four reasons cities are warmer than the countryside around them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The urban heat island raises city temperatures 2–7°C above the surrounding countryside because of dark surfaces storing heat, waste heat from cars and buildings, sheltered streets reducing wind, and a lack of vegetation to cool the air.</p>
</div>

<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Climate is the 30-year average; weather is today. The UK has a temperate maritime climate but huge regional variation in temperature and rainfall.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Latitude, altitude, distance from the sea and the North Atlantic Drift create the UK’s north-south and west-east climate splits.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Micro-climates form at the local scale; the urban heat island is the most important example, making London 2–7°C warmer than the surrounding countryside.</li></ul>
