--- DESCRIPTION ---
Air masses, depressions, anticyclones and the weather they bring to the UK across the seasons.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why is UK Weather So Changeable?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK lies at around 50°–60°N, in the path of every kind of air the North Atlantic can throw at us. In a single week we can have stormy westerlies, freezing easterlies and warm southerlies. The reason is the global circulation of the atmosphere — a set of giant convection cells that move warm air away from the equator and cold air down from the poles. Where warm tropical air meets cold polar air, the boundary is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The boundary in the atmosphere where cold polar air meets warm tropical air, where most UK weather is born.">polar front</dfn>. The UK sits right under this front, which is why our weather changes so quickly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the five air masses that affect the UK and the weather each one brings.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK is influenced by five main air masses — Tropical Maritime (warm, wet, from the south-west), Tropical Continental (hot, dry, from southern Europe), Polar Maritime (cool, showery, from the north-west), Polar Continental (cold, dry in summer / very cold in winter, from Scandinavia) and Arctic Maritime (very cold, snowy, from the north).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Air Masses Bringing UK Weather</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A large body of air that takes on the temperature and humidity of the region where it formed.">air mass</dfn> is a vast body of air that takes on the temperature and humidity of its source region. Tropical Maritime air, our most common visitor, picks up moisture as it crosses the warm Atlantic and arrives mild and rainy — this is why western Scotland and Wales get more than 2,000 mm of rain a year. Polar Continental air swept the UK in February 2018’s ‘Beast from the East’, drawing freezing air from Siberia and dumping more than 50 cm of snow in parts of Cumbria. The direction the air comes from sets up the season-by-season patchwork of UK weather.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Depressions: Low Pressure Weather</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A weather system of rising warm air and low surface pressure, bringing cloud, rain and strong winds.">depression</dfn> forms when warm tropical air meets cold polar air at the polar front. The warm air is forced to rise, water vapour condenses into cloud and rain, and surface pressure falls. Depressions usually arrive from the west, last 3–5 days and pass over the UK in three stages: the <strong>warm front</strong> (gentle steady rain, then warmer air), the <strong>warm sector</strong> (mild, drizzly), and the <strong>cold front</strong> (heavy rain, gusty wind, then a clear bright sky). Storm Eunice in February 2022 was a deep depression with a central pressure of 969 mb that produced gusts of 196 km/h at the Needles — the highest ever recorded in England.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">On a Met Office synoptic chart, depressions show as tightly spaced isobars curving around a central low. The closer the lines, the steeper the pressure gradient and the stronger the winds. A warm front is drawn as a red line with semicircles pointing in the direction of travel; a cold front is a blue line with triangles. Where they catch up — an occluded front — both symbols sit on a purple line. Knowing which front is approaching tells forecasters whether to expect drizzle (warm front) or thunderstorms (cold front).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Anticyclones: High Pressure Weather</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A weather system of sinking air and high surface pressure, bringing settled, dry weather.">anticyclone</dfn> is the opposite of a depression. Air sinks, warms and dries, so cloud cannot form. Surface pressure is high (often above 1,025 mb) and isobars are widely spaced, giving light winds. In summer this means clear blue skies, hot days and cool nights — but it can also bring heatwaves, like July 2022 when temperatures reached 40.3°C at Coningsby in Lincolnshire, the UK’s first ever 40°C reading. In winter the same sinking air brings cold clear nights with frost and fog: November 2010’s anticyclone trapped −19°C readings in Scotland for over a fortnight. Anticyclones drift slowly, so the same weather can sit over the UK for ten days or more.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK Weather Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Both pressure systems can become hazards. Deep depressions cause storms, gales and surface flooding — Storm Desmond (December 2015) dropped 341 mm of rain on Honister Pass in 24 hours, flooding more than 5,000 homes in Carlisle. Slow-moving summer anticyclones can lock in heatwaves: the 2022 heatwave was linked to over 3,000 excess deaths in England, started moorland fires in Yorkshire and buckled rails on the East Coast Mainline. Winter anticyclones bring fog that closes airports and freezing temperatures that overload the energy grid.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A warmer atmosphere holds about 7% more water vapour for every 1°C of warming. That means depressions arriving from the Atlantic can carry more moisture and dump heavier rainfall when they hit the UK — one reason floods like Storm Desmond and Storm Eunice are becoming more frequent. Heatwaves are becoming hotter and longer too, because slow anticyclones over Europe can now tap into much warmer continental air. The Met Office estimates the 40°C July 2022 reading was made at least ten times more likely by climate change.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the difference between a depression and an anticyclone in pressure, air movement and weather.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Depressions = low pressure, rising air, cloud and rain. Anticyclones = high pressure, sinking air, settled clear weather. Whether the UK is dry or stormy on any given day depends on which system is sitting over it.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Five main air masses bring different weather to the UK; the polar front is where warm and cold air meet to produce the depressions that dominate our climate.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">Depressions are low-pressure systems with warm and cold fronts, bringing cloud, rain and strong wind; anticyclones are high-pressure systems bringing settled, dry weather.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Both systems can become hazards: depressions cause storms and floods (Storm Desmond, Storm Eunice), anticyclones cause heatwaves and cold snaps (July 2022, December 2010).</li></ul>
