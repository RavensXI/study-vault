--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Buddha's life, Four Sights, enlightenment and the core teachings of dukkha, tanha and nirodha.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Was the Buddha?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The word &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="A title meaning 'the awakened one' or 'the enlightened one', referring to Siddhattha Gotama, the founder of Buddhism.">Buddha</dfn>&rsquo; is not a personal name but a title meaning &lsquo;the awakened one&rsquo; or &lsquo;the enlightened one.&rsquo; The person to whom it refers was Siddhattha Gotama (Sanskrit: Siddhartha Gautama), a prince born into the Shakya clan in what is now southern Nepal, traditionally dated to around the 5th&ndash;4th century BCE. According to the tradition preserved in the Pali texts, Siddhattha&rsquo;s birth was accompanied by predictions that he would become either a great king or a great spiritual teacher.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">His father, wishing Siddhattha to become a king, raised him in great luxury within a palace, shielding him from anything unpleasant. Siddhattha married, had a son, and seemed set for a life of privilege &mdash; yet the tradition records that he remained inwardly unsatisfied. The turning point came when he ventured outside the palace grounds.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Four Sights</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">According to the tradition, Siddhattha encountered four sights on separate journeys outside the palace that transformed his understanding of life. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Four encounters that prompted Siddhattha Gotama to abandon palace life and seek enlightenment: an old person, a sick person, a corpse, and a wandering holy man.">Four Sights</dfn> were:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>An old person</strong> &mdash; stooped and frail, showing Siddhattha that youth is temporary and all beings are subject to ageing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>A sick person</strong> &mdash; suffering from illness, revealing that physical wellbeing cannot be relied upon.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>A corpse</strong> &mdash; showing directly that death is unavoidable for every living being.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>A wandering holy man</strong> (a <em>samana</em>) &mdash; a spiritual seeker who had renounced ordinary life to pursue liberation from suffering. Unlike the first three sights, this one offered possibility: perhaps there was a way beyond the suffering that the other three had revealed.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the Four Sights in order, writing what each one taught Siddhattha about the nature of life.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Four Sights are: old age, sickness, death, and a holy man. The first three showed Siddhattha the reality of suffering; the fourth showed him that liberation might be possible through the spiritual life.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Renunciation and the Ascetic Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Siddhattha left his palace, his wife and his young son in the middle of the night &mdash; an act of <dfn class="term" data-def="Giving up worldly possessions, comforts and family ties in order to pursue the spiritual life, as Siddhattha did when he left the palace.">renunciation</dfn>. He became a wandering ascetic, practising severe self-denial &mdash; eating almost nothing, exposing his body to extremes of heat and cold &mdash; to see whether physical mortification could bring liberation. After years of this &lsquo;ascetic&rsquo; life, he concluded that it was as far from the truth as his earlier life of luxury had been. Neither extreme led to freedom. This insight led him to formulate what Buddhism calls the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Buddhist principle of avoiding both extreme self-indulgence and extreme self-denial, steering a balanced path between luxury and asceticism.">Middle Way</dfn> &mdash; a path that avoids both excess and harsh deprivation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Enlightenment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Siddhattha sat in deep meditation beneath a pipal tree (later called the Bodhi tree, &lsquo;tree of awakening&rsquo;) at Bodh Gaya and resolved not to rise until he had understood the true nature of existence. Through the night he achieved <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of perfect understanding and freedom from suffering, achieved by the Buddha under the Bodhi tree, in which the true nature of existence is seen clearly.">Enlightenment</dfn> &mdash; seeing the world &lsquo;as it really is.&rsquo; He understood the nature of suffering, its cause, its end, and the path that leads to its end. At this point Siddhattha became the Buddha: the awakened one.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Deer Park Sermon: First Turning of the Wheel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">After his enlightenment the Buddha travelled to Isipatana (near present-day Varanasi, India) where he found five former companions in the Deer Park. There he delivered his first teaching &mdash; an event known as the &lsquo;first turning of the wheel of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Buddha's teaching and the cosmic law that underlies reality (Pali term; equivalent Sanskrit term is dharma).">dhamma</dfn>&rsquo;. In this sermon the Buddha set out what would become the core of Buddhist teaching: the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. The Deer Park Sermon marks the beginning of the Buddhist community (the sangha) and the first communication of the dhamma to others.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The four fundamental teachings of the Buddha diagnosing the problem of suffering and prescribing the path to freedom: dukkha, samudaya, nirodha and magga.">Four Noble Truths</dfn> are structured like a medical diagnosis: identify the illness, find its cause, confirm that a cure exists, and prescribe the treatment.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>1. Dukkha &mdash; the truth of suffering.</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali term meaning suffering, dissatisfaction or unsatisfactoriness; the first Noble Truth, which states that all conditioned existence involves dukkha.">Dukkha</dfn> is often translated as &lsquo;suffering&rsquo;, but the Pali word is richer than this. It encompasses obvious suffering (pain, grief, illness) but also the deeper dissatisfaction that arises from impermanence: pleasures do not last; relationships end; even joy carries the shadow of its own passing. The Dhammapada, verse 1 (Pali: <em>Manopubbangama dhamma</em>&hellip;) teaches that mental states precede all actions, meaning that suffering is rooted in the mind as much as in external circumstances. The Buddha is not a pessimist &mdash; he acknowledges joy &mdash; but insists that clinging to things that are impermanent creates suffering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>2. Samudaya &mdash; the cause of suffering.</strong> The cause of dukkha is <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for craving or thirst; the second Noble Truth identifies tanha as the root cause of suffering.">tanhā</dfn> (craving or thirst). There are three types: craving for sensory pleasure, craving for existence (clinging to life), and craving for non-existence (wishing for annihilation). The Dhammapada, verse 5, reinforces this with the teaching that hatred (a form of craving) is not overcome by hatred but by its opposite. Tanhā drives the cycle of rebirth (<em>samsāra</em>) because unfulfilled craving leads to further becoming.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>3. Nirodha &mdash; the cessation of suffering.</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for the cessation or extinction of craving, the third Noble Truth, which teaches that suffering can be brought to an end.">Nirodha</dfn> means cessation &mdash; the total letting go of tanhā. When craving is extinguished, the conditions for suffering are removed. This state of complete liberation is <em>nibbāna</em> (Sanskrit: nirvāna) &mdash; not a place but an experience of freedom from craving, hatred and delusion. The Buddha himself is the proof that nirodha is possible, not merely a theoretical ideal.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>4. Magga &mdash; the path.</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="The fourth Noble Truth; the Eightfold Path that leads to the cessation of suffering and the attainment of nibbana.">Magga</dfn> is the path to nirodha: the Noble Eightfold Path. The Buddha is presenting not just a diagnosis but a practical programme for living.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the four Pali terms for the Noble Truths, then give a one-sentence meaning for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Four Noble Truths in Pali are: <strong>Dukkha</strong> (suffering/dissatisfaction), <strong>Samudaya</strong> (craving/tanhā as the cause), <strong>Nirodha</strong> (cessation &mdash; the end of craving is possible), and <strong>Magga</strong> (the Eightfold Path as the way to nirodha). This structure mirrors a medical diagnosis and treatment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">The Noble Eightfold Path: Three Sections</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The Eightfold Path is not eight sequential steps but eight interrelated practices grouped into three sections. All three sections are cultivated together.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Three Sections of the Eightfold Path</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Wisdom (<dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for wisdom; the first section of the Eightfold Path, comprising right understanding and right intention.">paññā</dfn>)</strong> &mdash; right understanding (understanding the Four Noble Truths and the nature of reality) and right intention (committing to renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness rather than craving, ill-will and cruelty).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Morality (<dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for ethical conduct; the second section of the Eightfold Path, comprising right speech, right action and right livelihood.">sīla</dfn>)</strong> &mdash; right speech (avoiding lying, harsh speech, gossip), right action (avoiding killing, stealing, sexual misconduct), and right livelihood (not earning a living through harmful means).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Meditation (<dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for mental cultivation or concentration; the third section of the Eightfold Path, comprising right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration.">samādhi</dfn>)</strong> &mdash; right effort (cultivating positive states of mind and abandoning negative ones), right mindfulness (clear, present-moment awareness of body, feelings, mind and mental objects), and right concentration (developing the deep stillness of meditation, the <em>jhānas</em>).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n28">Living by the Eightfold Path is the Buddhist way of progressively loosening the grip of tanhā until craving is extinguished and nibbāna is attained. The Buddha presented this not as a set of commandments from a divine authority but as a map tested through his own experience. The invitation is to try the path and see for oneself whether it works.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The Buddha was Siddhattha Gotama, a prince who left palace life after encountering the Four Sights (old age, sickness, death, holy man). He practised extreme asceticism, then discovered the Middle Way before achieving Enlightenment under the Bodhi tree.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">The Four Noble Truths, taught in the Deer Park Sermon, are: <em>dukkha</em> (suffering exists), <em>tanhā</em> as its cause (craving), <em>nirodha</em> as its end (craving can be extinguished), and <em>magga</em> as the path (the Eightfold Path). This is Buddhism&rsquo;s core diagnostic framework.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">The Noble Eightfold Path is divided into three sections &mdash; wisdom (<em>paññā</em>), morality (<em>sīla</em>) and meditation (<em>samādhi</em>) &mdash; providing a complete practical guide to ending suffering and attaining <em>nibbāna</em>.</li>
</ul>
