<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five Precepts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Buddhist ethical life is guided by the <dfn class="term" data-def="The five ethical guidelines for Buddhist lay practitioners: not harming, not taking what is not given, avoiding sexual misconduct, avoiding false speech, and not taking intoxicants.">Five Precepts</dfn> (pa&#241;casila). These are voluntary commitments, not commandments imposed by an external authority. Dhammapada 183 describes the teaching of the Buddhas as: not to do evil, to cultivate good and to purify the mind. The Five Precepts operationalise this in daily life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Five Precepts are: (1) not harming any living being; (2) not taking what has not been given; (3) avoiding sexual misconduct; (4) avoiding false speech; (5) abstaining from substances that cloud the mind. Together they reflect the Buddhist commitment to <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for morality or ethical conduct — one of the three sections of the Eightfold Path alongside wisdom (panna) and meditation (samadhi).">s&#299;la</dfn> (morality), which alongside wisdom (pa&#241;&#241;&#257;) and meditation (sam&#257;dhi) forms the three sections of the Eightfold Path.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n4">The first precept &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Non-harming: the principle of not causing harm to any living being, which has implications for diet, career, lifestyle and relationships in Buddhist practice.">ahimsa</dfn> (non-harming) &mdash; has wide implications. Many Buddhists adopt a vegetarian or vegan diet as a natural expression of not harming animals. Career choices may also be affected: a committed Buddhist following the first precept might avoid occupations involving weapons manufacture, slaughterhouse work or pest control.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n5">The third precept (avoiding sexual misconduct) requires interpreting what &ldquo;misconduct&rdquo; means. For monastics, celibacy is the standard. For lay practitioners, it means avoiding relationships that cause harm to oneself or others &mdash; such as infidelity or relationships with those unable to freely consent. The fifth precept (avoiding intoxicants) has implications for social life: alcohol and recreational drugs cloud the mind, making clear perception and ethical action more difficult.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Challenges for <dfn class="term" data-def="A modern British-based Buddhist movement (formerly known as the Western Buddhist Order), practising across Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions, which encourages lay practitioners to live according to the Five Precepts.">Triratna</dfn> Buddhists (the British-based movement formerly known as the Western Buddhist Order) are particularly interesting. Living according to the Five Precepts in a Western consumer culture &mdash; where alcohol is normalised, meat-eating is common and sexual permissiveness is widespread &mdash; presents a daily counter-cultural challenge. Triratna communities emphasise communal support and regular retreat as ways to maintain ethical practice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Places of Practice: Vihara and Home Shrine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A Buddhist monastery or place of practice where monks and nuns live, meditate and keep the dhamma alive; also used for lay meditation centres.">vihara</dfn> (monastery&thinsp;/&thinsp;place of practice) is the traditional centre of Buddhist communal life. It houses monks or nuns who follow the Vinaya (monastic code), practise daily meditation and provide teaching for lay Buddhists. Lay people visit viharas for teachings, meditation sessions and festivals. The vihara also preserves the Pali texts of the Therav&#257;da tradition.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Many Buddhist households maintain a home shrine as a focus for daily practice. A typical home shrine contains an image of the Buddha (as a focus for reflection, not as an object of worship in the sense of prayer to a deity), candles representing the light of the dhamma, incense representing the spread of good qualities, flowers representing impermanence, and water offerings. Daily bowing and the recitation of the refuges (&ldquo;I take refuge in the Buddha...&rdquo;) before the shrine maintains mindfulness and devotion throughout ordinary life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe two differences between Theravada and Mahayana puja from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Therav&#257;da puja typically venerates a single image of the historical Buddha (Siddhattha Gotama). Mah&#257;y&#257;na puja may venerate multiple Buddhas, bodhisattvas and use mudras (hand gestures), mantras (sacred sounds) and mandalas (sacred diagrams) as part of elaborate ritual practice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Therav&#257;da and Mah&#257;y&#257;na Puja</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Theravada Buddhism: the 'teaching of the elders', practised mainly in South and South-East Asia, which closely follows the Pali canon and emphasises individual liberation through the Eightfold Path.">Therav&#257;da</dfn> puja is relatively austere. A single image of the historical Buddha is honoured through offerings (flowers, incense, water, light). The act is understood as an expression of reverence and aspiration &mdash; not as petitionary prayer. The shrine reminds the practitioner of the qualities of the enlightened mind that they are cultivating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Mahayana Buddhism: the 'great vehicle', widespread in East Asia, which extends the Buddhist path to include the bodhisattva ideal of seeking enlightenment for the benefit of all beings.">Mah&#257;y&#257;na</dfn> puja is typically more elaborate. It may incorporate veneration of multiple Buddhas (such as Amida Buddha, associated with Pure Land Buddhism) and <dfn class="term" data-def="In Mahayana Buddhism, an enlightened being who delays their own final liberation in order to help all other sentient beings reach enlightenment.">bodhisattvas</dfn> (beings who delay their own final liberation to assist all sentient beings). Ritual elements include mudras (symbolic hand gestures), mantras (sacred sounds or phrases repeated as part of devotion) and mandalas (intricate sacred diagrams used as visual meditation objects). <dfn class="term" data-def="Ritual offerings and acts of devotion in Buddhist practice, carried out before a shrine or image as an expression of reverence, aspiration and gratitude.">Dana</dfn> (giving) is central to both traditions: lay people offer food to monks and monasteries, generating merit and supporting the monastic community.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Wesak</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The most important Buddhist festival, celebrating the birth of the Buddha (and, in Theravada tradition, also his enlightenment and death), observed at the full moon of May.">Wesak</dfn> is the most widely celebrated Buddhist festival, observed at the full moon of May. For Therav&#257;da Buddhists, Wesak commemorates all three of the most significant events in the Buddha&rsquo;s life: his birth, his enlightenment under the Bodhi tree and his death (par&#305;nibb&#257;na). For Mah&#257;y&#257;na Buddhists, these events are commemorated separately at different times of year.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Wesak celebrations include visiting viharas, making offerings, listening to dhamma teachings, releasing caged birds (symbolising liberation), lantern processions and acts of generosity. The festival reinforces the central Buddhist narrative &mdash; that a human being (Siddhattha Gotama) achieved enlightenment through effort and practice, and that this achievement is available to all.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Buddhist festival of robe-offering, celebrated at the end of Vassa (the monks' rainy-season retreat), when lay people present new robes and supplies to the monastic Sangha.">Kathina</dfn> is a festival of robe-offering that marks the end of <dfn class="term" data-def="The traditional three-month retreat observed by Theravada Buddhist monks during the rainy season, during which they remain in one place to practise intensively.">Vassa</dfn> &mdash; the traditional three-month rainy-season retreat for Therav&#257;da monastics. During Vassa, monks remain in one location, practise intensively and do not travel. At Kathina (held in October&thinsp;/&thinsp;November), lay supporters bring new robes and supplies to the monasteries as an act of dana. This mutual relationship &mdash; monastics offering dhamma teaching and ethical example, lay people offering material support &mdash; expresses the interdependence of the Sangha.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Parinirvana Day (observed in February in Mah&#257;y&#257;na traditions) marks the Buddha&rsquo;s death and his passing into final nibb&#257;na &mdash; the complete cessation of the cycle of rebirth. It is observed with meditation, readings from the Buddhist texts and reflection on impermanence &mdash; the very teaching the Buddha exemplified by dying.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three Buddhist festivals in this lesson. For each, write one sentence about what it commemorates and one sentence about how it is observed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three key Buddhist festivals: Wesak (birth, enlightenment and death of the Buddha &mdash; for Therav&#257;da), Kathina (robe-offering, end of monks&rsquo; Vassa retreat) and Parinirvana Day (Buddha&rsquo;s final death, observed in Mah&#257;y&#257;na traditions in February).</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Five Precepts (not harming, not stealing, avoiding sexual misconduct, avoiding false speech, not taking intoxicants) provide the ethical framework for Buddhist lay life &mdash; they are voluntary commitments, not commandments, rooted in s&#299;la (morality).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Therav&#257;da puja venerates the historical Buddha through simple offerings; Mah&#257;y&#257;na puja may include bodhisattvas, mudras, mantras and mandalas. Dana (giving) supports the monastic Sangha and generates merit across both traditions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Key festivals &mdash; Wesak (Buddha&rsquo;s birth, enlightenment and death), Kathina (robe-offering at end of Vassa) and Parinirvana Day (Buddha&rsquo;s final nibb&#257;na) &mdash; reinforce core Buddhist teachings through communal celebration and reflection.</li>
</ul>
