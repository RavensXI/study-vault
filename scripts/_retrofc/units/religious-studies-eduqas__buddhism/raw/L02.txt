<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Three Marks of Existence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Buddha taught that all conditioned phenomena share three fundamental characteristics, known in Pali as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three fundamental characteristics of all conditioned existence in Buddhist teaching: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (suffering/dissatisfaction) and anatta (no fixed self).">Three Marks of Existence</dfn> (tilakkha&#7751;a). Understanding these marks is central to Buddhist practice because ignorance of them is, according to the dhamma, the root cause of continued suffering. The Three Marks are: <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for impermanence — the Buddhist teaching that all conditioned phenomena are constantly changing and nothing lasts forever.">anicca</dfn> (impermanence), <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for suffering or dissatisfaction — the first of the Four Noble Truths, teaching that all conditioned existence involves some degree of unsatisfactoriness.">dukkha</dfn> (suffering&thinsp;/&thinsp;dissatisfaction) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for 'no fixed self' — the Buddhist teaching that there is no permanent, unchanging soul or essence in any person or thing.">anatta</dfn> (no fixed self).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the Pali term for each of the Three Marks of Existence, plus a one-line explanation of what each means.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Three Marks of Existence are: anicca (impermanence &mdash; nothing lasts), dukkha (suffering / dissatisfaction &mdash; unsatisfactoriness runs through all conditioned life) and anatta (no fixed self &mdash; there is no permanent, unchanging soul). These are Pali terms from the Therav&#257;da tradition.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Anicca: Impermanence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Anicca teaches that everything changes. Feelings, thoughts, relationships, physical bodies and the entire material world are in a constant state of flux. Nothing that exists in the conditioned world stays the same from one moment to the next. The Buddha is recorded in the dhamma as teaching that clinging to things as if they were permanent is the source of much suffering: because we treat impermanent things as if they will last, we suffer when they change or end. Recognising anicca &mdash; truly internalising it rather than just accepting it intellectually &mdash; is a step toward liberation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Anatta and the Story of Nagasena</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Anatta is perhaps the most philosophically distinctive of the Three Marks. It teaches that there is no permanent, unchanging self or soul. This differs from Hindu teaching (which speaks of an eternal atman) and from popular conceptions of the self as a fixed entity that persists through time. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali teaching story in which the monk Nagasena uses the image of a chariot to explain anatta — just as a chariot has no fixed essence beyond its parts, neither does a person.">Story of Nagasena</dfn> (from the Milindapanha text) has the monk Nagasena explain anatta to King Milinda using the analogy of a chariot. He asks: where is the chariot? Is it the wheels? The axle? The frame? None of the individual parts is &ldquo;the chariot&rdquo;, and neither is the sum of them &mdash; the chariot is merely a convenient label. In the same way, &ldquo;I&rdquo; am not any of the aggregates (khandas) that make up experience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Dhammapada 113 connects anatta and the aggregates. Without citing this verse&rsquo;s exact phrasing (to avoid fabrication), its context affirms that a moment of insight into the impermanent, selfless nature of existence is of greater value than a long life lived in ignorance. This supports the Buddhist emphasis on wisdom (pa&#241;&#241;&#257;) as essential to liberation.</p>

None of these is permanent, and none of them, individually or combined, constitutes a fixed self. This &ldquo;bundle&rdquo; view of personal identity supports the teaching of anatta: what we call &ldquo;me&rdquo; is a constantly changing process, not a fixed thing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Three Jewels: Taking Refuge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">At the heart of Buddhist identity is the act of taking refuge in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three objects of refuge for Buddhists: the Buddha (the enlightened teacher), the Dhamma (the teachings) and the Sangha (the community of practitioners).">Three Jewels</dfn> (tiratana): the Buddha, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The teachings of the Buddha, which guide Buddhists toward liberation from suffering — one of the Three Jewels.">Dhamma</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The community of Buddhist practitioners — monks, nuns and lay followers — who keep the dhamma alive; one of the Three Jewels.">Sangha</dfn>. To &ldquo;take refuge&rdquo; means to commit one&rsquo;s life to these three sources of guidance. The formula repeated by Buddhists is: &ldquo;I take refuge in the Buddha; I take refuge in the Dhamma; I take refuge in the Sangha.&rdquo;</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Each jewel has a distinct role. The Buddha is the example of an enlightened being &mdash; he shows that liberation (nibb&#257;na) is achievable by a human being. The Dhamma is the path and the teachings that guide practitioners toward liberation. The Sangha is the community of practitioners &mdash; including monks, nuns and lay people &mdash; who support one another and keep the teachings alive across generations. In Therav&#257;da Buddhism, the Sangha primarily refers to those who have attained one of the stages of enlightenment; in broader usage it includes all Buddhist communities worldwide.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Write the three elements of the Three Jewels from memory, plus one sentence explaining what each offers to a Buddhist.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Three Jewels (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) are the foundation of Buddhist identity and practice. Taking refuge in them is the formal act of becoming a Buddhist and commits the practitioner to a life guided by the Buddha&rsquo;s example, the dhamma&rsquo;s wisdom and the Sangha&rsquo;s community.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Meditation: Samatha</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of Buddhist meditation that cultivates calm, stillness and concentration by focusing the mind on a single object such as the breath.">Samatha</dfn> meditation (calm&thinsp;/&thinsp;tranquillity meditation) develops concentration and stillness of mind. In the most common form, the meditator focuses attention on the natural rhythm of the breath. When the mind wanders &mdash; which it inevitably does &mdash; the meditator gently returns attention to the breath without judgement. Over time, this practice builds the mental stability (sam&#257;dhi) needed for deeper insight. The Dhammapada verse 282 teaches that wisdom arises through meditation and that without meditation, wisdom is lost &mdash; affirming the central place of meditative practice in the path to liberation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Meditation: Mett&#257;bh&#257;van&#257;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Buddhist meditation practice that cultivates loving-kindness toward oneself and all living beings, progressively expanding goodwill outward from self to difficult people to all beings.">Mett&#257;bh&#257;van&#257;</dfn> (loving-kindness meditation) systematically develops goodwill and compassion. The practitioner begins by generating warm feelings toward themselves, then extends those feelings outward &mdash; to loved ones, to neutral people, to people they find difficult, and finally to all living beings without exception. The goal is to break down the mental barriers between &ldquo;self&rdquo; and &ldquo;other&rdquo;, which accords with the teaching of anatta. Mett&#257;bh&#257;van&#257; is closely linked to the quality of mett&#257; (loving-kindness), one of the four brahmavih&#257;ras (divine abodes) taught in the Therav&#257;da tradition.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Insight meditation in Theravada Buddhism, which involves closely observing the arising and passing of mental and physical phenomena to directly experience the Three Marks of Existence.">Vipassan&#257;</dfn> meditation (insight meditation) involves closely observing mental and physical experiences as they arise and pass away. Rather than fixing attention on one object (as in samatha), vipassan&#257; trains the meditator to notice the impermanent, unsatisfying and selfless nature of all experience directly &mdash; not just as a concept but as a lived reality. This direct insight into the Three Marks is considered, in Therav&#257;da teaching, the proximate cause of enlightenment. Samatha and vipassan&#257; are often practised together: samatha builds the mental stability required for vipassan&#257; to arise.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">In contemporary Britain, Buddhist retreat centres offer residential meditation courses in both samatha and vipassan&#257; traditions. The Lam Rim Buddhist Centre is a well-known British retreat offering intensive residential practice across meditation traditions.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Three Marks of Existence &mdash; anicca (impermanence), dukkha (suffering) and anatta (no fixed self) &mdash; describe the fundamental nature of all conditioned experience. Anatta is illustrated by the Story of Nagasena and the chariot.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Three Jewels (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) are the foundation of Buddhist identity. Taking refuge in them is the formal entry into the Buddhist path.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The three meditation practices &mdash; samatha (calm), mett&#257;bh&#257;van&#257; (loving-kindness) and vipassan&#257; (insight) &mdash; develop the concentration and wisdom (pa&#241;&#241;&#257;) needed to see the Three Marks directly and progress toward nibb&#257;na.</li>
</ul>
