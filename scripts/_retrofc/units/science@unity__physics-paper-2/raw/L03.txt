--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Distance and Displacement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before you can understand motion properly, you need to know the difference between <dfn class="term" data-def="The total length of the path travelled by an object, measured in metres (m). Distance is a scalar quantity.">distance</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The straight-line distance from the start point to the end point in a given direction. Displacement is a vector quantity.">displacement</dfn>. Distance is the total length of the path an object travels — it doesn’t matter which direction. If you walk 3 m north and then 3 m south, you’ve covered a distance of 6 m. Displacement, however, measures the straight-line distance from start to finish <em>in a given direction</em>. In the same example, your displacement is 0 m because you ended up back where you started.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which is scalar (magnitude only) and which is vector (magnitude and direction) - distance or displacement?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Distance is a <strong>scalar</strong> quantity (magnitude only). Displacement is a <strong>vector</strong> quantity (magnitude <em>and</em> direction). The specification requires you to know this distinction for.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">This difference between <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has magnitude (size) only, such as distance, speed or energy.">scalar</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has both magnitude (size) and direction, such as displacement, velocity or force.">vector</dfn> quantities runs through the whole of this topic. Scalars have size only; vectors have both size and direction. You will meet this again with speed and velocity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Speed and Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The quantity known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled per unit of time, measured in metres per second (m/s). Speed is a scalar quantity.">speed</dfn> tells you how fast an object is moving. It is calculated using the equation:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Hide the equation and write out the speed formula using the symbols v, s, and t, including all units (m/s, m, s).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>\(speed = distance  \div  time\)</strong>   or   <strong>\(v = s  \div  t\)</strong><br>where v = speed in m/s, s = distance in m, t = time in s.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Speed is a scalar — it has no direction. <dfn class="term" data-def="The speed of an object in a given direction. Velocity is a vector quantity, measured in metres per second (m/s).">velocity</dfn> on the other hand, is the speed in a stated direction, making it a vector. An object moving at 5 m/s to the east has a velocity of 5 m/s east. If it turns around and moves at the same speed to the west, its speed is still 5 m/s but its velocity has changed to 5 m/s west.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">the specification expects you to recall typical speeds for everyday situations:</p>

<li><strong>Running:</strong> approximately 3 m/s</li>
<li><strong>Cycling:</strong> approximately 6 m/s</li>

<li><strong>Aeroplane:</strong> approximately 250 m/s</li>
<li><strong>Sound in air:</strong> approximately 330 m/s</li>

<p data-narration-id="n10">These values are approximate. Speed varies with age, terrain, fitness, weather, and other factors. In the exam, you may be asked to estimate a speed or explain why it changes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Distance–Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. The gradient of the line equals the speed of the object.">distance–time graph</dfn> plots distance on the y-axis against time on the x-axis. The key rule is simple: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The steepness of a line on a graph. On a distance-time graph, gradient equals speed; on a velocity-time graph, gradient equals acceleration.">gradient</dfn> of the line equals the speed.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch what horizontal lines, straight diagonal lines, and curves each represent on a distance-time graph.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>On a distance–time graph: a <strong>horizontal line</strong> means the object is stationary, a <strong>straight diagonal line</strong> means constant speed, and a <strong>curve</strong> means the speed is changing (accelerating or decelerating).</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n14">To find the speed at any point on a curve, draw a <dfn class="term" data-def="A straight line that touches a curve at exactly one point. The gradient of the tangent gives the instantaneous speed or velocity at that point.">tangent</dfn> to the curve at that point and calculate its gradient. The gradient is calculated as the change in distance divided by the change in time (rise ÷ run).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Velocity–Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and velocity on the y-axis. The gradient gives acceleration; the area under the line gives the distance travelled.">velocity–time graph</dfn> plots velocity on the y-axis against time on the x-axis. These graphs tell you two things:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>The gradient</strong> of the line gives the <strong>acceleration</strong> of the object.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>The area under the line</strong> gives the <strong>distance travelled</strong> (or displacement).</li>
</ul>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n19">A horizontal line on a velocity–time graph means constant velocity (zero acceleration). A line sloping upwards means the object is accelerating. A line sloping downwards means <dfn class="term" data-def="A decrease in velocity over time. Sometimes called negative acceleration. Measured in m/s².">deceleration</dfn> (negative acceleration). To calculate the area under a straight-line graph, split it into rectangles and triangles and add the areas together.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example — Reading a Velocity–Time Graph</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">A car accelerates from rest to 20 m/s in 10 seconds, then travels at a constant 20 m/s for 5 seconds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Acceleration phase:</strong> gradient = (20 − 0) ÷ (10 − 0) = 2 m/s². The area under this section is a triangle: ½ × 10 × 20 = 100 m.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Constant speed phase:</strong> gradient = 0 (no acceleration). The area under this section is a rectangle: 5 × 20 = 100 m.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Total distance:</strong> 100 + 100 = 200 m.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The quantity <dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in metres per second squared (m/s²). Acceleration is a vector quantity.">acceleration</dfn> is the rate at which velocity changes. It is calculated using:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Hide the formula and write out a = (v - u) ÷ t, stating what each symbol means and the unit for acceleration.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>\(acceleration = \text{change in velocity}  \div  \text{time taken}\)</strong>   or   <strong>\(a = (v  -  u)  \div  t\)</strong><br>where a = acceleration in m/s², v = final velocity in m/s, u = initial velocity in m/s, t = time in s.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n27">A positive value for acceleration means the object is speeding up. A negative value means it is slowing down (decelerating). Near the surface of the Earth, an object in free fall accelerates at approximately 9.8 m/s² due to gravity (often rounded to 10 m/s² in calculations).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Uniform Acceleration and the Equation v² = u² + 2as</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n28">For objects undergoing <dfn class="term" data-def="Acceleration that stays the same over time. The velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.">uniform acceleration</dfn> (constant acceleration), AQA requires you to use this equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>\(v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as\)</strong><br>where v = final velocity (m/s), u = initial velocity (m/s), a = acceleration (m/s²), s = distance (m).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">This equation is particularly useful when you are not given a time value. For example, a cyclist accelerates uniformly from 4 m/s at 0.5 m/s² over a distance of 50 m. To find the final velocity: v² = 4² + 2(50) = 16 + 50 = 66. So v = √66 ≈ 8.1 m/s.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example — Calculating Acceleration</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n31">A car increases its velocity from 10 m/s to 25 m/s in 5 seconds. Calculate its acceleration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Write the equation: a = (v − u) ÷ t<br><strong>Step 2:</strong> Substitute: a = (25 − 10) ÷ 5<br><strong>Step 3:</strong> Calculate: a = 15 ÷ 5 = <strong>\(3\,m/s^{2}\)</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n35">Distance is scalar (no direction); displacement is vector (has direction). Speed is scalar; velocity is vector. Use v = s ÷ t for speed and a = (v − u) ÷ t for acceleration.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">On a distance–time graph, the gradient gives speed. On a velocity–time graph, the gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives distance travelled.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">For uniform acceleration without a time value, use v² = u² + 2as. Always show your working in calculation questions for full marks.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">In calculation questions, always <strong>write the equation first</strong>, then <strong>substitute your values</strong>, then <strong>calculate</strong>. You earn marks for each step even if you get the final answer wrong. For graph questions, draw clear construction lines when finding gradients or areas — the examiner needs to see how you read values from the graph. Remember: gradient of a distance–time graph = speed, gradient of a velocity–time graph = acceleration, area under a velocity–time graph = distance.</p>
