=== L9 Sources of Finance for Start-Ups ===

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Short-term and long-term funding options for a small business owner.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Start-Ups Need Finance?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every new business needs money before it earns any. A sole trader renting a market stall needs cash for the pitch fee. A caf&eacute; owner needs funds for equipment, stock and deposits before the first customer walks through the door. Without finance, a business idea stays an idea. The key skill is choosing the <em>right</em> source &mdash; one that matches the amount needed, the time available to repay it, and the risk the owner is willing to carry.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which two sources are classed as short-term finance, and which five are long-term?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sources of finance divide into <strong>short-term</strong> (overdraft, trade credit) and <strong>long-term</strong> (personal savings, venture capital, share capital, loans, retained profit, crowd funding). Short-term sources cover temporary cash gaps; long-term sources fund growth and capital investment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Short-Term Sources of Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Short-term finance is designed to plug a temporary gap in cash flow rather than fund a major purchase. The two main short-term sources for small businesses are <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement with a bank that allows a business to spend more than is in its current account, up to an agreed limit.">overdraft</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="When a supplier allows a business to receive goods now and pay for them at a later agreed date, typically 30 to 90 days.">trade credit</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">An overdraft is flexible &mdash; the business only borrows what it needs, when it needs it, and only pays interest on the amount overdrawn. However, banks can withdraw the overdraft facility at short notice, and interest rates on overdrafts are often higher than on loans. Trade credit costs nothing if the invoice is paid on time, giving the business weeks of &ldquo;free&rdquo; finance. A florist ordering stock for Valentine&rsquo;s Day, for example, might take delivery in January and pay the wholesaler in March. The risk is that missing a payment deadline damages the relationship with the supplier.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">An overdraft is arranged with the bank and carries interest; it is most useful when a business cannot predict exactly when it will run short of cash. Trade credit is arranged with suppliers and is interest-free if paid on time; it is most useful when a business needs to buy stock or materials but has not yet received payment from its own customers. A start-up with no trading history may find it difficult to obtain either &mdash; banks are cautious about lending to new businesses, and suppliers may demand payment upfront until trust is established.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Long-Term Sources of Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Long-term finance is used for larger purchases or to sustain the business over years. Start-ups most commonly use personal savings because there is no application process, no interest, and no loss of ownership. However, using personal savings puts the owner&rsquo;s own money at risk &mdash; a particular concern for <dfn class="term" data-def="A business owned and run by one person who has unlimited liability for all debts of the business.">sole traders</dfn> who have unlimited liability.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A loan from a bank or financial institution repaid over an agreed term, usually with fixed monthly payments and a set interest rate.">bank loan</dfn> provides a fixed sum repaid over time. Because the repayment schedule is predictable, loans make cash-flow planning easier. The downside is that interest adds to the total cost, and banks may require security (an asset the business forfeits if it cannot repay). A small bakery borrowing &pound;20,000 over five years at 8% per year would pay roughly &pound;1,600 in interest each year on top of the capital repayment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: what does a venture capitalist typically want in return for funding a start-up?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="An investor who provides large amounts of funding to businesses with high growth potential, usually in exchange for a share of ownership.">Venture capital</dfn> is not just money &mdash; it often comes with business expertise and contacts. But venture capitalists expect a significant share of the business and a say in decisions. This suits high-growth start-ups but not sole traders who want to remain in full control.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Share Capital and Crowd Funding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money raised by a private limited company selling shares to investors, who then own a portion of the business.">Share capital</dfn> is raised when a business sells shares. For a private limited company (Ltd), shares are sold to a small group of known investors &mdash; friends, family or private backers. The advantage is that there are no repayments and no interest; the disadvantage is that new shareholders gain a say in the business and a share of future profits.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, typically through an online platform, in exchange for rewards, equity or early access to a product.">Crowd funding</dfn> has grown rapidly as an alternative to traditional finance. Platforms such as Kickstarter allow start-ups to pitch ideas to thousands of potential backers. If successful, the business raises its target without giving up significant ownership (in reward-based models) or gives up small equity stakes to many small investors. The risk is that the campaign must often be publicised widely, revealing the idea to competitors. A failed campaign can also damage the business&rsquo;s reputation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Profit kept in the business after tax and dividends, used to fund future investment rather than being paid out to owners.">Retained profit</dfn> is profit that the business keeps rather than paying out. It is the cheapest long-term source of finance because there is no interest and no loss of ownership. However, brand-new start-ups have not yet traded and therefore have no profit to retain. Retained profit is more relevant to established small businesses looking to expand. Students commonly list it as a source for any business &mdash; remember to check whether the scenario describes a brand-new business or one that is already trading.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Choosing the Right Source</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The best source of finance depends on three factors: the amount needed, how quickly it must be repaid, and the implications for ownership and control. A sole trader wanting a small cash-flow buffer may choose an overdraft. A start-up with a growth idea and no security may approach crowd funders. A new Ltd company with investors may raise share capital. In every case, the business owner must weigh the <em>cost</em> of the finance (interest, loss of profit share, loss of control) against the <em>benefit</em> of having the funds available.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Short-term finance (overdraft, trade credit) covers temporary cash shortfalls; long-term finance (loans, savings, share capital, venture capital, crowd funding, retained profit) funds larger or ongoing needs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The right source depends on the amount needed, the cost (interest or loss of ownership), and whether the business is a brand-new start-up or an established firm.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Retained profit is the cheapest source but is only available to businesses that have already made a profit &mdash; it cannot be used by a brand-new start-up.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For a 6-mark &ldquo;Explain&rdquo; question on sources of finance, examiners only credit the best two developed points &mdash; so write two well-structured paragraphs, each naming a source, explaining why it suits the business type in the question, and adding a further expansion (for example, the impact on ownership or the cost of interest). Do not list four or five sources in bullet points: breadth without depth earns few marks. On &ldquo;Justify a Recommendation&rdquo; questions, you do not have to evaluate both options &mdash; a single well-developed recommendation with a clear contextualised reason can reach the top tier. A common error is calculating interest incorrectly: use the formula ((total repayment &minus; amount borrowed) &divide; amount borrowed) &times; 100 and show each step.</p>
