--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Crisis Looking for a Pretext</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the spring of 1990, Saddam Hussein had a list of grievances against Kuwait long enough to fit any rhetoric he chose. Iraq owed Kuwait around $14&ndash;15 billion. Kuwait was producing oil above its OPEC quota and pushing prices down. Iraq had a long-running border claim to two Kuwaiti islands, Bubiyan and Warbah, which would give Iraqi ports better access to the Gulf. And Saddam alleged that Kuwait was practising <dfn class="term" data-def="Drilling oil wells at an angle to extract crude from beneath a neighbouring country&rsquo;s territory.">slant-drilling</dfn> at the <dfn class="term" data-def="A large oil field straddling the Iraq-Kuwait border, the focus of Iraqi accusations of Kuwaiti theft in 1990.">Rumaila oil field</dfn>, stealing crude from beneath Iraqi soil.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Whether or not the slant-drilling claim was technically true, it became the public face of the crisis. In a July 1990 speech Saddam called Kuwaiti and Emirati overproduction &ldquo;a kind of war against Iraq&rdquo;, and warned that Iraq would not accept death by slow economic strangulation. The grievances were real; what is harder to know is whether Saddam genuinely believed Kuwait was robbing him, or whether he was assembling a justification for an invasion he had already decided to launch.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the four main grievances Saddam used against Kuwait in 1990.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Saddam&rsquo;s public case for action against Kuwait rested on four grievances &mdash; unpaid war debts, Kuwaiti oil overproduction, a long-running border dispute over Bubiyan and Warbah islands, and accusations of slant-drilling at the Rumaila oil field.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Jeddah Talks and the Glaspie Meeting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In late July 1990 Saudi mediation produced a last-ditch negotiation in Jeddah between Iraqi and Kuwaiti officials. Iraq demanded debt forgiveness, $2.4 billion in compensation for Rumaila, the lease of Bubiyan and Warbah islands, and a binding agreement on oil quotas. Kuwait offered some financial easing but refused the territorial demands. The talks broke down on 1 August 1990; the next day Iraqi tanks crossed the border.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Days before the invasion, on 25 July, Saddam met the US ambassador April Glaspie in Baghdad. Glaspie said the United States had &ldquo;no opinion on Arab-Arab conflicts, like your border disagreement with Kuwait&rdquo;. Critics later argued that the line gave Saddam a green light. Glaspie&rsquo;s defenders countered that she also warned against the use of force, and that Saddam was always going to read what he wanted to read. Either way, Saddam appears to have come away believing the United States would not fight to defend Kuwait.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Internal Iraqi planning had originally focused on a limited operation: seize Bubiyan and Warbah, occupy the Rumaila field, and force a humiliated Kuwait to renegotiate. By the time orders were issued, the plan had grown. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Annexing a country means absorbing its territory into your own state, ending its existence as a separate country.">annexation</dfn> announcement on 8 August declared Kuwait the &ldquo;19th province&rdquo; of Iraq, with Iraqi flags raised over Kuwait City and Iraqi licence plates issued for civilian cars. The shift from coercion to annexation was Saddam&rsquo;s critical miscalculation: a limited grab might have provoked condemnation but not war, full annexation forced every other state to choose a side.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Invasion, 2 August 1990</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Iraqi forces crossed the Kuwaiti border at around 2am local time on 2 August 1990. The spearhead was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The elite Iraqi armoured forces directly loyal to Saddam Hussein and used as the regime&rsquo;s most reliable shock troops.">Republican Guard</dfn>, supported by helicopter-borne special forces who seized Kuwait City&rsquo;s government buildings, the airport and the Dasman Palace before sunrise. Kuwait&rsquo;s military, around 16,000 strong, was outnumbered roughly six to one and was not on full alert. By the end of the first day, organised resistance had effectively collapsed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The ruling family of Kuwait, in power since the eighteenth century, including Emir Jaber al-Ahmad al-Sabah in 1990.">al-Sabah dynasty</dfn> &mdash; Emir Jaber al-Ahmad al-Sabah and his family &mdash; fled to Saudi Arabia in the early hours, taking the country&rsquo;s sovereign-wealth records with them. Iraqi troops looted gold reserves, art collections and supermarket shelves. Within a week Iraq announced an interim government in Kuwait City led by an Iraqi-appointed officer; on 8 August it formally annexed the country. Saddam now had a fait accompli: occupying Kuwait was already a done deed, and reversing it would require force.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">A Miscalculation, Not a Masterstroke</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">From inside Baghdad the invasion looked logical. It cancelled the debts (because Kuwait no longer existed as a separate state). It seized Kuwait&rsquo;s vast oil reserves, doubling Iraq&rsquo;s share of world reserves at a stroke. It would intimidate the rest of the Gulf into deference. And it offered the spectacle of a pan-Arab leader correcting an old colonial border, repackaging a financial crisis as a nationalist triumph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">From outside Baghdad it looked very different. Saudi Arabia now feared its eastern oil fields were next. The United States saw a state seizing twenty per cent of the world&rsquo;s oil reserves. Even the Soviet Union, Iraq&rsquo;s old patron, was not willing to defend an outright annexation at the United Nations. Within hours the UN Security Council passed Resolution 660 condemning the invasion. Within days, the US and the UK had begun deploying forces to Saudi Arabia. Saddam&rsquo;s gamble had not silenced his enemies; it had unified them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">It is a recurring claim that the US ambassador April Glaspie green-lit the invasion in her 25 July meeting. The transcript shows a more complicated picture: Glaspie repeated standard diplomatic language about not taking sides in Arab-Arab disputes, but she also said the United States expected disagreements to be resolved peacefully. The deeper point is that Saddam was looking for evidence to support a decision he was already making. Treating Glaspie as the cause of the invasion underplays Saddam&rsquo;s own agency &mdash; the cash crisis, the Rumaila claim and the army he could not afford to demobilise are the real drivers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: three reasons the invasion gave Saddam a short-term gain, and one reason it backfired strategically.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The invasion of Kuwait was launched at 2am on 2 August 1990 and Kuwait City fell within hours. By 8 August Iraq had annexed Kuwait as its &ldquo;19th province&rdquo;. The short-term gain was huge &mdash; doubled oil reserves, cancelled debts &mdash; but the move united outside powers against Iraq almost overnight.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Saddam&rsquo;s case against Kuwait combined unpaid debts, oil overproduction, a border dispute and slant-drilling claims at Rumaila &mdash; real grievances stitched into a public justification.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The 25 July Glaspie meeting and the failed Jeddah talks shaped Saddam&rsquo;s reading of the international response, but the deeper drivers were Iraqi cash needs and an army he could not afford to keep idle.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The 2 August invasion succeeded in hours but the 8 August annexation transformed it from a coercive grab into an open challenge, uniting outside powers in a way Saddam had not expected.</li></ul>
