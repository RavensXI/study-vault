--- DESCRIPTION ---
How an eight-year war left Iraq armed, indebted and looking outward for someone to blame.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A War That Refused to End Cleanly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the guns finally fell silent on the Iran-Iraq border in August 1988, neither side had won. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1980&ndash;88 war between Iraq and Iran, triggered by Saddam&rsquo;s invasion of Iran and ending in stalemate after roughly a million casualties.">Iran-Iraq War</dfn> had begun in September 1980 when Saddam Hussein gambled that a swift attack on a revolutionary Iran would seize the disputed <dfn class="term" data-def="The waterway forming part of the Iran-Iraq border, used to export oil from both countries and a long-standing source of dispute.">Shatt al-Arab</dfn> waterway and topple Ayatollah Khomeini. Eight years later he stood with roughly the same border, around 250,000 Iraqi soldiers dead, and a country built around a million-strong army he could not afford to keep paying.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The war set the stage for everything that followed in the Gulf. It taught Saddam that chemical weapons worked &mdash; he had used mustard gas and nerve agents on Iranian troops and on his own Kurdish citizens at Halabja in March 1988. It hardened a regime ideology built on <dfn class="term" data-def="A pan-Arab nationalist and socialist ideology dominant in Iraq under Saddam, claiming to unite the Arab nation against foreign domination.">Ba&rsquo;athism</dfn> and the cult of the leader. And it left the Iraqi economy in ruins at exactly the moment Saddam needed it to deliver peace dividends to a shattered population.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how big was Iraq&rsquo;s post-war debt, what share of it was external, and roughly how much was owed to Kuwait?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1989 Iraq was carrying around $80 billion of war debt, with $35 billion owed externally and roughly $14&ndash;15 billion of that owed to Kuwait. Saddam emerged from the war militarily intact but financially trapped.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Cost of Eight Years of Stalemate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Iraq&rsquo;s population in 1988 was around 17 million. Its army was the fourth largest in the world. Its oil revenue, the only realistic engine of repayment, had been crushed by the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1984&ndash;88 phase of the Iran-Iraq war when both sides attacked oil tankers in the Gulf, drawing in US and other Western navies.">Tanker War</dfn> phase of the conflict, when both sides had attacked Gulf shipping and frightened off insurance markets. Saddam expected the rich Arab states &mdash; Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates &mdash; to write off the loans they had made him in the early 1980s. He framed the war as a defence of the Arab world against the Iranian revolution: in his telling, Iraq had bled so the Gulf monarchies did not have to.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Gulf states disagreed. They had bankrolled Iraq, but they expected to be repaid &mdash; particularly Kuwait, which insisted that the loans were not <dfn class="term" data-def="Payments demanded from a defeated or weakened state to compensate for damage caused during a war.">reparations</dfn> Iraq had earned but commercial debts Iraq owed. By 1989 Saddam was publicly calling for OPEC oil prices to rise so he could pay his bills, and quietly rehearsing the argument that wealthy Arabs who refused to help were stabbing the Arab nation in the back.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">A million men under arms with no jobs to return to was not a peace dividend, it was a political crisis. Iraq&rsquo;s pre-war civilian economy had been gutted: factories destroyed by Iranian air raids, ports silted up, the southern marshlands drained for military use. Sending demobilised soldiers home into mass unemployment risked unrest. Keeping them in uniform meant continuing to spend on a war footing in peacetime &mdash; the worst of both worlds. Saddam&rsquo;s solution would, eighteen months later, be to point that army at Kuwait.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">A Regime Hardening, Not Softening</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Outside Iraq, observers in 1989 hoped that a war-weary Saddam might mellow. Inside Iraq the opposite was happening. The Ba&rsquo;ath Party tightened its grip; the security services that had hunted deserters now hunted dissenters. Saddam&rsquo;s family clan from Tikrit dominated key military and intelligence posts. State-controlled television showed him in field-marshal&rsquo;s uniform laying wreaths for the war dead, casting himself as the indispensable defender of the Arab world.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Halabja gas attack of 1988, which killed around 5,000 Kurdish civilians in a single day, drew limited Western condemnation at the time. The United States had tilted towards Iraq during the war as a counterweight to Iran, and Washington was reluctant to break the relationship over Kurdish casualties. Saddam read the silence as permission. Within his regime, the lesson of 1980&ndash;88 was that brutality paid and the world looked the other way.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">It is tempting to treat August 1988 as a tidy full stop &mdash; UN Resolution 598, ceasefire, end of story. In reality nothing was settled. The Shatt al-Arab dispute remained legally unresolved. Roughly $80 billion of war debt was unpaid. Hundreds of thousands of unemployed veterans needed wages. Halabja and the Kurdish question simmered. The Tanker War had taught Saddam how easily Gulf shipping could be choked. Each loose end fed directly into the August 1990 invasion of Kuwait. The war did not end &mdash; it paused, and the pause ran out.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why It Mattered for the Gulf</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">By the start of 1990, three things were true at once. Iraq had the military hardware to invade a neighbour. Iraq had the financial pressure to need to. And Saddam had a story &mdash; the Arab defender betrayed by ungrateful neighbours &mdash; that he believed could justify it to the wider Arab world. Regional instability was not an accident waiting to happen; it was being authored by a regime whose post-war problems looked, from inside Baghdad, like they had a quick external solution.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: list three direct consequences of the Iran-Iraq war that fed into the 1990 invasion of Kuwait.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Iran-Iraq war did not resolve regional tension &mdash; it manufactured the next crisis. Massive debt, an oversized army, normalised use of chemical weapons and an unforgiving Saddam Hussein were all bequeathed straight to the Kuwait crisis of 1990.</p>
</div>
