<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Bush&rsquo;s &lsquo;War on Terror&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">On the evening of 11 September 2001, a stunned President George W. Bush told Americans from the Oval Office that the United States would not distinguish between terrorists and the governments that harbour them. Nine days later, on 20 September, he addressed Congress with a single demand to the Taliban: &lsquo;Deliver to United States authorities all the leaders of al-Qaeda who hide in your land.&rsquo; He went on, &lsquo;Every nation in every region now has a decision to make. Either you are with us, or you are with the terrorists.&rsquo; The phrase the <dfn class="term" data-def="The collective name given by the Bush administration to the post-9/11 US-led campaign against al-Qaeda, the Taliban and other militant Islamist networks worldwide.">War on Terror</dfn> was now public policy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Bush&rsquo;s three demands to the Taliban after 9/11, and the Taliban&rsquo;s response.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Bush demanded the Taliban hand over al-Qaeda&rsquo;s leaders, close every training camp and allow US inspection. The Taliban offered to try bin Laden in an Islamic court if shown evidence, but refused unconditional extradition. Bush rejected the counter-offer; military action followed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Bush&rsquo;s Aims and the Doctrine of Pre-emption</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Bush&rsquo;s aims in launching Operation Enduring Freedom were openly stated. He wanted to destroy al-Qaeda&rsquo;s leadership and training camps, capture or kill Osama bin Laden, and remove the regime that protected him. Beyond Afghanistan, he set out a wider doctrine in his 2002 State of the Union speech, naming Iraq, Iran and North Korea as an <dfn class="term" data-def="The phrase used by President Bush in his 2002 State of the Union to group Iraq, Iran and North Korea as states allegedly seeking weapons of mass destruction and sponsoring terror.">axis of evil</dfn>. The new <dfn class="term" data-def="The Bush administration&rsquo;s policy that the United States would strike threatening regimes before they could attack, rather than waiting for evidence of imminent attack.">pre-emption doctrine</dfn> argued that the United States need not wait to be attacked: the speed of modern terrorism justified striking first. The 2002 National Security Strategy made this official.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Blair and the &lsquo;Shoulder to Shoulder&rsquo; Promise</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">On 11 September 2001 itself, Tony Blair told reporters in Brighton that Britain would stand &lsquo;shoulder to shoulder&rsquo; with the United States. Blair&rsquo;s political case rested on three points: an Article 5 obligation through NATO, a moral case that no civilised state could harbour mass murderers, and a strategic case that British and American security were now indivisible. Within days the British government had committed Royal Navy submarines to a strike role and SAS reconnaissance teams to forward operations. The first cruise missiles fired into Afghanistan on the opening night of the war were British as well as American.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">On 12 September 2001 the North Atlantic Council invoked Article 5 of the NATO treaty &mdash; the mutual-defence clause &mdash; for the first time in the alliance&rsquo;s history. The clause says that an armed attack on one member is to be considered an attack on all. The legal symbolism mattered: the alliance built to deter the Soviet Union was being mobilised against a stateless network of religious radicals based in central Asia. Allied AWACS aircraft began flying patrols over American skies within weeks.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Operation Enduring Freedom: The Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Pentagon&rsquo;s plan was deliberately unconventional. Rather than send a heavy invasion force, the United States would combine three elements: heavy air strikes on Taliban command and air-defence sites; small CIA Special Activities and US Special Forces teams on the ground; and the existing Afghan opposition forces of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A loose anti-Taliban alliance based in northern Afghanistan, dominated by Tajik, Uzbek and Hazara commanders; the US ground proxy in 2001.">Northern Alliance</dfn>, who would do most of the conventional fighting. Britain contributed cruise missiles, SAS detachments, refuelling tankers and reconnaissance flights. The total ground force at the start was a few hundred Western soldiers, not a few hundred thousand.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Campaign of October&ndash;December 2001</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The bombs began to fall at 8:57 pm Kabul time on 7 October 2001 &mdash; less than four weeks after 9/11. Cruise missiles and B-1, B-2 and B-52 bombers struck Taliban airfields, training camps and air-defence radars. For three weeks the Taliban front lines held remarkably firm; the Northern Alliance, advised by US Special Forces calling in air strikes, made slow gains. Then the line collapsed. The northern city of Mazar-i-Sharif fell on 9 November, Kabul on 13 November, Jalalabad two days later. Cornered Taliban and al-Qaeda fighters retreated south to Kandahar and to the cave complexes of <dfn class="term" data-def="A mountainous cave complex in eastern Afghanistan, near the Pakistan border, where al-Qaeda fighters made a stand in December 2001 and from which Osama bin Laden escaped into Pakistan.">Tora Bora</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">In early December 2001, US air power and Afghan militias closed in on Tora Bora, where bin Laden had taken refuge. American commanders later admitted they had relied on Afghan proxies rather than committing US troops to seal the mountain passes. Bin Laden and several hundred fighters slipped over the border into Pakistan&rsquo;s tribal areas. A 2009 US Senate Foreign Relations Committee report called the failure to commit ground troops at Tora Bora the moment Washington missed its best chance to capture or kill bin Laden, an opportunity that would not recur until the Abbottabad raid in 2011.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why the Regime Collapsed So Fast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Kandahar fell on 7 December 2001. The Taliban regime had lasted barely two months under Western pressure. Three factors explain the speed of the collapse. First, the Taliban&rsquo;s air force and air defences were destroyed within ten days, removing any defence against precision bombing. Second, US special forces and CIA officers paid local commanders enormous sums in cash to switch sides; whole districts changed hands without a fight. Third, the Northern Alliance had spent five years preparing for this moment and pushed forward with conviction once Western air power broke the Taliban front line. The Western footprint was deliberately small &mdash; the model would later be called &lsquo;the Afghan model&rsquo; &mdash; and it gave the United States a quick military victory that it could not later translate into political stability.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the chain: 9/11 &rarr; Bush ultimatum &rarr; air war 7 October &rarr; Northern Alliance ground assault &rarr; Kabul falls 13 November &rarr; Tora Bora &rarr; Kandahar falls 7 December.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Taliban regime fell in just 63 days from the opening air strikes on 7 October 2001 to the fall of Kandahar on 7 December. The victory was won mostly by Afghan ground troops backed by American and British air power, not by massed Western forces &mdash; with major consequences for what came next.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Bush&rsquo;s &lsquo;war on terror&rsquo; doctrine combined ultimatum (deliver al-Qaeda or share their fate), pre-emption (strike before being attacked) and the &lsquo;axis of evil&rsquo; framing of Iraq, Iran and North Korea.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Operation Enduring Freedom from 7 October 2001 used US/UK air power, CIA Special Activities teams and Northern Alliance ground troops; NATO invoked Article 5 for the first time in its history.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The Taliban regime fell in 63 days, but bin Laden escaped at Tora Bora &mdash; a missed opportunity that shaped the next decade of the war.</li></ul>
