--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a horrified White House and a steely Downing Street turned outrage into a 35-nation army.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The First Phone Calls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Iraqi tanks rolled into Kuwait City in the early hours of 2 August 1990, President George H. W. Bush was at his Maine summer house in Kennebunkport. Margaret Thatcher was in Aspen, Colorado, attending a conference. Within hours both leaders had spoken; within a day they were sitting together at the Aspen Institute. The image of the two of them &mdash; Bush cautious, Thatcher already certain &mdash; would shape the diplomacy of the next six months.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Thatcher&rsquo;s line at Aspen, &ldquo;remember George, this is no time to go wobbly&rdquo;, captured Britain&rsquo;s position. The annexation of Kuwait was, in her view, an unambiguous breach of international law that had to be reversed by force if necessary. Bush, more cautious by temperament, agreed within days. By 5 August he was telling reporters at Andrews Air Force Base, &ldquo;this will not stand, this aggression against Kuwait&rdquo;. The choice had been made: Kuwait would be liberated, not negotiated.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two key UN resolutions, what each demanded, and the deadline set by the second one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two UN resolutions framed the international response. <dfn class="term" data-def="UN Security Council resolution passed on 2 August 1990 condemning the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait and demanding immediate withdrawal.">UN Resolution 660</dfn> on 2 August demanded immediate Iraqi withdrawal. <dfn class="term" data-def="UN Security Council resolution passed on 29 November 1990 authorising &lsquo;all necessary means&rsquo; to expel Iraq from Kuwait if not withdrawn by 15 January 1991.">UN Resolution 678</dfn> on 29 November set the deadline of 15 January 1991, after which member states could use &ldquo;all necessary means&rdquo;.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Operation Desert Shield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The first military priority was not liberating Kuwait but defending Saudi Arabia. Iraqi armoured divisions sat on the Saudi border, and the Saudi eastern oil fields were unprotected. King Fahd had to choose between trusting the United States to send forces or watching Saddam set the agenda for the whole region. After tense diplomacy &mdash; Defense Secretary Dick Cheney flew to Riyadh to brief the Saudi cabinet &mdash; King Fahd issued a formal invitation on 7 August 1990.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The US-led deployment of forces to Saudi Arabia from August 1990, designed to deter further Iraqi advance and prepare for liberating Kuwait.">Operation Desert Shield</dfn> moved the largest American military force overseas since Vietnam. By autumn around 250,000 US troops were in the Gulf; by December the total coalition force exceeded 500,000. Britain&rsquo;s contribution, codenamed <dfn class="term" data-def="The British military operation supporting the 1990&ndash;91 Gulf War, contributing around 53,000 personnel to the coalition.">Operation Granby</dfn>, eventually included some 53,000 personnel including the 1st Armoured Division.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Bush and his Secretary of State James Baker insisted that the coalition had to be visibly Arab as well as Western. If the operation looked like a colonial war by Christian powers against a Muslim country, Saddam&rsquo;s pan-Arab framing would gain traction. The price of Arab cover was relentless <dfn class="term" data-def="Diplomacy aimed at building and holding together a multi-state alliance for joint action.">coalition diplomacy</dfn>. Egypt sent two divisions, Syria sent one (a remarkable diplomatic moment given Hafez al-Assad&rsquo;s usual hostility to Washington), and Saudi Arabia provided forces as well as bases. Crucially, Israel was kept out of the coalition entirely &mdash; bringing Israel in would have driven the Arab states straight back out.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The UN Campaign</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Bush ran the diplomatic campaign through the United Nations rather than around it. Resolution 660 condemned the invasion within hours of the first reports. Resolution 661, four days later, imposed comprehensive economic <dfn class="term" data-def="Restrictions on trade and finance imposed by states or international bodies to pressure another state into changing its behaviour.">sanctions</dfn>. Through autumn 1990 a stream of further resolutions tightened the noose: blockading Iraqi shipping, banning air travel, demanding the release of foreign hostages Saddam was using as &ldquo;human shields&rdquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Resolution 678 was the decisive step. Passed on 29 November 1990 by twelve votes to two (Cuba and Yemen) with one abstention (China), it authorised member states to use &ldquo;all necessary means&rdquo; to enforce the previous resolutions if Iraq had not withdrawn by 15 January 1991. The phrase &ldquo;all necessary means&rdquo; was the diplomatic permission for war &mdash; without it, military action would have lacked legitimacy and Arab support would have crumbled.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Funding and the Strange New World Order</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Wars are expensive. The 1990&ndash;91 deployment cost roughly $61 billion in 1991 dollars, and the United States insisted that allies who benefited should pay. Saudi Arabia and Kuwait-in-exile each contributed around $16 billion. Germany and Japan, both forbidden by their constitutions from sending combat troops, contributed roughly $10 billion and $13 billion respectively. The result was a war the United States led but did not pay for &mdash; a model that would not be repeated.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Soviet Union&rsquo;s position was a quiet revolution. Mikhail Gorbachev refused to veto the UN resolutions, even though Iraq had been a Soviet client. Soviet diplomats pressed Saddam to withdraw, while keeping channels open in case a last-minute deal could be reached. For Bush, this was the &ldquo;new world order&rdquo; he spoke of in a September 1990 address to Congress &mdash; a post-Cold War system in which the great powers would cooperate against aggression. For Saddam, it was the most unwelcome surprise of the crisis.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Students often picture a US-UK operation with a few foreign flags pinned on for show. The reality was 35 contributing nations &mdash; including, importantly, Egypt, Syria, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Morocco. The Arab and Muslim share of the ground force was a deliberate diplomatic achievement. Soviet acquiescence at the UN, although not active participation, denied Saddam the cover of superpower rivalry. Without that wide coalition the operation would not have happened, or would have collapsed politically before it could begin.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: three reasons Bush ran the campaign through the UN rather than acting unilaterally.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By January 1991 a 35-nation coalition with around 700,000 troops sat in Saudi Arabia, paid for largely by allies, with Soviet acquiescence at the UN and a Resolution 678 deadline counting down. Saddam had assumed the world was too divided to act; in fact it had assembled in front of him.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Bush and Thatcher aligned within forty-eight hours of the invasion; their early certainty drove a UN-led campaign rather than unilateral US action.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Operation Desert Shield deployed roughly 700,000 coalition troops to Saudi Arabia at King Fahd&rsquo;s invitation, with Britain&rsquo;s Operation Granby contributing around 53,000.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">UN Resolutions 660, 661 and 678 turned outrage into a legal and diplomatic framework, with the 15 January 1991 deadline making war legitimate if Iraq did not withdraw.</li></ul>
