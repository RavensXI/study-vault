<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Promote?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="All the ways a business communicates with customers to inform, persuade and remind them about a product.">Promotion</dfn> is how a business talks to customers. It is the third of the four Ps. Even a brilliantly designed product at the right price will not sell if nobody knows it exists.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The specification lists four reasons businesses promote: (1) to <strong>inform or remind</strong> customers about the product, (2) to <strong>create or increase sales</strong>, (3) to <strong>create or change</strong> the image of the product, and (4) to <strong>persuade</strong> customers to buy. A Nike Air Max advert does three or four of these at once — showing the product, building the image, and pushing viewers towards the checkout.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down, from memory, the four AQA reasons businesses promote products.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four reasons for promotion are: inform/remind, create or increase sales, create or change image, and persuade customers to buy. Most adverts aim to do more than one at once.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Promotional Methods</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Advertising</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Paid communication from a business to customers via newspapers, magazines, TV, internet or billboards.">Advertising</dfn> covers newspapers, magazines, television, internet and billboards. Cadbury’s Christmas TV adverts have been a UK tradition for years — expensive to make, but they reach millions and build emotional connection. TV reaches mass audiences; internet adverts can be targeted at specific segments; billboards work locally; magazines reach niche readerships.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>PR, Sales Promotion, Sponsorship and Social Media</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Public Relations — managing the way a business communicates with the media and the public to build a positive image.">PR (Public Relations)</dfn> covers press releases, news stories and community events. It is cheaper than advertising and more credible — a news article carries more weight than a paid advert — but the business cannot control exactly what journalists write.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Short-term offers designed to push customers to buy now, including discounts, coupons, free gifts and BOGOF.">Sales promotion</dfn> includes point-of-sale displays, 2-for-1 offers, free gifts, samples, coupons and competitions. Tesco and Boots Meal Deals are a textbook example — customers feel they get extra value and the supermarket shifts more items per visit.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Paying to have a brand associated with a sports team, event, artist or cause to reach that audience.">Sponsorship</dfn> is paying to put a brand on a team, event or show. Emirates sponsoring Arsenal’s stadium and Aviva sponsoring the Premiership Rugby league link these brands with the positive feelings fans have for their sport.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using platforms like Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and X to reach customers directly, often through influencers or user content.">Social media</dfn> lets brands talk to customers directly through Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and X. Gymshark built a billion-pound brand almost entirely through fitness influencers on Instagram and TikTok — no TV ads needed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Choosing the Promotional Mix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of promotional methods a business uses at a given time.">promotional mix</dfn> is the blend of methods a business uses together. Most businesses combine several — Nike runs TV adverts AND sponsors athletes AND pushes Instagram content. Five factors shape the mix:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Finance available</strong> — TV and Premier League sponsorship are out of reach for a new start-up; social media and local flyers are not.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Competitor actions</strong> — if rivals are pouring money into TikTok, a business often has to match them to stay visible.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Nature of the product or service</strong> — complex products (pensions, software) need informative PR and expert reviews; impulse products (chocolate, crisps) suit sales promotions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Nature of the market</strong> — consumer markets differ from business-to-business. A bakery promotes to local families; a flour wholesaler promotes through trade magazines.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Target market</strong> — where does the target customer spend their time? Teenagers are on TikTok; over-60s read newspapers and watch ITV.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and name all five factors that shape the promotional mix, matching each to a brief example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The promotional mix is shaped by five factors: finance available, competitor actions, nature of the product/service, nature of the market, and the target market. The right method reaches the right people without wasting budget.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Generic Answers Cap at ~4/6</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Examiners are explicit about this: an answer that just describes ‘TV adverts reach lots of people’ without naming a business caps out at about 4 marks on a 6-mark question. Top-band answers name a real UK business, describe <em>their</em> chosen methods, and link each method to <em>their</em> target market. Nike + athlete sponsorship for sports fans. Gymshark + Instagram for Gen Z gym-goers. Cadbury + TV adverts for family audiences at Christmas.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits and Drawbacks of Each Method</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>TV advertising</strong> reaches mass audiences with strong images and sound, but is very expensive and hard to target precisely. <strong>Internet advertising</strong> is highly targeted (Google Ads, Meta) and measurable, but viewers use ad-blockers and scroll past. <strong>Billboards</strong> are cheap per viewer in local areas but cannot carry detailed messages.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>PR</strong> is cheap and credible but hard to control. <strong>Sales promotion</strong> drives quick sales but can cheapen the brand if overused — Iceland’s constant half-price offers trained customers to wait for discounts. <strong>Sponsorship</strong> builds long-term brand image but is slow to pay back. <strong>Social media</strong> is cheap and two-way, but a bad post can go viral for the wrong reasons — Ratners’ jewellery collapse in the 1990s shows what happens when brand communication goes wrong, even before social media amplified every mistake.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">The Mix Has to Match the Stage of the Product</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Promotion also shifts across the product life cycle. At <strong>launch</strong>, promotion is heavy and informative — customers need to know the product exists. During <strong>growth</strong>, adverts shift to persuasion, showing off the USP. At <strong>maturity</strong>, reminder adverts keep customers loyal while sales promotions defend against rivals. In <strong>decline</strong>, promotion is cut back to save cash unless the firm is running an extension strategy.</p>

<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">Businesses promote for four reasons: to inform, create sales, change image, and persuade to buy.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">The main methods are advertising, PR, sales promotion, sponsorship and social media — each suits different products and markets.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">The right mix depends on five factors: finance, competitors, product type, market type and target market. Name a real UK business to hit top marks.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">For any ‘Analyse’ or ‘Evaluate’ question on promotion, ALWAYS name a real UK business and describe which methods they use. Generic ‘advertising is good because it reaches lots of people’ answers are capped around 4/6. For 9-mark ‘Justify’ questions, explain why your chosen promotional mix beats at least one alternative — e.g. why a new start-up should use TikTok rather than TV adverts.</p>
