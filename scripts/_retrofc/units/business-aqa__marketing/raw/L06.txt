--- DESCRIPTION ---
Distribution channels, e-commerce, m-commerce and how the whole marketing mix has to work as one.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Fourth P — Place</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The routes a business uses to get a product from producer to final customer.">Place</dfn> is the fourth of the four Ps. It covers every route a product takes from factory to customer — shelves, websites, apps, phone orders. The wrong place decision can sink a business: ASOS thrives online because its teen-to-thirties customers shop on their phones. Primark, by contrast, refuses to sell online because impulse-buying in store is central to its model.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Distribution Channels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The AQA specification names three main distribution routes: retailers, wholesalers and telesales.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Three Distribution Channels</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A business that sells products directly to the final customer — including shops, supermarkets and online stores.">Retailers</dfn> are shops that sell directly to the final customer. Tesco, Boots, JD Sports and Primark are retailers. They handle displays, customer service and payments — but take a slice of the final price in return.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A business that buys large quantities from producers and sells smaller quantities to retailers.">Wholesalers</dfn> sit between producer and retailer. They buy in bulk, break it down, and sell smaller quantities to lots of small shops. A corner-shop owner buys from Booker rather than Coca-Cola direct. Wholesalers cut hassle for both sides but add cost.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Selling products to customers over the phone, either inbound or outbound.">Telesales</dfn> is selling over the phone. Still used by insurance, energy and software sellers, though its share is falling as customers prefer online.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three distribution channels in the specification, with one UK example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three distribution channels in the specification are retailers, wholesalers and telesales. A business may use one or several, and the best choice depends on where the target customer actually wants to buy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">E-commerce and M-commerce</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Buying and selling products online through websites.">E-commerce</dfn> is buying and selling through websites. <dfn class="term" data-def="Buying and selling through mobile phones — typically via apps.">M-commerce</dfn> is the mobile version — apps on phones. ASOS is a UK pioneer: 100% online, no physical shops, reaching customers in over 200 countries. Low overheads mean prices can be competitive, and stock can be updated instantly. The same is true for Ocado in groceries and Zalando in European fashion.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits and Drawbacks of E-commerce</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Benefits:</strong> reach customers anywhere in the world — ASOS can sell a UK-designed dress in Australia overnight. Low rent and staff costs compared to high-street shops. Open 24/7. Sales data can be tracked and used to refine the product range. Easy to target promotions to segments.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Drawbacks:</strong> customers cannot see or try before buying, so returns rates are high — fashion returns can hit 40% and destroy margins. Cyber-security and data protection costs are real. Delivery problems damage the brand. Competition is intense because rivals are only a click away. And traditional strengths like staff expertise (John Lewis) are lost.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n13">E-commerce also opens up <dfn class="term" data-def="Selling to customers in other countries.">international markets</dfn>. A small Manchester-based soap maker can sell to Japan through its own website or through marketplaces like Amazon, without opening a single overseas shop.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why the Whole Mix Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Each of the four Ps — product, price, promotion, place — matters. But they are not independent. A premium product like an Apple iPhone (product) has a premium price (price), sleek TV adverts (promotion) and is sold in modern Apple Stores or through their online site (place). If Apple suddenly started selling through Poundland, the whole brand image would collapse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="When all four elements of the marketing mix work together and reinforce the same customer message.">integrated marketing mix</dfn>. Every element sends the same message to the target customer. Primark: low price, high-street shops, no promotions needed, fashion product. Apple: premium price, flagship stores, slick TV ads, premium product. Consistency is what makes a marketing mix work.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence what an ‘integrated marketing mix’ means, giving one UK example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An integrated marketing mix is one where all four Ps — product, price, promotion and place — reinforce the same message to the target customer. Primark and Apple are both examples, even though they target opposite ends of the market.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Mix Evolves Over Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The mix is never fixed — customer behaviour, technology and rivals all keep moving. Debenhams failed partly because its mix stopped evolving: same high-street shops, same mid-tier prices, same promotions, while Next invested in e-commerce and ASOS captured the online segment. A business that notices change early — like Domino’s adding the app-ordering ‘place’ channel — wins long-term.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The same logic applies to all four Ps together. Tesco’s marketing mix in 2000 was very different from 2025: the product range has stretched to Finest, Express stores and plant-based ready meals; prices now include Clubcard-only offers; promotion has moved heavily into digital; and place has added online groceries, Tesco Express convenience stores and third-party delivery via Whoosh. Because every element updated together, the mix stayed integrated while each part modernised.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>When the Mix Falls Out of Step</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">When one P falls out of step with the others, customers get confused and sales suffer. Marks & Spencer spent years with premium-quality clothing (product) and premium prices (price), but promotion and store fit-out felt old-fashioned compared to rivals like Zara and Next — so younger customers never came. M&S had to refresh all four Ps together (modernised stores, refreshed ranges, app-led promotion, new Sparks loyalty scheme) to recover.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Small businesses have the opposite problem. A new Manchester coffee shop may have a brilliant product and great Instagram promotion, but if its place (the shop location) is hidden down a side street, footfall is too low to support the business. All four Ps have to pull their weight.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25">Distribution channels in the spec are retailers, wholesalers and telesales — plus e-commerce and m-commerce.</li><li data-narration-id="n26">E-commerce opens up international markets and cuts overheads, but raises returns, delivery and cyber-security issues.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">An integrated marketing mix works when all four Ps send the same message. The mix must evolve as customers, technology and rivals change.</li></ul>

For 12-mark evaluations, show that you understand the four Ps must work together: a premium product with a cheap distribution channel sends mixed signals. A balanced answer with a named UK business for each side will reach the top band.</p>
