--- DESCRIPTION ---
Product Life Cycle stages, extension strategies and the Boston Matrix for balancing a portfolio.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Products Have a Life Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every product goes through stages over time — it is born, grows, peaks and eventually declines. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A model showing the stages a product goes through from launch to withdrawal: R&D, introduction, growth, maturity and decline.">product life cycle (PLC)</dfn> is the model businesses use to track this journey and decide what to do at each stage. Understanding it stops a business pumping money into a declining product or charging too little for one that is about to take off.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Five Stages</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Each Stage Explained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Research & development (R&D)</strong> — the product is being designed and tested. Costs are high; revenue is zero. Dyson famously spent years developing the cyclone vacuum before the first one was sold.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Introduction</strong> — the product launches. Sales grow slowly as customers hear about it. Price may be set high (skimming) or low (penetration). Adverts are heavy. The business is often still making a loss on this product.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Growth</strong> — sales rise quickly as word spreads and rivals take notice. Profits turn positive as fixed development costs get spread over more units.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Maturity</strong> — sales peak and flatten. The market is saturated, competition is fierce, and prices may need to fall. The iPhone has been in maturity for several years — annual refreshes keep sales steady rather than booming.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Decline</strong> — sales fall as customers move to newer products. Nokia’s 2000s dominance in mobile phones collapsed into decline once Apple and Samsung launched smartphones.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the five stages of the product life cycle in order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five stages of the product life cycle in order are: research & development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Drawing the PLC curve is not a tested exam skill — focus on interpreting what happens to sales and profit at each stage.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Extension Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Actions a business takes to stretch the life of a product and delay its decline.">Extension strategies</dfn> are how businesses push back the decline stage. The specification lists five: updating packaging, adding more or different features, changing the target market, advertising and price reduction. Cadbury’s Dairy Milk — in the UK market since 1905 — has survived through constant extension: Fruit & Nut, Whole Nut, Caramel, Oreo, Marvellous Creations, vegan plant-based versions, limited-edition flavours, new packaging designs. Each launch refreshes the brand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Not every strategy suits every product. A simple <em>price reduction</em> may boost sales short-term but trains customers to expect cheaper prices. <em>Adding features</em> can backfire if the product becomes too complicated. Extension works best when it gives customers a genuine new reason to buy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Product Portfolio and the Boston Matrix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Most businesses sell more than one product. Their <dfn class="term" data-def="The full collection of products or product lines that a business sells.">product portfolio</dfn> is the full set. A healthy portfolio has products at different life cycle stages — so that as one declines, a younger one is rising to take its place.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A tool that plots a firm's products on two axes: market growth rate and market share.">Boston Matrix</dfn> (also called the Boston Box) helps a business see this balance. It plots each product against two things: <strong>market growth rate</strong> on the vertical axis, and <strong>market share</strong> (relative to the biggest rival) on the horizontal axis. This creates four categories.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Four Boston Matrix Categories</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Stars — high growth, high share.</strong> Fast-growing market and the business is winning it. Needs cash to stay on top, but will become a cash cow later. Electric vehicles for Tesla.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Cash cows — low growth, high share.</strong> The market has matured but the business still dominates. Generates steady profits that can fund Stars and Question Marks. Cadbury Dairy Milk chocolate bars.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Question marks — high growth, low share.</strong> Fast market but the business has a small slice. Could become a Star with investment, or flop. Risky — needs a decision.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Dogs — low growth, low share.</strong> Slow market and the business is losing. Usually withdrawn or sold. Nokia feature phones after the smartphone takeover.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the two axes of the Boston Matrix — and say what the vertical axis is NOT.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Boston Matrix axes are <strong>market growth rate</strong> (vertical) and <strong>market share</strong> (horizontal). The vertical axis is NOT how fast the product itself is growing — it is how fast the whole market is growing. This is the most common misconception in the exam.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">What Do You Do With Each Category?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The Boston Matrix is useful because each category suggests an action. <strong>Stars</strong> need reinvested cash — the aim is to protect market share as the market grows. Tesla pours money into new Gigafactories to keep its Star position in electric vehicles. <strong>Cash Cows</strong> are milked — keep them steady with small refreshes, and use the profit to fund riskier products elsewhere. Cadbury Dairy Milk subsidises Mondelez’s newer experimental brands.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Question Marks</strong> need a decision: invest heavily to turn them into Stars, or pull the plug. Netflix’s early gaming push was a Question Mark — fast-growing market, low Netflix share — and the firm decided to invest. <strong>Dogs</strong> are usually withdrawn, sold or quietly wound down because they no longer pay their way. Cadbury discontinued Wispa for seven years in the 1990s when it looked like a Dog, then relaunched it in 2008 after fan campaigns revived demand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The Boston Matrix has limits too. Drawing it is not a tested exam skill — the focus is on interpretation. And it only uses two axes, so it ignores things like profit margin, brand value and how strategic a product is. A small but iconic product (like Rolls-Royce’s flagship Phantom) might look like a Dog on paper but is crucial for the brand’s image.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">The product life cycle has five stages: R&D, introduction, growth, maturity and decline. Each stage needs a different pricing and promotion approach.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">Extension strategies — new packaging, new features, new target markets, advertising, price cuts — stretch the life of a product and delay decline.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">The Boston Matrix plots products against <em>market growth</em> and <em>market share</em>, sorting them into Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks and Dogs to balance a portfolio.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">When a question asks you to place a product on the Boston Matrix, always explain both axes: is the market growing fast? And does the business have a big share of that market? The top misconception examiners see is confusing market growth with product sales growth — lose this and you lose the point. For 6-mark ‘Analyse’ questions, link the category (Star, Cash Cow, Question Mark, Dog) to the action the business should take.</p>
