--- DESCRIPTION ---
Pricing methods, product design and USP — two mix elements that decide whether customers buy.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The ‘Product’ and ‘Price’ Ps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Product and price are two of the four elements of the marketing mix. If the product does not solve a customer’s problem or look appealing, no promotion will save it. If the price is wrong — too high for the quality, or too low to cover costs — even a great product will fail. This lesson covers how UK businesses design products that stand out, and how they choose a pricing method that fits their strategy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Product: Design, Image and Target Market</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A successful product matches the target customer’s needs and communicates the right image through its design. Apple’s iPhone is a classic UK-relevant example: clean lines, premium glass and aluminium, and a box that feels expensive before you even turn the phone on. Every design choice says ‘high-end’, justifying a premium price.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Designing new products brings benefits — higher sales, reaching new customers, staying ahead of rivals — but also risks. R&D is expensive, launches can flop, and existing customers may feel the brand has changed too much. Businesses manage this risk with market research (last lesson) before committing to design.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down three things good product design must deliver for the target customer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Good product design must deliver: (1) the function the customer needs, (2) an image that matches the target market (premium, budget, eco-friendly), and (3) differentiation from rival products.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Unique Selling Point (USP) and Brand Image</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A feature that makes one product clearly different from its rivals and gives customers a reason to choose it.">unique selling point (USP)</dfn> is what makes a product clearly different from rivals. BrewDog’s USP in the beer market is punk branding and full ingredient transparency. innocent’s USP in smoothies is natural ingredients with a chatty, ethical tone. A USP lets a business charge a premium and build customer loyalty because customers cannot get the same thing elsewhere.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The personality and values that customers associate with a brand — built through logo, packaging, advertising and experience.">Brand image</dfn> is how customers see a brand in their head. Nike means performance and confidence. Primark means cheap and cheerful. Rolls-Royce means luxury and heritage. Brand image is built slowly through consistent design, advertising and experience — and once it exists, it shapes every buying decision customers make. A strong brand image also lets a business launch new products more easily because customers are already willing to trust the name: Apple’s move from laptops into phones, watches and headphones worked because the brand image was already established.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Pricing Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The specification requires five pricing methods. Each one suits different situations — a business picks the method that matches its product, competition and objectives.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Five Pricing Methods</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Setting a high launch price on a new, innovative product and lowering it later as rivals enter the market.">Price skimming</dfn> — used by Apple on new iPhones. Launch high, attract early adopters willing to pay a premium, then cut the price once rivals catch up. Works best when the product has a real USP.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Setting a low launch price to win market share fast, then raising it later.">Penetration pricing</dfn> — used by Aldi and Lidl when entering the UK supermarket market. Low prices attracted cost-conscious shoppers fast, and both chains have since nudged prices up while keeping their value image.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Setting prices roughly in line with what rivals are charging.">Competitive pricing</dfn> — used everywhere in the petrol market. Prices at neighbouring garages move together because customers check the board across the road. Protects market share but means the business cannot stand out on price alone.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Selling a product at or below cost to attract customers into the store where they will buy other, more profitable items.">Loss leader</dfn> — supermarkets selling milk, bread or baked beans at a loss. Customers come in for the bargain and fill their trolley with higher-margin items.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Working out the cost of making a product and adding a fixed percentage mark-up to set the price.">Cost-plus</dfn> — used by tradespeople and manufacturers. Calculate the unit cost, add (say) 40%, and that is the price. Simple and guarantees a margin, but ignores what customers will actually pay.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Factors That Influence Price</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Four factors pull price up or down. <strong>Costs</strong> set a floor — a business must eventually cover its costs to survive. <strong>Nature of the market</strong> matters: luxury markets accept premium prices; budget markets do not. <strong>Degree of competition</strong> shapes how free the business is to set its own price — where there are many rivals (petrol, coffee), prices move together. <strong>Product life cycle</strong> stage matters too: launch prices differ from mature-stage prices, which we cover next lesson.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Underpinning all of this is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The general rule that as price rises, demand falls — and vice versa.">price and demand relationship</dfn>: as price rises, demand usually falls. Raise the price of a Greggs sausage roll by 50p and some customers will walk past. This is why small price changes are tested carefully.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits and Risks of New Product Development</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Benefits of launching a new product:</strong> it can open up new customer segments, stretch the brand into higher-margin areas, keep the business ahead of rivals, and refresh an ageing product portfolio. Tesla’s move into the Model 3 at a lower price point brought in mass-market buyers it could not reach with the premium Model S.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Risks:</strong> research and development is expensive, the new product may fail to sell (New Coke in the 1980s is the classic failure), and the existing brand can be damaged if customers feel it has moved too far from what they liked. Good businesses use market research — the last lesson — to reduce the risk before committing cash.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and name all five pricing methods, giving one UK example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five pricing methods in the specification are: price skimming, penetration pricing, competitive pricing, loss leader and cost-plus. Each method suits a different type of product, market and objective.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25">Good product design matches target-customer needs, projects the right brand image, and differentiates through a USP.</li><li data-narration-id="n26">The five pricing methods are skimming, penetration, competitive, loss leader and cost-plus — each suits different products and markets.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Price is influenced by costs, market type, competition and product life cycle — and raising price usually lowers demand.</li></ul>

For 9-mark ‘Justify’ answers, say <em>why</em> your chosen method beats at least one alternative in this scenario. Writing about USP and brand image from a real named business lifts you straight into the top band for 12-mark evaluations.</p>
