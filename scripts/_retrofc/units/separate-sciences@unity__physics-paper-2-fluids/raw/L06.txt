--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Waves?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter.">wave</dfn> is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. When you drop a stone into a pond, ripples spread outward — the water itself does not travel across the pond, but the energy does. This is the key idea behind all waves: energy is transferred, but the particles of the medium return to their original position after the wave passes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Waves can travel through a medium (such as water, air, or a solid) or, in the case of electromagnetic waves, through a vacuum. In this lesson we focus on <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave that needs a medium (air, water or a solid) to travel through, such as a sound wave.">mechanical waves</dfn> — waves that require a medium to travel through.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why particles in a medium oscillate about a fixed point rather than traveling with the wave.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. The particles of the medium oscillate (vibrate) about a fixed point but do not travel with the wave.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Transverse Waves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave in which the oscillations of the particles are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction the wave travels.">transverse wave</dfn>, the oscillations of the particles are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction in which the wave travels. Think of a rope: if you flick one end up and down, the wave moves horizontally along the rope, but each point on the rope moves up and down.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The highest point of a transverse wave is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The highest point on a transverse wave, above the rest position.">crest</dfn> (or peak), and the lowest point is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The lowest point on a transverse wave, below the rest position.">trough</dfn>. Examples of transverse waves include water surface waves, light waves, and all other electromagnetic waves. S-waves produced by earthquakes are also transverse.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Longitudinal Waves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave in which the oscillations of the particles are parallel to the direction the wave travels, producing compressions and rarefactions.">longitudinal wave</dfn>, the oscillations of the particles are parallel to the direction in which the wave travels. A slinky spring demonstrates this well: if you push and pull one end back and forth, regions where the coils bunch together (<dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are pushed close together.">compressions</dfn>) and regions where they spread apart (<dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are spread further apart.">rarefactions</dfn>) move along the spring.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Sound is the most common example of a longitudinal wave. When a speaker vibrates, it pushes air molecules together and pulls them apart, creating compressions and rarefactions that travel through the air to your ear. P-waves from earthquakes are also longitudinal.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close the box and draw diagrams showing how oscillations differ between transverse and longitudinal waves, labeling light and sound.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Transverse:</strong> oscillations perpendicular to direction of travel (e.g. light, water surface waves). <strong>Longitudinal:</strong> oscillations parallel to direction of travel (e.g. sound). Both types transfer energy, not matter.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Transverse waves</strong> have crests and troughs. The particles move up and down (or side to side) while the wave moves forward. Examples: light, radio waves, water surface ripples, S-waves.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Longitudinal waves</strong> have compressions and rarefactions. The particles move back and forth in the same direction as the wave. Examples: sound waves, ultrasound, P-waves.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A useful way to remember: “Transverse = T = The oscillations go 'T'all (up and down)”. In longitudinal waves, the particles move “along” the direction of travel.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Wave Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">All waves — whether transverse or longitudinal — can be described using four key properties:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Amplitude</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum displacement of a point on a wave from its undisturbed (rest) position, measured in metres.">amplitude</dfn> is the maximum displacement of a point on the wave from its undisturbed (rest) position. For a transverse wave, it is the distance from the rest position to the top of a crest (or bottom of a trough). A larger amplitude means the wave carries more energy. Amplitude is measured in metres (m).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Wavelength</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance from one point on a wave to the equivalent point on the next wave, such as crest to crest, measured in metres.">wavelength</dfn> (λ, the Greek letter lambda) is the distance from one point on a wave to the equivalent point on the next wave — for example, from crest to crest or trough to trough. Wavelength is measured in metres (m).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Frequency</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of complete waves passing a fixed point per second, measured in hertz (Hz).">frequency</dfn> (f) is the number of complete waves that pass a fixed point each second. It is measured in <dfn class="term" data-def="The unit of frequency, equal to one wave per second. Symbol: Hz.">hertz</dfn> (Hz). A frequency of 100 Hz means 100 complete waves pass every second.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Period</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for one complete wave to pass a fixed point, measured in seconds. Period = 1 ÷ frequency.">period</dfn> (T) is the time taken for one complete wave to pass a point. It is the inverse of frequency: T = 1 ÷ f. If a wave has a frequency of 50 Hz, its period is 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 seconds.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Wave Speed and the Wave Equation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The speed at which a wave front travels through a medium, measured in metres per second (m/s).">Wave speed</dfn> (v) is the speed at which the wave front moves through the medium. It is measured in metres per second (m/s). Wave speed, frequency, and wavelength are linked by a simple equation that you must learn for the exam:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Hide the equation and rearrange v = f × λ to solve for wavelength, then state the units for v, f, and λ.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Wave equation:</strong> wave speed = frequency × wavelength, or <strong>\(v = f  \times  \lambda\)</strong>. Units: v in m/s, f in Hz, λ in m. You must be able to rearrange this to find any of the three quantities.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n24">To rearrange: <strong>\(f = v  \div  \lambda\)</strong> and <strong>\(\lambda  = v  \div  f\)</strong>. Always check your units before substituting — convert centimetres to metres (divide by 100) and kilometres to metres (multiply by 1000).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Measuring Waves: The Ripple Tank</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A shallow tray of water used to study wave behaviour by observing the pattern of ripples created by a vibrating dipper.">ripple tank</dfn> is a piece of apparatus used in required practical work to investigate the properties of waves in water. A motor-driven dipper creates regular waves on the surface of shallow water. A lamp above the tank projects the wave pattern onto a white card below, making the crests and troughs visible as bright and dark lines.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">To measure the wavelength, you can use a ruler to measure the distance across several wave crests and then divide by the number of waves. For example, if 10 wavelengths span 30 cm, the wavelength is 30 ÷ 10 = 3 cm (0.03 m). Measuring multiple wavelengths and dividing gives a more accurate result than measuring a single wave.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">To measure frequency, count the number of waves passing a fixed point in a known time using a stopwatch. For example, if 20 waves pass in 10 seconds, the frequency is 20 ÷ 10 = 2 Hz. Once you know the wavelength and frequency, you can calculate the wave speed using v = f × λ.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Method:</strong> Set up the ripple tank with a motor-driven dipper. Switch on the lamp above the tank. Adjust the frequency of the dipper and observe the wave pattern projected below. Use a ruler to measure the distance across several wavelengths and calculate the wavelength. Count waves passing a point over a set time to determine frequency. Calculate wave speed using v = f × λ.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Key tips:</strong> Use a stroboscope (flashing light) to “freeze” the wave pattern, making measurements easier. Measure across as many wavelengths as possible to reduce percentage error. Keep the water depth constant across the tank for consistent results. Note that changing the depth of water changes the wave speed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Common Exam Pitfalls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35">Students often confuse amplitude with wavelength. Remember: <strong>amplitude</strong> is measured vertically (rest position to crest), while <strong>wavelength</strong> is measured horizontally (crest to crest). Another common error is forgetting to convert units — wavelengths given in cm or nm must be converted to metres before using the wave equation. Finally, remember that increasing the amplitude does <strong>not</strong> change the frequency or wavelength; it only changes the energy carried by the wave.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n38">Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. Transverse waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel (e.g. light); longitudinal waves oscillate parallel (e.g. sound).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">The four key wave properties are amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and period. Amplitude is vertical (rest to crest); wavelength is horizontal (crest to crest).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n40">The wave equation v = f × λ links wave speed, frequency, and wavelength — learn it, practise rearranging it, and always convert units to SI before calculating.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n36">In “calculate” questions on waves, always write out the equation first (v = f × λ), show your substitution with correct units, then give the answer with the right unit. Examiners award marks for each step. When asked to “compare” transverse and longitudinal waves, structure your answer with one similarity and one difference — and always refer to the direction of oscillation relative to the direction of energy transfer.</p>
