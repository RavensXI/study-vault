--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reducing the Risk of Injury</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In any sporting activity, there is always a risk of injury. Coaches, players and support staff all share a responsibility to identify safety <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that has the potential to cause harm or injury.">hazard</dfn>s and take steps to reduce the chance of harm. Playing surfaces and equipment are obvious potential hazards, but risks can also come from the surrounding environment, the weather, and even the characteristics of the participants themselves.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about responsibility for safety does not fall on.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Responsibility for safety does not fall on one person alone. Coaches, officials, players and support staff must all play their part in identifying hazards and following safety procedures to protect everyone involved.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Safety Checks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Safety checks should be carried out for all activities <strong>before</strong> and <strong>during</strong> participation. These checks help to identify hazards early and prevent injuries from occurring.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Before Participation</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>Check the playing surface</strong> — look for leaves, debris, glass, wet patches or uneven ground that could cause slips, trips or falls.</li>
<li><strong>Check the area around the pitch</strong> — ensure padding is in place on goalposts, corner flags are secure, and there are no obstructions near the playing area.</li>
<li><strong>Check the players</strong> — ensure participants are wearing suitable clothing and footwear, all jewellery has been removed, and long hair is tied back.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n8">During Participation</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li><strong>Check the weather throughout</strong> — if conditions become unsafe (lightning, heavy rain, extreme heat), the activity should be stopped immediately.</li>
<li><strong>Monitor players for medical issues</strong> — watch for signs of dehydration, exhaustion, breathing difficulties, or any emerging injury that could worsen if play continues.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Risk Assessment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A written document that identifies possible hazards and proposes ways to remove or reduce the risk of injury.">risk assessment</dfn> is a written document that identifies possible hazards and proposes ways to remove or reduce the risk. It must be written by someone with relevant knowledge and experience of the activity — typically the coach or a qualified safety officer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A risk assessment serves several important purposes:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li>It <strong>identifies hazards</strong> that could cause harm to participants, officials or spectators.</li>
<li>It <strong>evaluates</strong> whether the level of risk is acceptable or too high.</li>
<li>It identifies <strong>who could be injured</strong> and how.</li>
<li>It enables <strong>steps to be taken</strong> to minimise or eliminate the risks identified.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define the main purpose of a risk assessment in your own words.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The main purpose of a risk assessment is to <strong>reduce the chances of injury</strong> by identifying hazards before they cause harm and putting measures in place to control them. Risk assessments must be carried out <strong>before AND during</strong> the activity.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A risk assessment also considers the <strong>surrounding areas</strong> that could cause injury, such as advertising boards, spectator seating areas, fencing, or hard surfaces near the edge of the playing area.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Actions put in place to remove a hazard or reduce the level of risk.">Control measure</dfn>s are the specific actions put in place to remove a hazard or reduce a risk. Examples include ensuring players wear correct footwear, cleaning spillages immediately, checking equipment before use, and padding any hard surfaces near the playing area.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17">A typical risk assessment follows a three-column format:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hazard:</strong> Wet playing surface → <strong>Risk:</strong> Players could slip and suffer a sprain or fracture → <strong>Control Measure:</strong> Inspect the pitch before the session; postpone if waterlogged.</li>
<li><strong>Hazard:</strong> Unpadded goalposts → <strong>Risk:</strong> Players could collide with the post and suffer a concussion or fracture → <strong>Control Measure:</strong> Attach protective padding to all posts before the session.</li>
<li><strong>Hazard:</strong> Player wearing jewellery → <strong>Risk:</strong> Jewellery could catch on another player or equipment, causing a laceration → <strong>Control Measure:</strong> All jewellery must be removed before participation.</li>
</ul>
<p>This format — <strong>hazard → risk → control measure</strong> — is exactly what the exam expects you to use when answering risk assessment questions.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Characteristics of the Individual or Group</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">When assessing risk, the coach must also consider the specific characteristics of the people taking part. What is safe for one group may not be safe for another.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n20">
<li><strong>Age</strong> — younger children require more supervision and may not be physically developed enough for high-intensity activity.</li>
<li><strong>Individual fitness levels</strong> — less fit members of the group face a greater risk of injury if they push too hard or are asked to do activities beyond their current ability.</li>
<li><strong>Experience</strong> — experienced performers may safely take part in more demanding sessions, while beginners need simpler activities with closer supervision.</li>
<li><strong>Medical conditions</strong> — conditions such as asthma or diabetes must be considered as high-risk factors. The coach must know about these conditions and have appropriate medication (e.g. inhalers) available.</li>
<li><strong>Group size</strong> — large groups on a court or pitch can lead to collisions, difficulty hearing instructions, and reduced supervision per player.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Strategies to Reduce Risk</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Medicals</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">A medical is an examination for people intending to take part in sport. It detects conditions that may limit participation, assesses current fitness levels, and evaluates both mental and physical health. Medicals are typically carried out around <strong>one month before</strong> the intended activity begins.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Screening</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A series of tests carried out on elite athletes to detect potential health problems before any symptoms appear.">Screening</dfn> is a more thorough process used for <strong>elite athletes</strong>. It detects potential problems before any symptoms occur and is usually carried out during the <strong>off-season</strong>. Screening includes a medical but goes further, with specialist tests such as cardiac screening. CRY (Cardiac Risk in the Young) screening is particularly important for young athletes, as it can detect hidden heart conditions that could be life-threatening during intense exercise.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Medicals vs Screening</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26">Students sometimes confuse medicals and screening. The key differences are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Medicals</strong> are for <strong>anyone</strong> intending to do sport. They assess general fitness and detect conditions that might limit participation. Done around one month before the activity.</li>
<li><strong>Screening</strong> is for <strong>elite athletes</strong>. It goes further than a medical by looking for hidden problems before symptoms appear. Done in the off-season. Includes specialist tests like CRY cardiac screening.</li>
</ul>
<p>Think of it this way: a medical asks “Are you fit to play?” while screening asks “Is there anything hidden that could put you at risk?”</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">National Governing Body (NGB) Policies</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Every sport has a <dfn class="term" data-def="The organisation responsible for governing a sport, setting its rules, and ensuring safety standards are maintained.">National Governing Body (NGB)</dfn> — for example, the FA (football), the RFU (rugby union), and the ECB (cricket). NGBs set rules and regulations specifically designed to make the sport safer. These include laws of the game, equipment standards, age-group restrictions, and concussion protocols. Coaches and officials must follow NGB policies to ensure the safety of all participants.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Emergency Action Plans (EAPs)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A written plan that states the procedures to follow in an emergency, ensuring a fast and effective response.">Emergency Action Plan (EAP)</dfn> is a written plan that states the procedures to follow in an emergency. It ensures the safety of performers, enables supervisors to know exactly what to do, and reduces the risk of life-threatening outcomes. Every sports venue and event should have an EAP in place.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">An EAP has three main sections:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n32">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The section of an EAP that identifies who to contact and who takes charge in an emergency.">Emergency Personnel</dfn></strong> — who to contact, who is the designated first responder, who are the qualified first aiders, and who is the coach responsible for the group.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency Communication</strong> — contact numbers for emergency services (999), the location of the nearest telephone or mobile phone, and how to direct emergency services to the venue.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency Equipment</strong> — the location of the first aid kit, defibrillator, inhaler, ice packs, stretcher, and evacuation chair.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does EAP stand for? List each part from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The three sections of an EAP are <strong>Emergency Personnel</strong>, <strong>Emergency Communication</strong>, and <strong>Emergency Equipment</strong>. Having all three in a written plan means everyone knows their role and can respond quickly — which can be the difference between a minor incident and a life-threatening emergency.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n34">Imagine a player suffers a severe asthma attack during a football training session. Here is how the three sections of the EAP work together:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Emergency Personnel:</strong> The coach recognises the attack and calls over the qualified first aider on site. They know exactly who to go to because the EAP names the first responder.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency Communication:</strong> The first aider assesses the player and decides an ambulance is needed. They use the mobile phone listed in the EAP to call 999 and give the venue’s address and directions.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency Equipment:</strong> While waiting for the ambulance, the first aider retrieves the player’s inhaler from the first aid kit (the EAP states its exact location), helping to stabilise the player’s breathing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Without the EAP, there could be confusion about who takes charge, time wasted searching for a phone or first aid kit, and a delayed response that puts the player at greater risk.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Reducing the risk of sports injuries requires a combination of safety checks, thorough risk assessments, consideration of individual characteristics, and formal strategies such as medicals, screening and NGB policies. Emergency Action Plans ensure that when injuries do occur, the response is fast, organised and effective.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Risk assessments</strong> identify hazards, evaluate risk levels, and put control measures in place — they must be carried out before and during every activity.</li>
<li><strong>Individual characteristics</strong> such as age, fitness, experience, medical conditions and group size all affect the level of risk and must be factored into planning.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency Action Plans</strong> have three sections — Personnel, Communication, and Equipment — ensuring everyone knows their role and can respond quickly in an emergency.</li>
</ul>
