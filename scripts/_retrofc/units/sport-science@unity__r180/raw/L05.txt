--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sudden-onset injuries including fractures, dislocations, sprains, strains, concussion and abrasions — their causes, symptoms and initial treatment.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Acute Injuries?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="An injury caused by sudden trauma during a single, specific event.">acute injury</dfn> is an injury caused by sudden trauma — a single, specific event such as a tackle, a fall, or a collision. Unlike chronic injuries that develop gradually over time, acute injuries happen in an instant and have an obvious cause.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 2 items listed here?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Acute vs chronic:</strong> An acute injury is caused by a sudden event (e.g. a broken bone from a tackle). A chronic injury develops over time through repeated stress or overuse (e.g. a stress fracture from long-distance running). In the exam, you must be able to distinguish between the two.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Acute injuries fall into two broad categories:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n5">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Body tissue including muscles, tendons, ligaments and skin.">Soft tissue</dfn> injuries</strong> — damage to muscles, tendons, ligaments or skin (e.g. strains, sprains, cuts, bruises).</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Body tissue that forms the skeleton, primarily bone.">Hard tissue</dfn> injuries</strong> — damage to bones (e.g. fractures, dislocations, concussion).</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Soft Tissue Injuries</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Strains</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Damage to a muscle or tendon caused by overstretching or tearing.">strain</dfn> is damage to a muscle or <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong band of tissue that connects muscle to bone.">tendon</dfn>. A useful way to remember this is that the “T” in s<strong>T</strong>rain stands for <strong>T</strong>endon. Strains are caused by overstretching, twisting, or sudden high-intensity movements.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li><strong>Symptoms:</strong> pain, swelling, bruising, loss of movement in the affected area.</li>
<li><strong>Treatment:</strong> PRICE protocol (Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation).</li>
<li><strong>Sporting example:</strong> a hamstring strain in sprinting, caused by the muscle being overstretched during a powerful stride.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n10">Sprains</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Damage to a ligament caused by a joint being forced beyond its normal range of motion.">sprain</dfn> is damage to a <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong band of tissue that connects bone to bone at a joint.">ligament</dfn> — the tissue that connects bone to bone at a joint. Sprains are caused by a joint being forced beyond its normal range of motion.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n12">
<li><strong>Symptoms:</strong> pain, swelling, bruising, instability of the joint.</li>
<li><strong>Treatment:</strong> PRICE protocol.</li>
<li><strong>Common examples:</strong> ankle sprain from landing awkwardly after a jump; ACL (anterior cruciate ligament) injury in the knee from twisting during a tackle.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n13">Students often confuse strains and sprains. The key difference is in the tissue affected:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Strain</strong> = damage to a <strong>muscle</strong> or <strong>tendon</strong> (connects muscle to bone). Remember: s<strong>T</strong>rain = <strong>T</strong>endon.</li>
<li><strong>Sprain</strong> = damage to a <strong>ligament</strong> (connects bone to bone at a joint).</li>
</ul>
<p>Both cause pain, swelling, and bruising. However, a sprain also causes <strong>instability at the joint</strong> because the ligament holding the bones together has been damaged. Both are treated with the PRICE protocol.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Skin Damage</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Several types of skin damage count as acute soft tissue injuries:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n16">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A graze where the surface layer of skin is scraped away by friction.">Abrasion</dfn> (graze)</strong> — the surface layer of skin is scraped away. Common when sliding on astroturf or rough ground.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A deep cut in the skin caused by a sharp object or impact.">Laceration</dfn> (cut)</strong> — a deeper cut caused by a sharp object or direct impact, such as being struck by a hockey stick or studded boot.</li>
<li><strong>Blister</strong> — friction causes fluid-filled pockets on the skin. Common from ill-fitting shoes or repeated rubbing on the hands (e.g. rowing, gymnastics).</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A bruise caused by impact that leads to bleeding under the skin.">Contusion</dfn> (bruise)</strong> — impact causes bleeding under the skin, resulting in discolouration and tenderness. Common in contact sports from tackles and collisions.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Hard Tissue Injuries</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Fractures</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A break or crack in a bone.">fracture</dfn> is a break or crack in a bone. Fractures are caused by direct impact, falls, or excessive force applied to the bone.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Types of Fracture</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">There are three main types of fracture you need to know:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Simple (closed) fracture</strong> — the bone breaks but does not pierce through the skin. The skin remains intact over the break.</li>
<li><strong>Compound (open) fracture</strong> — the bone breaks through the skin, creating an open wound. This carries a much higher risk of infection and is more serious.</li>
<li><strong>Stress fracture</strong> — a small hairline crack in the bone caused by repeated stress over time. Strictly speaking, this is a chronic injury, but it is examinable alongside other fractures.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Treatment for fractures involves immobilising the area, getting an X-ray to confirm the break, and then applying a plaster cast or sling. Compound fractures and severe breaks may require surgery.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Dislocations</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An injury where a bone is forced out of its normal position at a joint.">dislocation</dfn> occurs when a bone is forced out of its normal position at a joint. This is commonly seen at the shoulder, finger, and elbow joints.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li><strong>Causes:</strong> a direct impact or a fall that forces the joint out of alignment.</li>
<li><strong>Symptoms:</strong> severe pain, visible deformity at the joint, complete loss of movement.</li>
<li><strong>Treatment:</strong> do <strong>NOT</strong> attempt to relocate the bone yourself. Seek immediate medical attention. A medical professional will manipulate the bone back into place.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Concussion</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="A brain injury caused by a blow to the head, resulting in temporary disruption of normal brain function.">Concussion</dfn> is a brain injury caused by a blow to the head. It is increasingly recognised as one of the most serious injuries in sport, particularly in rugby and football where head-on collisions are common.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n27">
<li><strong>Symptoms:</strong> headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea, memory loss, blurred vision, and in severe cases, loss of consciousness.</li>
<li><strong>Treatment:</strong> the player must be immediately removed from play. They need complete rest and a monitored recovery period before any return to activity.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does GRTP stand for? List each part from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Players who suffer a concussion must follow the <dfn class="term" data-def="Graduated Return to Play protocol: a step-by-step process for safely returning to sport after a concussion.">GRTP</dfn> (Graduated Return to Play) protocol. This is a step-by-step process where the player gradually increases activity levels over several days or weeks, only progressing to the next stage if symptom-free. Returning too soon risks a second, potentially more dangerous head injury.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Acute injuries are caused by sudden trauma and include both soft tissue damage (strains, sprains, skin injuries) and hard tissue damage (fractures, dislocations, concussion). Each injury type has specific causes, symptoms and treatment methods that you must be able to identify and explain.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Soft tissue injuries</strong> affect muscles, tendons, ligaments and skin — strains damage muscles/tendons, sprains damage ligaments, and both are treated with PRICE.</li>
<li><strong>Hard tissue injuries</strong> affect bones — fractures require immobilisation, dislocations need medical attention, and concussion demands immediate removal from play.</li>
<li><strong>Concussion</strong> is increasingly taken seriously in sport, with players required to follow the GRTP protocol before returning to activity.</li>
</ul>
