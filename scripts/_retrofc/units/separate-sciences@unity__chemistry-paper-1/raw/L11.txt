--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Percentage Yield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In any chemical reaction, the amount of product you actually make is almost always less than the amount you expected. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum mass of product that could be formed from a given amount of reactants, calculated from a balanced equation.">theoretical yield</dfn> is the maximum mass of product calculated from the balanced equation, but the <dfn class="term" data-def="The actual mass of product obtained from a chemical reaction.">actual yield</dfn> is whatever you collect at the end of the experiment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">To compare the two, chemists calculate the <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of the theoretical yield that is actually obtained, expressed as a percentage.">percentage yield</dfn> using this equation:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover the formula and write out how to calculate percentage yield using actual yield and theoretical yield.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A percentage yield of 100% would mean you collected every gram of product the equation predicted. In practice this never happens because:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6">The reaction may be <strong>reversible</strong>, so not all reactants convert to products.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">Some product is always <strong>lost during transfer</strong> — for example, liquid left behind on glassware or solid stuck to filter paper.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">Unexpected <strong>side reactions</strong> may form different products from those intended.</li>
</ul>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example — Percentage Yield</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A student expected to produce 495 tonnes of product A but only obtained 400 tonnes. Calculate the percentage yield.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Percentage yield = (400 ÷ 495) × 100 = <strong>80.8%</strong></p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Atom Economy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of the total relative formula mass of all reactants that ends up in the desired product, expressed as a percentage.">atom economy</dfn> measures how much of the total mass of reactants ends up as the useful product. It is a way of judging how wasteful a reaction is before you even carry it out.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this box and write the atom economy formula using Mr of desired product and total Mr of all reactants.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Atom economy = (M<sub>r</sub> of desired product ÷ total M<sub>r</sub> of all reactants) × 100</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A high atom economy means most atoms from the reactants end up in the useful product, generating less waste. Industries prefer reactions with high atom economy because they are more <strong>sustainable</strong> and more <strong>cost-effective</strong>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example — Atom Economy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Calculate the atom economy for making hydrogen by reacting zinc with hydrochloric acid:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">M<sub>r</sub> of desired product (H<sub>2</sub>) = 2. Total M<sub>r</sub> of all reactants = Zn (65) + 2HCl (2 × 36.5 = 73) = 138.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Atom economy = (2 ÷ 138) × 100 = <strong>1.4%</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">This extremely low atom economy shows that this method wastes almost all the reactant mass as zinc chloride — an unwanted by-product.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Reactions that produce only one product, such as addition reactions, have an atom economy of 100%. Reactions that produce several products, where only one is useful, have a low atom economy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Molar Concentrations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">You already know that <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, measured in g/dm³ or mol/dm³.">concentration</dfn> can be measured in g/dm³. For more advanced calculations, chemists express concentration in <strong>\(mol/dm^{3}\)</strong> (moles per cubic decimetre). This tells you how many moles of solute are dissolved in each dm³ of solution.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Hide the equation and recall how to find concentration in mol/dm³ using moles of solute and volume in dm³.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Concentration (mol/dm³) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution (dm³)</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n24">To convert between units, remember: 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³. So if a volume is given in cm³, divide by 1000 to get dm³.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example — Molar Concentration</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">A student dissolves 0.5 mol of sodium hydroxide in 250 cm³ of water. Calculate the concentration in mol/dm³.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Volume in dm³ = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.25 dm³</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Concentration = 0.5 ÷ 0.25 = <strong>\(2 mol/dm^{3}\)</strong></p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n28">You can also rearrange the equation to find moles or volume:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Moles = concentration × volume (in dm³)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Volume (dm³) = moles ÷ concentration</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Molar Volume of Gases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Equal numbers of moles of any gas occupy the same volume at the same temperature and pressure. At <dfn class="term" data-def="Room temperature and pressure: 20°C (293 K) and 1 atmosphere (101.3 kPa).">room temperature and pressure (RTP)</dfn>, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a specified temperature and pressure; at RTP it is 24 dm³.">molar volume</dfn> of any gas is <strong>\(24 dm^{3}\)</strong> (or 24,000 cm³).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this fact and remember the number you multiply moles by to get gas volume at RTP for any gas.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Volume of gas (dm³) = moles × 24. This works for <em>any</em> gas at RTP — hydrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide or any other.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n34">You can rearrange to find moles from a volume: moles = volume (dm³) ÷ 24.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example — Gas Volume</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n35">Calculate the volume of 7 moles of oxygen gas at RTP.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36">Volume = 7 × 24 = <strong>\(168 dm^{3}\)</strong></p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Combining Gas Volumes with Reacting Masses</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n37">You can combine gas volume calculations with reacting mass problems. First use the balanced equation to find the moles of gas produced, then multiply by 24 to get the volume at RTP.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38">For example, if a reaction produces 0.5 mol of CO<sub>2</sub>, the volume at RTP is 0.5 × 24 = <strong>\(12 dm^{3}\)</strong>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n39">Yield vs Atom Economy — What’s the Difference?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n40"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the efficiency of a reaction based on how much of the reactants become useful product, compared to the theoretical maximum.">percentage yield</dfn> tells you how efficiently the reaction was <em>carried out</em> in practice — it compares what you actually got to what you should have got. <dfn class="term" data-def="A theoretical measure of the proportion of reactant atoms that end up in the desired product.">atom economy</dfn> tells you how efficient the reaction <em>itself</em> is by design — whether the equation produces waste products or not.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n41">A reaction can have a high atom economy but a low percentage yield (if the method was poorly carried out), or a low atom economy but a high percentage yield (if you collected nearly all the useful product, but the reaction still creates lots of waste).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n43">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n44">Percentage yield compares actual yield to theoretical yield — it is always less than 100% because of losses, reversibility, or side reactions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n45">Atom economy compares the M<sub>r</sub> of the desired product to the total M<sub>r</sub> of all reactants — higher is more sustainable.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n46">Concentration in mol/dm³ = moles ÷ volume (dm³). One mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³ at RTP.</li>
</ul>
