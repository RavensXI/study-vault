--- DESCRIPTION ---
JIT and JIC stock, choosing suppliers, and running an effective supply chain.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Procurement Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of finding, agreeing terms with and buying goods, services or materials from suppliers.">Procurement</dfn> is how a business buys in everything it needs to trade — raw materials, packaging, parts, even office stationery. Get it right and costs drop, quality rises and customers are served on time. Get it wrong and the business can be paying over the odds for unreliable parts that arrive too late to use.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Alongside procurement sits <dfn class="term" data-def="The planning, organisation and movement of goods from suppliers through the business to customers.">logistics</dfn> — moving goods from the supplier into the business and then out to customers. Procurement and logistics together decide how much stock a business holds, where it holds it, and how quickly it can fulfil an order.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: what is the difference between JIT and JIC, and which one uses buffer stock?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two stock strategies dominate the exam: just in time (JIT) keeps almost no stock, with deliveries arriving exactly when needed. Just in case (JIC) keeps spare buffer stock so the business can always meet demand. Each has a clear trade-off — low cost versus low risk.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Managing Stock — JIT and JIC</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A business running <strong>just in time (JIT)</strong> has parts delivered to the factory only as they are needed. Nissan’s Sunderland plant schedules seat deliveries hour by hour. The benefits are powerful: almost no cash tied up in warehouse stock, less wasted space, and obsolete parts become a rare problem. But the business loses its safety net — if a supplier stops delivering, production stops within hours.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The opposite approach is <dfn class="term" data-def="A stock strategy where the business deliberately holds spare ‘buffer’ stock so it can always meet unexpected demand.">just in case (JIC)</dfn>, where the business deliberately holds buffer stock. UK supermarket chains keep several days’ worth of long-life goods in regional distribution centres so that a delivery hiccup does not empty shelves. The cost is obvious: more warehouse space, more cash tied up in stock, more risk of waste if tastes change.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">JIT suits businesses with reliable, nearby suppliers, predictable demand and products where parts are expensive to store (cars, electronics). JIC suits businesses where running out would be disastrous — supermarket chains, hospitals with medical supplies, or a toy retailer stocking up before Christmas. In practice many businesses combine the two, using JIT for predictable items and JIC buffer stock for critical parts that are hard to replace.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">You will not be asked to draw or interpret a stock control chart in the exam, but you should be able to evaluate the use of JIT for a given business.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Choosing Suppliers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The decision a business faces is not just ‘which supplier is cheapest’ but which supplier balances <strong>price</strong>, <strong>quality</strong> and <strong>reliability</strong>. A cheap supplier that delivers poor-quality goods costs the business twice — once in the inflated price of rework, and again in lost customers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Price drives cost per unit. Quality affects the final product and, therefore, the brand. <dfn class="term" data-def="The degree to which a supplier can be trusted to deliver the right quantity, at the right quality, on time, every time.">Reliability</dfn> decides whether the business can fulfil its own promises to customers. A JIT factory cannot use an unreliable supplier, no matter how low the price — one missed delivery halts the whole line.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the three factors that affect the choice of supplier, plus one extra factor beyond those three.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three exam-critical factors for choosing a supplier are <strong>price</strong>, <strong>quality</strong> and <strong>reliability</strong>. Businesses must balance them. Marks & Spencer works closely with a small number of food suppliers, accepting higher prices in return for consistent quality and reliable delivery windows.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Procurement and Logistics in Action</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Well-run procurement and logistics bring two clear benefits: <strong>efficiency</strong> (things happen faster, with less wasted effort) and <strong>lower unit costs</strong> (bulk deals, fewer delays, less stock sitting idle). But the business must weigh the cost savings against the risk to quality and customer service — a supermarket that skipped on fresh-produce logistics would soon have wilted vegetables on the shelf.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">What Is a Supply Chain?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The full network of businesses, suppliers and transport links that moves a product from its raw materials to the final customer.">supply chain</dfn> is the full journey a product takes from raw material to final customer. A loaf of bread on a Tesco shelf has travelled through farmers, millers, bakeries, packers and distribution centres before it reaches the store. If any link in the chain breaks — harvest failure, haulage shortage, factory fire — the end customer notices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Effective supply chain management means working <em>with</em> suppliers, not just against them on price. Strong relationships produce three concrete benefits: processes run smoothly because everyone knows what to expect, the business secures goods and services at the best value rather than always the lowest sticker price, and waste is stripped out so lead times fall and production speeds up.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Supply chain disruption in the real world</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The 2021 global semiconductor shortage hit UK car manufacturers hard. Nissan, Jaguar Land Rover and Mini (Cowley, Oxford) all paused production because chips for engine management, entertainment and safety systems simply were not available. Businesses running pure JIT had no stockpile to fall back on. Many have since added a ‘strategic buffer’ of critical components — a partial shift towards JIC — even though it costs more to hold.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Post-Brexit border delays have had a similar effect, particularly on fresh food exporters who cannot wait hours at a port with perishable goods. Strong supplier relationships and contingency planning have become part of the operations job description.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A business that manages procurement, stock and its supply chain well enjoys lower costs, faster deliveries and a better reputation with customers. One that fumbles these decisions ends up with too much cash in the warehouse, or not enough stock on the shelves — and in both cases, lost profit.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">JIT cuts stock holding costs but leaves the business exposed to supplier delays; JIC uses buffer stock that costs more but absorbs shocks.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Suppliers are chosen on price, quality and reliability — cheapest is rarely best if it fails on the other two.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Effective supply chain management means cutting waste, working closely with suppliers and getting best value — not simply the lowest price.</li>
</ul>

Examiners advise around four minutes on the evaluative judgement itself; a one-line conclusion is not enough. Remember that you will not be asked to draw or interpret stock control charts.</p>
