<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Autonomous Vehicles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A vehicle capable of navigating and operating without direct human input, using sensors, cameras, artificial intelligence and mapping data to move safely.">autonomous vehicle</dfn> is a vehicle that can drive itself, using sensors (cameras, radar, lidar), artificial intelligence and real-time mapping to navigate roads without a human at the controls. Partially autonomous features &mdash; such as lane-keeping assistance, automatic braking and motorway cruise control &mdash; are already widely available. Fully self-driving taxis and freight lorries are being tested in several countries. The potential benefits are significant: reduced road accidents caused by human error, improved mobility for people who cannot drive, smoother traffic flow, and reduced need for parking in cities.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Ethical Challenges of Self-Driving Cars</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Autonomous vehicles raise ethical questions that have no easy answers. These questions are sometimes framed around thought experiments involving unavoidable collision scenarios: if an autonomous vehicle cannot stop in time, should its algorithm prioritise the passenger, a pedestrian, or act in a way that minimises total harm? No consensus has emerged on how these algorithms should be programmed, and different cultural attitudes produce different intuitions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Decision-making algorithms:</strong> When a collision is unavoidable, who or what should the vehicle&rsquo;s programming prioritise? Choosing the passenger&rsquo;s safety above others seems self-serving; choosing to minimise total harm treats the passenger as expendable. These decisions are currently made by engineers and, ultimately, by manufacturers &mdash; raising questions of accountability and transparency.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Liability after collisions:</strong> With a human driver, blame is relatively straightforward to assign. With an autonomous vehicle, responsibility may lie with the vehicle manufacturer, the software developer, the road authority, the person who set the destination, or some combination of all of these. Current legal frameworks are not well-equipped to deal with this complexity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Displacement of workers:</strong> Large-scale adoption of autonomous vehicles would affect millions of people employed as taxi drivers, lorry drivers, bus drivers and delivery workers. The technology could improve safety and efficiency, but at a significant social cost to those workers and the communities that depend on those jobs. Retraining and social support would be needed at scale.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Security and hacking:</strong> An autonomous vehicle is essentially a computer on wheels. If its systems were hacked, a vehicle could be manipulated remotely &mdash; creating a physical safety risk as well as a digital security risk.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Environmental Impacts of Digital Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Digital technology is often described as &ldquo;clean&rdquo; compared with heavy industry, but its environmental footprint is substantial. There are three main categories of environmental impact: manufacturing, energy consumption in use, and disposal.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Manufacturing Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Producing electronic devices requires mining rare earth minerals and metals including lithium, cobalt, gold and tantalum. Mining operations can cause habitat destruction, water pollution and generate significant carbon emissions. The energy-intensive processes used to fabricate processors and circuit boards also contribute to carbon output. Every new device bought represents environmental cost before it has even been switched on.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Energy Consumption</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Large facilities housing hundreds or thousands of servers that store and process data for online services, apps and cloud storage. Data centres consume significant amounts of electricity and require cooling systems that use further energy and water.">Data centres</dfn> power the internet, cloud storage, streaming services and online applications. Running servers continuously requires enormous amounts of electricity; cooling systems &mdash; which prevent equipment from overheating &mdash; consume additional power and water. As more services move online and global data traffic grows, the energy demands of data centres grow with it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Individual devices also consume energy throughout their lifetimes. Keeping smartphones, laptops and televisions running adds up across billions of users. Wireless networks (mobile masts, Wi-Fi routers) require continuous power to operate.</p>

Digital documents replace paper, reducing deforestation. Smart energy-management systems in buildings use sensors and software to optimise heating and lighting, saving energy. Precision agriculture uses sensors and data analysis to apply fertilisers and water only where needed, reducing runoff. Monitoring systems can track wildlife, deforestation and pollution in real time, enabling faster responses to environmental damage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">E-Waste</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Discarded electronic devices and components — including old phones, laptops, televisions and circuit boards — that contain toxic materials and contribute to environmental pollution when improperly disposed of.">E-waste</dfn> (electronic waste) is one of the fastest-growing waste categories globally. Devices contain hazardous materials including lead, mercury and cadmium. When old devices end up in landfill, these substances can leach into soil and groundwater. In some parts of the world, informal recycling of e-waste exposes workers to toxic chemicals. Planned <dfn class="term" data-def="A manufacturing practice where products are designed to become outdated or fail after a fixed period, encouraging consumers to buy replacements sooner than necessary.">obsolescence</dfn> &mdash; where manufacturers design products to have a limited lifespan or stop providing software updates after a few years &mdash; accelerates the rate at which devices are discarded.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Explain to someone: what is e-waste, why is it harmful, and what is planned obsolescence? Can you link all three in one explanation?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>E-waste is discarded electronic equipment containing hazardous materials. Planned obsolescence &mdash; designing products to become outdated quickly &mdash; increases e-waste by encouraging frequent replacement. Reducing e-waste requires longer device lifespans, easier repairability and responsible recycling schemes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Reducing Environmental Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Several approaches can reduce the environmental impact of technology. Consumers can extend device lifespans by repairing rather than replacing, choosing refurbished devices, and using official recycling and take-back schemes rather than sending old electronics to general waste. Manufacturers can design products that are easier to repair, use more recycled materials, and provide software support for longer periods. Organisations can host their services on data centres that run on renewable energy or use waste heat recovery systems. Collectively, these choices reduce mining demand, energy consumption and toxic landfill.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Autonomous vehicles raise ethical issues around algorithmic decision-making in unavoidable accidents, liability after collisions, and large-scale displacement of professional drivers. Benefits include reduced human-error accidents and greater mobility.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Environmental impacts span the full device lifecycle: resource-intensive manufacturing, high energy consumption in data centres and during use, and toxic e-waste at disposal. Planned obsolescence worsens e-waste by shortening device lifespans.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Technology can also reduce environmental harm through remote working, smart energy systems, precision agriculture and environmental monitoring &mdash; exam answers should present both sides in a balanced way.</li>
</ul>
