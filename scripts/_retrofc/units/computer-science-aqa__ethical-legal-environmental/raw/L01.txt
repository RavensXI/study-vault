<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Technology Raises Ethical Questions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Digital technology has transformed how we communicate, shop, access healthcare and use public services. But with those benefits come serious questions: who controls your personal data, what happens when someone accesses a computer without permission, and who owns the software or music you stream? <dfn class="term" data-def="Issues concerning what is morally right and wrong in the development and use of technology, where reasonable people may disagree.">Ethical issues</dfn> are questions about right and wrong that often have no single correct answer. <dfn class="term" data-def="Issues governed by laws that set clear boundaries on what is and is not permitted when using or developing technology.">Legal issues</dfn> are those that the law defines as permitted or prohibited.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Privacy and Personal Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Privacy is one of the central ethical concerns of the digital age. Every time you use a smartphone, search engine, social media platform or banking app, data about you is collected. This data may include your location, browsing history, financial transactions, health metrics and social connections. Most people value the ability to control what others know about them, and many feel uncomfortable when they discover how much data organisations hold.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">New technologies intensify this challenge:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Mobile technologies</strong> &mdash; smartphones carry GPS sensors, cameras and microphones that can record location and activity continuously. Apps often request permissions to access these sensors beyond what is strictly necessary for the app to function.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Wireless networking</strong> &mdash; public Wi-Fi hotspots can be monitored; poorly secured home routers can expose household devices. Data transmitted without encryption can be intercepted.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cloud storage</strong> &mdash; saving files to a cloud service means a third-party company holds copies of your data on remote servers. Questions arise about who can access those files and what the provider does with your metadata.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Wearable technologies</strong> &mdash; fitness trackers, smartwatches and medical monitoring devices collect continuous biometric data such as heart rate, sleep patterns and physical activity. This data is highly personal and could affect insurance premiums or employment decisions if shared inappropriately.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Computer-based implants</strong> &mdash; devices implanted in the body (such as pacemakers or neural interfaces) raise profound ethical questions about bodily autonomy, data security and the consequences of a security breach on a person&rsquo;s health.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three technologies from the specification that raise privacy concerns, and state one ethical concern for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Technologies explicitly named in this topic include: mobile technologies, wireless networking, cloud storage, wearable technologies, computer-based implants and autonomous vehicles. For each, you should be able to describe at least one ethical or privacy concern and one benefit.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Hacking and Unauthorised Access</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Gaining access to a computer system or data without the permission of the owner — also called unauthorised access.">Hacking</dfn> is the act of gaining access to a computer system without permission. This may involve exploiting security vulnerabilities, using stolen passwords or writing malicious software. Hacking can result in theft of personal data, financial fraud, disruption of services (such as hospital systems going offline) and reputational damage to organisations. Ethically, even &ldquo;white hat&rdquo; security researchers who probe systems to find weaknesses must operate within defined legal agreements &mdash; probing a system without permission is illegal regardless of intent.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Government Access vs. Citizen Privacy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">One of the most debated ethical dilemmas is whether governments and security services should be able to access citizens&rsquo; private data. Most people value their privacy and are uncomfortable with the idea of their messages being read or their locations tracked by the state. However, governments and security agencies argue that access to private communications is sometimes necessary to prevent terrorism, serious crime and national security threats. There is genuine tension here, with no universally agreed answer: stronger government surveillance may prevent attacks but also risks misuse of that power and chilling effects on free speech.</p>

Without access to encrypted messages, criminals can organise undetected. Proponents argue that citizens with nothing to hide have nothing to fear.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Arguments against:</strong> Mass surveillance undermines the presumption of innocence. Governments could misuse data to target political opponents or journalists. Mandatory encryption backdoors would weaken security for everyone, including ordinary users and businesses, since the same backdoor that governments use could also be exploited by criminal hackers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">UK Legal Frameworks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Three key Acts of Parliament govern the legal use of technology in the UK. These are not optional guidelines &mdash; breaking them can result in fines, civil claims or criminal prosecution.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Three Key UK Laws</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="UK law passed in 2018 that regulates how organisations collect, store, process and share personal data, incorporating the requirements of UK GDPR and giving individuals rights over their own data.">Data Protection Act 2018</dfn> governs how organisations handle personal data. It incorporates the requirements of the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR). Organisations must collect data only for a specific, stated purpose; keep it secure; not hold it longer than necessary; and give individuals the right to see, correct or request deletion of their own data. Failure to comply can result in significant fines issued by the Information Commissioner&rsquo;s Office. This Act replaced the earlier Data Protection Act 1998.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">The <dfn class="term" data-def="UK law passed in 1990 (with later amendments) that makes unauthorised access to computer systems, unauthorised access with intent to commit further offences, and unauthorised modification of computer material all criminal offences.">Computer Misuse Act 1990</dfn> makes it a criminal offence to access a computer system without authorisation. It covers three categories of offence: unauthorised access to computer material (for example, logging into someone else&rsquo;s account); unauthorised access with intent to commit or facilitate further offences (for example, accessing a bank&rsquo;s server to commit fraud); and unauthorised modification of computer material (for example, installing malware or deleting data). A 2015 amendment to the Act increased maximum penalties and added offences relating to attacks on computer infrastructure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">The <dfn class="term" data-def="UK law passed in 1988 that protects creative works — including software, music, images, literature and film — from being copied, distributed or used without permission from the rights holder.">Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988</dfn> protects intellectual property. Software, music, images, films and written work are automatically protected from the moment of creation. Copying, distributing or publicly performing copyrighted material without permission &mdash; whether commercial or personal &mdash; is illegal. This is why downloading music or films through unauthorised channels is not just unethical but against the law.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the name and year of each of the three UK laws, with one example of something each law prohibits.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three UK laws you must know: <strong>Data Protection Act 2018</strong> &mdash; controls personal data handling; <strong>Computer Misuse Act 1990</strong> &mdash; makes hacking and malware creation criminal offences; <strong>Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988</strong> &mdash; protects software, music and other creative works from unauthorised copying.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Mobile technologies, cloud storage, wearable devices and computer-based implants all raise privacy concerns because they collect personal data that could be accessed or misused by others, including by governments and security services.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">The Data Protection Act 2018 controls how organisations use personal data; the Computer Misuse Act 1990 makes hacking and malware creation illegal; the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 protects creative works including software.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Government surveillance vs. individual privacy is a genuine ethical dilemma with valid arguments on both sides &mdash; your exam may ask you to present and evaluate both perspectives.</li>
</ul>

If asked to &ldquo;discuss&rdquo; or &ldquo;evaluate&rdquo;, give arguments on both sides &mdash; do not just list benefits. When naming a law, include the year (Data Protection Act <em>2018</em>, not &lsquo;the 1998 one&rsquo;) and say what it does, not just its name. Avoid inventing specific clause numbers or case names &mdash; a clear descriptive explanation of what each law prohibits earns full marks without invented detail.</p>
