--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Fight Against Lung Cancer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Lung cancer is one of the biggest killers in 21st-century Britain. It is the third most common cancer and accounts for more deaths than any other cancer type. The fight against lung cancer is a required case study in the Edexcel specification because it illustrates how modern medicine uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of identifying a disease through tests, scans, and medical examinations.">diagnosis</dfn>, treatment, prevention, and government action to tackle a major health challenge.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Unlike many diseases studied in this course, lung cancer is closely linked to <dfn class="term" data-def="Personal choices about diet, exercise, smoking, and alcohol that directly affect a person's health and risk of disease.">lifestyle factors</dfn> — particularly smoking. The link between smoking and lung cancer was established in the 1950s by researchers Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, whose landmark study proved that smokers were far more likely to develop the disease.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain how the discovery of the smoking-lung cancer link led to government action.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1950, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill published research proving a direct link between smoking and lung cancer. This was one of the most significant medical discoveries of the 20th century, but it took decades for government action to follow — showing how attitudes in society and economic interests can slow progress.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Diagnosis and Treatment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Modern diagnosis of lung cancer relies heavily on science and technology. Doctors use <dfn class="term" data-def="A medical imaging technique that uses X-rays and computer processing to create detailed cross-sectional images of the body.">CT scans</dfn>, X-rays, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A medical procedure where a small sample of tissue is removed from the body and examined under a microscope to check for disease.">biopsies</dfn> to detect tumours. Blood tests can identify certain markers associated with cancer. Early detection significantly improves survival rates, which is why screening programmes are important.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Treatment for lung cancer has advanced significantly but remains challenging. Options include:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Surgery:</strong> Removing the tumour or part of the lung. This is most effective when the cancer is caught early.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Chemotherapy:</strong> Using powerful drugs to kill cancer cells. Chemotherapy can have severe side effects because it also damages healthy cells.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Radiotherapy:</strong> Using targeted radiation to destroy cancer cells. Modern <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of high-energy radiation beams targeted precisely at cancer cells to destroy them while minimising damage to surrounding tissue.">radiotherapy</dfn> is more precise than ever, reducing damage to surrounding tissue.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Immunotherapy:</strong> A newer approach that helps the body’s own immune system recognise and attack cancer cells.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Despite these advances, lung cancer survival rates remain lower than for many other cancers, partly because it is often diagnosed late. This makes prevention even more important.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Government Action & Lifestyle Campaigns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The government has played an increasingly active role in preventing lung cancer and other lifestyle-related diseases. This represents a significant shift from the <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that the government should not interfere in people's lives or businesses.">laissez-faire</dfn> attitudes of the 19th century.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n15">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1965</div>
    <h4>TV Advertising Ban</h4><p>Cigarette advertising was banned on television in the UK.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">2003</div>
    <h4>Advertising Ban Extended</h4><p>All tobacco advertising and sponsorship was banned in the UK.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">2007</div>
    <h4>Smoking Ban</h4><p>Smoking was banned in all enclosed public places and workplaces in England.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">2017</div>
    <h4>Plain Packaging</h4><p>All cigarettes sold in the UK had to use standardised plain packaging with graphic health warnings.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Beyond smoking, the government runs <dfn class="term" data-def="Government-led public information campaigns designed to encourage healthier behaviour, such as anti-smoking, healthy eating, and exercise programmes.">lifestyle campaigns</dfn> promoting healthy eating, exercise, and reduced alcohol consumption. Campaigns like “Change4Life” and “Stoptober” aim to change public behaviour and reduce the burden of preventable diseases on the NHS.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three differences between the government's role in public health in 1848 versus today.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The government’s role in health prevention has expanded dramatically since the 19th century. From voluntary public health acts in 1848 to compulsory smoking bans in 2007, the state now actively intervenes in citizens’ lifestyle choices to prevent disease — a clear example of change over time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Mass Vaccinations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Modern prevention also relies on <dfn class="term" data-def="Government programmes to vaccinate large sections of the population against infectious diseases.">mass vaccination</dfn> programmes. The NHS provides free vaccinations against diseases including measles, mumps, rubella, whooping cough, flu, and HPV (which can cause cervical cancer). These programmes have dramatically reduced death rates from infectious diseases and represent the culmination of work that began with Jenner’s smallpox vaccine in 1796.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Genetic and Lifestyle Factors in Modern Medicine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Modern medicine increasingly recognises that disease is caused by a combination of <dfn class="term" data-def="Inherited characteristics passed down through DNA that can increase or decrease a person's risk of developing certain diseases.">genetic factors</dfn> and lifestyle choices. Some people inherit genes that make them more susceptible to certain cancers, heart disease, or diabetes. However, lifestyle factors — smoking, diet, exercise, alcohol — can significantly increase or decrease these risks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">The Human Genome Project (completed 2003) mapped the entire human DNA sequence, opening the door to <dfn class="term" data-def="Medical treatment tailored to an individual's genetic makeup, allowing more targeted and effective therapies.">personalised medicine</dfn> — treatments tailored to an individual’s genetic profile. This represents the cutting edge of medical science, building on the understanding of disease that began with Pasteur’s Germ Theory over 150 years ago.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Factors and the Process of Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The fight against lung cancer illustrates the key factors that drive change in medicine. <strong>Science and technology</strong> have provided diagnostic tools (CT scans, biopsies) and treatments (surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy). <strong>Government and institutions</strong> have funded the NHS, banned smoking in public places, and run prevention campaigns. <strong>Attitudes in society</strong> have shifted — smoking was once seen as glamorous; now it is widely understood to be harmful. <strong>Individuals</strong> like Doll and Bradford Hill provided the evidence that linked smoking to cancer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Continuity and Change in Prevention</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Looking across the whole Medicine in Britain theme, there is a clear pattern of change in prevention. In the medieval period, people relied on prayer, pilgrimages, and purifying the air to ward off disease. By the 19th century, Jenner had developed vaccination and the government had begun to improve sanitation through the Public Health Acts. In the modern period, prevention has become far more sophisticated — the government funds mass vaccination programmes, runs national advertising campaigns, and passes legislation to restrict harmful behaviours like smoking.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">However, there is also continuity. The challenge of persuading people to change their behaviour has been a constant theme from medieval attempts to enforce quarantine during the Black Death to modern efforts to reduce smoking and obesity. In every era, there has been tension between individual freedom and government intervention in public health — a tension that continues to this day.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">The link between smoking and lung cancer was proved in 1950 by Doll and Bradford Hill, but it took decades for the government to act with advertising bans (1965, 2003) and a public smoking ban (2007).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Modern diagnosis uses CT scans, X-rays, and biopsies; treatments include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy — but lung cancer survival rates remain low because it is often diagnosed late.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The government’s role in prevention has expanded enormously: mass vaccinations, lifestyle campaigns, and legislation represent a major shift from 19th-century laissez-faire.</li>
</ul>
