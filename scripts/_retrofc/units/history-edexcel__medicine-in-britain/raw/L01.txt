<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Ideas About the Cause of Disease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In medieval England (c1250–1500), people had very limited understanding of what actually caused disease. Without microscopes or scientific methods, they relied on explanations that seem strange today but made perfect sense within the knowledge available at the time. These explanations fell into two broad categories: <strong>supernatural and religious</strong> explanations, and <strong>rational</strong> explanations based on ancient Greek and Roman ideas.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the two categories of medieval disease explanation, with two specific examples under each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Medieval people explained disease through two main approaches: <strong>supernatural and religious explanations</strong> (God’s punishment, astrology, evil spirits) and <strong>rational explanations</strong> (the Theory of the Four Humours and miasma theory).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Supernatural and Religious Explanations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The all-powerful Christian God whom medieval people believed controlled every aspect of life, including health and disease.">Church</dfn> dominated medieval life and taught that God controlled everything — including illness. Many people believed disease was a <strong>punishment from God</strong> for sinful behaviour. Prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage to holy shrines were therefore seen as logical treatments. Some also believed that <dfn class="term" data-def="The alignment and movement of planets and stars, which medieval people believed could influence health and cause disease.">astrology</dfn> influenced health, with physicians consulting star charts before diagnosing patients.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Church was the most powerful institution in medieval England. It controlled education, hospitals, and most people’s daily lives. Priests told their congregations that illness was God’s will, and few people had the literacy or knowledge to challenge this. The Church also discouraged <dfn class="term" data-def="The cutting open and examination of a dead body to study its internal structure and understand disease.">dissection</dfn> of human bodies, which prevented people from learning how the body actually worked. With no alternative explanations available, supernatural beliefs went unchallenged for centuries.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Theory of the Four Humours</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The most important rational explanation for disease came from the ancient Greek physician <dfn class="term" data-def="An ancient Greek physician (c460–370 BC) who developed the Theory of the Four Humours, arguing that illness resulted from an imbalance in the body’s four key fluids.">Hippocrates</dfn> (c460–370 BC). He argued that the body contained four key fluids — or humours — and that illness occurred when these became unbalanced. The four humours were: <strong>blood</strong> (associated with spring and air), <strong>yellow bile</strong> (summer and fire), <strong>black bile</strong> (autumn and earth), and <strong>phlegm</strong> (winter and water).</p>



<p data-narration-id="n9">This theory was developed further by the Roman physician <dfn class="term" data-def="A Roman physician (AD 129–216) who built on Hippocrates’ work, promoting the Theory of Opposites and writing influential medical texts used for over 1,000 years.">Galen</dfn> (AD 129–216), who added the <strong>Theory of Opposites</strong>. If a patient had too much phlegm (cold and wet), they should be treated with something hot and dry — such as pepper. Galen’s ideas were promoted by the Church because he believed the body was designed by a creator, which fitted Christian teaching. His writings dominated medical training for over 1,000 years.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain why Galen's ideas lasted over 1,000 years, including the role of the Church.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Galen’s ideas went unchallenged for over 1,000 years. The Church supported his work because he believed in a single creator God, and anyone who questioned Galen was seen as questioning the Church itself.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Miasma Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval belief that disease was caused by poisonous or foul-smelling air (bad air) rising from rotting matter or swamps.">miasma theory</dfn> held that disease was caused by “bad air” — foul-smelling vapours that rose from rotting matter, swamps, or human waste. This was a rational explanation because it was based on observation: disease did seem more common in dirty, smelly areas. Although the theory was wrong, it sometimes led to useful public health measures such as cleaning streets and draining marshes. Town authorities occasionally ordered the removal of dunghills and slaughterhouse waste from near homes, believing that eliminating foul smells would stop disease spreading. These measures accidentally reduced some infections by improving sanitation, even though the reasoning behind them was incorrect.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Approaches to Prevention and Treatment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Medieval treatments were directly connected to beliefs about disease. Because people thought illness came from God, the humours, or bad air, they used treatments designed to address those causes. <strong>Bloodletting</strong> and <strong>purging</strong> were used to rebalance the humours. Patients were cut to release blood, or given laxatives to purge excess humours. <strong>Purifying the air</strong> with sweet-smelling herbs and flowers was used to combat miasma. <strong>Prayer</strong>, attending church, and going on pilgrimages were used to seek God’s forgiveness.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">Three main types of medical practitioners existed in medieval England. <strong>Physicians</strong> were university-trained doctors who studied Galen’s texts — they were expensive and only available to the wealthy. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who prepared and sold herbal remedies and medicines in medieval England, similar to a modern pharmacist.">apothecary</dfn> mixed and sold herbal remedies, acting like an early pharmacist. <strong>Barber surgeons</strong> performed basic surgery such as pulling teeth, setting broken bones, and bloodletting — they were the most accessible practitioners for ordinary people.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Most people, however, could not afford any of these. They relied on <strong>community and home care</strong> — family members (usually women) used <strong>herbal remedies</strong> passed down through generations. Recipes combined ingredients like honey, garlic, sage, and vinegar. Some of these remedies were surprisingly effective — honey, for example, has genuine antiseptic properties.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Hospitals and Care</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Medieval hospitals were run by the Church and staffed by monks and nuns. They focused on <strong>spiritual care</strong> rather than medical treatment — patients were given food, shelter, and prayers, but rarely received medicine. Hospitals turned away people with infectious diseases to protect other patients. They were small, typically housing only a few dozen people, and functioned more like hospices than modern hospitals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down three ways medieval hospitals differed from modern ones.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Medieval hospitals provided care for the soul, not the body. They offered food, shelter, and prayer but rarely treated illness. People with contagious diseases were turned away.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Medieval people explained disease through supernatural beliefs (God’s punishment, astrology) and rational theories (Four Humours, miasma) — both were wrong but logical given the knowledge available.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Galen’s ideas dominated for over 1,000 years because the Church supported them, preventing challenge or progress.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Treatments like bloodletting, purging, and prayer were directly linked to beliefs about disease — most ordinary people relied on herbal remedies and community care.</li></ul>
