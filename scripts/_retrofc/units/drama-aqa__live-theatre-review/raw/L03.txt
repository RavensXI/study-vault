--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reading design choices in a production and explaining the meanings they generate for the audience.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Design Is Not Decoration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A common misconception is that set, lighting, sound and costume are the &ldquo;backdrop&rdquo; to the real action of the performers. In any well-made production, design is as much a language as speech. A single lighting choice &mdash; a green wash sliding in at the moment a character tells their first lie &mdash; can communicate to the audience something the dialogue never says. Your job in the exam is to decode that language.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The principle is the same as for performers: name the specific design choice, explain how it was executed technically, and evaluate its effect on the audience. The vocabulary is different &mdash; you are now working in lantern types, <dfn class="term" data-def="A shaped template placed inside a lantern to project a pattern of light onto the stage, such as window bars, leaves or abstract shapes.">gobos</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A layer of non-diegetic music or ambient sound played continuously beneath the dialogue to support mood and atmosphere.">underscore</dfn>, set materials &mdash; but the analytical method is identical.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two types of lantern, two ways lighting can be varied, and one property of a gobo. Check yourself against this lesson.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Design vocabulary the examiner rewards: Fresnel (soft-edged wash), profile (hard-edged beam), flood (wide area wash), moving light, gobo (patterned effect), gel (colour filter), lighting special (a single focused lantern on one area), blackout, cross-fade, <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific combination of lanterns active at a given moment, defined by direction, colour, intensity and type.">lighting state</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lighting: Four Questions to Ask</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When you observe a lighting moment in a production, run through four questions:</p>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Direction</strong>: where is the light coming from? Front lighting is naturalistic; side lighting gives a dramatic split-face effect and implies conflict or duality; top lighting isolates and can suggest divine or oppressive power; back lighting creates silhouette, suggesting mystery or the loss of individual identity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Colour</strong>: what gel or filter is being used? Cool blues and greens signal isolation, threat or the supernatural; warm ambers and yellows signal domestic warmth, nostalgia or golden-age memory; deep red can signal passion, danger or violence. The specific combination matters &mdash; a blue wash with a single warm special on one character separates them from the world around them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Intensity</strong>: what is the brightness? A fade to near-darkness before a final moment creates dread; a sudden full-white wash can feel clinical, exposing, accusatory. The rate of the fade or the snap of the change is also a choice &mdash; a snap blackout shocks; a slow two-minute fade mourns.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Equipment and effect</strong>: if a gobo projects bars across the stage, does that suggest imprisonment? If a <dfn class="term" data-def="A single lantern focused on one small area of the stage, creating an isolated pool of light around one performer or object.">lighting special</dfn> follows a character across the stage, does that suggest they are watched, celebrated or exposed? Note the effect, not just the equipment.</li>
</ol>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Weak version:</strong> &ldquo;The lighting was dark and dramatic, which suited the tense mood.&rdquo;</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Strong version:</strong> &ldquo;As the villain turned to face the protagonist, the stage lighting cross-faded from a warm amber general wash to a single cold tungsten profile focused tightly on the villain&rsquo;s face from a steeply raked angle above and stage left. The cross-fade took approximately four seconds, so by the time the speech began the protagonist&rsquo;s face had vanished into shadow while the villain&rsquo;s was fully lit &mdash; a visual metaphor for the shift in power within the scene. The bar gobo projected across the downstage area suggested confinement; the protagonist was already, in lighting terms, trapped before a word had been spoken. The effect on the audience was palpable: the laughter from earlier scenes disappeared completely, replaced by a sustained, uncomfortable silence.&rdquo;</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Notice: lantern type (profile), direction (steeply raked, stage left), colour (cold tungsten), a gobo type (bar), the rate of the change (four seconds), the metaphorical meaning and a specific audience reaction. All from one lighting cue.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Sound: Diegetic, Non-Diegetic and Soundscape</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Sound that exists within the world of the play and can be heard by the characters, such as a ringing telephone or birdsong through an open window.">Diegetic sound</dfn> is sound the characters can hear; <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound that exists outside the world of the play and is heard only by the audience, such as a musical underscore or a theatrical sound effect.">non-diegetic sound</dfn> is heard only by the audience. A creaking door is diegetic; the unsettling low-frequency drone beneath a scene of domestic abuse is non-diegetic underscore. Both can be analysed for meaning.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">When analysing sound, note: the texture of the sound (is it crisp and close, or distant and reverberant?), its placement (coming from specific speakers &mdash; stage left, from above, or surrounding the audience?), its relationship to the action (does it start before the scene begins, establishing mood before the performers enter?), and any sudden change (a silence that was filled with underscore is often more powerful than the music itself).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A composite of multiple sound layers &mdash; ambient noise, effects and musical textures &mdash; built up to create an immersive sonic environment for the audience.">soundscape</dfn> is a layered sonic environment rather than a single cue: the simultaneous combination of rain, distant thunder, traffic and an indistinct crowd for an opening city scene, for instance. Note how many layers you can identify and what the cumulative effect was.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Set and Costume: Symbol and Material</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Set designers make choices about material, colour, scale, level and symbolic meaning. A single chair on an otherwise bare stage can communicate isolation, power or waiting. A set that rotates during the performance communicates flux, the relentlessness of time, or the character&rsquo;s inability to escape their world. When analysing set, note: what objects are present and what they symbolise; whether the set changed during the performance and what the change communicated; the relationship between the set&rsquo;s scale and the performer&rsquo;s body (a small figure dwarfed by an enormous set reads as the individual overwhelmed by forces beyond their control).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">For costume, examiners want specific fabric, colour and silhouette analysis, not just era or genre. A costume that is visually impeccable but slightly too large for the performer communicates something very different from one that fits perfectly. Note colour against the set and against other characters&rsquo; costumes &mdash; if every character wears grey and one wears deep scarlet, that contrast is a design intention, not an accident.</p>

    <span>Misconception: General Descriptions Do Not Earn Marks</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Students frequently write design analysis at a level of generality that earns little credit: &ldquo;the lighting was atmospheric,&rdquo; &ldquo;the costumes were period-appropriate,&rdquo; &ldquo;there was dramatic music.&rdquo; These sentences do not tell the examiner anything analytical.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Compare &ldquo;the costumes were period-appropriate&rdquo; with &ldquo;the lead character&rsquo;s costume was a knee-length coat in heavyweight charcoal wool with a silhouette that was structured at the shoulders and narrowed at the waist, giving the performer the physical presence of authority even when standing still.&rdquo; Both sentences are about costume. Only one demonstrates that you understand how design communicates meaning.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">The rule is simple: if you cannot say <em>which</em> lantern, <em>what</em> colour, <em>which</em> fabric and <em>what</em> effect, your note-taking in the theatre was not specific enough. Build the habit of technical precision at the performance itself.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="For each design discipline (lighting, sound, set, costume), write one specific sentence about the production you have seen. Make each sentence contain at least two technical terms from this lesson.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Design disciplines to cover in your review: lighting (lantern type, direction, colour, intensity, gobo, lighting state), sound (diegetic / non-diegetic, texture, placement, underscore, soundscape), set (material, level, scale, symbolic props), costume (colour, fabric, silhouette, fit).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Design analysis requires specific technical vocabulary: lantern type, direction, colour, intensity and gobo for lighting; diegetic or non-diegetic, texture, placement and soundscape for sound; fabric, colour, silhouette and fit for costume; material, scale, level and symbolic meaning for set.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Run four questions on any lighting moment you observe: What is the direction? What is the colour? What is the intensity? What equipment or effect is being used and what does it mean?</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">General descriptions earn no marks. If you cannot specify which lantern, which colour and which effect at a named moment, return to your production notes and find more detail before you write.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For questions asking you to analyse design choices, the most common error is describing general mood rather than naming specific elements. &ldquo;The lighting was dark and scary&rdquo; earns no credit. &ldquo;A Fresnel flood at 15% intensity with a deep blue gel created a cold, low-contrast environment in which every performer appeared shadowless and exposed&rdquo; earns marks at every level.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">For the 8-mark designer question, you must specify a design discipline (lighting, sound, set or costume) and name concrete elements within it &mdash; lantern type, gobo, fabric, colour, fitting. Justify each choice with reference to the moment&rsquo;s meaning or the directorial intention. Generic descriptors like &ldquo;dark mood&rdquo; or &ldquo;period costume&rdquo; alone do not demonstrate design knowledge.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">In the 32-mark live theatre review, you must address at least one design discipline alongside your performer analysis. The mark scheme rewards responses that show the <em>interaction</em> between performance and design &mdash; how a performer&rsquo;s vocal choice and a lighting state worked together at a specific moment to produce a combined effect on the audience.</p>
