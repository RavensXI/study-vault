<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Prologue — A Spoiler on Purpose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare opens <em>Romeo and Juliet</em> with a fourteen-line <dfn class="term" data-def="A fourteen-line poem with a specific rhyme scheme; here used as a Chorus to frame the audience’s expectations before the action begins.">sonnet</dfn> spoken by a Chorus. This is remarkable because it tells the audience the ending before the play has started: the lovers are “star-cross’d” and their “death-mark’d love” will end in suicide. Shakespeare creates <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension and emotional engagement.">dramatic irony</dfn> that saturates every joyful moment with foreboding. Every time Romeo and Juliet speak of love, the audience already knows they will die.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Prologue also frames the feud as the root cause. The phrase “From ancient grudge break to new mutiny” tells us the hatred is inherited and cyclical. The oxymoron “fatal loins” fuses sex and death, love and violence — the play’s central paradox. For the Eduqas C720U exam, the Prologue is a goldmine for Part (i) extract questions on language and structure.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three key ideas does the Prologue establish in its opening four lines?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Prologue establishes the play’s three driving forces in its opening quatrain: the feud (“ancient grudge”), the doomed love (“star-cross’d lovers”), and the link between love and death (“fatal loins”). It is a sonnet — the form associated with love poetry — used to announce a tragedy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Act 1, Scene 1 — The Opening Brawl</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The play’s first action is a street fight between Montague and Capulet servants. Sampson and Gregory use crude sexual <dfn class="term" data-def="A joke or expression that has two meanings, one of which is usually rude or sexual. Shakespeare uses puns to reveal characters’ attitudes.">puns</dfn> (“thrust his maids to the wall”), linking violence to sexual aggression from the outset. The brawl escalates rapidly — Benvolio tries to keep the peace, but Tybalt declares: “peace? I hate the word, / As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee.” Tybalt’s absolute hatred defines the feud’s destructive energy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Prince Escalus intervenes with a death threat: “If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace.” This decree is vital for the plot — it makes Romeo’s later killing of Tybalt an act that must result in banishment or death. The Prince represents civic order struggling to contain private hatred.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Romeo enters the play mooning over Rosaline, a woman who has sworn a vow of chastity and will never love him. His language is full of Petrarchan <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech combining two contradictory terms, such as ‘loving hate’ or ‘cold fire.’ Used by Romeo to express the painful contradictions of unrequited love.">oxymorons</dfn>: “O brawling love, O loving hate, / … feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health.” Shakespeare is deliberately exaggerating the courtly love convention to show that Romeo’s feelings for Rosaline are performative and shallow. He is in love with the <em>idea</em> of being in love.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This matters because when Romeo meets Juliet, his language transforms completely. The contrast makes the audience recognise that what Romeo feels for Juliet is genuine. Examiners frequently set extract questions on Romeo’s Rosaline speeches precisely because they invite comparison with his later language.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Act 1, Scene 5 — The Capulet Ball</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Romeo gatecrashes the Capulet ball (masked, which adds dramatic irony — he is literally hiding his identity). When he sees Juliet, his language shifts immediately: “O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! / It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night / As a rich jewel in an Ethiope’s ear.” Light imagery replaces the darkness of his Rosaline speeches. Juliet is described through images of brightness, jewels and sanctity rather than through Petrarchan cliché.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The lovers share a sonnet when they first speak (lines 95–108). Romeo casts himself as a “pilgrim” and Juliet as a “holy shrine.” The religious imagery elevates their attraction beyond the physical. Crucially, Juliet is an active participant — she matches Romeo line for line, wit for wit, completing the sonnet together. This shared authorship signals mutual love, unlike the one-sided Petrarchan worship of Rosaline.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write out two quotations that show Romeo’s language change from Rosaline to Juliet.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Romeo’s shift from oxymorons about Rosaline (“feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire”) to light imagery about Juliet (“she doth teach the torches to burn bright”) signals the difference between performative infatuation and genuine love. Their shared sonnet (Act 1, Scene 5) shows love as reciprocal and mutual.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Tybalt recognises Romeo’s voice and is furious: “this, by his voice, should be a Montague. / Fetch me my rapier, boy.” Capulet restrains him, but Tybalt vows revenge: “I will withdraw; but this intrusion shall, / Now seeming sweet, convert to bitt’rest gall.” The word “gall” (poison/bitterness) foreshadows the poisonous consequences that will follow. Shakespeare juxtaposes the sweetness of the lovers’ meeting with Tybalt’s threat, interweaving love and violence in the same scene.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Juliet’s Discovery — “My Only Love Sprung from My Only Hate”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">When the Nurse reveals Romeo is a Montague, Juliet responds: “My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!” The rhyming couplet has the neatness of a proverb but the weight of tragedy. The antithesis of “love” and “hate,” “early” and “late,” encapsulates the play’s central paradox: the very feud that should keep them apart is what makes their love so urgent and so dangerous. This line is one of the most frequently quoted in Eduqas extract questions.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The Prologue is a sonnet that reveals the ending, creating dramatic irony that haunts every moment of joy in the play.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Romeo’s language shifts from hollow Petrarchan oxymorons (Rosaline) to genuine light imagery and a shared sonnet (Juliet) — signalling real love.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Act 1 interweaves love and violence: the lovers’ meeting at the ball is framed by the opening brawl and Tybalt’s threat, foreshadowing tragedy.</li></ul>
