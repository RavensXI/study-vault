<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Recording Studio Equipment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A recording studio is a controlled environment designed to capture high-quality audio. Understanding the equipment in a studio — and how to use it safely — is essential for anyone working in music technology. This lesson covers the core equipment you need to know for the exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Microphones</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Microphones convert sound waves into electrical signals. There are two main types you must know:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Dynamic microphones</strong> use a moving coil attached to a diaphragm. They are robust, do not require external power, and handle loud volumes well. They are ideal for live performance and recording loud sources like drums and guitar amplifiers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sensitive microphone that uses an electrically charged diaphragm and requires phantom power. Best suited for capturing detail and nuance in studio recordings.">Condenser microphones</dfn> use an electrically charged diaphragm. They are more sensitive than dynamic microphones and capture greater detail and nuance. They require <dfn class="term" data-def="A 48-volt DC power supply sent through an audio cable to power condenser microphones, provided by an audio interface or mixing desk.">phantom power</dfn> (48V) to operate and are best suited for studio vocals, acoustic instruments, and room ambience.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">A microphone’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The three-dimensional area around a microphone from which it is most sensitive to sound. Common patterns include cardioid, omnidirectional, and figure-of-eight.">polar pattern</dfn> describes the directions from which it picks up sound. A <strong>cardioid</strong> pattern picks up mainly from the front, rejecting sound from the sides and rear — useful for isolating a single source. An <strong>omnidirectional</strong> pattern picks up equally from all directions — useful for room ambience. A <strong>figure-of-eight</strong> pattern picks up from the front and rear but rejects the sides — useful for recording two facing sources simultaneously.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">A microphone’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph showing how sensitive a microphone is at different frequencies, from low bass to high treble.">frequency response</dfn> shows how it responds to different frequencies. A flat frequency response means the microphone treats all frequencies equally, reproducing sound accurately. Some microphones have a “presence peak” — a boost in the upper frequencies that adds brightness to vocals.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the difference between dynamic and condenser microphones, including which needs phantom power.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Dynamic</strong> microphones: robust, no external power needed, handle loud sources. <strong>Condenser</strong> microphones: more sensitive, capture detail, require 48V phantom power. Choose dynamic for live/loud sources, condenser for studio/detailed recordings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">DI (Direct Injection)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that converts an unbalanced, high-impedance instrument signal into a balanced, low-impedance signal suitable for a mixing desk or audio interface.">DI box</dfn> (Direct Injection box) is used to connect instruments like electric guitars, bass guitars, and keyboards directly to a mixing desk or audio interface. The DI box performs <strong>level matching</strong> — it converts the instrument’s high-impedance, unbalanced signal into a low-impedance, balanced signal. This reduces noise and allows the signal to travel over long cable runs without quality loss.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Audio Interfaces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A hardware device that converts analogue audio signals into digital data for a computer, and digital data back to analogue for monitoring.">audio interface</dfn> is the bridge between the analogue world (microphones, instruments) and the digital world (the computer and DAW). Key features include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Phantom power</strong> — 48V supply for condenser microphones, activated per channel.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Balanced and unbalanced inputs</strong> — balanced inputs (XLR or TRS) reject interference; unbalanced inputs (standard jack) are for shorter cable runs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Pre-amps</strong> — built-in preamplifiers boost the weak microphone signal to a usable level.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Outputs</strong> — connections for speakers (monitors) and headphones.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>DAW connectivity</strong> — the interface connects to the computer via USB, Thunderbolt, or similar connection.</li>
</ul>



<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Multitrack Recorder & Recording Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device (hardware or software) that records multiple audio signals simultaneously on separate tracks, allowing independent editing and mixing.">multitrack recorder</dfn> can be hardware (a standalone unit) or software (a DAW on a computer). It records multiple audio signals on separate tracks so each instrument or voice can be edited and mixed independently.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The recording process involves several stages: selecting the correct <strong>input source</strong> (microphone or DI), <strong>mic placement</strong> (close-mic for isolation, room-mic for ambience), <strong>gain optimisation</strong> (setting the input level so the signal is strong but does not clip or distort), and <strong>overdubbing</strong> (recording additional parts on new tracks while listening back to previously recorded tracks).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Additional Equipment & Monitoring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Studios also require: <strong>stands</strong> (mic stands, boom stands), <strong>cables</strong> (XLR, jack, speaker), <strong>connectors</strong> (XLR, 6.35mm jack, 3.5mm jack), <strong>pop shields</strong> (mesh screens that reduce plosive sounds from vocals), <strong>cases</strong> (protective storage), and <strong>rack units</strong> (outboard processors mounted in standard 19-inch racks).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n24">The studio is divided into two spaces: the <strong>control room</strong> (where the engineer works, with speakers and the mixing desk) and the <strong>live room</strong> (where the performer records, acoustically treated to control reflections). <strong>Speakers</strong> (studio monitors) provide accurate, flat-response playback in the control room. <strong>Headphones</strong> allow the performer in the live room to hear the backing track while recording. <strong>Talkback</strong> is an intercom system that lets the engineer communicate with the performer in the live room.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Health & Safety in the Recording Studio</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Health and safety is a legal and practical requirement. You must understand the hazards, risks, and control measures for a recording studio environment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four studio hazards and their associated risks from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Hazards and risks:</strong> <strong>Noise</strong> → hearing loss. <strong>Displays</strong> (screens) → eye strain. <strong>Slip/trip/fall</strong> (cables on the floor, equipment) → sprains and injuries. <strong>Electrical</strong> (faulty equipment, liquids near electronics) → electrocution. A <strong>risk assessment</strong> must be carried out before every session, and <strong>control measures</strong> (e.g. cable management, volume limits, PAT testing) must be in place. Incidents and near-misses must be <strong>reported</strong>.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name a control measure for each of the four studio hazards.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Control measures:</strong> For noise — set volume limits, use hearing protection. For displays — take regular breaks, adjust screen brightness. For slip/trip/fall — tape cables to the floor, keep walkways clear. For electrical — use PAT-tested equipment, keep liquids away from electronics. Always complete a risk assessment and know the reporting procedure for incidents.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n31">Dynamic microphones are robust and handle loud sources; condenser microphones are sensitive, detailed, and require 48V phantom power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32">An audio interface converts analogue signals to digital and provides phantom power, pre-amps, inputs, outputs, and DAW connectivity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Studio health and safety covers four main hazards (noise, displays, slip/trip/fall, electrical) with corresponding risks, control measures, risk assessment, and reporting.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">Health and safety questions appear regularly. Always structure your answer around: identify the <strong>hazard</strong>, state the <strong>risk</strong>, and describe the <strong>control measure</strong>. For equipment questions, explain <em>why</em> a piece of equipment is used, not just <em>what</em> it is — for example, “phantom power is needed because condenser microphones require a 48V supply to charge their diaphragm.”</p>
