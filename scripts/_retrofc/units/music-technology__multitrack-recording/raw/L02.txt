<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Planning a Recording Session</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A successful multitrack recording does not start when the red light goes on — it starts with careful planning. Before any microphone is positioned or any cable is connected, the recording team must agree on roles, timelines, goals, and requirements. Poor planning leads to wasted studio time, frustrated artists, and poor-quality recordings.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Roles in the Studio</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Three key roles are involved in a recording session:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The performer or performers whose music is being recorded in the studio.">artist</dfn> is the musician or singer being recorded. They perform the parts and may have specific preferences about monitoring, headphone mix, and studio setup.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The technical specialist who operates the recording equipment, sets levels, positions microphones, and manages the audio signal chain.">engineer</dfn> operates the recording equipment. They set up microphones, manage the audio interface and DAW, optimise gain levels, monitor audio quality, and handle all technical aspects of the session.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The creative leader of a recording session who guides the artistic direction, making decisions about arrangement, performance quality, and overall sound.">producer</dfn> takes the creative lead. They guide the artistic direction of the recording, make decisions about arrangement, performance quality, and overall sound. In some sessions, the engineer and producer may be the same person.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three studio roles with one responsibility for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three key studio roles are: <strong>Artist</strong> (performs the music), <strong>Engineer</strong> (operates equipment and manages the technical side), and <strong>Producer</strong> (guides the creative direction and makes artistic decisions).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Planning Elements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Before a recording session, several planning elements must be agreed:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Time constraints and timeline</strong> — when does the recording need to be finished? Working backwards from the deadline helps allocate time for recording, editing, and mixing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Session length</strong> — how many hours are booked? Sessions must be realistic — booking too little time creates pressure, while booking too much wastes budget.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Agreed goals</strong> — what needs to be achieved in this session? For example: “Record drums and bass for three songs” or “Capture lead vocals for the single.”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hardware and software requirements</strong> — what equipment is needed? Are specific microphones, instruments, amplifiers, or software plug-ins required?</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Artist requirements</strong> — does the artist need a specific headphone mix, lyric sheets, a particular room setup, or breaks at set intervals?</li>
</ul>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Without clear planning, sessions often run into problems: the wrong microphone is booked and has to be substituted at the last minute; the session overruns because goals were unrealistic; the artist is uncomfortable because their requirements were not discussed in advance; or the engineer wastes time searching for the right cable because the equipment list was not checked. Each of these issues costs time and money, and reduces the quality of the final recording.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Setting Up for Recording</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Once planning is complete, the engineer sets up the studio. This involves selecting and positioning microphones, connecting cables, routing signals to the audio interface, and creating a new project in the DAW with correctly labelled tracks.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Testing</strong> is essential before the session begins. The engineer checks every microphone, cable, and input to confirm the signal is reaching the DAW correctly. They listen for noise, hum, or faulty connections and fix any issues before the artist arrives or begins performing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Appropriate use</strong> of equipment means using each item for its intended purpose — for example, not running a condenser microphone without phantom power, or not plugging an instrument into a microphone input without a DI box.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Health and safety measures</strong> must be in place: cables taped down, volume limits set, risk assessment completed, and emergency exits clear.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Gain Optimisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of setting the input level on a microphone pre-amp so the recorded signal is strong enough for a clean recording but does not clip or distort.">Gain optimisation</dfn> is one of the most important technical skills in recording. The engineer adjusts the gain (input level) on the audio interface or pre-amp so that the signal is loud enough to produce a clean recording, but not so loud that it <dfn class="term" data-def="Distortion that occurs when an audio signal exceeds the maximum level a system can handle, causing harsh, unwanted noise.">clips</dfn> (distorts). The target is to have peaks reaching around −6 dB to −12 dB on the meter, leaving headroom for unexpected loud moments.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Monitoring During Recording</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Monitoring means listening to the audio during and after recording. The <strong>engineer</strong> monitors through studio speakers or headphones in the control room, checking levels, quality, and balance. The <strong>artist</strong> wears headphones in the live room to hear a mix of the backing track and their own performance. The headphone mix the artist hears should be comfortable — if they cannot hear themselves clearly, their performance will suffer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Overdubbing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of recording new audio onto a new track while playing back previously recorded tracks, allowing musicians to build up parts one at a time.">Overdubbing</dfn> is the process of recording additional parts on new tracks while playing back previously recorded material. For example, a band might record drums first, then overdub bass, then guitars, then vocals. Each part gets its own track, allowing independent editing and mixing later. The engineer must monitor for timing issues, check gain on each new track, and ensure the artist’s headphone mix is updated for each overdub.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the five planning elements that must be agreed before a recording session.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five planning elements are: <strong>time constraints/timeline</strong>, <strong>session length</strong>, <strong>agreed goals</strong>, <strong>hardware/software requirements</strong>, and <strong>artist requirements</strong>. These must all be confirmed before the session begins.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">File Management</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Good file management prevents lost work and confusion. Three rules apply:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Naming audio files</strong> — each recorded take should have a clear, descriptive name (e.g. “LeadVocal_Take03.wav” rather than “Audio_001”).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Labelling folders</strong> — audio files should be organised into folders by song, instrument, or session date.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Naming projects</strong> — the DAW project file itself should have a clear name that includes the song title and date (e.g. “SunriseDemo_2026-03-30”).</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34">Always save the project at regular intervals and create backups. Use “Save As” with a new version number when making significant changes (e.g. “SunriseDemo_v2”). Keep all audio files within the project folder so the DAW can always find them. If files are moved or renamed outside the DAW, the project may lose its links and audio will go missing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n37">Three key roles in a recording session are the artist, engineer, and producer — each with distinct responsibilities.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Planning covers time constraints, session length, agreed goals, hardware/software requirements, and artist requirements.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Good file management (naming files, labelling folders, naming projects) prevents lost work and keeps sessions organised.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">When answering questions about planning, always link each planning element to a practical reason. For example, do not just write “you need to plan session length” — explain <em>why</em>: “Session length must be planned so there is enough time to record all parts without rushing, which would affect performance quality.” For file management questions, give specific examples of good naming conventions.</p>
