<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Mixing Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Once all parts have been recorded, the next stage is <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of combining and balancing multiple recorded tracks into a final stereo or surround sound output.">mixing</dfn>. Mixing is where raw multitrack recordings are transformed into a polished, professional-sounding piece of music. The engineer adjusts volume, panning, EQ, effects, and dynamics on every track to create a balanced, engaging final mix. This lesson covers all the mixing concepts and tools you need for the exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Balance & the Stereo Field</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Relative volume</strong> is the starting point of any mix. The engineer adjusts the faders so that each instrument sits at the right level — vocals are typically the loudest element in a pop mix, with drums and bass providing the foundation, and other instruments filling in around them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The left-to-right space between the two speakers in a stereo system, within which sounds can be positioned using panning.">stereo field</dfn> is the space between the left and right speakers. Sounds can be placed anywhere across this field using <strong>panning</strong> controls. Placing instruments in different positions across the stereo field creates width and separation, preventing a cluttered, mono-sounding mix.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Soundstage Placement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A good mix creates a three-dimensional <dfn class="term" data-def="The perceived three-dimensional space of a mix, described in terms of height (frequency), width (stereo placement), and depth (ambience).">soundstage</dfn> — the illusion that the listener is hearing a real performance in a physical space. The soundstage has three dimensions:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Height, Width & Depth</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Height</strong> is controlled by <em>frequency</em> and <em>timbre</em>. Bass-heavy instruments (kick drum, bass guitar) sit at the bottom of the soundstage, while bright instruments (cymbals, high synths) sit at the top. The ear naturally perceives high frequencies as “higher” and low frequencies as “lower.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Width</strong> is controlled by <em>stereo placement</em>, <em>panning</em>, and the <em>stereo image</em>. Panning an instrument hard left or hard right pushes it to the edge of the stereo field. Keeping lead vocals and bass in the centre provides a solid anchor. Spreading guitars, keyboards, and backing vocals across the field creates width.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Depth</strong> is controlled by <em>ambience</em> (using <strong>delay</strong> and <strong>reverb</strong>) and <em>dynamic processing</em>. A sound with lots of reverb appears further away. A dry, close-miked sound appears right in front of the listener. Adjusting the balance of dry signal versus reverb/delay controls how near or far each element sounds.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the three dimensions of a soundstage and what controls each one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three dimensions of a soundstage are: <strong>Height</strong> (controlled by frequency and timbre), <strong>Width</strong> (controlled by stereo placement, panning, and stereo image), and <strong>Depth</strong> (controlled by ambience — reverb and delay — and dynamic processing).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Corrective Editing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Before applying creative effects, the engineer may need to fix problems in the recordings:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Unwanted audio removal</strong> — deleting coughs, chair creaks, count-ins, or background noise between phrases.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Pitch correction</strong> — using tools like auto-tune to fix slightly out-of-tune notes in a vocal or instrument performance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Rhythmic correction</strong> — nudging audio clips or quantising MIDI notes so that timing is tight and consistent.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">EQ (Equalisation)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Equalisation — an audio processing tool that boosts or cuts specific frequency ranges to shape the tonal character of a sound.">EQ</dfn> shapes the tonal character of each track. Key EQ controls include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Low-pass filter</strong> — removes frequencies above a set point, keeping only the low end.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>High-pass filter</strong> — removes frequencies below a set point, keeping only the high end. Commonly used to cut rumble from vocal tracks.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Frequency selection</strong> — choosing which specific frequency to boost or cut.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Q</strong> — the width of the frequency band being affected. A narrow Q targets a precise frequency; a wide Q affects a broad range.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Gain</strong> — how much the selected frequency is boosted.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Attenuation</strong> — how much the selected frequency is cut (reduced).</li>
</ul>



<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Effects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Effects add character, space, and interest to a mix. You must know the following:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Reverb</strong> simulates the reflections of a room. <strong>Delay</strong> creates repeating echoes. <strong>Chorus</strong> thickens a sound by doubling it with slight pitch and timing variations. <strong>Flanging</strong> creates a sweeping, jet-like effect. <strong>Phasing</strong> produces a similar sweeping effect by cancelling certain frequencies. <strong>Wah-wah</strong> sweeps a filter to mimic a human vocal sound. <strong>Amp sim</strong> (amplifier simulation) recreates the sound of a guitar amplifier digitally. <strong>Overdrive</strong> adds harmonic distortion for warmth or aggression.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Dynamics Processing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><dfn class="term" data-def="Audio processing tools that control the volume range of a signal, including compressors, limiters, and gates.">Dynamics processors</dfn> control the volume range of a signal. Three types are essential:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Compressor, Limiter & Gate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n30">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A dynamics processor that reduces the volume of audio that exceeds a set threshold, evening out the dynamic range.">compressor</dfn> reduces the volume of audio that exceeds a set <strong>threshold</strong>. The <strong>ratio</strong> controls how much reduction is applied (e.g. 4:1 means for every 4 dB over the threshold, only 1 dB passes through). <strong>Attack</strong> controls how quickly compression starts. <strong>Release</strong> controls how quickly it stops. <strong>Gain</strong> (make-up gain) compensates for the volume lost during compression.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">A <strong>limiter</strong> is a compressor with an extreme ratio (typically infinity:1). It prevents any audio from exceeding the threshold, acting as a hard ceiling. Limiters are used on the master bus to prevent clipping.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A dynamics processor that silences audio below a set threshold, used to remove background noise or bleed between loud passages.">gate</dfn> works in reverse — it silences audio that falls <em>below</em> the threshold. Gates are used to remove background noise, headphone bleed, or room ambience between drum hits or vocal phrases.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the five compressor parameters from memory: threshold, ratio, attack, release, gain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five compressor parameters are: <strong>Threshold</strong> (level at which compression begins), <strong>Ratio</strong> (amount of reduction, e.g. 4:1), <strong>Attack</strong> (how quickly compression starts), <strong>Release</strong> (how quickly it stops), and <strong>Gain</strong> (make-up gain to restore lost volume).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Connections, Automation & Monitoring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Inserts</strong> place an effect directly in the signal path of a single track (e.g. EQ and compressor on a vocal). <strong>Auxiliaries (bus sends)</strong> route a copy of the signal to a shared effect — <strong>pre-fade</strong> sends are independent of the fader (used for headphone monitoring), while <strong>post-fade</strong> sends follow the fader level (used for shared reverb/delay). <strong>Routing</strong> is the path audio follows through the mixer from input to output.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n36"><dfn class="term" data-def="Recorded parameter changes that play back automatically during mixing, such as volume, panning, or effect send adjustments over time.">Automation</dfn> records changes to volume, panning, or effect sends over time, so adjustments play back automatically. For example, the engineer might automate a vocal volume boost during the chorus, or gradually increase reverb send during a fade-out.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Monitoring</strong> during mixing uses <strong>headphones</strong> for detail checks, <strong>main speakers</strong> (large monitors) for full-range listening, and <strong>near-field speakers</strong> (smaller monitors) that simulate a typical listening environment. The engineer also checks the mix against <strong>reference material</strong> — a professionally mixed track in a similar style — to judge tonal balance and loudness.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n38">Stereo Mixdowns & Export</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n39">The final step is creating a <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of combining all tracks in a multitrack project into a single stereo audio file for distribution.">stereo mixdown</dfn> — bouncing all tracks into a single stereo audio file. The engineer can also export individual <strong>stems</strong> (groups of tracks, e.g. all drums, all vocals) for further processing or remixing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n40" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three export settings you must choose for a stereo mixdown.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When exporting a stereo mixdown, you must choose: <strong>Bit depth</strong> (e.g. 16-bit for CD, 24-bit for professional), <strong>Sample rate</strong> (e.g. 44.1 kHz for CD, 48 kHz for video), and <strong>Format</strong> (WAV or AIF for lossless, MP3 for compressed). Higher bit depth and sample rate mean better quality but larger file sizes.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Bit Depth & Sample Rate Explained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Bit depth</strong> determines the dynamic range of the recording — how much difference there is between the quietest and loudest sounds. 16-bit provides 96 dB of dynamic range (CD standard). 24-bit provides 144 dB (professional standard). <strong>Sample rate</strong> determines how many times per second the audio signal is measured. 44.1 kHz is the CD standard (44,100 samples per second). 48 kHz is common for video work. Higher sample rates capture more high-frequency detail.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n43">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n44">A mix creates a three-dimensional soundstage using height (frequency), width (stereo panning), and depth (reverb/delay).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n45">Key mixing tools include EQ (frequency shaping), effects (reverb, delay, chorus, etc.), and dynamics processors (compressor, limiter, gate) with five parameters: threshold, ratio, attack, release, and gain.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n46">Stereo mixdowns are exported with a chosen bit depth, sample rate, and format (WAV, AIF, or MP3).</li></ul>
