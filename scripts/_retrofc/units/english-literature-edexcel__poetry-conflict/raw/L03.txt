--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Blake, Wordsworth and Rossetti present inner conflict — suppressed anger, nature's power and betrayal.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Conflict Within</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not all conflict involves armies and battlefields. Three poems in the Edexcel Conflict anthology explore internal struggles: <em>A Poison Tree</em> by William Blake examines how suppressed anger grows into something destructive; <em>Extract from The Prelude ‘Boating’</em> by William Wordsworth dramatises a terrifying encounter with the power of nature; and <em>Cousin Kate</em> by Christina Rossetti voices a woman’s fury at being used and discarded by a powerful man. Each poem shows how inner conflict can be just as devastating as physical violence.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">A Poison Tree — William Blake (1794)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Blake was a <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary and artistic movement of the late 18th and early 19th centuries that emphasised emotion, imagination and individualism over reason and order.">Romantic</dfn> poet who challenged the rigid social and religious structures of his time. <em>A Poison Tree</em> is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A short story with a moral message, often using symbolic characters or events to teach a lesson.">allegory</dfn> about the dangers of suppressed anger. The speaker tells his friend about his wrath and it ends; he hides his anger from his enemy and it grows.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Blake uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended comparison that runs throughout a text, connecting different images to a single idea.">extended metaphor</dfn> of a tree to chart the growth of hidden rage. The speaker “waterd it in fears, / Night & morning with my tears” and “sunned it with smiles, / And with soft deceitful wiles.” The natural imagery of watering and sunlight is ironic — nurturing language is applied to something toxic. The anger grows until it bears “an apple bright” which the speaker’s enemy steals and eats, and is found “outstretchd beneath the tree” — dead.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The “apple bright” is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A reference to a well-known text, story, person or event, used to add layers of meaning.">biblical allusion</dfn> to the forbidden fruit in the Garden of Eden. Just as the serpent tempted Eve, the speaker’s suppressed anger creates a deadly temptation. Blake implies that dishonesty and repression are more destructive than honest confrontation.</p>
</div>

This simplicity is deceptive — the childlike form makes the dark subject matter (murder through deceit) even more disturbing. The contrast between innocent form and sinister content is itself a kind of conflict. Each stanza advances the narrative one step: anger expressed, anger concealed, anger growing, enemy destroyed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Extract from The Prelude ‘Boating’ — William Wordsworth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Wordsworth’s autobiographical epic <em>The Prelude</em> (published 1850) traces the development of his mind and imagination. This extract describes a boy stealing a boat at night and rowing across a lake. At first, the experience is joyful: “lustily / I dipped my oars into the silent lake.” But as he rows further, a “huge peak, black and huge” seems to rise from behind the smaller hills and pursue him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The conflict here is between the boy’s confidence and nature’s overwhelming power. Wordsworth personifies the mountain: it “Strode after me” with “measured motion like a living thing.” The boy is terrified and rows back “with trembling oars.” For days after, his mind is haunted by “huge and mighty forms, that do not live / Like living men.” Nature has permanently altered his understanding of himself.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wordsworth wrote this as <dfn class="term" data-def="Unrhymed poetry written in iambic pentameter (ten syllables per line with a regular stress pattern), often used for serious, reflective subjects.">blank verse</dfn> — unrhymed iambic pentameter. The lack of rhyme and stanza breaks creates a continuous, unbroken flow that mirrors the boy’s rowing and the relentless advance of the mountain. The form reinforces the sense of an experience that overwhelms normal structures of thought.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Cousin Kate — Christina Rossetti (1862)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Rossetti writes from the perspective of a “cottage maiden” who was seduced and abandoned by a lord. The lord then married her cousin Kate instead. The speaker is left shamed and outcast: “The neighbours call me an unclean thing.” Her anger is directed at both the lord (“He lured me to his palace home”) and at Kate (“O cousin Kate, my love was true”).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative poem with a regular rhyme scheme and rhythm, often telling a story of love, loss or adventure.">ballad</dfn> form (ABCB rhyme, regular metre) gives the poem a folk-song quality, which suits its story of love, betrayal and social injustice. Rossetti uses possessive language to show how the lord treats women as property: “He wore me like a silken knot, / He changed me like a glove.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech comparing two unlike things using 'like' or 'as'.">similes</dfn> reduce the speaker to an object — decorative, disposable, easily replaced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The poem’s final twist is powerful: the speaker has a son (“my fair-haired son, my shame, my pride”), while Kate remains childless. In Victorian society, producing an heir was a woman’s most important role. The speaker’s “shame” (an illegitimate child) becomes her triumph — she has something Kate, for all her status, lacks.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Blake (A Poison Tree):</strong> The conflict is between expressing and suppressing emotion. Blake argues that repression breeds something far worse than honest anger — it breeds calculated cruelty.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Wordsworth (The Prelude):</strong> The conflict is between human confidence and nature’s sublime, terrifying power. The boy’s self-assurance is destroyed by an encounter he cannot control or understand.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Rossetti (Cousin Kate):</strong> The conflict is between a woman’s love and the patriarchal society that exploits and discards her. The speaker channels her betrayal into defiant pride.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">All three poets are from the Romantic or Victorian period. A strong AO3 response would discuss how each reflects the tensions of their era: Blake’s challenge to religious repression, Wordsworth’s worship of the <dfn class="term" data-def="An experience of awe, terror and beauty so intense it overwhelms the human mind — a key concept in Romantic poetry.">sublime</dfn>, and Rossetti’s critique of Victorian gender inequality.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22"><em>A Poison Tree</em> uses an extended tree metaphor and deceptively simple rhyme to show how suppressed anger grows into deadly violence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><em>Extract from The Prelude</em> uses blank verse, personification and the shift from joy to terror to dramatise the overwhelming power of nature.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><em>Cousin Kate</em> uses ballad form, possessive similes and a defiant final twist to voice a woman’s anger at sexual exploitation and social hypocrisy.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">These three poems work well as comparison pairs because they share the theme of inner conflict but differ hugely in tone, form and focus. In the exam, if one of these is the named poem, consider choosing another from this group. For example: compare Blake’s allegorical treatment of suppressed anger with Rossetti’s dramatic monologue of personal betrayal. Both present destructive emotions, but Blake’s is coldly calculated while Rossetti’s is raw and passionate. Always link your analysis to AO2 (specific language and structural choices) and AO3 (what the poets’ contexts reveal about their intentions).</p>
