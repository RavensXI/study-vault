--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Agard, Zephaniah, Casey and Carson use language to challenge racism, class prejudice and sectarianism.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Language as a Weapon and a Shield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Four poems in the Edexcel Conflict anthology explore social conflict — the everyday battles over identity, race, class and belonging. <em>Half-caste</em> by John Agard challenges racial categorisation; <em>No Problem</em> by Benjamin Zephaniah confronts racism with defiant humour; <em>The Class Game</em> by Mary Casey attacks class snobbery; and <em>Belfast Confetti</em> by Ciaran Carson fragments language itself to convey the chaos of sectarian violence. In each poem, language is both the subject and the weapon.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Half-caste — John Agard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Agard, born in Guyana and living in Britain, wrote <em>Half-caste</em> to challenge the term “half-caste,” which was used to describe people of mixed heritage. The poem is a sustained <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a word or phrase is applied to something it does not literally describe, to suggest a comparison.">rhetorical attack</dfn> on the absurdity of the label. Agard asks: if Picasso mixes red and green, is that a “half-caste canvas”? If Tchaikovsky blends a black key and a white key, is that a “half-caste symphony”?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">These examples expose the ridiculousness of calling a person “half” anything. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Informal, everyday language that imitates natural speech patterns, often specific to a region or community.">colloquial</dfn> Caribbean dialect (“Explain yuself / wha yu mean”) is a deliberate political choice: Agard refuses to adopt “standard English” because doing so would accept the hierarchy that labels him as inferior. His dialect is his identity, and using it is an act of resistance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain both sides of the contrast described here from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Agard writes without punctuation or standard capitalisation throughout. This rejection of conventional English grammar mirrors his rejection of the social conventions that label him “half.” The form of the poem enacts the argument: if you refuse to accept someone as whole, why should they accept your rules?</p>
</div>

This <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of humour, exaggeration or ridicule to expose and criticise foolishness or injustice.">satire</dfn> forces the listener to confront how dehumanising the term is. The poem ends with a challenge: “but yu must come back tomorrow / wid de whole of yu eye / an de whole of yu ear / an de whole of yu mind.” Agard demands that the listener engage with him fully — as a complete person, not a category.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">No Problem — Benjamin Zephaniah</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Zephaniah, a British-Jamaican <dfn class="term" data-def="A poet who performs poetry aloud, often with rhythm, music and audience interaction, rather than primarily writing for the page.">dub poet</dfn>, wrote <em>No Problem</em> as a direct response to everyday racism in Britain. The speaker lists the prejudices he faces — “I am not de problem” — and turns each accusation back on the accuser. The repetition of “I am not de problem” is both a denial and an assertion of identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The poem’s rhythm is rooted in <dfn class="term" data-def="A style of Jamaican music-poetry that combines reggae rhythms with political spoken word.">dub</dfn> culture — a blend of reggae and spoken word. Read aloud, the poem has a musical, almost chant-like quality that turns protest into performance. Zephaniah uses humour (“I am born academic”) and defiance (“Black is not de problem”) together, refusing to be defined by others’ prejudice. The final message is clear: racism is the problem of the racist, not the victim.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Class Game — Mary Casey</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Casey’s poem attacks class snobbery in Britain by listing the markers used to judge someone’s social status: “How can you tell what class I’m from? / I can tell by your hands.” She catalogues the differences between working-class and middle-class life: “a deck chair, or a yacht,” “a corgi, or a whippet.” The poem’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of a series of words, phrases or clauses listed one after another for emphasis or to build an argument.">listing</dfn> technique builds an overwhelming case that class divisions are arbitrary and absurd.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Casey writes in a strong working-class voice, using dialect and colloquial language (“me mam,” “bog standard”). Like Agard, her refusal to adopt “proper” English is politically charged — she speaks as she is, rejecting the idea that her accent or vocabulary makes her inferior. The poem’s direct address (“How can you tell?”) confronts the reader, demanding they examine their own prejudices.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Belfast Confetti — Ciaran Carson</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Carson writes about the <dfn class="term" data-def="A conflict in Northern Ireland (c.1968–1998) between unionists (who wanted to remain part of the UK) and nationalists (who wanted a united Ireland), involving violence, bombings and military presence.">Troubles</dfn> in Northern Ireland. “Belfast confetti” was a slang term for the shrapnel — nuts, bolts, nails — packed into homemade bombs. The poem describes a bomb explosion and its aftermath, but what makes it extraordinary is how Carson uses punctuation marks as shrapnel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">“Suddenly as the riot squad moved in it was raining exclamation marks, / Nuts, bolts, nails, car keys. A fount of broken type.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a word or phrase is applied to something it does not literally describe, to suggest a comparison.">metaphor</dfn> of punctuation as debris is brilliant: the explosion shatters language itself. The speaker cannot complete a sentence: “I know this labyrinth so well — / Balaklava, Raglan, Inkerman, Odessa Street —” The dashes interrupt thought, just as the violence interrupts life. Even the street names reference the Crimean War, layering historical conflict onto present-day Belfast.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Carson ends with a flurry of unanswered questions: “What is / My name? Where am I coming from? Where am I going?” The violence has stripped the speaker of his most basic certainties — identity, origin, direction. The poem’s fragmented form mirrors the fragmentation of the speaker’s mind and city.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Casey</strong> uses working-class dialect for the same purpose: refusing to speak “properly” is itself a challenge to the class system.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Carson</strong> shows language breaking down under violence — rather than using language as a weapon of resistance, he shows how conflict destroys the ability to communicate at all.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">In the exam, these poems pair well together for comparisons about <strong>how language and form reflect identity and resistance</strong>. Agard + Casey is a particularly strong pairing: both reject “standard” language conventions to challenge prejudice (racial vs class).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24"><em>Half-caste</em> uses Caribbean dialect, satire and the rejection of punctuation to challenge racial categorisation and demand full recognition as a human being.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><em>No Problem</em> uses dub rhythm, repetition and humour to confront racism and assert that prejudice is the racist’s problem, not the victim’s.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><em>The Class Game</em> uses listing, direct address and working-class dialect to expose the arbitrary markers of class division.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><em>Belfast Confetti</em> uses fragmented punctuation as a metaphor for shrapnel, showing how sectarian violence destroys language, identity and the ability to think clearly.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">These poems reward close analysis of <strong>form</strong> (AO2). Agard’s lack of punctuation, Zephaniah’s dub rhythm, Casey’s working-class voice and Carson’s fragmented sentences all demonstrate how the <em>way</em> a poem is written can be as important as what it says. In the exam, always link form to meaning: “Agard rejects standard punctuation to mirror his rejection of the social conventions that label him as ‘half.’” For AO3, discuss how each poet’s personal or cultural context shapes their use of language: Agard’s Guyanese heritage, Zephaniah’s Jamaican-British identity, Casey’s working-class background, Carson’s experience of the Troubles.</p>
