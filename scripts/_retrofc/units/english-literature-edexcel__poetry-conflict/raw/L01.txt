--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Owen, Tennyson and Hardy present the soldier's experience of battle, duty, futility and horror.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">War Through the Soldier’s Eyes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Three poems in the Edexcel Conflict anthology examine what it feels like to be a soldier in battle. <em>Exposure</em> by Wilfred Owen, <em>The Charge of the Light Brigade</em> by Alfred Lord Tennyson, and <em>The Man He Killed</em> by Thomas Hardy each offer radically different perspectives on war — from the grinding misery of the trenches, to the glory and horror of a cavalry charge, to the quiet absurdity of killing a stranger. Together they show us how poets use language and structure to shape our response to conflict.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the key contextual details and how they connect to the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Owen’s <em>Exposure</em> was written from the trenches of World War I. Its central message is that the soldiers’ worst enemy is not the opposing army but the relentless, deadly cold. The refrain “But nothing happens” emphasises futility — these men suffer and die without even engaging in battle.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Exposure — Wilfred Owen (1917–18)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Owen served in the trenches and was killed one week before the <dfn class="term" data-def="The end of fighting in World War I, signed on 11 November 1918.">Armistice</dfn>. <em>Exposure</em> describes soldiers waiting in freezing conditions through the night. Nature — wind, snow, frost — is <dfn class="term" data-def="Giving human qualities to a non-human thing, such as nature or weather.">personified</dfn> as an active attacker: “merciless iced east winds that knive us.” The verb ‘knive’ turns weather into a weapon, suggesting nature is more dangerous than any enemy soldier.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The soldiers hallucinate about home — “Slowly our ghosts drag home” — suggesting they are already psychologically dead. Owen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A question asked for rhetorical effect, not expecting an answer, used to make the reader think.">rhetorical questions</dfn> (“What are we doing here?”) to voice their despair. The half-rhymes throughout (“knive us … nervous,” “wire … war”) create a dissonant, unsettling sound that mirrors the soldiers’ unease.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The final stanza is devastating. Owen writes “For love of God seems dying” — faith itself is a casualty of the war. The closing image of the dead being buried with “half-known faces” dehumanises them; they are so frozen and disfigured they are barely recognisable. The repeated refrain “But nothing happens” closes the poem with crushing <dfn class="term" data-def="The feeling that something is pointless or has no purpose; a sense of wasted effort.">futility</dfn> — all this suffering achieves nothing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Charge of the Light Brigade — Alfred Lord Tennyson (1854)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Tennyson wrote this poem in response to a newspaper report about a disastrous cavalry charge during the <dfn class="term" data-def="A war (1853-1856) between Russia and an alliance including Britain, fought largely in the Crimean Peninsula.">Crimean War</dfn>. Due to a miscommunicated order, 600 British cavalrymen charged directly into Russian artillery. Tennyson both celebrates their bravery and criticises the officers’ blunder: “Someone had blundered.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The driving <dfn class="term" data-def="A metrical foot consisting of one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed syllables, creating a galloping rhythm.">dactylic</dfn> rhythm (“HALF a league, HALF a league, HALF a league ONward”) mimics the thundering hooves of the horses. Repetition of “valley of Death” and “cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them” surrounds the reader with danger, just as the soldiers were surrounded. Tennyson honours obedience (“Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die”) while simultaneously exposing the futility of following orders that lead to slaughter.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Man He Killed — Thomas Hardy (1902)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Hardy wrote this <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem in which a single speaker addresses the reader or another character, revealing their thoughts and feelings.">dramatic monologue</dfn> from the perspective of an ordinary soldier reflecting on killing an enemy during the <dfn class="term" data-def="Wars fought between Britain and the Boer republics in South Africa (1880-1881 and 1899-1902).">Boer War</dfn>. The speaker realises that in any other situation, the man he killed could have been his friend: “Had he and I but met / By some old ancient inn, / We should have sat us down to wet / Right many a nipperkin!”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The conversational tone and simple <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative poem with a regular rhyme scheme and rhythm, often telling a story of love, loss or adventure.">ballad</dfn> form (ABAB rhyme, regular metre) contrast sharply with the gravity of the subject. The dashes and hesitations in “I shot him dead because — / Because he was my foe” reveal the speaker struggling to justify his actions. The repetition of “because” sounds unconvincing, as though the speaker himself does not believe his own reasoning.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the contextual facts and explain how they shape the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hardy’s speaker reveals that both he and the enemy enlisted because they were out of work: “He thought he’d ’list, perhaps, / Off-hand like — just as I.” This highlights how war exploits the poor — men with no personal quarrel kill each other because they have no other options.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Perspective on duty:</strong> Tennyson glorifies obedience (“Theirs not to reason why”), while Owen and Hardy both question it. Owen’s soldiers endure pointless suffering; Hardy’s speaker realises the absurdity of killing a stranger for a cause he doesn’t understand.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Tone:</strong> Tennyson uses an elevated, heroic register; Owen is bleak, cold and despairing; Hardy is conversational and darkly ironic. These tonal differences shape how the reader responds emotionally to each poem.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Form:</strong> Tennyson’s galloping dactyls drive forward with energy. Owen’s half-rhymes and long stanzas create a dragging, exhausting feel. Hardy’s neat ballad stanzas feel deceptively simple, which makes the moral complexity more shocking.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Exam Focus: AO2 + AO3</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">In the Edexcel exam (Component 2, Section B Part 1), you will be given a named poem printed in the booklet and asked to compare it with one poem of your choice from the anthology. You must discuss <strong>how language and structure create meaning (AO2)</strong> and <strong>the influence of context on the poems (AO3)</strong>. For these three poems, strong AO3 points include: Owen’s first-hand trench experience, Tennyson’s response to a real news report within weeks of the event, and Hardy’s pacifist beliefs shaped by the Boer War.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22"><em>Exposure</em> uses personification, half-rhyme and the refrain “But nothing happens” to convey the futility and deadly boredom of trench warfare.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><em>The Charge of the Light Brigade</em> uses dactylic rhythm, repetition and a heroic register to celebrate courage while exposing the incompetence of commanders.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><em>The Man He Killed</em> uses a simple ballad form and hesitant dashes to reveal the absurdity of killing a man who could have been a friend.</li></ul>

A strong paragraph might say: “Owen uses personification of nature to convey the futility of trench warfare, whereas Tennyson uses a driving dactylic rhythm to honour the soldiers’ bravery during the Crimean War.”</p>
