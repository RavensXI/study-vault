<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Battles, Forty Years Apart</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Waterloo (18 June 1815) and Balaclava (25 October 1854) frame an extraordinary period of change. Waterloo was the climax of the Napoleonic Wars, fought with smoothbore muskets and disciplined squares of red-coated infantry. Balaclava was an early action of the Crimean War, fought as <dfn class="term" data-def="Spiral grooves cut inside a gun barrel so that the bullet spins, dramatically improving range and accuracy.">rifling</dfn>, the telegraph and the camera were entering the battlefield. The two battles let us trace continuity (the bravery of the British soldier, the importance of leadership) and change (long-range weapons, public scrutiny, the rise of the war reporter).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the date and British commander for both Waterloo and Balaclava without checking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Waterloo (1815) the <dfn class="term" data-def="British general; defeated Napoleon at Waterloo (18 June 1815) using infantry squares, combined arms and disciplined defence on a chosen ridge.">Duke of Wellington</dfn> defeated Napoleon using infantry squares and disciplined defence on the Mont-Saint-Jean ridge. At Balaclava (1854) <dfn class="term" data-def="British commander in the Crimea 1854&ndash;55; his vague order via Captain Nolan triggered the Charge of the Light Brigade.">Lord Raglan</dfn>&rsquo;s vague order led to the doomed Charge of the Light Brigade.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Waterloo, 18 June 1815 &mdash; Wellington&rsquo;s Defensive Masterpiece</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Wellington positioned around 68,000 British, Dutch, Belgian and German troops along the ridge of Mont-Saint-Jean, screened by walled farms (Hougoumont, La Haye Sainte and Papelotte). His plan was defensive: hold the ridge until the Prussians under Blücher could march from Wavre to attack Napoleon&rsquo;s right flank. He used reverse-slope tactics &mdash; placing infantry just behind the crest so that French artillery could not easily target them, and so that French troops could not see the British until they crossed the ridge themselves.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">When the French <dfn class="term" data-def="A close-formation of infantry, four ranks deep on each side, presenting a hedge of bayonets in every direction; the standard British defence against cavalry attack.">infantry square</dfn> was tested. Massed French cavalry attacked uphill across boggy ground; the squares held under repeated charges, the cavalry could not break the bayonet wall, and French casualties mounted as British and KGL infantry kept up controlled volley fire. Wellington committed his last reserves only when the Imperial Guard finally advanced; the Guard, until that day undefeated, was thrown back at point-blank musket range. By dusk the Prussians had arrived in force on the French right and the army of Napoleon disintegrated.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The battle showed <dfn class="term" data-def="Coordinated use of different troop types &mdash; infantry, cavalry, artillery &mdash; so that each compensates for the weaknesses of the others.">combined arms</dfn> at maturity in the smoothbore era: infantry squares to defeat cavalry, cavalry counter-charges to break enemy infantry, and artillery to soften both. Wellington&rsquo;s personal presence on the line, riding among his men under fire, mattered for morale at least as much as the formations themselves.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Balaclava, 25 October 1854 &mdash; Confusion and the Light Brigade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Forty years later British soldiers were still wearing red and obeying officers who had bought their commissions, but the battlefield itself had moved on. At Balaclava the Russians attacked the British supply port. The action produced three episodes that Britain remembers: the &lsquo;Thin Red Line&rsquo; of the 93rd Highlanders, who repelled Russian cavalry without forming square; the Charge of the Heavy Brigade, which broke a much larger Russian cavalry force; and the disastrous Charge of the Light Brigade.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The Light Brigade charge has become a case study in <dfn class="term" data-def="The control a leader has over his army (command) and how clear his orders are; at Balaclava confusion over Raglan&rsquo;s order led to the wrong objective being attacked.">command failure</dfn>. From his hilltop position Lord Raglan could see Russian infantry beginning to drag away captured British guns from the Causeway Heights. He sent Captain Nolan with a written order to recover the guns. Down on the valley floor, neither the cavalry commander Lord Lucan nor the brigade commander Lord Cardigan could see the same guns. When Lucan asked &lsquo;What guns?&rsquo;, Nolan made a sweeping gesture in the direction of the Russian artillery battery at the far end of the North Valley. The Light Brigade obeyed and rode straight at it. Nearly 700 horsemen rode into accurate Russian rifle, cannon and shell fire down a mile-long valley with batteries on three sides. Around 110 were killed and many more wounded; the brigade was effectively destroyed in twenty minutes.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">The charge is famous, but it was one symptom of a wider problem. The Crimean army was supplied by horse-drawn carts on a few unmade tracks; thousands died of cholera, exposure and starvation in the winter of 1854&ndash;55. Officers had bought their commissions and many had no recent campaign experience. Russell&rsquo;s reporting (see below) made all of this national news. The Crimean scandal forced the Cardwell reforms of 1868&ndash;74, which abolished the purchase of commissions and reorganised the army. Treat the Light Brigade as the visible peak of a structural Victorian problem, not a one-off mistake.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why Outcomes Differed: Leadership and Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Comparing the two battles makes leadership the most visible variable. Wellington wrote orders that left no room for interpretation; he supervised key sectors personally. Raglan was at fault on multiple counts: from his hilltop he saw a different battlefield from the cavalry on the valley floor, his order was ambiguous, and Captain Nolan&rsquo;s gesture made the misreading worse. The Russian commander Prince Menshikov was little better, but it was the British failures that mattered for the Light Brigade.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Technology had also moved. By 1854 the British infantry carried the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle, accurate to 600 yards. Russian field artillery was breech-loading and rifled. The Light Brigade was a mounted unit attempting to ride a mile down a valley dominated by industrial-era weapons; the same charge in 1815 against smoothbore guns at the same range would have lost far fewer men. The Crimean War foreshadowed the killing power of industrialised weapons that would dominate the second half of the nineteenth century.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">A New Public: Russell, Fenton and the Reading Nation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">What made Balaclava different from any earlier British battle was that the public knew. <dfn class="term" data-def="Anglo-Irish journalist (1820&ndash;1907) who reported for The Times from the Crimea, exposing supply failures and battlefield conditions; widely regarded as the first modern war correspondent.">William Howard Russell</dfn> filed dispatches for The Times via the new electric telegraph, often within days of events. His report on the Light Brigade (&lsquo;some hideous blunder&rsquo;) reached London while the wounded were still being treated. The photographer Roger Fenton was sent out under government sponsorship in 1855 to take posed photographs of officers and camp life; technical limits meant he could not photograph battle, but his images circulated as engravings in newspapers. Within months, ordinary middle-class Britons were reading and looking at war as an ongoing news event &mdash; not a finished story.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The result was a permanent change in the relationship between army and public. Florence Nightingale&rsquo;s nursing mission was launched in direct response to Russell&rsquo;s reporting on hospital conditions at Scutari. Parliament debated supply failures while the war was still being fought. By the Boer War of 1899&ndash;1902, war reporting was an established profession, photographs accompanied articles and concentration-camp policy became a national scandal. The press did not just describe wars: it began to shape them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences how Russell&rsquo;s telegraphed reports changed how Victorians thought about the army.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Crimean War was the first British war to be reported by telegraph and photographed for publication. William Howard Russell of The Times exposed supply failures and battlefield blunders within days; this triggered Florence Nightingale&rsquo;s nursing mission and, eventually, the Cardwell reforms.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">1850s wet-plate photography needed exposures of several seconds and a portable darkroom in a horse-drawn van &mdash; useless for action shots. Fenton&rsquo;s images therefore show camp life, parked artillery, and his own &lsquo;Valley of the Shadow of Death&rsquo; (a Crimean ravine littered with cannonballs). The photographs framed war as a managed, dignified British project &mdash; arguably a kind of early <dfn class="term" data-def="Information &mdash; words, images or film &mdash; designed to influence opinion or behaviour, often emphasising one side and selecting which facts to show.">photographic reportage</dfn> shaped by official sponsorship. They are still useful as evidence, but examiners reward students who can weigh provenance (a government-sponsored photographer in 1855) against content.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">At Waterloo (1815) Wellington won by combining the reverse-slope ridge, infantry squares, walled farms and the late arrival of the Prussians under Blücher.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">At Balaclava (1854) Lord Raglan&rsquo;s ambiguous order and Captain Nolan&rsquo;s gesture sent the Light Brigade up the wrong valley into Russian artillery, destroying the brigade in twenty minutes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The Crimean War was the first reported by telegraph and photographed for publication &mdash; Russell of The Times and Roger Fenton turned battlefields into ongoing public news, reshaping Victorian attitudes to the army.</li>
</ul>
