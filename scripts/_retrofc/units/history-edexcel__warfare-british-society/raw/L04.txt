--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Longbows to Gunpowder: The Early Modern Battlefield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between c1500 and c1700, English warfare changed shape almost completely. The longbowman who had decided Agincourt disappeared from the field. In his place came the <dfn class="term" data-def="A foot soldier armed with a long heavy musket fired with a slow-burning match cord; effective at about 50 yards but slow to reload.">musketeer</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A foot soldier armed with a long pike (around 16&ndash;18 feet) used to defend musketeers from cavalry charges.">pikeman</dfn>, fighting in linked blocks known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The combined formation of musketeers and pikemen used in early modern Europe; the musketeers fired and reloaded under the protection of the pike block.">pike-and-shot</dfn>. By the 1640s, kings and Parliaments had also begun to keep small bodies of paid soldiers in arms permanently &mdash; the first English moves towards a <dfn class="term" data-def="A permanent, professionally trained and paid army kept in service in peacetime as well as wartime.">standing army</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The reasons were technological as much as political. Slow but heavy <dfn class="term" data-def="The early gunpowder firing mechanism in which a slow-burning match cord was lowered onto the powder pan when the trigger was pressed.">matchlock</dfn> firearms could punch through the plate armour that had once protected the mounted knight. Cannon, now reliable enough to bring onto the open field, broke up squares of foot soldiers from a distance. Fortifications had to be rebuilt as low, angled bastions to deflect shot rather than tall stone walls to repel ladders. By the end of the seventeenth century, war was fought by drilled blocks of paid soldiers &mdash; not by feudal hosts.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four main troop types of an English early modern army around 1645.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The early modern army was made up of musketeers (handgun firepower), pikemen (cavalry protection), <dfn class="term" data-def="A mounted infantryman of the seventeenth century who travelled by horse but normally fought on foot with a musket.">dragoons</dfn> (mounted infantry) and cavalry. They worked together in pike-and-shot formations supported by cannon.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Pike-and-Shot: How the Formation Worked</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A matchlock musket of 1640 took perhaps a minute to reload. While reloading, the musketeer was defenceless against any horseman who reached him. The answer was the pikeman: armed with a 16&ndash;18 foot pike, he stood in tight blocks beside the musketeers, ready to receive a cavalry charge as a hedgehog of points. Musketeers fired in volleys, then retired through gaps in the pike block to reload while a fresh rank stepped forward.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">By 1645 a typical infantry regiment had two musketeers for every pikeman; this ratio would shift further towards muskets as the bayonet was introduced (after this period). Cavalry shifted from heavy lance-and-armour knights towards <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavy cavalry trooper of the 1640s, armed with a sword and pistols and protected by a back-and-breastplate; both Royalists and Parliamentarians used them.">cuirassiers</dfn> in three-quarter armour, armed with sword and pistols. Dragoons rode to the battlefield and dismounted to fight on foot &mdash; an early answer to the speed problem that would later become standard in European armies.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">A Standing Army Begins to Emerge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">For most of this period, English kings still raised armies the medieval way &mdash; through indentures, county trained bands and (when desperate) impressed levies. Henry VIII fielded large but temporary armies on French expeditions; Elizabeth I depended on the trained bands of each county for home defence. But two pressures pulled towards permanence. The first was the new technology &mdash; pikemen and musketeers needed long, drilled training to work together; you could not throw a peasant a musket and expect him to fire in volley. The second was the cost of fortifications and warships, which only a permanent administration could maintain.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">After the Restoration of 1660, Charles II kept a small body of paid soldiers in arms &mdash; the original household regiments of the British army (the Coldstream Guards trace their unbroken lineage to the New Model Army through this transition). By 1700 the principle of a permanent professional army was established, even if Parliament still distrusted it and required an annual Mutiny Act to keep it legal.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Recruitment and Civilian Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Recruitment in this period was a tangle of methods. The county trained bands continued, with each shire required to keep a body of armed men ready for muster. Volunteers were attracted by signing-on bounties. <dfn class="term" data-def="The compulsory enlistment of men into the navy or army, sometimes by force; survived in the navy until the early nineteenth century.">Impressment</dfn> &mdash; the forced enlistment of vagrants, debtors and the unwilling &mdash; supplied the rest. The English Civil War (1642&ndash;1651) saw both sides relying on volunteers at first and then on local conscription; Royalist commissions of array and Parliamentary militia ordinances summoned men in the thousands.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Civilians paid heavily. The right of <dfn class="term" data-def="The right of soldiers to take board, lodging and food from civilians without paying immediately, leaving promises (often unhonoured) for later settlement.">free quarter</dfn> was widely used by both sides &mdash; soldiers ate at the householder&rsquo;s table, slept in his rooms and gave nothing in return except a paper IOU that might never be redeemed. Towns were sacked when they refused to surrender; armies on the march took horses, carts, fodder and food. Whole regions of the West Country and Yorkshire suffered repeated quarterings, and many smaller towns never fully recovered until after 1660.</p>

What changed in 1914 was the machine gun, the high-explosive shell and the railway, not the digging. This is a useful continuity to flag in the Western Front lesson later in the course.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The New Model Army &mdash; A Different Kind of Force</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Parliamentary army established in 1645 under Sir Thomas Fairfax; the first English national, paid, professionally drilled army.">New Model Army</dfn> was created by Parliament in February 1645 to break the deadlock of the Civil War. Its design was radical for England: a single national army of 22,000 men, paid regularly, drilled to a uniform standard, and led by officers chosen for ability rather than inherited rank. The Self-Denying Ordinance forced most aristocratic commanders to resign; <dfn class="term" data-def="Lord General of the New Model Army from 1645; commander-in-chief at Naseby and chief organiser of its drill and pay.">Sir Thomas Fairfax</dfn> became Lord General, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Cavalry commander of the New Model Army; led the &lsquo;Ironsides&rsquo; at Marston Moor and Naseby; later Lord Protector of the Commonwealth.">Oliver Cromwell</dfn> commanded the cavalry.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Cromwell&rsquo;s cavalry, nicknamed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Cromwell&rsquo;s heavy cavalry of the New Model Army, drilled to charge tightly and wheel back into formation rather than scatter chasing fugitives.">Ironsides</dfn>, were drilled in a way Royalist horse were not: after charging an enemy formation, they rallied immediately and turned to charge again rather than chase fugitives off the field. Foot regiments practised <dfn class="term" data-def="Coordinated movements (loading, firing, advancing, wheeling) practised together so that a unit could perform them under battle stress.">regimental drill</dfn> until the manoeuvres &mdash; loading, firing, advancing, retiring &mdash; were almost automatic. Pay was steady (when Parliament could find the money) and supply was organised through a national administration in London. The New Model was, in effect, England&rsquo;s first standing army &mdash; even before the term was widely used.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Naseby, 14 June 1645</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Three months after its creation, the New Model Army met the Royalist field army at Naseby in Northamptonshire. Charles I commanded around 7,500 men; Fairfax and Cromwell had roughly 14,000. The Royalist horse charged on the right wing under Prince Rupert and broke the Parliamentary cavalry &mdash; but instead of regrouping, Rupert&rsquo;s cavalry rode off the field to plunder the Parliamentary baggage train.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">On the other wing, Cromwell&rsquo;s Ironsides defeated the Royalist horse and then turned in disciplined formation to attack the Royalist infantry from the flank. The Royalist foot, surrounded and outnumbered, broke. Charles I&rsquo;s field army was effectively destroyed in an afternoon. Naseby ended Royalist hopes of winning the war by force; the king surrendered in 1646.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Royalist cavalry under Rupert were brave, well-mounted and good at charging. They were not good at re-forming. After breaking the enemy, they tended to chase fugitives off the field for plunder. Cromwell drilled the Ironsides specifically not to do this: after a charge, trumpeters sounded the recall and squadrons reformed under their officers within minutes. At Naseby this discipline let Cromwell turn his cavalry inwards onto the Royalist infantry while Rupert was still away looting. The result was a textbook example of what drill and command could achieve.</p>

  <p>The New Model Army (1645) was a national, paid, professionally drilled army of 22,000 men, led by officers chosen on ability. At Naseby it destroyed the Royalist field army and ended Charles I&rsquo;s hopes of winning the Civil War.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">By 1700 the English army was a pike-and-shot force of musketeers, pikemen, dragoons and cavalry &mdash; replacing the medieval longbowman and the mounted knight as gunpowder weapons squeezed both off the battlefield.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Slow matchlock muskets needed pikemen to protect them from cavalry; armies were drilled in tight pike-and-shot formations until the bayonet later combined the two roles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">The New Model Army (1645) was paid, drilled and led by ability; under Fairfax and Cromwell it destroyed the Royalist field army at Naseby and laid the foundations of a permanent standing army after 1660.</li>
</ul>
