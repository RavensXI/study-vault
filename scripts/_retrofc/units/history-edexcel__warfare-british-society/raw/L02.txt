--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Edward I crushed Wallace at Falkirk and how Henry V’s archers triumphed at Agincourt.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Battles, One Argument</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Falkirk (1298) and Agincourt (1415) sit at either end of the medieval period and tell the same story from two angles. At Falkirk an English king crushed a Scottish foot army by combining knights and archers. At Agincourt an English king defeated a French knightly army by relying on archers and dismounted men-at-arms. Together they show why the longbow archer became the deciding figure on the medieval battlefield, and why <dfn class="term" data-def="The coordinated use of different troop types &mdash; cavalry, infantry, archers, artillery &mdash; so that each compensates for the weaknesses of the others.">combined arms</dfn> was already a real idea long before the modern era.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the date and key commander for both Falkirk and Agincourt without checking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Falkirk (1298) Edward I defeated William Wallace by using longbowmen to break the Scottish schiltrons. At Agincourt (1415) Henry V used longbowmen and dismounted men-at-arms to destroy a much larger French army.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Falkirk, 22 July 1298 &mdash; The First Time the Longbow Decided a Battle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Scottish commander who led the Scots to victory at Stirling Bridge (1297) and was defeated at Falkirk in 1298 by Edward I.">William Wallace</dfn> had won at Stirling Bridge a year earlier by trapping an English army on a narrow crossing. Now <dfn class="term" data-def="King of England 1272&ndash;1307; reformed the English army, used longbows at Falkirk and is sometimes called the &lsquo;Hammer of the Scots&rsquo;.">Edward I</dfn> was determined to fight on his own terms. He brought a large army northwards: heavy cavalry, men-at-arms, and crucially around two thousand Welsh and English longbowmen.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Wallace formed his foot soldiers into four <em>schiltrons</em> &mdash; tightly packed circles of pikemen presenting outward-pointing 12-foot spears. Cavalry could not ride into them. Edward&rsquo;s knights charged anyway and were repulsed. Then Edward did something new: he pulled the cavalry back and ordered his longbowmen forward. The arrows tore the schiltrons apart from a safe distance. Once the rings broke, the cavalry rode through the gaps and the Scottish foot army was massacred. Wallace escaped, but his military reputation was destroyed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Falkirk mattered because of <em>field of fire</em>. The schiltron was effective only as long as the enemy stayed in front of pikepoints. As soon as ranged weapons could safely shoot from outside spear-reach, the formation became a death trap. Edward I had stumbled onto a basic principle of all later medieval warfare: combined arms beats any single troop type.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Agincourt, 25 October 1415 &mdash; The Lesson Repeated</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A century later <dfn class="term" data-def="King of England 1413&ndash;1422; commander at Agincourt (1415); his decisions on terrain, archers and dismounting men-at-arms are central to the English victory.">Henry V</dfn> faced the same problem in reverse. He had landed in Normandy with around 12,000 men but his army was reduced by dysentery and the long march; by the time he reached Agincourt in Picardy he had perhaps 6,000 fighting men. Most of them were longbowmen. The French had assembled a far larger army &mdash; estimates range from 12,000 to 25,000 &mdash; including thousands of mounted knights eager to wipe out the English invader.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Henry chose his ground carefully. He fought in a narrow funnel of recently ploughed field between two woods, soaked by autumn rain. The French could not deploy their numbers; mud trapped horses and armoured men. The English longbowmen drove sharpened wooden stakes into the ground in front of their positions. The dismounted English men-at-arms stood between the archers as a steady, slow-moving line.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">When the French knights charged, the longbowmen released arrows in massed flights. Horses fell in the mud. Knights pressed forward on foot through ploughed earth in 60 lb of plate armour. The first French line crashed into the English line exhausted. Behind them, more French knights kept arriving and trampling the wounded. By the end of the day the French had lost thousands of nobles; English casualties were a few hundred. The longbow had again decided a battle &mdash; but only because Henry had picked the field, the timing and the formation.</p>

Modern scholars (Anne Curry of Southampton, the Royal Armouries, Juliet Barker) emphasise the muddy ground, the funnel terrain, French command failures and Henry V&rsquo;s decisions on dismounting and stake-driving. The longbow was crucial &mdash; but logistics, leadership and luck of weather all sat alongside it. <h2 data-narration-id="n13">Logistics, Command and Morale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Both battles also turned on <dfn class="term" data-def="The supply of soldiers with food, weapons, transport and shelter; can decide a campaign as much as the fighting itself.">logistics</dfn>. Edward I had marched a large, well-fed army into Scotland on prepared roads with supply trains. Wallace, by contrast, had been shadowing the English to deny them food, and his men were tiring. Henry V&rsquo;s army had eaten badly for weeks and many were sick &mdash; but the French were also disorganised, with no overall commander, multiple grand lords competing for the right to fight in the front rank.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The control a leader has over his army (command) and the willingness of soldiers to fight on (morale); both shaped medieval battle outcomes as much as weapons.">Command and morale</dfn> sealed both outcomes. Wallace&rsquo;s army began Falkirk demoralised after a year of evading battle, while Edward I had personally led his men since dawn. Henry V was famous for walking among his archers the night before Agincourt; Shakespeare invented many of the details, but the underlying point &mdash; that an English king physically present with his men was a force-multiplier &mdash; is real. The French at Agincourt had no such single voice: orders were ignored, ranks were jumbled and the rear pushed the front into the mud.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">A schiltron worked by packing men shoulder to shoulder so that the spears formed a solid wall. To protect every direction the men had to stay in close formation &mdash; they could not fan out without losing the wall of points. Once English longbowmen began shooting from outside spear-reach, every Scottish soldier was a stationary target. Loosening the formation meant losing the protection from cavalry; staying tight meant being shot down. Falkirk shows the basic problem of any single-weapon system: it has to be supported by something else, or it can be cancelled by ranged fire.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences how Henry V used terrain at Agincourt to make the most of his archers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both battles show combined arms in action: Edward I used longbows to break the Scottish schiltrons before unleashing his cavalry, and Henry V used a narrow muddy funnel and sharpened stakes to channel French knights into massed arrow fire.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">At Falkirk (1298) Edward I&rsquo;s longbowmen broke the Scottish schiltrons before his cavalry charged through the gaps &mdash; the first time the longbow decided a major battle.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">At Agincourt (1415) Henry V used narrow funnel terrain, deep mud, sharpened stakes and dismounted men-at-arms to turn massed longbow fire into a crushing victory over a much larger French army.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Neither outcome was the longbow alone &mdash; combined arms, command, terrain and logistics all mattered. Examiners reward students who weigh several factors against each other.</li>
</ul>
