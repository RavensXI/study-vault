--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the feudal host, the longbow archer and gunpowder reshaped English medieval warfare.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Army Built on Land and Loyalty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between c1250 and c1500 the English army was not a paid, full-time force. It was a <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval army raised when a king summoned his lords; lords brought knights and foot soldiers in return for land held under feudalism.">feudal host</dfn> &mdash; a temporary army summoned by the king from his lords, who in turn brought their tenants. Society and command were one and the same: the king led the great lords, the lords led the knights, and the knights led the foot soldiers from their estates. <dfn class="term" data-def="The duty of a vassal to fight (or supply armed men) for his lord in return for land and protection.">Knight service</dfn> meant that a lord who held land from the king owed roughly 40 days of military service each year &mdash; on horseback, fully armed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">A medieval English army therefore had three layers. At the top sat the <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavily armoured horseman from the noble class, central to medieval warfare until the rise of the longbow and gunpowder.">mounted knight</dfn>, the most expensive and prestigious soldier on the field. Below him stood the foot infantry &mdash; spearmen and men-at-arms drawn from yeomen and tenants. And from the late thirteenth century, archers became the third pillar, as kings realised that ranged firepower could decide a battle before the cavalry charged.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three components of a medieval English army and what tied them to the king.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A medieval English army was raised through feudal duty: the king summoned lords, who brought knights, who brought their tenants. The three main components were the mounted knight, the infantry and the archer.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Mounted Knight &mdash; and Why He Started to Decline</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A fully equipped knight cost as much as a small village. He needed mail armour (later plate), a war horse, lance, sword and a long apprenticeship. On the battlefield he charged shoulder-to-shoulder, hoping to break the enemy line by sheer weight and shock. Against unprepared infantry this was devastating. Against well-organised foot soldiers with the right weapons, it was suicidal.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">At Falkirk in 1298, Scottish foot soldiers formed <dfn class="term" data-def="A tight Scottish circle or ring of foot soldiers presenting outward-pointing pikes; difficult for cavalry to break but vulnerable to massed missile fire.">schiltrons</dfn> &mdash; tight rings of pike-points like a hedgehog &mdash; and English knights could not break them. The ring was eventually destroyed not by horsemen but by English longbowmen who shot the schiltrons apart from a safe distance. The same pattern was repeated at Cr&eacute;cy (1346), Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415): mounted French knights charged across muddy ground into a wall of arrows. The mounted knight remained socially dominant, but militarily he was already in retreat.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Longbow Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A six-foot bow drawing around 100&ndash;150 lb, capable of shooting heavy arrows up to 250 yards; the dominant English missile weapon c1300&ndash;c1500.">longbow</dfn> was around six feet tall and required a draw weight of 100&ndash;150 lb &mdash; far heavier than a modern hunting bow. A trained archer could shoot ten or twelve arrows a minute and reach 250 yards. Edward III made longbow practice compulsory by law, and Sunday archery became a legal obligation in every village. By Agincourt (1415), perhaps 80% of an English army was longbowmen.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The longbow was decisive because it was cheap, fast and used by men of low status. A peasant with two pence worth of yew and a quiver could kill a knight worth two hundred pounds. This challenged the social logic of the feudal host, where battlefield power was supposed to mirror landowning rank. Wars now turned on what large numbers of trained commoners could do.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Schiltrons, Pikes and Other Foot Formations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The schiltron was a Scottish answer to mounted knights, but it was not the only foot formation that mattered. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavily armoured foot or mounted soldier of knightly rank, often dismounted to fight on foot in late-medieval English armies.">men-at-arms</dfn> &mdash; the heavily armoured swordsmen of knightly rank &mdash; increasingly fought dismounted. At Agincourt the English knights got off their horses, drove sharpened stakes into the ground and stood with the archers. The horseback charge was becoming an option, not the default.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Gunpowder Arrives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">From the early fourteenth century, English armies used <dfn class="term" data-def="An explosive mixture of saltpetre, charcoal and sulphur; arrived in England via Asia and Islamic Spain in the early fourteenth century.">gunpowder</dfn> in primitive cannon. Edward III deployed small bombards at Cr&eacute;cy in 1346. By the fifteenth century, large siege guns were knocking down castle walls that had stood for centuries. At the siege of Constantinople (1453), Ottoman cannon ended the medieval city defence. In England, Henry VIII&rsquo;s gun foundries laid the basis of artillery that would change war for good in the next century.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Cannon did not yet dominate the open battlefield &mdash; they were slow, heavy and unreliable in 1400. But they ended the supremacy of the high stone wall, which had defined siege warfare for a thousand years. Combined with the longbow, gunpowder squeezed the mounted knight from two sides: cheap missile troops broke up cavalry charges, and cannon broke the castle that had been the knight&rsquo;s refuge.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">Students writing about the Western Front sometimes call trench warfare an invention of the twentieth century. The basic ideas &mdash; ditches, earthworks, layered defensive lines &mdash; are far older. Castle ditches, the prepared ground at Agincourt, and later the field fortifications of the New Model Army all use the same logic. What changed in 1914 was the machine gun, the artillery shell and the railway, not the digging. Across this whole unit you should look for continuity in tactics even when weapons change.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1500 the longbow had broken the mounted knight on the battlefield, and gunpowder cannon had begun to break the castle walls he sheltered behind. Both weapons let trained commoners challenge a noble warrior class.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Medieval English armies were feudal hosts: the king summoned lords, who brought knights, who brought their tenants. Command and society were the same hierarchy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The longbow gave trained commoners battle-deciding firepower &mdash; up to 250 yards, ten arrows a minute &mdash; and broke the dominance of the mounted knight at Cr&eacute;cy and Agincourt.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Gunpowder cannon arrived in the early fourteenth century and, by 1500, had ended the supremacy of the high stone wall that had defined siege warfare for centuries.</li>
</ul>
