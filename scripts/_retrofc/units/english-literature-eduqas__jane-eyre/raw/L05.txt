--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s time at Moor House, St John Rivers’s proposal, and the choice between duty and love.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Moor House: Recovery and Independence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After leaving Thornfield, Jane wanders the moors starving and desperate until she collapses at the door of Moor House, home of the Rivers siblings: St John, Diana, and Mary. They take her in, and Jane slowly recovers. Moor House represents a middle ground between Thornfield’s passionate intensity and Lowood’s institutional coldness. Diana and Mary become genuine friends — intellectual, warm, and independent. For the first time, Jane has female companionship based on equality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jane takes a position as a village schoolteacher, a role that gives her financial independence and social purpose. She discovers that she is related to the Rivers family (they share an uncle) and inherits £20,000 — a fortune that transforms her social position entirely. Crucially, Jane divides the inheritance equally among all four cousins. This generosity is not sentimental — it is principled. Jane values family and equality more than wealth. The inheritance also ensures that when she eventually returns to Rochester, she comes as his financial equal, not his dependent.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why Jane divides her inheritance and what this reveals about her values.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane inherits £20,000 and divides it equally among the four Rivers cousins. This act proves that Jane values relationships over money and ensures she returns to Rochester as his equal. The inheritance is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative device where unlikely events occur to resolve problems or advance the plot.">plot device</dfn> that removes the class barrier between Jane and Rochester, but Jane’s choice to share it reveals her moral character.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">St John Rivers: Duty Without Love</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">St John Rivers is handsome, intelligent, and deeply religious — but he is also cold, controlling, and emotionally repressed. He plans to become a missionary in India and asks Jane to accompany him as his wife. His proposal is the mirror opposite of Rochester’s: where Rochester offered love without legal marriage, St John offers legal marriage without love. He tells Jane: “God and nature intended you for a missionary’s wife.” Jane recognises this as a different kind of imprisonment — a marriage of duty that would kill her spirit as surely as becoming Rochester’s mistress would have compromised her integrity.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">St John and Rochester represent opposite extremes. Rochester is all passion: reckless, deceptive, burning with feeling. St John is all duty: disciplined, honest, but emotionally frozen. He loves Rosamond Oliver but suppresses his feelings because she is not “suitable” for missionary work. St John’s self-denial is presented not as virtue but as a form of violence against his own nature — and against Jane’s. He asks Jane to sacrifice her capacity for love on the altar of religious duty.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Jane almost yields. St John’s will is powerful, and his religious conviction gives his arguments moral authority. Jane feels herself being “gathered under a spell” by his influence. It takes a supernatural intervention — she hears Rochester’s voice calling her name across the moors — to break the spell. Whether this is literal telepathy, divine guidance, or Jane’s own subconscious reasserting itself, Brontë leaves ambiguous. The point is that Jane’s passionate nature <em>will not</em> be suppressed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Supernatural Voice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jane hears Rochester’s voice crying “Jane! Jane! Jane!” across the moors just as she is about to accept St John’s proposal. She responds: “I am coming! Wait for me!” This moment is the novel’s most explicitly <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that cannot be explained by natural laws or science, suggesting a force beyond ordinary experience.">supernatural</dfn> event (other than the red room ghost). It can be read as genuine telepathy, divine intervention, or a psychological projection of Jane’s deepest desire. In all three readings, it functions as the voice of passion overruling the voice of duty — Jane’s heart refusing to be silenced by St John’s logic.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jane’s Rejection of St John</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Jane tells St John she will go to India as his “sister” but not his wife. She recognises that a loveless marriage would be a form of death: “If I join St John, I abandon half myself.” This rejection mirrors her departure from Rochester — in both cases, she refuses a relationship that would require her to surrender part of her identity. The novel argues that Jane must find a relationship that satisfies both passion <em>and</em> principle, both heart and conscience.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">St John’s final portrayal is complex. Brontë gives him the novel’s last lines: he dies as a missionary, “sure of his reward.” His devotion is genuine, and Brontë respects it even while showing its human cost. St John represents a path Jane could have taken — and her rejection of it confirms that the novel values emotional wholeness over spiritual self-sacrifice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why Jane refuses both Rochester’s and St John’s proposals, and what both refusals have in common.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane refuses Rochester (love without legal marriage) and St John (legal marriage without love) for the same reason: both would require her to surrender half her identity. Brontë structures these two proposals as mirrors, arguing that a fulfilling life requires both passion and principle in equal measure.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Jane’s inheritance and her decision to share it ensure she returns to Rochester as his equal — the class barrier is removed by her generosity, not by compromise.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">St John offers duty without love, mirroring Rochester’s offer of love without legality — Jane refuses both because both require her to abandon half of herself.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The supernatural voice calling Jane back to Rochester represents passion overruling duty, confirming that the novel values emotional wholeness alongside moral integrity.</li></ul>

Show how Jane’s refusal of St John parallels her departure from Thornfield, proving that her moral standards are consistent. This structural awareness impresses examiners.</p>
