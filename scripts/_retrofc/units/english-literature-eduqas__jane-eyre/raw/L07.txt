Character analysis of Jane, Rochester, Bertha, and the supporting cast with key quotations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jane Eyre: Passion and Principle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane is one of the most revolutionary <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character whose internal journey drives the narrative.">protagonists</dfn> in Victorian literature. She is plain, poor, and powerless — yet she refuses to accept any of these as reasons for submission. Her defining quality is the combination of fierce passion with unwavering moral integrity. She will not suppress her feelings (she tells Rochester exactly what she thinks), but she will not compromise her principles (she leaves him rather than become his mistress).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Key quotations define her arc: “Unjust! — unjust!” (childhood defiance), “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me” (demand for freedom), “Do you think, because I am poor… I am soulless?” (demand for equality), “I care for myself” (moral integrity), “Reader, I married him” (agency). Each marks a step in her journey from powerless child to self-determined woman.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall five key Jane quotations that trace her character arc from childhood to marriage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane’s character arc is traced through five key quotations: “Unjust!” (childhood rebellion), “I am no bird” (freedom), “I am soulless and heartless?” (equality), “I care for myself” (integrity), “Reader, I married him” (agency). Memorise all five and practise linking each to a theme for the exam.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Rochester: The Byronic Hero</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Rochester is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of antihero characterised by intelligence, passion, moodiness, and a troubled or secretive past.">Byronic hero</dfn>: dark, brooding, intelligent, flawed, and magnetically attractive. He is not conventionally handsome (“broad and jetty eyebrows… grim mouth, chin, and jaw”), but his intensity draws Jane to him. His flaw is deception: he hides Bertha, manipulates Jane with Blanche, and attempts a bigamous marriage. Yet his love for Jane is genuine, and his suffering after the fire is real.</p>

    <span>Bertha Mason: Madwoman or Victim?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Bertha is described in animalistic terms: she “grovelled, seemingly, on all fours; it snatched and growled like some strange wild animal.” Brontë’s language denies Bertha humanity, reducing her to a creature. Rochester calls her “my Indian Messalina” — a reference to a notoriously dissolute Roman empress, combined with a <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the European colonisation of India and other countries, with implications of racial assumptions.">colonial</dfn> label that links her madness to her Creole origins.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Modern readings challenge this presentation. Bertha was married off by her family, transported to England, and locked in an attic for years. Her “madness” may be the result of isolation and powerlessness. She can also be read as Jane’s doppelgänger — the dark mirror of female passion that Victorian society refused to acknowledge. In the exam, acknowledging this complexity shows sophisticated critical awareness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Supporting Cast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Mrs Reed</strong> represents the cruelty of those who abuse power over the defenceless. She dies still resenting Jane’s spirit. <strong>Helen Burns</strong> represents passive Christian endurance — noble but incomplete. <strong>St John Rivers</strong> represents religious duty without human warmth — a mirror of Rochester’s excess of passion. <strong>Diana and Mary Rivers</strong> represent the female friendship and intellectual equality Jane craves.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Adèle, Mrs Fairfax, and the Minor Characters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Adèle Varens</strong> is Rochester’s ward (possibly his illegitimate daughter). She represents innocence and Jane’s capacity for nurturing care. <strong>Mrs Fairfax</strong> is the kind but conventional housekeeper who warns Jane about the social gulf between her and Rochester: “Gentlemen in his station are not accustomed to marry their governesses.” Her concern is practical, not cruel — she understands the social reality that Jane’s passion overlooks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Mr Brocklehurst</strong> is the hypocritical clergyman who runs Lowood. He embodies the abuse of religious authority: he starves and humiliates the girls while his own family lives in luxury. He is Brontë’s sharpest critique of institutional religion and one of the novel’s most effective <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who represents a single quality or idea, often used for satirical effect.">caricatures</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and name five characters and explain what each represents thematically.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brontë’s characters represent different responses to the same central tension between passion and duty: Jane (balanced synthesis), Rochester (excessive passion), St John (excessive duty), Helen (passive faith), Bertha (passion denied any outlet). Understanding what each character represents thematically is more valuable than simply describing their actions.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Jane’s character arc is traced through five key quotations, each marking a stage from powerless child to self-determined woman.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Rochester is a Byronic hero whose genuine love is compromised by deception — he must be humbled before he can achieve honest partnership with Jane.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Bertha Mason can be read as a madwoman, a victim of colonial and patriarchal oppression, or Jane’s doppelgänger — acknowledging these interpretations shows critical sophistication.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n14">For character analysis in the exam, always connect the character to a theme. Rochester is not just ‘flawed’ — he represents the danger of passion without honesty. St John is not just ‘cold’ — he represents duty that destroys human feeling. This thematic linkage turns description into analysis and pushes your response into the higher bands.</p>
