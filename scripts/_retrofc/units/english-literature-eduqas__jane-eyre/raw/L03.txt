--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Thornfield Hall: A Gothic Setting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane arrives at Thornfield Hall as a governess to Adèle Varens, Rochester’s young ward. The house itself is a character: a grand, rambling mansion with dark corridors, locked doors, and a third storey from which Jane hears “a distinct, formal, mirthless laugh.” Mrs Fairfax, the housekeeper, attributes the sound to a servant called Grace Poole, but the explanation never quite satisfies. Brontë uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A hint or suggestion of what will happen later in the narrative, building suspense and tension.">foreshadowing</dfn> to create an atmosphere of concealed danger: something is hidden at Thornfield, and the reader feels it before the truth is revealed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For Jane, Thornfield represents both freedom and confinement. She has escaped Lowood’s institutional cruelty, but she is still a dependent — paid, housed, and ultimately controlled by her employer. Her famous speech from the rooftop captures this frustration: “Women feel just as men feel; they need exercise for their faculties, and a field for their efforts.” This is Brontë’s most explicitly <dfn class="term" data-def="The advocacy of women’s rights on the grounds of equality between the sexes.">feminist</dfn> statement, embedded in a passage where Jane paces the third floor — directly above the room where Bertha is imprisoned.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write Jane’s rooftop speech from memory, then explain its feminist significance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane’s rooftop declaration — “Women feel just as men feel; they need exercise for their faculties, and a field for their efforts” — is a direct challenge to the Victorian belief that women should be content with domesticity. The fact that she makes this speech while pacing above Bertha’s prison creates a powerful structural irony: Jane articulates the desire for freedom while another woman is literally caged beneath her feet.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Meeting Rochester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane meets Rochester in Chapter 12 when his horse slips on ice and she helps him. The meeting is unconventional: neither knows who the other is, and Jane assists Rochester as an equal rather than a servant. Rochester is immediately unlike any man Jane has known. He is blunt, moody, sardonic, and intellectually challenging. He treats Jane not as a governess but as a companion — someone whose mind he respects. Their dialogue crackles with energy: “Do you think me handsome?” he asks, and Jane replies: “No, sir.” This directness delights him.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Power Imbalance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Rochester is Jane’s employer, her social superior, and significantly older. The power imbalance is real and Brontë does not ignore it. Rochester uses his authority to control situations: he invites high-society guests to Thornfield partly to make Jane jealous, and he disguises himself as a fortune-teller to extract her feelings. These are <dfn class="term" data-def="Behaviour intended to influence or control others, often through indirect or deceptive means.">manipulative</dfn> tactics, and Brontë presents them as flaws in Rochester’s character, not romantic gestures. Jane calls him out: “Do you think I am an automaton? — a machine without feelings?”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The tension between Rochester’s genuine love for Jane and his willingness to deceive her (about Bertha, about his past, about Blanche Ingram) is central to the novel. Brontë asks: can love be real if it is built on unequal knowledge? Jane’s answer, when she discovers the truth about Bertha, is no. She leaves Thornfield not because she stops loving Rochester but because she refuses to compromise her moral integrity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Mystery of Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Strange events accumulate: the “mirthless laugh,” a fire in Rochester’s bedroom (Jane saves his life), a violent attack on the mysterious visitor Mason. Each incident is attributed to Grace Poole, but the explanations grow increasingly thin. Brontë uses these Gothic <dfn class="term" data-def="Situations where the audience knows more than the character, or where appearances conceal a hidden truth.">mysteries</dfn> to build suspense while also developing the metaphorical connection between Jane and the hidden woman in the attic. Both are passionate women trapped by social constraints; both love Rochester; both are, in different ways, imprisoned by Thornfield.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Blanche Ingram: Jane’s Rival</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Rochester invites Blanche Ingram and other aristocratic guests to Thornfield, and Jane suffers watching their flirtation. Blanche is everything Jane is not: beautiful, wealthy, accomplished, and socially confident. But Brontë carefully shows that Blanche is also shallow, cruel (she mocks governesses), and mercenary (she loses interest when she hears Rochester may not be rich). Jane draws a portrait of herself and Blanche to discipline her feelings, titling them “Portrait of a Governess” and “Blanche, an accomplished lady of rank.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Blanche functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who contrasts with another to highlight particular qualities.">foil</dfn> to Jane: outwardly superior but inwardly empty. Rochester eventually admits he used Blanche to provoke Jane’s jealousy, which is morally questionable. Brontë does not excuse Rochester’s behaviour — she makes Jane strong enough to challenge it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain two ways Rochester treats Jane as an equal and two ways he maintains power over her.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rochester’s relationship with Jane is built on genuine intellectual respect (“the liveliness of your mind” mirrors Darcy’s attraction to Elizabeth), but also on power imbalance and deception. He hides Bertha’s existence, manipulates Jane with Blanche, and disguises himself as a fortune-teller. Brontë presents love as requiring both passion and honesty — and Rochester fails the honesty test until Jane forces him to change.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Jane’s rooftop speech directly challenges Victorian gender expectations, and its placement above Bertha’s prison creates structural irony about women’s confinement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Rochester treats Jane as an intellectual equal but also manipulates her through deception and power imbalance — Brontë presents both the attraction and the danger of their relationship.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Gothic mysteries at Thornfield (the laugh, the fire, the attack on Mason) build suspense while developing the metaphorical connection between Jane and the imprisoned Bertha.</li></ul>
