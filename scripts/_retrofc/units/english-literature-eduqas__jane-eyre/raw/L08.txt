--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme: Independence and Self-Respect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel’s central theme is Jane’s fight for <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to support oneself and make decisions without relying on others.">independence</dfn> in a world that denies it to women, the poor, and the powerless. Every stage of Jane’s journey tests her self-respect: at Gateshead she defies Mrs Reed, at Lowood she endures Brocklehurst, at Thornfield she demands equality, and at Moor House she refuses to surrender to St John. Her final choice to return to Rochester is an act of freedom, not submission — she comes with her own money, her own will, and her own terms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">“I care for myself” is the novel’s moral compass. It does not mean selfishness — it means that integrity cannot be sacrificed for love, comfort, or duty. Jane’s self-respect is the foundation on which every other relationship is built. Without it, love becomes dependence; with it, love becomes partnership.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain how independence is tested at each stage of Jane’s journey.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Independence is tested at every location: Gateshead (defiance), Lowood (endurance), Thornfield (equality), Moor House (refusal of duty without love), Ferndean (return as equal). The novel argues that true love is only possible between independent people — dependence corrupts relationship into ownership.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme: Love and Equality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Brontë presents love as requiring <dfn class="term" data-def="A state where both people in a relationship have equal power, voice, and respect.">equality</dfn>. Jane’s equality speech (“it is my spirit that addresses your spirit”) demands recognition at the level of the soul. The novel rejects three false models of love: Rochester’s first proposal (love without honesty), St John’s proposal (marriage without love), and Blanche Ingram’s pursuit (marriage for money). Only when Jane and Rochester meet as equals — financially, emotionally, and morally — can their love be fulfilled.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Jane occupies the painful middle ground of the <dfn class="term" data-def="In Victorian England, the social position of a governess: educated enough to teach but too poor to be a social equal.">governess class</dfn>: educated but dependent, refined but powerless. She challenges class assumptions throughout: “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?” Brontë argues that moral worth transcends social rank. The Gardiners in <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> make the same argument through quiet respectability; Jane makes it through passionate confrontation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The inheritance resolves the class barrier, but Brontë ensures that Jane’s moral claim to equality is established <em>before</em> she gets money. Her worth is proven by character, not by fortune. The money simply removes the practical obstacle.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Theme: Religion and Morality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brontë presents three flawed models of religion (Brocklehurst’s hypocrisy, Helen’s passivity, St John’s cold duty) and asks: what is the right way to live? Jane’s answer is <dfn class="term" data-def="The moral sense of right and wrong that guides a person’s decisions independently of external authority.">personal conscience</dfn>. She follows her own moral compass rather than any institution or authority figure. When she says “I will keep the law given by God” while leaving Rochester, she means God’s law as she understands it — not as any clergyman interprets it.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Most 19th-century prose questions give you a printed extract and ask you to write about the extract <em>and</em> the wider novel. You will be assessed against the standard GCSE English Literature assessment objectives — AO1 (response with quotations), AO2 (language, form, structure analysis), AO3 (context), and AO4 (written accuracy). Spend a few minutes reading and annotating the extract before writing. Structure: 4–5 paragraphs. Paragraph 1–2: close analysis of the extract (language, technique, imagery). Paragraph 3–4: broaden to the wider novel, connecting the extract’s themes to other key moments. Final paragraph: conclude with a direct answer to the question. Embed context throughout — never as a standalone paragraph. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for exact mark weightings and timings.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Quotation bank for Jane Eyre:</strong> “Unjust! — unjust!” (defiance), “I am no bird” (freedom), “Women feel just as men feel” (feminism), “Do you think I am soulless?” (equality), “I care for myself” (integrity), “Reader, I married him” (agency), “” (Rochester’s growth). Learn them all.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the four AOs and explain what each requires.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>GCSE English Literature responses are assessed against four AOs: AO1 (response with quotations), AO2 (language, form, structure analysis), AO3 (context), and AO4 (written accuracy). Top-band responses embed all four within every paragraph and show awareness of the whole text, not just the extract.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Quotation Revision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Practise linking each quotation to multiple themes. “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me” connects to independence (Jane rejects confinement), feminism (women are not to be caged), and love (she will not be a kept creature). The best exam responses use a single quotation to discuss several ideas, showing the examiner that you understand how themes interconnect.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Independence and self-respect are the novel’s central themes — Jane’s journey is from powerless dependence to autonomous partnership.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Love requires equality: Brontë rejects love without honesty (Rochester’s deception), marriage without love (St John), and marriage for money (Blanche). Only mutual respect sustains lasting partnership.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">For the 40-mark Eduqas question, embed AO1–AO4 in every paragraph, start with close extract analysis, and broaden to the whole novel with embedded context.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">Use the PEEA structure for each paragraph: Point (state your argument), Evidence (quotation), Explanation (analyse language/technique), Application (connect to context or the wider novel). Aim for 4–5 developed paragraphs in 45 minutes. Start with the extract and broaden out. Always end with a clear answer to the question.</p>
