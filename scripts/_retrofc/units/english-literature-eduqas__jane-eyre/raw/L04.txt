<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rochester’s Proposal: The Garden Scene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Rochester proposes to Jane in Chapter 23, in the garden at Thornfield beneath a chestnut tree. It is Midsummer Eve — a time associated in folklore with magic and transformation. Jane initially believes Rochester intends to marry Blanche Ingram and is sending her away. When he confesses his love, Jane responds with the novel’s most famous speech: “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? — You think wrong! — I have as much soul as you — and full as much heart!”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This is the speech the entire novel has been building towards. Jane does not beg for Rochester’s love or wait passively to be chosen. She <em>demands</em> recognition as his equal: “it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God’s feet, equal — as we are!” The reference to standing before God strips away all social distinctions: wealth, class, gender, employer and employee. Jane claims equality at the deepest possible level — the soul.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write Jane’s equality speech from memory, focusing on the key phrases.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane’s equality speech is the novel’s emotional and thematic climax: “I am not talking to you now through the medium of custom, conventionalities, nor even of mortal flesh: it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God’s feet, equal — as we are!” This statement of absolute human equality challenged Victorian assumptions about class and gender.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Chestnut Tree: Pathetic Fallacy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">After Rochester proposes and Jane accepts, a storm breaks and lightning strikes the chestnut tree, splitting it in two. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of weather, landscape, or nature to reflect or foreshadow a character’s emotional state or coming events.">pathetic fallacy</dfn> at its most dramatic: the tree’s destruction foreshadows the shattering of their relationship. The two halves of the tree, still connected at the roots but split apart, symbolise Jane and Rochester’s future — separated by the revelation of Bertha, but ultimately reconnected.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">On the morning of their wedding in Chapter 26, a solicitor named Briggs and Richard Mason interrupt the ceremony to declare that Rochester is already married. His wife, <dfn class="term" data-def="Rochester’s first wife, a Creole woman from Jamaica, imprisoned in the attic at Thornfield after descending into madness.">Bertha Mason</dfn>, is alive and imprisoned in the attic at Thornfield. Rochester takes the wedding party upstairs to see her: “That is my wife. Such is the sole conjugal embrace I am ever to know.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The revelation is devastating. Rochester has deceived Jane — he knew the marriage would be bigamous. He argues that Bertha’s madness makes the marriage meaningless and begs Jane to stay as his mistress: “Is it better to drive a fellow-creature to despair than to transgress a mere human law?” Rochester frames his plea as compassion, but Jane recognises it as self-serving. If she stays, she becomes a dependent mistress with no legal rights — exactly the powerless position she has fought against her entire life.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Jane’s Decision to Leave</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jane’s decision to leave Rochester in Chapter 27 is the novel’s moral climax. She is torn between passion (“Who in the world cares for you?” her conscience asks) and principle (“I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself”). This internal battle is presented as a genuine struggle — Brontë does not pretend it is easy. Jane loves Rochester and knows that leaving will cause her immense suffering. But she chooses integrity over comfort, self-respect over dependence.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Jane Cannot Stay</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Jane’s refusal is not about conventional morality (though Victorian readers would have understood it that way). It is about <dfn class="term" data-def="A person’s self-respect and sense of their own worth, which cannot be compromised without psychological damage.">self-respect</dfn>. Jane has spent her life fighting to be treated as an equal. Becoming Rochester’s mistress would make her a kept woman, financially dependent on a man who has already deceived her. She would lose the independence she has struggled to achieve since childhood. The decision is agonising but consistent with everything the novel has established about her character.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Brontë reinforces the point through Jane’s language: “I will keep the law given by God; sanctioned by man. I will hold to the principles received by me when I was sane, and not mad — as I am now.” Jane acknowledges that she is in an emotional state close to madness (linking her to Bertha) but insists on holding to rational principle. The novel argues that true love requires moral equality, and moral equality requires honesty.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write Jane’s self-respect declaration from memory and explain why it matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane’s declaration — “I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself” — is the novel’s moral centre. She chooses self-respect over passion, integrity over dependence. This decision makes her departure not a defeat but a victory: she leaves Thornfield as a woman who has proven she cannot be bought or broken.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Jane’s equality speech — ‘it is my spirit that addresses your spirit’ — is the novel’s emotional climax, demanding recognition of human worth beyond class and gender.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The split chestnut tree is a powerful symbol of foreshadowing: the relationship will be torn apart by Bertha’s revelation but ultimately reconnected.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Jane’s decision to leave is the moral climax: she chooses self-respect over passion, proving that her integrity cannot be compromised even by love.</li></ul>
