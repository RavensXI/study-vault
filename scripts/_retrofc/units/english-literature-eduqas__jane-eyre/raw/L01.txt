--- DESCRIPTION ---
Victorian attitudes to women, class, religion, and the Gothic tradition in Jane Eyre.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The World Charlotte Brontë Wrote For</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charlotte Brontë published <em>Jane Eyre</em> in 1847 under the male pseudonym Currer Bell. She used a pen name because Victorian society judged women writers harshly — a novel by a woman about a woman’s passionate inner life would have been dismissed or scandalised. The novel was an immediate sensation: readers were gripped by its intensity, its first-person voice, and its heroine’s fierce demand for equality. Understanding the <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), a period characterised by strict social hierarchies, moral codes, and rapid industrialisation.">Victorian</dfn> context is essential because Brontë was writing against the expectations of her time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Victorian women were expected to be passive, obedient, and domestic — the ideal of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Victorian concept that women should be pure, pious, domestic, and submissive, confined to the home as moral guardians.">Angel in the House</dfn>. They could not vote, own property after marriage, or access higher education. A woman’s choices were limited to marriage, becoming a governess (the only respectable paid employment for a middle-class woman), or dependence on relatives. Jane experiences all three: she is dependent on the Reeds, employed as a governess at Thornfield, and ultimately chooses marriage on her own terms.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three ways Victorian society restricted women that directly affect Jane’s story.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brontë published under a male pseudonym because Victorian society marginalised women writers. Jane’s declaration — “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me: I am a free human being with an independent will” — was radical for 1847. The novel challenges the Angel in the House ideal by presenting a heroine who demands equality in love and refuses to sacrifice her integrity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Class and the Governess</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane occupies an impossible position in the Victorian class system. As a governess, she is educated enough to teach but too poor to be a social equal. She tells Rochester: “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?” This speech directly challenges the Victorian assumption that social rank determines human worth. Jane’s refusal to accept inferiority despite her poverty is the novel’s most revolutionary quality.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Religion is presented through three contrasting figures. <strong>Mr Brocklehurst</strong> represents religious <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of claiming moral standards to which one’s own behaviour does not conform.">hypocrisy</dfn>: he preaches self-denial for the Lowood girls while his own daughters wear silk and curls. <strong>Helen Burns</strong> represents patient, otherworldly faith: she endures suffering without complaint, trusting in heaven. <strong>St John Rivers</strong> represents religion as cold duty: he tries to recruit Jane as a missionary wife, offering a loveless marriage in God’s service. Jane rejects all three models, seeking a faith that accommodates human passion and individual conscience.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Brontë’s critique of religious hypocrisy (Brocklehurst) and cold piety (St John) was controversial. Victorian readers expected novels to endorse conventional Christianity. Jane’s insistence on following her own moral compass — rather than any institutional authority — was seen as dangerously independent.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Gothic Tradition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><em>Jane Eyre</em> draws heavily on the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary genre featuring dark, mysterious settings, supernatural elements, emotional intensity, and themes of entrapment and madness.">Gothic</dfn> tradition. Thornfield Hall is a classic Gothic setting: a grand house with dark corridors, a mysterious third-floor room, and unexplained laughter in the night. The figure of Bertha Mason — Rochester’s first wife, locked in the attic — is the novel’s Gothic centrepiece. Bertha’s madness, her midnight wanderings, and the fire she sets recall the Gothic tradition of imprisoned women and dark family secrets.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Brontë’s Use of the Gothic</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Brontë uses Gothic elements not just for atmosphere but to externalise Jane’s inner conflicts. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A room painted entirely in red where Jane is imprisoned as a child, symbolising anger, passion, and the trauma of her early life.">red room</dfn> at Gateshead — where the young Jane is locked after fighting John Reed — is both a literal prison and a symbol of her suppressed anger. The room is red (passion, rage), it is the room where Mr Reed died (death, trauma), and Jane believes she sees a ghost. The Gothic terror is inseparable from the psychological reality of a child being punished for standing up for herself.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Similarly, Bertha Mason can be read as Jane’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary double, representing the hidden or repressed aspects of a character’s personality.">doppelgänger</dfn> — the dark mirror of her passionate nature. Where Jane controls her emotions, Bertha has lost all control. Where Jane suppresses her rage, Bertha expresses it through violence and fire. Brontë uses the Gothic to explore what happens when women’s emotions are denied any legitimate outlet.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three Gothic elements in Jane Eyre and explain what each symbolises.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brontë uses Gothic conventions to externalise psychological states. The red room symbolises Jane’s suppressed anger and childhood trauma. Thornfield’s dark corridors and mysterious laughter represent hidden secrets and female imprisonment. Bertha Mason functions as Jane’s doppelgänger — the uncontrolled version of the passion Jane must learn to balance with reason.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Brontë wrote against Victorian expectations: Jane’s demand for equality (“I am a free human being with an independent will”) challenged the Angel in the House ideal.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Religion is critiqued through three contrasting figures: Brocklehurst (hypocrisy), Helen Burns (passive faith), and St John Rivers (cold duty). Jane seeks her own moral path.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Gothic elements — the red room, Thornfield’s secrets, Bertha Mason — externalise Jane’s inner conflicts and explore the consequences of suppressing women’s emotions.</li></ul>
