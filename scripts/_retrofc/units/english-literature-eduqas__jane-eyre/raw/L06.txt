--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s return to a changed Rochester, Bertha’s fire, and the novel’s resolution.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Return to Thornfield: Devastation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane returns to Thornfield to find it a “blackened ruin.” Bertha Mason set fire to the house and died in the blaze. Rochester survived but was blinded and lost a hand trying to save the servants and Bertha. The destruction of Thornfield is both literal and symbolic: the house of secrets, deception, and imprisonment has been purged by fire. The Gothic <dfn class="term" data-def="An image or idea that recurs throughout a text, developing or reinforcing a theme.">motif</dfn> of the imprisoned woman in the attic resolves through Bertha’s self-destructive liberation — she chooses to jump from the roof rather than be saved.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Rochester’s injuries have transformed him. He is no longer the powerful, commanding figure who controlled Thornfield and manipulated Jane. His blindness makes him physically dependent, and his disfigurement strips him of the social authority that came with his appearance and wealth. Brontë recalibrates the power balance: Jane now comes to Rochester with her own money, her own will, and her own choice. The reunion can only happen because both characters have been fundamentally changed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain how the fire changes the power balance between Jane and Rochester.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The fire at Thornfield destroys the obstacles to Jane and Rochester’s union: Bertha (the legal barrier) dies, Rochester’s power is reduced by blindness (the social barrier), and Jane’s inheritance provides financial independence (the class barrier). Brontë ensures that when they reunite, they meet as equals for the first time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Ferndean: The Reunion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane finds Rochester at Ferndean, a small manor house deep in the woods. The setting is significant: Ferndean is modest, hidden, and natural — the opposite of Thornfield’s grand deception. Rochester is broken: “His form was of the same strong and stalwart contour as ever: his port was still erect, his hair was still raven black… But in his countenance I saw a change.” Jane approaches him gently, and their reunion is one of the most emotionally powerful scenes in Victorian literature.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Equalised Marriage</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Jane declares: “Reader, I married him.” The famous sentence is significant for its grammar: <em>Jane</em> is the subject, not the object. She married him — she was not given away, bestowed, or taken. This reverses the conventional Victorian marriage plot where the woman is passive. Jane’s final statement of their happiness — “I am my husband’s life as fully as he is mine” — confirms the complete <dfn class="term" data-def="A state where both partners in a relationship have equal power, respect, and emotional investment.">mutuality</dfn> Brontë has been building towards throughout the novel.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Rochester partially recovers his sight (he can see their first child), which some critics read as a reward for his repentance. He has learned humility, honesty, and dependence on another — qualities he lacked before the fire. The novel’s moral architecture demands that both characters grow: Jane learns to balance passion with principle, and Rochester learns to balance authority with vulnerability.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Bertha’s Death: Freedom or Tragedy?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Bertha’s death raises difficult questions. She is the novel’s most controversial figure: a woman driven mad, locked away, and ultimately self-destructive. Modern critics, following Jean Rhys’s novel <em>Wide Sargasso Sea</em> (1966), have argued that Bertha is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person from the former European colonies, whose treatment in literature may reflect assumptions about race, empire, and otherness.">colonial</dfn> subject — a Creole woman from Jamaica whose “madness” may be the result of imprisonment and cultural dislocation rather than inherent insanity. Her death clears the way for Jane’s happiness, but it also silences the one character who was never allowed to tell her own story.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Novel’s Ending: Triumph or Compromise?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Some readers see the ending as a feminist triumph: Jane achieves love, independence, and equality on her own terms. Others see it as a compromise: Rochester had to be physically diminished before a woman could be his equal, which suggests Brontë could not imagine equality between two able-bodied, wealthy people. The novel’s final image — Jane and Rochester living quietly at Ferndean, away from society — can be read as either freedom from social constraints or retreat from a world that cannot accommodate their relationship.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The final paragraph shifts to St John, dying as a missionary in India: “His hope of the next world was sure… Amen; even so, come, Lord Jesus!” This ending is deliberately open: Brontë places Jane’s earthly happiness alongside St John’s spiritual ambition without ranking them. The novel does not declare one path superior — it asks the reader to judge.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write ‘Reader, I married him’ and explain why the grammar of this sentence matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Reader, I married him” places Jane as the active subject of her own story. She is not given in marriage or taken as a wife — she marries. This grammatical choice encapsulates the novel’s feminist argument: Jane’s life is her own to direct, and her marriage is an act of agency, not submission.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">The fire at Thornfield removes all barriers to Jane and Rochester’s union: Bertha dies, Rochester is humbled, and Jane’s inheritance ensures financial equality.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">‘Reader, I married him’ places Jane as the active subject of her own story — a grammatical and thematic statement of female agency.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Bertha’s death is both a plot resolution and a moral problem: modern critics question whether her story has been silenced to enable Jane’s happiness.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n14">If discussing the ending, consider multiple interpretations. Is Rochester’s blindness a necessary condition for equality, or a punishment for deception? Is the retreat to Ferndean freedom or escape? Examiners reward responses that explore complexity rather than settling for a single reading. Use ‘some critics argue’ and ‘alternatively’ to show you can evaluate different perspectives.</p>
