Balancing equations, using moles and reading symbol equations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Conservation of Mass</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">In any chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed — they are simply rearranged. This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products in a chemical reaction, because atoms are neither created nor destroyed.">law of conservation of mass</dfn>. It means a correctly written symbol equation must have the same number of each type of atom on both sides of the arrow. An equation with unequal atom counts is <strong>unbalanced</strong> and does not represent a real reaction.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the law of conservation of mass and explain why a chemical equation must be balanced.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a chemical reaction, total mass is conserved. A balanced symbol equation has the same number of atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow. You balance equations by adding large numbers (coefficients) in front of formulas — never by changing the subscripts inside a formula.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Balancing Symbol Equations</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n5">To balance a <dfn class="term" data-def="An equation using chemical symbols and formulas to show the reactants and products of a reaction and their relative amounts.">symbol equation</dfn>, count the atoms on each side and adjust the large numbers (coefficients) in front of formulas until both sides match. You must <strong>never</strong> change the subscripts inside a formula (that would change the substance itself). Work through one element at a time, leaving hydrogen and oxygen until last when they appear in water or acids.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">Balance the equation for the combustion of propane: \(C_3H_8 + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + H_2O\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Step 1: Count carbons. Left: 3; Right: 1. Put a 3 in front of \(CO_2\):<br>\(C_3H_8 + O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + H_2O\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Step 2: Count hydrogens. Left: 8; Right: 2. Put a 4 in front of \(H_2O\):<br>\(C_3H_8 + O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Step 3: Count oxygens. Right: 3×2 + 4×1 = 10. Left needs 5 \(O_2\):<br>\(C_3H_8 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Step 4: Check all atoms. C: 3=3 ✓ H: 8=8 ✓ O: 10=10 ✓. The equation is balanced.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n11">State symbols can be added in brackets after each formula to show the physical state: (s) = solid, (l) = liquid, (g) = gas, (aq) = aqueous (dissolved in water). For example: \(Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)\)</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Relative Formula Mass</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a compound's formula. Symbol: Mr. Has no units.">relative formula mass</dfn> (\(M_r\)) of a compound is calculated by adding together the relative atomic masses (\(A_r\)) of all the atoms shown in its formula. For example, for water (\(H_2O\)):</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">$$M_r(H_2O) = 2 \times A_r(H) + 1 \times A_r(O) = 2 \times 1 + 16 = 18$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">For sulfuric acid (\(H_2SO_4\)): \(M_r = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98\). You need the periodic table to look up \(A_r\) values. The relative formula mass tells you the mass of one formula unit in atomic mass units, and is numerically equal to the mass of one mole in grams.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Calculate the Mr of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) using Ar values Ca=40, C=12, O=16. Check your answer is 100.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Relative formula mass (Mr): add up the Ar of every atom in the formula, accounting for subscripts. Mr of CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 3×16 = 100. No units — it is a ratio relative to ¹²C.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Mole</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Atoms and molecules are far too small to count individually, so chemists use the <dfn class="term" data-def="The unit of amount of substance in chemistry. One mole contains approximately 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules or ions). Symbol: mol.">mole</dfn> as a counting unit. One mole of any substance contains the same enormous number of particles — approximately \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\) — known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of particles in one mole of any substance, approximately 6.02 × 10²³. Named after the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro.">Avogadro's constant</dfn>. The genius of the mole is that one mole of an element has a mass in grams exactly equal to its relative atomic mass.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Higher Tier: Mole Calculations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The key equation linking moles, mass, and molar mass is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">$$\text{number of moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g/mol)}}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Rearranged: mass = moles × molar mass, or molar mass = mass ÷ moles.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Worked example:</strong> How many moles are in 44 g of carbon dioxide (\(CO_2\), \(M_r = 44\))?<br>Moles = 44 ÷ 44 = <strong>1 mol</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Second example:</strong> What mass of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, \(M_r = 40\)) is needed to make 0.5 mol?<br>Mass = 0.5 × 40 = <strong>20 g</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Always show your working in full and include units at every step — examiners deduct marks for missing units on multi-step calculations.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Using Equations to Calculate Reacting Masses</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n26">A balanced symbol equation shows the <strong>molar ratios</strong> of reactants and products — the large numbers (coefficients) tell you how many moles of each substance react. Combined with molar masses, this allows calculation of the mass of any reactant or product from a known mass of another.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Calculate the mass of iron produced when 160 g of iron(III) oxide (\(Fe_2O_3\)) reacts with excess carbon:<br>\(2Fe_2O_3 + 3C \rightarrow 4Fe + 3CO_2\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Step 1: Find \(M_r\) of \(Fe_2O_3\): \(2 \times 56 + 3 \times 16 = 160\) g/mol.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29">Step 2: Calculate moles of \(Fe_2O_3\): \(\frac{160}{160} = 1 \text{ mol}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Step 3: Use the molar ratio. From the equation, 2 mol \(Fe_2O_3\) → 4 mol Fe, so 1 mol \(Fe_2O_3\) → 2 mol Fe.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Step 4: Convert moles of Fe to mass: \(2 \times 56 = \mathbf{112 \text{ g}}\).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the four steps for a reacting mass calculation from memory. What must you do before anything else?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reacting mass calculation (Higher): (1) write balanced equation, (2) calculate Mr of known substance and find moles, (3) use molar ratio from equation to find moles of unknown substance, (4) convert moles to mass. Always show every step and include units.</p>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Conservation of mass means balanced equations must have identical atom counts on both sides. Balance by adjusting coefficients, never subscripts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Relative formula mass (Mr) is the sum of all relative atomic masses in a formula. One mole of a substance has a mass in grams equal to its Mr.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Avogadro's constant (≈ 6.02 × 10²³) is the number of particles in one mole. Higher Tier: moles = mass ÷ molar mass; use molar ratios from balanced equations to calculate reacting masses.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">For balancing equations, always check every element after your final balance — it is easy to miss an oxygen. For mole calculations at Higher Tier, show every step clearly: molar mass calculation, moles calculation, ratio step, final mass. Examiners award marks for correct method even if the final answer is wrong due to an arithmetic slip. Common error: forgetting to multiply by the ratio from the balanced equation before converting back to mass.</p>
