--- DESCRIPTION ---
Nigeria as an EDC case study — trade, investment, social factors and Rostow’s model in action.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Nigeria?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Nigeria is a strong country case study because it is the most populous nation in Africa with around 220 million people, the continent’s largest economy, and an emerging and developing country (EDC). It is changing fast: oil wealth, a young workforce and a booming tech sector pull development forward, while regional inequality, conflict in the north and reliance on a single export hold it back. Studying Nigeria lets us see why development is rarely smooth or even.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down five quick facts about Nigeria: location, population, capital, currency and main export.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria sits in West Africa on the Gulf of Guinea. Its capital is Abuja, its largest city Lagos has over 15 million people, and its main export is crude oil, which still earns roughly 80% of export revenue.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Location and Environmental Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Nigeria stretches from the wet, forested south on the coast to the dry Sahel in the north. The Niger and Benue rivers cross the country, supporting farming and fishing. The south has a tropical climate suited to crops like cocoa, oil palm and yam. The north is drier and is more prone to drought and desertification, which damages crops and pushes families to migrate. Oil and natural gas reserves under the Niger Delta and offshore are the country’s most valuable resources.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Politics and International Relationships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Nigeria gained independence from Britain in 1960. After decades of military rule, democratic government returned in 1999. Nigeria is a member of the Commonwealth, the African Union and OPEC (the oil-exporting countries’ group). It works with the UK, USA and increasingly China on trade and investment. <dfn class="term" data-def="Long-running disputes between groups, such as the Boko Haram insurgency in north-east Nigeria, that affect security and development.">Internal conflicts</dfn> — especially the Boko Haram insurgency in the north — have damaged farming areas and pushed millions of people from their homes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Trade: Imports and Exports</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Crude oil and natural gas dominate exports, with India, Spain, the Netherlands and the USA as key buyers. Cocoa, sesame seeds and rubber are smaller exports. Imports are very different — Nigeria buys in machinery, vehicles, refined petroleum (because it has too few working refineries), wheat and medicines, mostly from China, India and Europe. This pattern, exporting low-value primary goods and importing high-value manufactures, is typical of many EDCs.</p>

Diversifying into farming, manufacturing and tech is now a major government goal.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">International Investment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Foreign companies, called <dfn class="term" data-def="Companies that operate in more than one country, like Shell, MTN or Dangote.">transnational corporations (TNCs)</dfn>, play a huge role in Nigeria. Shell, ExxonMobil and Chevron extract oil. South Africa’s MTN runs the largest mobile phone network. Chinese firms have funded railways, power stations and the new Lekki deep-sea port. Investment brings jobs, infrastructure and tax income, but profits often flow back overseas, and oil spills in the Niger Delta have damaged farming and fishing communities.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Population and Employment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Nigeria has a very young population — the median age is around 18, compared with 40 in the UK. Around half of workers are in farming, especially in the north. The service sector has grown quickly, including banking, retail and the ‘Nollywood’ film industry, which produces over 2,000 films a year. Manufacturing is still small. <dfn class="term" data-def="Work that is not officially recorded, taxed or regulated, such as street trading, taxi driving or small workshops.">The informal sector</dfn> employs millions, providing income but little protection.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why Nigeria’s young population is both an opportunity and a challenge.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nigeria’s median age is roughly 18, with around 60% of people under 25. This gives a huge potential workforce, but the country must create millions of new jobs and school places each year to match the growth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Social Factors: Education, Health and Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Education access has improved — primary school enrolment is now over 60% — but quality varies sharply between cities and rural areas, and over 10 million children are still out of school. Healthcare is mixed: life expectancy is around 55 years, well below the UK’s 81. Mobile phone use has leapt forward, with more than 200 million phone connections, and platforms like mobile banking apps have brought finance to people who never had a bank account. The Lagos tech hub, sometimes called ‘Yabacon Valley’, has produced fintech firms valued at over a billion dollars.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">An Aid Project: Polio Eradication</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Nigeria was the last African country with wild polio, but a partnership between the World Health Organization, UNICEF, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation and Rotary International funded vaccination teams that reached almost every village. In 2020 Africa was declared free of wild polio. The project shows how multilateral aid, when combined with local volunteers and clear targets, can deliver life-changing results.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="A 1960 model by Walt Rostow that pictures development as five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and high mass consumption.">Rostow’s model</dfn> sets out five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and high mass consumption. Most geographers place Nigeria around stage 3, ‘take-off’: a few sectors (oil, telecoms, fintech) are growing fast, infrastructure is improving, and a small middle class has appeared. Critics say the model is too simple — it assumes all countries follow the same path that Western nations did, ignoring colonialism, climate and global inequality.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Nigeria is an EDC of around 220 million people whose growth is driven by oil, telecoms and a fast-growing tech sector, but held back by regional inequality and over-reliance on oil exports.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Trade is unbalanced: high-value oil and gas exports against high-value manufactured imports, with TNCs and Chinese investment shaping the economy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Using Rostow’s model, Nigeria is often placed at the ‘take-off’ stage, although the model has limits because it ignores history and global power.</li>
</ul>
