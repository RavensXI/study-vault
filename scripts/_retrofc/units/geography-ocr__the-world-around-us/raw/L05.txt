--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why countries developed unevenly — colonialism, trade, natural resources, plus the role of aid.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Has Development Been Uneven?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Countries have not all developed at the same rate. Some advanced countries (ACs) such as the UK, Germany and Japan grew rich during industrialisation. Others, including many in sub-Saharan Africa, remain low-income developing countries (LIDCs). The reasons stretch back hundreds of years and continue to shape today’s world. The key causes are <dfn class="term" data-def="The system by which one country takes political and economic control of another, often by force.">colonialism</dfn>, unfair patterns of trade, dependence on a few <dfn class="term" data-def="Materials such as oil, minerals, timber and farmland that come from the Earth and have economic value.">natural resources</dfn>, and the way <dfn class="term" data-def="Money, food, equipment or expertise given by one country or organisation to help another.">aid</dfn> is given.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four main causes of uneven development with a one-line example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main causes of uneven development are colonialism, trade patterns, natural resources and aid. Each links to the others — colonialism shaped trade routes, which shaped which countries control resources today.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Colonialism and Its Long Shadow</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From the 1500s onwards, European powers including Britain, France, Spain, Portugal and Belgium took political control of large parts of Africa, Asia, the Caribbean and the Americas. By 1914 around a quarter of the world’s land area was under British rule alone. Colonisers extracted resources — gold, silver, sugar, cotton, rubber, palm oil — and shipped them back to Europe. Roads, railways and ports were built mainly to move raw materials to coastal export points, not to connect colonies internally.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When colonies gained independence in the 20th century, many were left without diverse industries, well-funded schools or trained professionals. Borders were often drawn by Europeans without regard for local groups, leading to later conflicts. The transatlantic slave trade also removed an estimated 12 million people from West Africa between 1500 and 1870, weakening communities for generations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Trade Patterns That Lock In Inequality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Trade can grow an economy — or trap it. ACs typically export <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods that have been manufactured or processed, like cars, electronics or chemicals, and usually have a higher value than raw materials.">manufactured goods</dfn> and services, which earn high prices. LIDCs often export raw <dfn class="term" data-def="Unprocessed goods such as cocoa beans, copper or coffee that are sold in their natural state.">primary commodities</dfn> like cocoa, coffee or copper, whose prices swing wildly on world markets. A poor harvest or a price crash can wipe out government income.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Tariffs and subsidies can make this worse. Wealthy countries sometimes place tariffs on processed imports (such as roasted coffee) but not on raw beans, discouraging LIDCs from adding value at home. The World Trade Organization tries to make rules fairer, but ACs still hold more bargaining power.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Natural Resources: Blessing or Curse?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A country’s natural resources can be a huge advantage. Norway used North Sea oil revenues to build a sovereign wealth fund worth over £1 trillion. But many resource-rich LIDCs experience the <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern in which countries with valuable natural resources often have weaker economies and more conflict than expected.">resource curse</dfn>. Profits flow to a small elite, transnational corporations or corrupt officials, leaving ordinary people poor.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Resource wealth can also fuel conflict. The Democratic Republic of Congo holds around 70% of the world’s cobalt — vital for smartphone and electric-car batteries — yet remains one of the poorest countries on Earth, with armed groups fighting for mining areas.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words why valuable resources do not always lead to development. Use one country example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Having valuable resources does not guarantee development. Without good governance, fair contracts and reinvestment of profits, resource wealth can deepen inequality and even cause conflict — this is the resource curse.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Aid: Helpful or Harmful?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Aid means help given from one country or organisation to another. There are several main types:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Bilateral aid</strong> — given directly from one government to another, often with conditions attached.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Multilateral aid</strong> — donated through international bodies like the World Bank or UNICEF.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Emergency (short-term) aid</strong> — sent quickly after disasters such as earthquakes or floods.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Long-term development aid</strong> — funds schools, clinics, water supplies and training over many years.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>NGO aid</strong> — from charities like Oxfam or WaterAid, often grassroots and small-scale.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Aid Can Promote Development</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Well-targeted aid can save lives and build long-term capacity. The Gavi vaccine alliance is estimated to have prevented over 17 million deaths since 2000. UK aid has built thousands of schools in Sierra Leone, raising literacy rates. Small-scale NGO projects, like installing handpumps or solar panels, often have lasting community benefits because they involve local people in planning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">When Aid Hinders Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Aid is not always positive. Tied aid forces the receiver to spend the money on goods or contractors from the donor country, so much of the value flows back to the donor. Large loans can leave LIDCs with debts they struggle to repay — some African countries spend more on debt interest than on healthcare. Free imported food can undercut local farmers, and corruption can divert aid before it reaches its target. Critics argue long-term reliance on aid creates a culture of <dfn class="term" data-def="Becoming dependent on aid donations rather than developing self-sufficient industries and services.">aid dependency</dfn>, slowing the growth of local industries.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Colonialism shaped today’s development gap by extracting resources, drawing arbitrary borders, and leaving weak local industries when colonies became independent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Unfair trade and the resource curse keep many LIDCs dependent on volatile primary commodities, while ACs add value through manufacturing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Aid can promote development through vaccines, schools and emergency relief, but tied aid, debt and dependency mean it can also hold countries back.</li>
</ul>
