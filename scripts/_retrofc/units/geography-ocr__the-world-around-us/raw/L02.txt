<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Tropical Rainforests Are</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tropical <dfn class="term" data-def="Dense forests in equatorial regions characterised by hot humid climate, rich biodiversity and tall multi-layered vegetation.">rainforests</dfn> stretch in a band around the equator. Six major rainforests cover most of the global total: the <strong>Amazon</strong> in South America (the largest, 5.5 million km<sup>2</sup>), the <strong>Congo Basin</strong> in central Africa (the second largest), <strong>South East Asian</strong> rainforests across Indonesia and Malaysia, the <strong>Madagascan</strong> rainforest, the <strong>Central American</strong> rainforest from Mexico to Panama, and the <strong>Australasian</strong> rainforests of Papua New Guinea and northern Queensland. Together they cover only about 6% of the planet's land surface but contain more than half of all known plant and animal species.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Their location is no accident. Rainforests cluster between 10°N and 10°S where consistent overhead sun warms air and ocean. Rising warm air produces convectional rainfall almost daily — over 2000 mm a year, with no real dry season. Combined with average temperatures of 26°C, conditions are perfect for the world's most productive ecosystems.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">How Rainforest Processes Work</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Two cycles drive everything in a rainforest. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous cycle in which water moves from oceans, through evaporation and precipitation, to plants and back.">water cycle</dfn> runs hot. Trees draw water up from the soil and release it through their leaves (transpiration). The released water joins evaporation from rivers and the wet ground to form clouds, which cool and drop heavy rainfall back onto the canopy. About half of the rainfall in the Amazon is recycled in this way — the forest, in effect, makes its own weather.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The cycle of nutrients between soil, plants, animals and decomposers within an ecosystem.">nutrient cycle</dfn> is fast but fragile. Most nutrients are stored in the biomass — the trees themselves — not in the soil. When leaves and animals die, decomposers (fungi, bacteria, termites) break them down rapidly in the warm wet conditions. The released nutrients are immediately taken up by tree roots before they can wash away. Cut down the trees and the nutrient store is gone within a few seasons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four layers of a tropical rainforest with one example species for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tropical rainforests have four vegetation layers: emergent (giant trees up to 60 m, sunlight specialists), canopy (the main 30-45 m layer where most species live), under-storey (smaller trees adapted to filtered light), and ground layer (sparse vegetation in deep shade). Each layer has its own species community.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Case Study: The Amazon Rainforest</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Amazon stretches across nine countries, with about 60% in Brazil. Its biodiversity is staggering: an estimated 16,000 tree species, 1,300 bird species, 430 mammal species and 2.5 million insect species. The forest interacts with global systems — locking up around 100 billion tonnes of carbon and producing 6% of the world's oxygen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Value to humans:</strong> the Amazon supports more than 30 million people directly, including 350+ indigenous groups. It supplies timber, rubber, Brazil nuts, acai and chocolate. Its plant compounds are the source of medicines including quinine (anti-malarial) and curare (anaesthetic). Globally, it stabilises rainfall as far south as Argentina and as far north as the US Midwest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Threats:</strong> deforestation for cattle ranching, soya farming, illegal logging and gold mining. Around 17% of the Amazon has already been cleared. Brazil's deforestation rate spiked between 2019 and 2022 (over 11,000 km<sup>2</sup> in 2021 alone) before falling sharply under tightened enforcement from 2023. Climate change adds further pressure: more frequent droughts kill mature trees and create conditions for forest fires.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Several strategies aim to balance rainforest protection with development. <strong>Indigenous land rights</strong> protect huge areas: indigenous-managed land in Brazil has lower deforestation than government-protected zones. <strong>Selective logging</strong> takes a few high-value trees per hectare, leaving the canopy intact. <strong>Ecotourism</strong> generates revenue without clearance — Brazil's Mamiraua Sustainable Reserve combines research, jobs and biodiversity protection. <strong>International payments</strong> through schemes like REDD+ pay rainforest countries to keep forests standing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Scientists warn that the Amazon may approach a tipping point at 20-25% deforestation, where the forest can no longer recycle enough rainfall to stay wet. Above that threshold, large parts could shift from rainforest to savannah, releasing decades of stored carbon. The current 17% deforestation level is uncomfortably close, which is why the slowing of clearance from 2023 has been celebrated globally.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write three values of the Amazon to humans and three threats it faces.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Amazon stores 100 billion tonnes of carbon, supports 30 million people including 350+ indigenous groups, and supplies global goods from timber to medicines. About 17% has been cleared, mainly for cattle and soya; scientists warn of a tipping point at 20-25%.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Tropical rainforests show the productive power of warm, wet equatorial conditions. The Amazon links climate, biodiversity, indigenous communities and the global economy — which is why protecting it is both a local and a planetary concern. Strong exam answers always quote specific Amazon statistics and connect deforestation to its full chain of causes and effects.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Tropical rainforests cluster within 10° of the equator, where constant warmth and rainfall produce the world's most diverse ecosystems.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Rainforest water and nutrient cycles are fast and fragile — about half the Amazon's rainfall is recycled by the forest itself, and most nutrients are stored in the trees, not the soil.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Amazon stores 100 billion tonnes of carbon, supports 30 million people, and faces threats from cattle ranching, soya farming and climate change; sustainable management combines indigenous rights, ecotourism and international payments.</li>
</ul>
