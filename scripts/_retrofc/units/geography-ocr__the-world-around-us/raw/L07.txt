--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why people move to cities, where megacities are, and a Lagos case study of urban challenge and change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A World That Lives in Cities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For most of human history, more people lived in the countryside than in cities. That changed in 2007. Today around 57% of the world’s population is urban, and the United Nations expects this to reach about 68% by 2050. Most of that growth will happen in low-income developing countries (LIDCs) and emerging and developing countries (EDCs). Three terms matter here. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A large town with its own local government, usually over 100,000 people.">city</dfn> is a large urban settlement. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A city with a population of over 10 million people.">megacity</dfn> has more than 10 million people. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A city that has worldwide political, economic and cultural influence, like London or New York.">world city</dfn> exerts global influence well beyond its size, in finance, politics or culture.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write definitions for ‘city’, ‘megacity’ and ‘world city’ from memory, with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The world has roughly 33 megacities (over 10 million people). Most are now in Asia — including Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, Dhaka and Mumbai — with growing African ones like Lagos and Kinshasa.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Where Are the Megacities and How Are They Changing?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In 1950 only New York and Tokyo were megacities. By 1990 there were ten, and by the early 2020s the count had passed 30. The shift has been clear — from advanced countries (ACs) towards LIDCs and EDCs. The fastest-growing cities now are in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where populations are young and rural-to-urban migration is high. Some megacities, like Tokyo, have stopped growing or are even shrinking, because birth rates have fallen.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why Do Cities Grow So Fast in LIDCs and EDCs?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Two forces drive rapid urbanisation. The first is migration from the countryside. People are pushed out by drought, falling farm incomes, lack of jobs or violence, and pulled towards cities by hopes of work, schools, hospitals and a more modern way of life. These reasons are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Reasons that drive people to leave a place (push) or to move to a new place (pull).">push and pull factors</dfn>. The second is <dfn class="term" data-def="When births in a city outnumber deaths, so the population grows from within.">natural growth</dfn> — many migrants are young, so birth rates inside the city stay high.</p>

Some effects are positive too — cities concentrate skilled workers, ideas and businesses, helping innovation and productivity grow.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Case Study: Lagos, Nigeria</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Lagos is one of the fastest-growing megacities in the world. It sits on the Atlantic coast of West Africa in southern Nigeria, on a series of islands and lagoons connected by bridges. The official population is over 15 million, and including outer suburbs the wider metropolitan area is estimated at more than 20 million. Lagos handles around 80% of Nigeria’s sea-borne imports and is the financial heart of West Africa.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Lagos in Its Region and the World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Lagos is connected to global trade through Apapa Port and the new Lekki deep-sea port, opened in 2023. Air links run from Murtala Muhammed International Airport. The city is West Africa’s film capital (Nollywood), a major fintech base for companies like Flutterwave and Paystack, and home to many regional headquarters of African and global firms. It is increasingly described as a world city for sub-Saharan Africa.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Migration and Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Migrants come from across Nigeria, especially the rural north, and from neighbouring countries such as Benin, Togo and Ghana. Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa and many other ethnic groups live and work side by side, making Lagos a multilingual, multicultural city. Markets like Balogun and Computer Village trade in fashion, food and electronics. Music genres like Afrobeats have spread globally from Lagos studios.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three different ways of life or cultural features of Lagos with one fact for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Lagos has gleaming high-rise districts like Victoria Island next to vast informal settlements such as Makoko, where homes stand on stilts above the lagoon. Around 60% of Lagos residents live in informal housing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Contemporary Challenges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Lagos faces serious urban challenges. Housing struggles to keep up with growth, so <dfn class="term" data-def="Unplanned housing built on land the residents do not own, often without official water, electricity or drainage.">informal settlements</dfn> like Makoko and Ajegunle have spread. Traffic congestion is famous — commuters spend hours in ‘go-slows’ on bridges into the islands — and air pollution from older diesel vehicles is a serious health risk. Waste collection cannot match population growth, so dumps like Olusosun have grown enormous. Flooding is worsened by climate change, blocked drains and rising sea levels.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Several strategies tackle transport. The Lagos Bus Rapid Transit scheme uses dedicated lanes to move thousands of commuters faster than cars. The Blue Line metro, running from Marina to Mile 2, opened in 2023 and aims to cut journey times from over an hour to about 20 minutes. The Eko Atlantic project is reclaiming land off Victoria Island to build a planned, flood-resilient new district powered partly by renewable energy. The Lekki port and free-trade zone are designed to spread economic activity east, away from the choked island core. None of these solves all of Lagos’s problems, but each adds capacity.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Most of the world is now urban, and the fastest-growing megacities are in LIDCs and EDCs, driven by rural-to-urban migration and natural growth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Lagos has over 15 million people, links West Africa to global trade and culture, and shows the social, economic and environmental challenges of rapid urbanisation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Lagos is tackling its problems through Bus Rapid Transit, the new Blue Line metro, the Lekki deep-sea port and the Eko Atlantic land-reclamation project.</li>
</ul>
