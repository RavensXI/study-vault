--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Elizabeth Bennet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth is the novel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character of a story, through whose perspective the reader experiences the narrative and whose growth drives the plot.">protagonist</dfn> and moral centre. She is intelligent, witty, and fiercely independent — qualities that attract both the reader and Darcy. However, Elizabeth is also the character whose flaw gives the novel its title: her <em>prejudice</em>. She trusts her own judgement so completely that she fails to question it, believing Wickham over Darcy because his story confirms what she already wants to believe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Elizabeth’s growth comes through painful self-recognition. After reading Darcy’s letter, she admits: “How despicably I have acted! I, who have prided myself on my discernment!” The irony is devastating — her <em>pride</em> in her judgement has been the very source of her <em>prejudice</em>. By the novel’s end, Elizabeth has learned to separate genuine character from surface impression, achieving the self-knowledge that Austen presents as the foundation of moral maturity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close your notes and write a paragraph tracking Elizabeth's character arc in four stages: initial confidence, Wickham's deception, the letter's revelation, and Pemberley's reassessment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth’s arc moves from confident misjudgement to painful self-knowledge. Her line “Till this moment I never knew myself” is the novel’s emotional pivot. In the exam, track her growth through specific moments: amused defiance at the assembly, trust in Wickham, shock at Darcy’s letter, humility at Pemberley, and mature love in the second proposal.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mr Darcy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Darcy is the novel’s most complex character. He begins as apparently proud and arrogant — “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” — but Austen gradually reveals that much of what the neighbourhood calls pride is actually social awkwardness and a principled refusal to perform charm he does not feel. As he tells Elizabeth: “I certainly have not the talent which some people possess of conversing easily with those I have never seen before.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Darcy’s first proposal reveals the tension between his genuine love and his class-based conditioning. He cannot declare his love without listing his objections, and Elizabeth’s accusation — that he proposed “with so evident a design of offending and insulting me” — forces him to confront how his pride has distorted his behaviour. His letter, the Pemberley reunion, and the secret rescue of Lydia trace a clear arc of <dfn class="term" data-def="A fundamental change in a character’s attitudes, behaviour, or understanding, usually driven by a significant event or revelation.">moral transformation</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Darcy’s “My Good Opinion Once Lost”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Darcy’s declaration — “My good opinion once lost, is lost forever” — is spoken before his transformation. He presents inflexibility as a virtue: he does not forgive easily and considers this principled. Elizabeth challenges him: “That is a failing indeed! — Implacable resentment <em>is</em> a shade in a character.” The exchange reveals both characters’ flaws: Darcy’s pride in his own rigidity and Elizabeth’s willingness to criticise a man she barely knows.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">By the novel’s end, Darcy <em>has</em> changed his opinion — of Elizabeth, of her family, of the Gardiners. His earlier claim is proven wrong by his own growth. Austen suggests that genuine moral development requires the flexibility to reassess, not the pride of fixed judgement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Wickham & Mr Collins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Wickham is the novel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character who opposes the protagonist, creating conflict and obstacles.">antagonist</dfn> — not in the dramatic sense of a villain, but as the embodiment of the surface charm that deceives the world. He lies about Darcy, pursues Miss King for her £10,000, abandons her when the opportunity fades, and elopes with fifteen-year-old Lydia without intending to marry her. Wickham’s charm is his weapon, and Austen uses him to demonstrate that in a society obsessed with manners, a pleasant appearance can disguise a predatory character.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Mr Collins is a comic figure, but Austen’s satire has a sharp edge. His <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive eagerness to please or serve someone of higher social status, often motivated by self-interest rather than genuine respect.">obsequiousness</dfn> to Lady Catherine, his pompous proposal to Elizabeth, and his complete inability to understand that a woman might refuse him all expose the absurdity of a social system that rewards deference over intelligence. Collins is not evil like Wickham — he is simply incapable of independent thought, parroting the values of his patroness with absolute sincerity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Mrs Bennet & Mr Bennet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Mrs Bennet is often read as purely comic — her “nerves,” her embarrassing public behaviour, her obsessive matchmaking. But Austen gives her a genuine motivation: with five daughters, no son, and an entailed estate, Mrs Bennet faces real poverty when her husband dies. Her frantic pursuit of wealthy sons-in-law is a survival strategy, not mere silliness. The exam rewards reading Mrs Bennet with this nuance rather than dismissing her.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Mr Bennet is intelligent and witty — qualities Elizabeth inherits — but he has retreated from his responsibilities into ironic detachment. He mocks his wife rather than managing his finances, indulges Lydia rather than restraining her, and takes no action until the elopement crisis forces him. His rare moment of self-awareness (“I am not afraid of being overpowered by the impression”) is a bitter acknowledgement that he deflects pain through humour rather than confronting it. Austen presents him as a cautionary figure: intelligence without moral responsibility is insufficient.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="From memory, name one quotation each for Elizabeth, Darcy, Wickham, and Collins that reveals their defining character trait.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Austen builds characters through contrast. Wickham’s charm versus Darcy’s awkwardness; Elizabeth’s wit versus Jane’s gentleness; Mrs Bennet’s anxiety versus Mr Bennet’s detachment; Collins’s obsequiousness versus Elizabeth’s independence. In the exam, always discuss characters <em>in relation to each other</em>, not in isolation.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jane Bennet & Lady Catherine de Bourgh</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Jane is Elizabeth’s foil: equally good-natured but without Elizabeth’s sharpness. Jane always sees the best in people — she tries to find excuses for Wickham even after his true character is revealed. Her inability to think ill of anyone nearly costs her Bingley, because her reserve makes her feelings invisible. Austen suggests that goodness without perceptiveness is as flawed as perceptiveness without goodness.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Lady Catherine de Bourgh represents unchecked class arrogance. She assumes her rank gives her the right to dictate other people’s marriages, careers, and behaviour. Her interrogation of Elizabeth at Rosings (“Are all your sisters out?”) reveals a woman who believes social rules should be enforced as rigidly as law. Elizabeth’s calm defiance of Lady Catherine is one of the novel’s most satisfying moments — individual worth triumphing over inherited privilege.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Elizabeth’s arc moves from confident misjudgement (“I, who have prided myself on my discernment!”) to painful self-knowledge (“Till this moment I never knew myself”) — her pride causes her prejudice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Darcy’s arc moves from social arrogance (“She is tolerable”) through honest self-defence (the letter) to genuine humility (the second proposal and Lydia’s rescue).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Austen builds all characters through contrast: Wickham vs Darcy (charm vs integrity), Collins vs Elizabeth (obsequiousness vs independence), Mrs Bennet vs Mr Bennet (anxiety vs detachment). Always discuss characters in relation to each other.</li></ul>
