--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Meryton assembly, Darcy's pride, Elizabeth's prejudice, and the power of first impressions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Opening Gambit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel’s famous opening line — “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife” — sets up the central irony immediately. Within the first three chapters, Austen establishes the key relationships, conflicts, and misunderstandings that will drive the entire plot. For Edexcel Component 2, Section A, these early chapters are prime extract territory — you must be able to analyse the language closely.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Meryton Assembly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Meryton assembly ball in Chapter 3 is the novel’s inciting incident. Bingley arrives and is immediately charming: he dances “every dance,” is “lively and unreserved,” and singles out Jane Bennet, dancing with her twice. In contrast, Darcy refuses to dance with anyone outside his own party and is judged “the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world.” The entire neighbourhood forms its opinion of both men in a single evening.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The pivotal moment comes when Bingley encourages Darcy to dance with Elizabeth. Darcy refuses with the cutting remark: “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me.” Elizabeth overhears this <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate insult or slight, especially one that damages a person’s pride or social standing.">slight</dfn> and responds not with humiliation but with amused contempt — she tells the story “with great spirit among her friends.” This single exchange establishes the novel’s central conflict: Darcy’s pride and Elizabeth’s resulting prejudice.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down Darcy's exact words about Elizabeth at the Meryton assembly and explain why this quotation is central to the novel's plot.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” — Darcy’s dismissal of Elizabeth at the Meryton assembly is the seed of her prejudice against him. It also reveals his class-based arrogance: he judges her by appearance and social standing, not character. This single line drives much of the novel’s conflict.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Austen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique where two characters, ideas, or situations are placed side by side to highlight their differences.">juxtaposition</dfn> to contrast Bingley and Darcy at the assembly. Bingley is open, sociable, and democratic — he dances with everyone and compliments Jane freely. Darcy is reserved, judgmental, and elitist — he speaks to no one outside his own circle and publicly insults Elizabeth. However, Austen complicates this simple contrast as the novel progresses: Bingley’s openness makes him easily manipulated (by his sisters and Darcy), while Darcy’s reserve conceals genuine depth of feeling.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The assembly also establishes Darcy’s £10,000 a year as public knowledge — the neighbourhood initially admires him for his wealth before rejecting him for his manner. Austen shows how quickly public opinion shifts based on superficial impressions, foreshadowing the novel’s broader theme that first impressions are unreliable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Jane & Bingley: The Romantic Parallel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jane and Bingley’s attraction at the assembly is immediate and mutual — but it also carries risk. Jane’s reserved nature means she does not openly display her feelings, which later allows Darcy to convince Bingley that she is indifferent. Austen uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="A secondary storyline that mirrors and illuminates the main plot, often involving different characters facing similar situations.">subplot</dfn> to explore the consequences of emotional restraint: Jane’s propriety nearly costs her the relationship, while Elizabeth’s spirited openness eventually wins Darcy over.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Charlotte Lucas warns Elizabeth: “If a woman conceals her affection with the same skill from the object of it, she may lose the opportunity of fixing him.” Charlotte’s pragmatism contrasts with Elizabeth’s romantic idealism — and Charlotte will later prove her own philosophy by securing Collins through calculated attentiveness.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Elizabeth’s Prejudice Takes Root</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Elizabeth’s response to Darcy’s insult reveals her character immediately. She does not sulk or weep — she laughs. She tells the story with “great spirit,” turning humiliation into comedy. This establishes Elizabeth as witty, resilient, and proudly independent. But it also plants the seed of <dfn class="term" data-def="A preconceived opinion formed without adequate evidence, leading to unfair judgement of a person or situation.">prejudice</dfn>: having been slighted, Elizabeth will interpret everything Darcy does through a negative lens for the first half of the novel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Austen’s original title for the novel was <em>First Impressions</em>, and these opening chapters show why. Both Darcy and Elizabeth form instant, confident judgements about each other — and both are wrong. Darcy dismisses Elizabeth as “tolerable”; Elizabeth dismisses Darcy as “the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world.” The novel’s entire arc is the slow, painful correction of these first impressions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain what Elizabeth's reaction to Darcy's insult reveals about her character — cover at least three personality traits.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The novel was originally titled <em>First Impressions</em>. Elizabeth’s amused reaction to Darcy’s insult reveals her wit, pride, and independence — but also her tendency to form quick judgements. Both protagonists must overcome their initial impressions to achieve understanding, making this opening exchange the structural foundation of the entire plot.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Role of Public Opinion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Austen shows how Regency society formed collective judgements at public gatherings. The assembly ball is not just entertainment — it is a social marketplace where reputations are made. Darcy arrives with £10,000 a year and is “looked at with great admiration for about half the evening.” But when he refuses to dance and appears “above his company,” the entire room turns against him: “his manners gave a disgust which turned the tide of his popularity.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The word “disgust” is powerful — it suggests physical revulsion, not just disapproval. Austen uses this rapid shift in public opinion to foreshadow how appearance and reality will be confused throughout the novel. Wickham will later exploit this same system, winning public approval through charm while concealing his true character.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The Meryton assembly establishes the novel’s central conflict: Darcy’s pride (“She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me”) triggers Elizabeth’s prejudice against him.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Austen uses juxtaposition (Bingley’s openness vs Darcy’s reserve) and the rapid shift in public opinion to introduce the theme that first impressions are unreliable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Elizabeth’s witty, defiant reaction to the insult establishes her as the novel’s moral centre — but also reveals her tendency to judge quickly, the flaw she must overcome.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For Part (a) of Component 2, Section A, if you get an extract from the Meryton assembly, focus on Austen’s use of contrast (Bingley vs Darcy), the ironic narrator’s tone, and the language of social judgement (“tolerable,” “disgust,” “handsome enough”). Always analyse individual word choices — “tempt” in Darcy’s insult implies Elizabeth is beneath his consideration, reducing her to an object of appraisal rather than a person.</p>
