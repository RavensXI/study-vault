--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to identify and analyse the key language techniques poets use to create meaning, mood, and imagery.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Language Analysis Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">AO2 (12 marks) specifically rewards your ability to analyse how poets use language to create effects. This means going beyond naming a technique (“the poet uses a metaphor”) to explaining <em>why</em> that technique works and what it makes the reader feel or understand. The strongest responses zoom in on individual word choices and explore their <dfn class="term" data-def="The associated meanings and feelings that a word carries beyond its dictionary definition. For example, ‘home’ denotes a building but connotes warmth, safety, and belonging.">connotations</dfn> — the layers of meaning beyond the dictionary definition.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: Is it better to analyse two techniques deeply or list ten with no analysis, and what does the examiner want to see?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You do not need to identify every technique in a poem. It is far better to analyse <strong>two or three techniques in depth</strong> than to list ten techniques with no analysis. The examiner wants to see how you think about language, not how many techniques you can name.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Metaphor and Simile</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A direct comparison that states one thing IS another, creating a powerful association between two unlike things. ‘Her voice was a knife’ compares voice to a weapon, suggesting sharpness and the ability to wound.">metaphor</dfn> states that one thing <em>is</em> another; a simile says one thing is <em>like</em> another. When analysing either, always ask: <strong>What qualities are being transferred?</strong> If a poet writes “her voice was a knife,” what qualities of a knife are being applied to the voice? Sharpness, the ability to cut, to wound, to penetrate. This is far more useful than simply writing “the poet uses a metaphor.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Extended metaphors</strong> (also called <dfn class="term" data-def="A metaphor that is developed and sustained over several lines or throughout an entire poem, creating a detailed parallel between two ideas.">conceits</dfn>) run through several lines or the whole poem. If you spot an extended metaphor, it is almost certainly the poem’s central idea and should form the basis of your response.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Personification, Pathetic Fallacy, and Symbolism</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Personification</strong> gives human qualities to non-human things. When a poet writes “the wind whispered secrets,” the wind is given the human ability to whisper and have knowledge. Ask: <em>Why</em> does the poet want us to see this object as alive? Usually to make it seem more powerful, more threatening, or more sympathetic.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of personification in which weather or natural phenomena reflect or mirror the emotions of a character or speaker. Stormy weather during conflict, sunshine during joy.">Pathetic fallacy</dfn></strong> is a specific type of personification where the weather or landscape reflects the speaker’s emotions. Dark skies for sadness, storms for anger, sunshine for joy. It is one of the easiest techniques to spot and analyse in unseen poetry.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Symbolism</strong> is when an object or image represents something beyond itself. A rose might symbolise love; a cage might symbolise imprisonment; a journey might symbolise life. Look for recurring images or objects that seem to carry more significance than their literal meaning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Sound Devices</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Poets choose words partly for how they <em>sound</em>. Sound devices are particularly effective for creating mood and atmosphere, which is a common focus of unseen poetry questions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Repetition of vowel sounds within words in close proximity, creating musicality, harmony, or mood. Long vowels (‘moaning’, ‘slow’) slow the rhythm; short vowels (‘crack’, ‘spit’) quicken it.">Assonance</dfn></strong> is the repetition of vowel sounds. Long vowel sounds (“moaning,” “alone,” “slow”) create a dragging, melancholy mood. Short, sharp vowels (“crack,” “spit,” “hit”) create energy, aggression, or speed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Sibilance</strong> (repetition of “s” sounds) can create a hissing, sinister effect or a soft, soothing whisper, depending on context. <strong>Plosives</strong> (“b,” “d,” “p,” “t,” “k,” “g”) create harsh, explosive sounds that suggest violence, force, or disruption.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What's the difference between saying 'the poet uses sibilance' and explaining how sibilance in 'snake slid silently' aff...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When analysing sound devices, always explain the <strong>effect</strong> of the sound, not just its presence. “The sibilance in ‘the snake slid silently’ creates a hissing sound that mirrors the snake’s movement, making the reader feel its dangerous, stealthy presence” is analysis. “The poet uses sibilance” is feature-spotting.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Semantic Fields</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of words within a text that relate to the same topic or idea, creating a pattern of meaning. For example, words like ‘chains,’ ‘cage,’ ‘locked,’ and ‘trapped’ create a semantic field of imprisonment.">semantic field</dfn> is a group of related words that create a pattern of meaning. If a poem repeatedly uses words like “chains,” “cage,” “locked,” and “trapped,” it has a semantic field of imprisonment, even if it never uses the word “prison.” Identifying semantic fields is one of the most sophisticated skills you can demonstrate, because it shows you are tracking patterns across the whole poem rather than just analysing individual lines.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Analysing Word Choice: A Step-by-Step Method</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Step 1: Pick a word that stands out</strong> — Look for words that seem surprising, unusual, or emotionally charged. Verbs and adjectives are often the richest for analysis.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Step 2: Consider its connotations</strong> — What associations does the word carry? “Crept” connotes stealth, secrecy, fear. “Strode” connotes confidence, power, purpose. The poet chose this word over alternatives — why?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Step 3: Consider its sound</strong> — Does the word’s sound contribute to its meaning? “Crash” sounds violent (plosive “cr” + sharp “ash”). “Murmur” sounds gentle (soft “m” sounds).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step 4: Connect to the poem’s message</strong> — How does this word choice contribute to the overall meaning or mood? Always link back to the poet’s purpose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Always analyse the <em>effect</em> of language techniques, not just their presence. Explain why the poet chose that word or image and what it makes the reader feel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Focus on connotations — the layers of meaning beyond the dictionary definition. This is what separates Level 4-6 responses from Level 1-3.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Look for semantic fields (patterns of related words) across the whole poem — they reveal the poet’s central concerns.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">When you find a powerful image or metaphor, spend time on it. The examiner would rather read one paragraph of deep, exploratory analysis than three paragraphs of surface-level comments. Use phrases like “This could suggest” and “Perhaps the poet is implying” to show that you are exploring multiple interpretations — this is exactly what the top mark bands reward.</p>
