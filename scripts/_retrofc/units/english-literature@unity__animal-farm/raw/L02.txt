<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Old Major’s Speech: Setting the Tone</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter 1 opens with Old Major, the prize-winning boar, calling the animals together for a meeting. His speech is the catalyst for everything that follows. Orwell crafts it as a masterpiece of <dfn class="term" data-def="The art of using language to persuade an audience, using techniques such as ethos, pathos and logos.">rhetoric</dfn> — Old Major uses emotional appeals, logical arguments and personal authority to convince the animals that rebellion is both necessary and just.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Old Major begins by establishing his authority: ‘I have had a long life, I have had much time for thought.’ This is an appeal to <dfn class="term" data-def="A rhetorical appeal to the speaker's character, credibility or authority.">ethos</dfn> — he asks the animals to trust his wisdom. He then shifts to <dfn class="term" data-def="A rhetorical appeal to the audience's emotions.">pathos</dfn>, painting a vivid picture of their suffering: ‘our lives are miserable, laborious, and short.’ The tricolon (list of three) hammers home the bleakness of their existence. He addresses individual animals directly — Clover, the hens, Boxer — making each feel personally included in his argument.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list which two historical figures Old Major represents, plus name the political document his speech mirrors.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Old Major represents both Karl Marx (whose ideas inspired the revolution) and Vladimir Lenin (who led the Bolsheviks to power). His speech mirrors the Communist Manifesto’s call for workers to unite against their oppressors.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The core of Old Major’s argument is a logical appeal: ‘Man is the only creature that consumes without producing.’ This is <dfn class="term" data-def="A rhetorical appeal to logic, reason and evidence.">logos</dfn> — he uses evidence and reasoning to identify Man as the source of all the animals’ suffering. His solution is clear: ‘Remove Man from the scene, and the root cause of hunger and overwork is abolished for ever.’ The simplicity of this argument is what makes it so powerful — and so dangerous, because it ignores the possibility that the animals themselves could become oppressors.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Old Major uses inclusive language (‘comrades’) to create unity. He employs rhetorical questions: ‘Is it not crystal clear, then, comrades, that all the evils of this life of ours spring from the tyranny of human beings?’ He uses repetition for emphasis: ‘All men are enemies. All animals are comrades.’ The binary opposition of Man versus Animal simplifies a complex political situation into a clear call to action. He also establishes rules that foreshadow the Seven Commandments: ‘No animal must ever live in a house, or sleep in a bed, or wear clothes, or drink alcohol.’</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">‘Beasts of England’: The Anthem of Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Old Major’s speech culminates in the song ‘Beasts of England’, which paints a <dfn class="term" data-def="A perfect world, free of corruption and injustice.">utopian</dfn> vision of a future without human oppression: ‘Bright will shine the fields of England, / Purer shall its waters be.’ The song serves a crucial structural purpose — it becomes the emotional glue that binds the animals to the revolutionary cause. Its later banning by Napoleon (Chapter 7) symbolises how the revolution has been completely betrayed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Rebellion and the Seven Commandments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In Chapter 2, the rebellion happens almost spontaneously when Jones forgets to feed the animals. The trigger is neglect, not planning — mirroring how the February Revolution in Russia was sparked by food shortages rather than organised revolt. After expelling Jones, the pigs immediately take charge. They have already developed Old Major’s ideas into a system called ‘Animalism’ and distilled the principles into Seven Commandments, painted on the barn wall.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this box and write out the original Seven Commandment about killing, then its altered version with the added phrase.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Seven Commandments are a structural device. Orwell uses their gradual alteration throughout the novella to track the pigs’ increasing corruption — from ‘No animal shall kill any other animal’ to ‘No animal shall kill any other animal <em>without cause</em>.’</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The commandments include rules such as ‘Whatever goes upon two legs is an enemy’ and ‘All animals are equal.’ These encode Old Major’s ideals in written form. However, the very act of writing them gives the pigs power — they are the only animals literate enough to read and interpret the rules, foreshadowing their manipulation of language to maintain control.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Early Signs of Corruption</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Orwell plants the seeds of corruption immediately. At the end of Chapter 2, Napoleon takes charge of the milk: ‘Never mind the milk, comrades!’ he cries, before sending the animals away to work. When they return, ‘it was noticed that the milk had disappeared.’ The <dfn class="term" data-def="A grammatical construction where the subject receives the action, often used to obscure who performed it.">passive voice</dfn> here is deliberate — Orwell avoids saying who noticed or who took the milk, mirroring how authoritarian regimes obscure their own corruption.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Several moments foreshadow the later betrayal. Squealer is introduced as a pig who ‘could turn black into white’ — a metaphor for his ability to make lies seem like truth. The dogs snap at the rats during Old Major’s speech, hinting at the violence that will later enforce Napoleon’s rule. Moses the raven tells stories of ‘Sugarcandy Mountain’ (representing religion as a tool to pacify the masses). The pigs’ immediate assumption of leadership — without being elected — mirrors the Bolsheviks’ seizure of power.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what disappears in Chapter 2 and write Napoleon's exact distraction quote about the harvest.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The disappearance of the milk at the end of Chapter 2 is the first concrete sign of the pigs’ corruption. Napoleon uses distraction (‘The harvest is more important’) to divert attention — a tactic that mirrors how authoritarian governments redirect public focus away from their abuses.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Old Major’s speech uses ethos, pathos and logos to inspire rebellion. He represents Marx and Lenin, and his idealistic vision contrasts sharply with what the revolution actually becomes.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The Seven Commandments are a structural device — their gradual alteration tracks the corruption of the revolution throughout the novella.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Orwell foreshadows the pigs’ betrayal from the very start: the passive voice, the disappearing milk, and Squealer’s ability to ‘turn black into white’ all signal what is to come.</li></ul>
