Napoleon's forced confessions, mass executions, and the banning of Beasts of England.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Purges Begin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter Seven marks the darkest turning point in <em>Animal Farm</em>. Facing food shortages and growing unrest, Napoleon responds not with reform but with <dfn class="term" data-def="The violent removal of political opponents, often through execution or imprisonment, to consolidate a leader's power.">terror</dfn>. He summons all the animals to the yard and, with his dogs at his side, forces a series of confessions. The four pigs who had protested against Napoleon’s abolition of Sunday meetings are the first to confess to conspiring with Snowball. The dogs ‘promptly tore their throats out.’</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This triggers a cascade of confessions. Hens, geese, and sheep all come forward to admit to various crimes — stealing corn, urinating in the drinking pool, killing an old ram. Each confession is followed by immediate execution. Orwell writes that ‘the air was heavy with the smell of blood, which had been unknown there since Jones was expelled.’ The sensory language creates an overwhelming atmosphere of horror, and the reference to Jones is devastating: the revolution has produced something just as brutal as what it replaced.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the four specific propaganda techniques Squealer uses in his speech, plus name the Soviet newspaper that used similar methods.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The purges in Chapter Seven directly parallel Stalin’s <strong>Great Purge</strong> (1936–1938), during which millions of Soviet citizens were imprisoned, forced to confess to fabricated crimes, and executed. Public ‘show trials’ were staged to create the illusion that Stalin was protecting the nation from internal enemies.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Language of Brutality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Orwell uses visceral, violent language to convey the horror of the executions. The dogs ‘seized four of the pigs by the ear’ and the victims came forward ‘trembling, with guilt written on every line of their countenances.’ The pigs are described as ‘squealing with pain and terror’ — a word choice that reduces them to their animal nature and strips them of dignity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The phrase ‘the dogs had tasted blood, and for a few moments they appeared to go quite mad’ is particularly chilling. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which human qualities are attributed to animals or objects.">personification</dfn> of the dogs as blood-crazed killers mirrors the behaviour of the NKVD, Stalin’s secret police, who carried out executions with increasing brutality. ‘A pile of corpses lying before Napoleon’s feet’ creates a stark visual image of Napoleon as a tyrant surveying his victims.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Stalin launched the Great Purge because he was deeply paranoid about losing control. He blamed economic failures — particularly the disastrous results of his Five-Year Plans — on ‘saboteurs’ and ‘enemies of the people.’ The NKVD (secret police) arrested thousands, extracting <dfn class="term" data-def="Admissions of guilt obtained through torture, threats, or psychological pressure, often to crimes the person did not commit.">forced confessions</dfn> through torture and the threat of death.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Nikolai Yezhov, head of the NKVD from 1936 to 1938, organised mass arrests and executions on Stalin’s orders. After serving his purpose, Yezhov himself was arrested and executed in 1940 — then erased from official photographs. This ‘rewriting of history’ directly parallels the pigs’ constant revision of events on Animal Farm.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Scapegoating Snowball</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Squealer’s propaganda reaches its most manipulative in Chapter Seven. He announces that Snowball ‘has sold himself to Frederick of Pinchfield Farm’ and was ‘in league with Jones from the very start.’ This <dfn class="term" data-def="Unfairly blaming a person or group for problems caused by others, diverting attention from the real source of failure.">scapegoating</dfn> rewrites history entirely — the animals saw Snowball fight bravely at the Battle of the Cowshed, yet Squealer insists that was ‘part of the arrangement.’</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Even Boxer questions this: ‘I do not believe that. Snowball fought bravely at the Battle of the Cowshed. I saw him myself.’ But Squealer counters by invoking Napoleon’s absolute authority: ‘Our Leader, Comrade Napoleon, has stated categorically — categorically, comrade — that Snowball was Jones’s agent from the very beginning.’ Boxer’s response — ‘If Comrade Napoleon says it, it must be right’ — is devastating. It shows how even the most loyal and well-meaning animals can be manipulated into accepting obvious lies.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this box and recall the exact years of Stalin's Great Purge, what happened to millions of citizens, and what type of trials were staged.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Squealer’s speech uses multiple <dfn class="term" data-def="Biased or misleading information used to promote a political cause or point of view.">propaganda</dfn> techniques: <strong>fear</strong> (‘Snowball’s secret agents are lurking among us’), <strong>scapegoating</strong> (blaming Snowball for all problems), <strong>appeal to authority</strong> (‘Comrade Napoleon has stated categorically’), and <strong>loaded language</strong> (‘in sheer malignity’). These parallel techniques used by Soviet newspaper <em>Pravda</em> under Stalin.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Banning ‘Beasts of England’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">After the executions, the surviving animals huddle together in shock. Clover looks out over the farm and reflects that ‘these scenes of terror and slaughter were not what they had looked forward to on that night when Old Major first stirred them to rebellion.’ This moment of clarity is heartbreaking — but also futile. Clover cannot articulate her thoughts and lacks the words to challenge the pigs.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Is ‘Beasts of England’ Banned?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The animals begin singing ‘Beasts of England’ — the revolutionary anthem that once inspired their dreams of freedom. Squealer announces that the song is now <strong>banned</strong>. His justification is that ‘the Rebellion is now completed’ and the song is ‘no longer needed.’ In reality, the song is dangerous because it reminds the animals of the revolution’s original promises — promises that Napoleon has comprehensively broken.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The replacement anthem, composed by Minimus, is a hollow piece of propaganda praising ‘Animal Farm, Animal Farm, never through me shalt thou come to harm.’ Where ‘Beasts of England’ was about liberation, the new song is about obedience. Orwell shows how totalitarian regimes suppress symbols of resistance and replace them with state-approved alternatives.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The purges in Chapter Seven parallel Stalin’s Great Purge. Orwell uses graphic, visceral language (‘tore their throats out,’ ‘the air was heavy with the smell of blood’) to convey the horror of <dfn class="term" data-def="Cruel and oppressive government or rule.">tyranny</dfn>.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Squealer’s scapegoating of Snowball uses multiple propaganda techniques — fear, loaded language, appeal to authority — to rewrite history and silence dissent.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The banning of ‘Beasts of England’ symbolises the death of the revolution’s original ideals. Totalitarian regimes suppress symbols of resistance to prevent people from remembering what was promised.</li></ul>
