=== L9 Elizabethan society: education and leisure ===

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Home tuition, petty and grammar schools, and the sports, pastimes and theatre of Elizabethan life.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">For most Elizabethans, formal education was a privilege determined by social rank, gender and where you lived. A nobleman&rsquo;s son might be tutored at home in Latin, Greek and the courtly arts; a yeoman&rsquo;s daughter might learn only enough reading to follow the Bible. Yet the second half of the sixteenth century saw a real expansion of schooling &mdash; over 130 new <dfn class="term" data-def="A school for boys aged about 7 to 14 that taught Latin grammar, classical literature and rhetoric, often founded by guilds, towns or wealthy individuals.">grammar schools</dfn> were founded in Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign. Beyond the classroom, the same decades produced bear-baiting pits, real tennis courts and the first purpose-built theatres in London. Education and leisure together reveal how rigidly Elizabethan society was stratified by class &mdash; and how much it was changing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n2" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three places a child might be educated in Elizabethan England, and write one detail of what each taught.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabethan education was layered by class and gender. Most children learned at home; nobility hired private tutors; some boys attended petty schools (ages 5&ndash;7) for basic reading and writing; grammar schools (ages 7&ndash;14) taught Latin to fee-paying or scholarship boys; girls were rarely formally schooled beyond reading.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Education in the Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">For nobility and gentry, the home was the main classroom. Boys destined for politics or the Church were taught Latin, Greek, French, dancing, riding and music by live-in tutors. Girls of the same rank learned reading, writing, music and needlework, alongside how to manage a large household. Elizabeth herself had been tutored at home by Roger Ascham &mdash; a humanist who later wrote that her Greek was better than most clergymen&rsquo;s.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For the lower orders, &lsquo;education&rsquo; meant practical training. A yeoman farmer&rsquo;s child learned to plough, sew or brew alongside parents. From the age of about seven, many poorer boys entered an <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal arrangement where a young person lived with a master craftsman for around seven years, learning a trade in exchange for labour.">apprenticeship</dfn> with a master craftsman, lasting around seven years. Girls usually went into domestic service. The Bible &mdash; in English after the 1559 Settlement &mdash; was often the only book a household owned, and parents who could read used it to teach the alphabet.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Petty Schools and Grammar Schools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A small minority of children attended <dfn class="term" data-def="Local elementary schools, often run from a teacher&rsquo;s home or a church porch, that taught children aged around 5 to 7 basic reading, writing and arithmetic.">petty schools</dfn> from around age five. These were run from a parish room or a teacher&rsquo;s home, often by a clergyman&rsquo;s wife, and taught reading, writing and a little arithmetic using a <em>hornbook</em> &mdash; a wooden paddle showing the alphabet under a thin layer of horn. The Lord&rsquo;s Prayer and Ten Commandments were standard texts, doubling as religious instruction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">From age seven, the academically able sons of the gentry, merchants and prosperous yeomen could move on to a grammar school. The day ran from 6am to 5pm in summer, with two short breaks. Pupils studied Latin grammar, classical literature (Cicero, Virgil, Ovid), rhetoric, and some Greek. Discipline was harsh: birching for poor work was routine. Fees varied &mdash; a few free places existed for poor boys with academic promise &mdash; but most pupils were the sons of those who could afford them. A handful of the brightest could go on to the universities of Oxford or Cambridge from around age fourteen.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Students sometimes assume that because grammar schools spread, most Elizabethans could read. They could not. By 1588 perhaps 30 per cent of men and far fewer women could sign their own name &mdash; and that was the rough test for basic literacy. Schooling was concentrated on noble and gentry boys, and London literacy ran higher than in rural Yorkshire or Cornwall. The expansion of schools matters because it widened access, not because it produced a reading public.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Sport and Pastimes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Leisure was as stratified as education. The wealthy hunted deer with hounds, hawked, played real tennis on enclosed courts, fenced and bowled on smooth lawns. Elizabeth was a keen rider and dancer, and the court&rsquo;s entertainments shaped national fashion. Working people had less time and rougher pleasures. Football was played between villages with whole parishes as teams &mdash; effectively a brawl with a leather ball &mdash; and was repeatedly banned by local authorities for the injuries it caused.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The most popular spectator sports were violent. <dfn class="term" data-def="A spectator sport in which a chained bear was attacked by trained mastiffs while crowds bet on the outcome; popular across all social classes in Elizabethan England.">Bear-baiting</dfn> drew crowds in Southwark, where chained bears fought packs of mastiffs while spectators wagered on the outcome. Cock-fighting, dog-fighting and bull-baiting were similar. The poor played skittles, dice and cards in alehouses; the gentry played chess and a fast-paced gambling card game called Primero. Dancing &mdash; from sedate court pavanes to leaping country galliards &mdash; crossed every social rank.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four Elizabethan pastimes, marking each as either upper-class or popular &mdash; and one shared by both.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabethan leisure split sharply by class. Hunting, hawking, real tennis, fencing and bowls were upper-class pursuits; football, bear-baiting, cock-fighting, skittles and alehouse cards were popular pastimes. Music and dancing were shared. The first purpose-built theatres in London opened from 1576, drawing audiences from every rank.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Theatre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Before Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign, plays were performed by travelling companies in inn yards or great halls. In 1576 James Burbage built the first permanent <dfn class="term" data-def="Round, partly open-air buildings constructed from 1576 in London (e.g. The Theatre, The Curtain, The Rose, The Globe in 1599) where companies performed plays for paying audiences of all classes.">playhouse</dfn> in London &mdash; called simply The Theatre &mdash; just outside the city walls in Shoreditch, beyond the reach of the Puritan-leaning London corporation. The Curtain followed in 1577 and Philip Henslowe&rsquo;s Rose opened in 1587 on Bankside. Companies needed a noble patron to perform legally: the Lord Chamberlain&rsquo;s Men and the Lord Admiral&rsquo;s Men were the leading troupes by 1588.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A penny got you into the standing yard with the &lsquo;groundlings&rsquo;; another penny bought a seat in the galleries; a third let you sit on the stage itself. Audiences mixed apprentices with merchants and the occasional nobleman. Plays mixed bawdy comedy, history and tragedy &mdash; Christopher Marlowe&rsquo;s <em>Tamburlaine</em> filled houses from 1587, and a young Shakespeare arrived in London at the end of the decade.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Puritans loathed the playhouses. They argued that crowds spread plague, that boys playing women&rsquo;s parts encouraged immorality, that pickpockets and prostitutes worked the audiences, and that plays distracted apprentices from their work. The City of London authorities tried repeatedly to close the theatres, which is why Burbage built outside the city walls in the first place. Elizabeth herself enjoyed plays at court, and her favour &mdash; through the licensing system &mdash; kept the theatres open against Puritan pressure. The very feature that made the theatre suspect (mixing classes in one space) is what makes it useful evidence of a more shared popular culture by 1588.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Education and leisure together show two truths about Elizabethan society. First, class and gender governed almost everything &mdash; what you read, what you played, where you sat in the theatre. Second, the boundaries were starting to bend. More boys reached grammar schools than ever before, and the new playhouses brought apprentices and gentry into the same building, even if they paid different prices. By the time the Armada threatened in 1588, the cultural life of Elizabethan England was wider, noisier and more confident than the cautious country Elizabeth had inherited in 1558.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Education was tightly stratified: home tuition for nobility, petty schools and 130+ new grammar schools for boys of the &lsquo;middling sort&rsquo;, apprenticeships for poorer boys, and very limited formal schooling for girls.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Leisure split by class &mdash; hunting, real tennis and bowls for the wealthy; football, bear-baiting and alehouse games for the poor &mdash; though music, dancing and (after 1576) the theatre crossed those lines.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">The first purpose-built playhouses opened in London from 1576 (The Theatre, The Curtain, The Rose by 1587), protected by noble patronage from Puritan and city opposition, and helped create a shared popular culture by 1588.</li></ul>
