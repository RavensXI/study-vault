--- DESCRIPTION ---
Spanish invasion plans, the key events of the Armada campaign, and why England won.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In May 1588 the Spanish Armada sailed from Lisbon: 130 ships, around 24,000 sailors and soldiers, under the reluctant command of the <dfn class="term" data-def="Reluctant Spanish nobleman appointed to command the 1588 Armada after the death of the experienced admiral Santa Cruz; not a sailor by background.">Duke of Medina Sidonia</dfn>. Philip II had ordered the largest amphibious invasion ever attempted in northern Europe. Eight weeks later the surviving fleet was being scattered by storms north of Scotland, and roughly half of it would never reach Spain. Examiners reward students who can explain why &mdash; weighing English ships, Spanish supply, leadership, the fireships at Calais and the storms together, not picking one factor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n2" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the four main reasons the Armada failed, plus one short specific detail for each reason.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Armada failed for four interconnected reasons: English ship design and gunnery (longer-range guns and faster &lsquo;race-built&rsquo; galleons designed by Hawkins), the failure of the &lsquo;join with Parma&rsquo; plan, English fireships at Calais on the night of 7&ndash;8 August 1588, and the storms that scattered the fleet around Scotland and Ireland in September.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Spanish Invasion Plans</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Philip II&rsquo;s plan rested on a difficult piece of geography. The Armada would sail from Lisbon up the Channel to Calais. There it would meet the Duke of Parma&rsquo;s 30,000 veteran troops in the Spanish Netherlands, escort them across the Channel in unprotected barges, and land in Kent. Once Parma&rsquo;s army was on English soil, Catholic English subjects would (Philip hoped) rise up. The fleet was therefore not just a war fleet but a transport bodyguard.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Two structural problems hurt the plan from the start. First, the &lsquo;original&rsquo; commander Santa Cruz &mdash; an experienced admiral &mdash; died in February 1588. Philip replaced him with the Duke of Medina Sidonia, a senior nobleman who frankly admitted in writing that he had no naval experience. Second, the rendezvous with Parma assumed perfect timing: the Armada had to arrive off Calais exactly when Parma&rsquo;s barges were ready. There was no deep-water port on the Flemish coast where the great Spanish galleons could shelter while they waited.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The English Fleet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Lord High Admiral, Lord Howard of Effingham, commanded around 200 English ships from Plymouth. Drake was vice-admiral; Hawkins and Frobisher led other squadrons. Sir John Hawkins had spent the 1570s and 1580s redesigning English warships into <dfn class="term" data-def="A streamlined English warship design pioneered by John Hawkins; faster and more manoeuvrable than the older high-fronted galleons and capable of firing broadsides at longer range.">race-built galleons</dfn> &mdash; lower hulls, no high &lsquo;castles&rsquo; at bow and stern, and a long line of long-range guns down each side. The Spanish fleet, by contrast, was designed to grapple-and-board: bigger, taller, packed with soldiers, but slower and with shorter-range cannon mounted differently.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The English knew they could not stop the Armada by ramming or boarding. Their plan was to use longer-range gunfire to keep the fight at a distance, harass the formation, and use the wind to attack from the rear &mdash; never letting Spain force a close-quarters fight on Spanish terms.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Channel Battles, 31 July &ndash; 6 August</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Armada was first sighted off the Lizard on 19 July. From 31 July to 6 August the two fleets fought a running battle up the English Channel: skirmishes off Plymouth (31 July), Portland Bill (2 August) and the Isle of Wight (3&ndash;4 August). The Spanish kept their disciplined crescent formation; the English snapped at the wings, picked off a few stragglers (most notably the <em>Rosario</em>, captured by Drake), but never broke the Spanish line. By 6 August the Armada was anchored off Calais, waiting for word from Parma.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Fireships at Calais, Night of 7&ndash;8 August</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Parma&rsquo;s news was bad: his barges were not ready, and the Dutch &lsquo;Sea Beggars&rsquo; were blockading the inshore waters around Dunkirk. The Armada could not approach to take Parma&rsquo;s troops on board; Parma could not come out. Lord Howard chose his moment. On the night of 7&ndash;8 August he sent eight unmanned <dfn class="term" data-def="Old or empty ships deliberately set on fire and drifted into an enemy fleet to spread chaos and force ships to scatter; the English used eight at Calais on the night of 7&ndash;8 August 1588.">fireships</dfn>, packed with pitch and gunpowder and set on fire, drifting on the tide into the anchored Spanish fleet.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The fireships caused little physical damage &mdash; the Spanish cut their anchor cables and scattered &mdash; but the loss of formation was disastrous. Anchors were left on the seabed off Calais. The crescent never reformed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Battle of Gravelines, 8 August</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The next day the English caught the broken Armada off Gravelines and fought the only sustained pitched battle of the campaign. Closing to within musket range, the English used their long-range guns to hammer the Spanish hulls for eight hours. Three Spanish galleons were sunk or driven aground; many others were badly damaged. The Spanish formation was wrecked, and the wind swung to the south-west, pushing the Armada into the North Sea, away from Parma, and away from any chance of return.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Long Way Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Medina Sidonia took the only route left: north around Scotland and west of Ireland to reach Spain. In September violent Atlantic storms scattered the fleet. The unseasoned water casks &mdash; a direct legacy of Drake&rsquo;s 1587 raid on Cadiz &mdash; leaked, leaving crews dehydrated. Around two dozen ships were wrecked on the Scottish and Irish coasts; thousands of sailors and soldiers drowned or were killed by Crown soldiers when they crawled ashore. Of 130 ships that left Lisbon, fewer than 70 made it home, and most of those were unfit for further service.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the date and place of the fireships, what they actually destroyed, and what they prevented.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On the night of 7&ndash;8 August 1588, Lord Howard sent eight English fireships into the anchored Spanish fleet at Calais. The fireships destroyed almost nothing but broke the Armada&rsquo;s formation, prevented the planned link-up with Parma, and exposed the fleet to the English broadsides at Gravelines the next day.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Why the English Won &mdash; Weighing the Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A weak answer says &lsquo;the weather beat the Armada&rsquo;. Storms certainly destroyed the fleet on the way home, but by then the campaign was already lost &mdash; the storms killed a defeated Armada, not a winning one. The deeper causes interconnect.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n21">Edexcel examiners specifically flag &lsquo;the weather beat the Armada&rsquo; as a single-factor answer that caps below the top band. The storms hit AFTER the campaign was already decided. By 9 August the Armada had lost its formation at Calais, been mauled at Gravelines, missed Parma, and been blown into the North Sea. Top-band answers explain that the storms were the killing blow on a fleet that English ship design, English fireships, Spanish supply failures and the broken Parma plan had already defeated. Storms are part of the answer, not the answer.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n22">English advantages came from preparation. Hawkins&rsquo;s race-built galleons gave the fleet superior speed and gunnery. Drake&rsquo;s Cadiz raid the previous April had destroyed the seasoned timber for water casks, leaving the Armada with leaking supplies. Lord Howard chose his moments well &mdash; the fireships at Calais were the single most important tactical decision of the campaign. Spanish problems were structural: Medina Sidonia&rsquo;s inexperience, the missing deep-water port for the Parma rendezvous, the impossible coordination required between Lisbon and the Netherlands, and the Dutch Sea Beggars blockading the Flemish coast. The storms then finished the work the campaign had already begun.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Philip II&rsquo;s plan was to sail the Armada (130 ships, 24,000 men under Medina Sidonia) up the Channel to escort the Duke of Parma&rsquo;s 30,000 troops from the Netherlands to invade Kent.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">The plan broke at Calais on the night of 7&ndash;8 August 1588 when English fireships scattered the Armada&rsquo;s formation, preventing the link-up with Parma; the next day Lord Howard&rsquo;s fleet hammered the broken formation at Gravelines.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">English victory was multi-factor &mdash; Hawkins&rsquo;s race-built galleons and gunnery, Drake&rsquo;s 1587 Cadiz raid destroying water-cask timber, leadership, the failure of the Parma rendezvous, and finally the storms that wrecked the retreating fleet around Scotland and Ireland.</li></ul>
