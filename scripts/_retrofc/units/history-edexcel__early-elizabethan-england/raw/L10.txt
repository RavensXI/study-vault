=== L10 The 'problem' of the poor ===

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why poverty and vagabondage rose under Elizabeth, and how attitudes and policies changed by 1588.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Behind the courtly image of an Elizabethan &lsquo;Golden Age&rsquo; sat a sharp social crisis. The numbers on the move &mdash; tramping the roads in search of work, food or shelter &mdash; rose dramatically between 1558 and 1588. Contemporaries called these wandering poor <dfn class="term" data-def="Wandering, able-bodied poor people moving between parishes in search of work or charity; treated by Tudor law as potentially criminal.">vagabonds</dfn>, and they frightened the propertied classes far more than they ever frightened the queen. The story of how Elizabeth&rsquo;s government responded &mdash; from whippings in 1572 to compulsory rates for the poor by 1601 &mdash; is the story of attitudes hardening, then thawing, into the first national system of poor relief in English history.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n2" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three causes of rising poverty in Elizabethan England, and one law passed in response.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabethan poverty rose because of population growth (from c.3 million in 1558 to c.4 million by 1600), rising prices, sheep enclosures replacing arable farming, the dissolution of the monasteries (which had given charity), bad harvests in the 1580s, and the demobilisation of soldiers from foreign wars. Elizabethans split the poor into the &lsquo;impotent&rsquo; (deserving) and the &lsquo;sturdy beggars&rsquo; or vagabonds (undeserving).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why Was Poverty Rising?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Several pressures combined. England&rsquo;s population grew by roughly a third in the second half of the sixteenth century &mdash; without a matching growth in food production. Demand pushed prices up; <dfn class="term" data-def="The sustained rise in food and goods prices across the sixteenth century, especially severe under Elizabeth, which outpaced wages and pushed many wage-earners into poverty.">price inflation</dfn> hit wage labourers hardest because their pay rose much more slowly than the cost of bread. Contemporaries blamed wickedness or laziness; modern historians point to demographic and economic causes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Land use shifted. Wool prices made sheep more profitable than crops, so landowners enclosed common fields with hedges and ditches. <dfn class="term" data-def="The conversion of open arable fields and common land into hedged or fenced fields, often used for sheep, which displaced rural labourers and reduced the land available for subsistence farming.">Enclosure</dfn> displaced labourers who had relied on common land for grazing and gleaning, and one shepherd could now manage land that had previously employed a dozen ploughmen. After Henry VIII&rsquo;s dissolution of the monasteries in the 1530s, the network of monastic almshouses, hospitals and direct charity that had cushioned medieval poverty was largely gone &mdash; the gap had still not been filled by Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign. Bad harvests in 1586 and 1587 pushed already-fragile households over the edge, and demobilised soldiers returning from the Netherlands and Ireland added to the wandering population.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Deserving and the Undeserving</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Tudor authorities did not see &lsquo;the poor&rsquo; as one group. They drew a sharp moral line. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Tudor term for those unable to work through age, sickness or disability, regarded as deserving of parish charity.">impotent poor</dfn> &mdash; the old, the sick, orphaned children, those with disabilities &mdash; were seen as deserving help. The able-bodied poor who refused work were <dfn class="term" data-def="Able-bodied poor, especially those moving between parishes; regarded by Tudor authorities as wilfully idle and dangerous, and punished severely under successive vagabondage laws.">sturdy beggars</dfn> or vagabonds &mdash; suspected of being criminal, idle and dangerous. The line between &lsquo;cannot work&rsquo; and &lsquo;will not work&rsquo; was not always honest, but it shaped every policy that followed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Students sometimes absorb a &lsquo;Golden Age&rsquo; image of Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign &mdash; The Theatre, Drake, fashion, Gloriana &mdash; and skip the same decades&rsquo; sharp rise in poverty. Both are true at the same time. Drake circumnavigated the globe in 1577&ndash;80 while the 1572 Vagabonds Act ordered first-offence vagabonds to be whipped and burned through the ear. The Edexcel spec deliberately puts poverty alongside exploration and Virginia in Key topic 3 to make this judgement testable. Top-band answers acknowledge both, rather than picking one image and writing about it alone.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Attitudes Hardening: The 1572 Vagabonds Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Early Elizabethan policy was punitive. The 1547 Vagrancy Act under Edward VI had even allowed vagabonds to be branded and enslaved &mdash; that was repealed, but the instinct to terrify wanderers off the roads survived. Elizabeth&rsquo;s 1572 Vagabonds Act ordered that any able-bodied person caught wandering without work could be whipped and bored through the right ear with a hot iron for a first offence. A second offence could mean execution. Travelling actors needed a noble patron, otherwise they too could be treated as vagabonds &mdash; the same Act that protected Burbage&rsquo;s playhouse companies threatened those without a master.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The 1572 Act made one important new decision: it required Justices of the Peace in every parish to keep a register of the &lsquo;impotent poor&rsquo; and to set a compulsory <dfn class="term" data-def="A compulsory local tax on property-owning parishioners, introduced under Elizabeth, used to fund relief for the impotent poor of the parish.">poor rate</dfn> &mdash; a local tax &mdash; to support them. For the first time, helping the deserving poor was not optional charity but a legal duty on every parish.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Attitudes Softening: The 1576 Poor Relief Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Within four years the tone shifted. The 1576 Poor Relief Act made parishes provide raw materials &mdash; wool, flax, hemp, iron &mdash; on which the able-bodied poor could be set to work. Those who refused work were sent to a <dfn class="term" data-def="Local institutions where able-bodied poor were forced to do hard labour as punishment for refusing work; often called &lsquo;Bridewells&rsquo; after the prototype near Fleet Street, London.">House of Correction</dfn> (also called a Bridewell, after the prototype in London) for hard labour. The change was small but significant: the law now recognised that some people genuinely wanted to work but could not find it. Punishment remained, but provision for honest labour appeared alongside it.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why did attitudes start to shift?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A few currents pushed in the same direction. Towns such as Norwich and Ipswich had already pioneered local schemes that distinguished the genuinely unemployed from the wilfully idle &mdash; Norwich&rsquo;s 1570 census of the poor identified deserving recipients ward by ward. Protestant clergy preached that the poor were Christ&rsquo;s neighbours, not just a public-order problem. And the simple visibility of unemployed but able-bodied men &mdash; demobilised soldiers, displaced labourers &mdash; made it harder to dismiss everyone on the road as a wilful vagabond. By the late 1580s, JPs were registering the impotent poor, levying rates, and putting the able-bodied to work in their own parishes rather than driving them away.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Where Things Stood by 1588</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By the year of the Armada, the framework of an early national system was in place. Each parish kept its own poor on its own books. Vagabonds caught moving between parishes were still whipped and returned to their place of birth. The deserving impotent received pensions, food and small payments funded by the compulsory poor rate. The able-bodied were given materials and put to work, with the House of Correction as the threat for those who refused. The fully developed Poor Laws of 1597 and 1601 &mdash; the famous Elizabethan Poor Law &mdash; built directly on these foundations laid between 1572 and 1576.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the date of each Elizabethan poor law in this lesson, and what each one did differently from the previous one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1572 Vagabonds Act introduced harsh punishments (whipping, burning through the ear) but also required JPs to keep a register of the impotent poor and levy a compulsory poor rate. The 1576 Poor Relief Act required parishes to provide work materials for the able-bodied, with Houses of Correction for those who refused. Together they laid the foundations for the 1601 Elizabethan Poor Law.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Two changes ran beneath the legislation. The first was a slow recognition that poverty had causes &mdash; harvest failure, demobilisation, enclosure, inflation &mdash; that were beyond an individual&rsquo;s control. The second was the principle that the parish, funded by compulsory taxation of its better-off inhabitants, carried legal responsibility for its own poor. Neither idea was fully accepted in 1588, and both would harden into law only after Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign &mdash; but both were in place before the Armada sailed.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Poverty rose under Elizabeth because of population growth, price inflation, enclosure, the dissolution of the monasteries, bad harvests in 1586&ndash;87 and demobilised soldiers &mdash; pressures that contemporaries blamed on idleness but historians explain economically.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Elizabethans split the poor into the &lsquo;impotent&rsquo; (deserving of help) and the &lsquo;sturdy beggars&rsquo; or vagabonds (undeserving and punished).</li><li data-narration-id="n23">The 1572 Vagabonds Act introduced harsh punishments but also required parishes to keep a register of the impotent poor and levy a poor rate; the 1576 Poor Relief Act required work materials and Houses of Correction &mdash; together laying foundations for the 1601 Poor Law.</li></ul>
