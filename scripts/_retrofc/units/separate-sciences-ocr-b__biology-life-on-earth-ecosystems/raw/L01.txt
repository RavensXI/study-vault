--- DESCRIPTION ---
How producers turn light into chemical energy, and what limits the rate.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Producers and the Flow of Energy</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">All food chains begin with a <dfn class="term" data-def="An organism that makes its own food using an external energy source such as sunlight. Green plants and algae are the main producers in most ecosystems.">producer</dfn>. Green plants and algae are producers because they capture light energy and use it to manufacture glucose &mdash; a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which plants and algae use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. It occurs in the chloroplasts.">photosynthesis</dfn>. Without producers, no energy would enter the food web and life as we know it could not exist. This makes understanding photosynthesis central to understanding ecosystems.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Photosynthesis is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, such as light energy absorbed during photosynthesis.">endothermic</dfn> reaction: it takes in energy from the surroundings rather than releasing it. The light energy absorbed by the green pigment <dfn class="term" data-def="The green pigment found inside chloroplasts that absorbs light energy and uses it to drive the reactions of photosynthesis.">chlorophyll</dfn> is converted into chemical energy stored in glucose molecules. This stored energy can then be released by respiration &mdash; in the plant itself and in every consumer that feeds on the plant.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: the word equation and balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis. Label which side is reactants and which is products, then identify the inputs and outputs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Photosynthesis is endothermic and occurs in chloroplasts:<br>
  carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (using light energy)<br>
  \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\)</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Inside the Chloroplast</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Photosynthesis occurs in <dfn class="term" data-def="Membrane-bound organelles found in plant cells that contain chlorophyll and are the site of photosynthesis.">chloroplasts</dfn>, which are found mainly in the mesophyll cells of leaves. Chloroplasts contain stacks of membrane discs called grana, which have a large surface area for capturing light. Chlorophyll molecules embedded in these membranes absorb light &mdash; mainly red and blue wavelengths &mdash; which is why leaves appear green (green light is reflected, not absorbed).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Photosynthesis can be thought of in two stages. In the first stage, light energy is used to split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a by-product. In the second stage, the hydrogen ions released are used to convert carbon dioxide into glucose. Both stages take place within the chloroplast. The oxygen released enters the atmosphere, making photosynthesis the source of almost all atmospheric oxygen on Earth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What Happens to Glucose in the Plant?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Once glucose is produced, the plant uses it in several ways:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Respiration</strong> &mdash; glucose is broken down to transfer energy for the plant&rsquo;s own metabolic processes, such as active transport and protein synthesis.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Starch storage</strong> &mdash; excess glucose is converted to <dfn class="term" data-def="An insoluble polysaccharide used by plants to store glucose. Because it is insoluble, it does not affect the cell's water potential by osmosis.">starch</dfn>, which is insoluble and can be stored without drawing water into cells by osmosis.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Cellulose</strong> &mdash; glucose is polymerised into cellulose for cell wall construction, giving the plant structural support.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Amino acids and proteins</strong> &mdash; by combining glucose with nitrate ions absorbed from the soil, the plant makes amino acids, which are assembled into proteins for growth and enzymes.</li>
</ul>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Plants absorb <dfn class="term" data-def="Charged particles of mineral elements dissolved in water, absorbed by plants through root hair cells for use in growth and metabolic processes.">mineral ions</dfn> from the soil through their root hair cells by active transport. Nitrate ions (NO₃⁻) are used to make amino acids and proteins, and hence DNA. A deficiency in nitrate leads to stunted growth and yellowing of leaves because the plant cannot make adequate protein.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Magnesium ions (Mg²⁺) are needed to make chlorophyll. Without sufficient magnesium, chlorophyll production falls and leaves turn pale yellow-green &mdash; a condition called chlorosis. In the absence of chlorophyll, photosynthesis is severely limited, reducing the plant&rsquo;s ability to grow.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Limiting Factors</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The rate of photosynthesis is controlled by whichever factor is in shortest supply. A <dfn class="term" data-def="The factor that is present in the lowest concentration or availability relative to the plant's needs, preventing the rate of photosynthesis from increasing further even if other conditions are favourable.">limiting factor</dfn> is the one variable that, if increased, would allow the rate to rise. When conditions are otherwise ideal, increasing a limiting factor speeds up photosynthesis; when that factor is no longer limiting, a different one takes over.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Three main abiotic factors act as limiting factors: <strong>light intensity</strong>, <strong>carbon dioxide concentration</strong>, and <strong>temperature</strong>. Understanding them allows growers to optimise crop yields &mdash; for example, in a commercial greenhouse where all three factors can be controlled.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Each Factor Affects the Rate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Light intensity:</strong> As light intensity increases, more energy is available for the light-driven stage of photosynthesis, so the rate rises. Eventually the rate plateaus because a different factor (CO₂ or temperature) becomes limiting. Light intensity decreases with the square of the distance from the source: double the distance, quarter the intensity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Carbon dioxide concentration:</strong> CO₂ is a raw material for the second stage of photosynthesis. Increasing CO₂ supplies more substrate for the carbon-fixation reactions, raising the rate. In greenhouses, farmers sometimes add CO₂ gas to boost yields.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Temperature:</strong> Photosynthesis is enzyme-controlled. As temperature rises, enzyme and substrate molecules move faster, increasing the collision rate and the reaction rate. Above the optimum temperature, enzymes begin to <dfn class="term" data-def="The permanent change in an enzyme's active site shape due to excessive heat or extreme pH, which prevents the substrate from binding.">denature</dfn> and the rate falls sharply.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Draw a sketch graph of photosynthesis rate vs. light intensity. Mark the plateau. Annotate it: what is the limiting factor on the steep part, and what becomes limiting at the plateau?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Limiting factors for photosynthesis: light intensity, CO₂ concentration, temperature. At the plateau on a rate-vs-light-intensity graph, light is no longer limiting &mdash; another factor (CO₂ or temperature) has taken over. Increasing all three together can allow the rate to rise further.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Transpiration and Water Loss</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n24">Water taken in through the roots travels up the plant in xylem vessels and eventually evaporates from the leaf surface through tiny pores called <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny pores, mainly on the underside of leaves, through which gas exchange (CO₂ in, O₂ out) and water vapour loss (transpiration) occur.">stomata</dfn>. This process &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="The evaporation of water from a plant, mainly through the stomata on the leaf surface.">transpiration</dfn> &mdash; drives a continuous column of water up from the roots. Stomata must be open to allow CO₂ in for photosynthesis, but opening them also allows water vapour to escape. Plants balance this trade-off using guard cells, which open and close the stomata in response to conditions such as light and water availability.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n25">At Higher Tier, you need to be able to interpret rate-of-photosynthesis graphs where multiple limiting factors interact. For example, if increasing CO₂ concentration raises the plateau on a rate-vs-light graph, this confirms that CO₂ was previously limiting at that light intensity. If increasing temperature also raises the plateau, temperature was also limiting. Only by holding two factors constant while varying the third can you identify each factor&rsquo;s individual contribution.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Photosynthesis is an endothermic process in chloroplasts: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\). Chlorophyll absorbs light energy to drive the reaction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Glucose is used for respiration, starch storage, cellulose synthesis, and making amino acids (when combined with nitrate ions).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Light intensity, CO₂ concentration and temperature are the three main limiting factors; whichever is in shortest supply controls the rate of photosynthesis.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">Questions on limiting factors commonly include a graph. Read the axes carefully: the x-axis is usually light intensity, CO₂, or temperature, and the y-axis is rate of photosynthesis. When the line plateaus, identify the new limiting factor. When explaining a plateau, state both what is no longer limiting AND what is now limiting. Avoid vague answers like &ldquo;there is no more light&rdquo; &mdash; instead write &ldquo;CO₂ concentration is now the limiting factor, even though light intensity continues to increase.&rdquo;</p>
