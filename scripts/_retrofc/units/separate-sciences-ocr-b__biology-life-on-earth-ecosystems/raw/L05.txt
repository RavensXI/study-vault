--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why diversity matters and how scientists group the living world.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Biodiversity?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of all the different species of organisms on Earth, or within a particular ecosystem.">Biodiversity</dfn> is the variety of species found in a given area. High biodiversity makes ecosystems more stable and resilient &mdash; if one species declines, others can take over its ecological role. Low biodiversity makes an ecosystem fragile and more likely to collapse when conditions change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why high biodiversity makes an ecosystem more resilient. Give one example of a threatened ecosystem.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>High biodiversity increases ecosystem resilience. A diverse community has more species to fill each ecological role, so the system can absorb disturbances without collapsing. Coral reefs and tropical rainforests are two of the most biodiverse &mdash; and most threatened &mdash; ecosystems on Earth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Threats to Biodiversity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Human activities are the main driver of biodiversity loss worldwide. Key threats include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Deforestation</strong> &mdash; clearing forests for agriculture, timber or development destroys habitats and the species within them. Tropical deforestation is particularly damaging because rainforests hold an exceptionally large proportion of the world&rsquo;s species.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Habitat destruction</strong> &mdash; converting land to farmland, draining wetlands and building on greenfield sites all reduce the area available for wild species.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Climate change</strong> &mdash; rising temperatures shift the ranges where species can survive. Coral bleaching, caused by warmer sea temperatures, is a clear example: when sea temperatures rise above a threshold, <dfn class="term" data-def="A reef structure built by colonies of coral polyps. Coral bleaching occurs when warmer water stresses the corals, causing them to expel their photosynthetic algae and turn white.">coral reefs</dfn> expel the algae they depend on, turning white and dying.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Pollution</strong> &mdash; pesticides, fertiliser runoff, plastic waste and air pollution all harm organisms. Fertiliser runoff triggers <dfn class="term" data-def="The rapid growth of algae in water caused by excess nutrients, which reduces oxygen levels and kills other aquatic organisms.">eutrophication</dfn> in freshwater, causing oxygen depletion and mass die-offs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Overexploitation</strong> &mdash; overfishing, illegal hunting and unsustainable harvesting reduce populations below sustainable levels.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>UK Pollinators: A Case Study in Biodiversity Loss</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">UK wild bee and hoverfly populations have declined significantly in recent decades. Intensification of farming &mdash; which reduces wildflower meadows and hedgerows &mdash; is a major driver, alongside pesticide use and habitat fragmentation. Pollinators matter far beyond their own survival: roughly three-quarters of flowering plant species depend on animal pollinators to reproduce. A collapse in pollinator diversity would cascade through food chains, threatening crop yields and wild plant communities alike.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">This case illustrates a key principle: <strong>biodiversity loss is rarely isolated</strong>. Losing one group of species weakens the ecological services that other species &mdash; including humans &mdash; depend on. Scientists describe this as the loss of <dfn class="term" data-def="The benefits that functioning ecosystems provide to humans, including food production, clean water, pollination, climate regulation and recreation.">ecosystem services</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Conservation Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Scientists and governments use a range of strategies to protect biodiversity:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Protected areas</strong> &mdash; national parks, nature reserves and Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs) restrict damaging human activity within ecologically important habitats.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Captive breeding programmes</strong> &mdash; zoos and wildlife centres breed endangered species with the aim of reintroducing them into the wild when conditions are safe.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Seed banks</strong> &mdash; repositories such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault preserve plant genetic diversity, safeguarding against extinction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Legislation</strong> &mdash; international agreements and national laws restrict hunting and trade of threatened species, and regulate pollution.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Habitat restoration</strong> &mdash; planting hedgerows, restoring wetlands and creating wildlife corridors help fragmented populations reconnect.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Classification: Grouping the Living World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The systematic grouping of organisms into hierarchical categories based on shared characteristics and evolutionary relationships.">Classification</dfn> is the process of grouping organisms based on shared features. The modern system was developed by the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus (1707&ndash;1778), who introduced a hierarchy of groups and a formal naming system.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write out the full classification hierarchy from largest to smallest group, then recall the two parts of a binomial name and what each represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The classification hierarchy from most inclusive to least is: Kingdom &rarr; Phylum &rarr; Class &rarr; Order &rarr; Family &rarr; Genus &rarr; Species. A useful mnemonic is: <em>King Philip Came Over For Good Soup</em>. Members of the same species can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">The Binomial Naming System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Every species is given a unique two-part Latin name &mdash; this is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A two-part scientific naming system for species, consisting of the genus name (capitalised) followed by the species name (lowercase), written in italics.">binomial system</dfn>. The first part is the genus name (always capitalised) and the second is the species name (always lowercase). For example, modern humans are <em>Homo sapiens</em> and the domestic dog is <em>Canis lupus familiaris</em>. Using Latin ensures scientists worldwide share the same name for each species, avoiding confusion caused by different common names in different languages.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">The Five Kingdoms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Organisms are traditionally divided into five <dfn class="term" data-def="The broadest level of biological classification, grouping all organisms into five major categories: animals, plants, fungi, protists and prokaryotes (bacteria).">kingdoms</dfn>:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Animals</strong> &mdash; multicellular, heterotrophic (cannot make their own food), no cell wall.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Plants</strong> &mdash; multicellular, autotrophic (photosynthesise), cellulose cell wall.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Fungi</strong> &mdash; multicellular (mostly), heterotrophic, chitin cell wall, reproduce by spores.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Protists</strong> &mdash; mostly unicellular eukaryotes that do not fit the other kingdoms (e.g. <em>Amoeba</em>, algae).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Prokaryotes (Bacteria)</strong> &mdash; unicellular, no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Evolutionary Relationships and DNA Evidence</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n32">Classification is more than a filing system &mdash; it reflects evolutionary history. Species that are closely related share a more recent common ancestor and tend to share more DNA sequences. Modern genomic techniques allow scientists to compare the DNA of different organisms directly, refining classifications that were previously based only on visible (morphological) features. This is an example of a scientific model being updated as new evidence becomes available &mdash; early classifications based purely on appearance sometimes grouped unrelated organisms together just because they looked alike (convergent evolution).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33">For instance, dolphins were historically mistaken for fish because of their body shape. DNA evidence places them firmly within mammals, closely related to even-toed ungulates (cattle, pigs and deer). This illustrates why multiple lines of evidence &mdash; morphology, biochemistry and DNA &mdash; are all considered in modern classification.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Biodiversity &mdash; the variety of species in an ecosystem &mdash; is being reduced by deforestation, climate change, pollution and overexploitation. High biodiversity underpins resilient ecosystems and the services they provide to humans.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Conservation strategies include protected areas, captive breeding, seed banks, habitat restoration and legislation. Each addresses a different threat but all share the goal of preventing irreversible species loss.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Classification groups organisms into a hierarchy (kingdom to species) using shared features and evolutionary relationships. The binomial system gives every species a unique Latin name: genus (capital) followed by species (lowercase), written in italics.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n34">For &lsquo;Evaluate&rsquo; questions on conservation, always weigh both practical and ethical dimensions &mdash; examiners want to see reasoned judgement, not just a list of strategies. When writing the binomial name in an answer, remember: genus is capitalised, species is lowercase, and the whole name is italicised (underlined in handwriting). On &lsquo;Explain why biodiversity matters&rsquo; questions, link biodiversity to ecosystem stability and to specific services humans rely on, such as pollination and food production &mdash; a vague &ldquo;it&rsquo;s good for nature&rdquo; will not score full marks.</p>
