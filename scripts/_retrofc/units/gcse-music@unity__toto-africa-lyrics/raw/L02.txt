<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chorus 1 — Bars 40–57</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the long Verse 1 in B major, the music shifts to <dfn class="term" data-def="The key used for every chorus in Africa, with three sharps: F#, C# and G#.">A major</dfn> for the chorus. Chorus 1 is 18 bars long (4 + 4 + 4 + 6) and uses a repeating 4-bar chord sequence. It falls into four phrases:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n3">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 1 — Bars 40–43</div>
    <h4>Solo vocal</h4><p>“It’s gonna take a lot to drag me away from you.” Sung by the lead vocalist alone.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 2 — Bars 44–47</div>
    <h4>Duet</h4><p>“There’s nothing that a hundred men or more could ever do.” A second voice joins to create a duet.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 3 — Bars 48–51</div>
    <h4>Three parts</h4><p>“I bless the rains down in Africa.” The texture thickens to three vocal parts.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 4 — Bars 52–57</div>
    <h4>Extended phrase (6 bars)</h4><p>“Gonna take some time to do the things we never had.” The chord pattern changes and the melody ends with a <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of notes sung to a single syllable of text.">melisma</dfn>.</p>
  </div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Chorus Melody</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The melody for the first three phrases uses only two pitches: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The first note (and home note) of a scale or key.">tonic</dfn> (A) and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The seventh note of a major scale, one semitone below the tonic.">leading note</dfn> (G#). This is a huge contrast to the verse, where the melody sat low in the voice and moved conjunctly. In the chorus the melody is high in the voice, syncopated and syllabic — giving it a catchy, driving feel.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you name all 3 phrases from memory?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The chorus chord pattern for the first three phrases is vi–IV–I–V (F#m–D–A–E). This is a typical pop song chord progression. Remember the mnemonic: <strong>Fat Dogs Always Eat</strong>. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which chords change in a piece of music.">harmonic rhythm</dfn> is one chord per bar, the same rate as the verse.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Extended Fourth Phrase</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The final phrase of each chorus (bars 52–57) is six bars long instead of four. It starts with the same vi–IV–I pattern, but the fourth bar introduces chord iii (C#m) instead of chord V. The full progression is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">vi (F#m) – IV (D) – I (A) – iii (C#m) – V (E) – vi (F#m) / Vb (E/G#)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The mnemonic for this is: <strong>Fat Dogs And Cats Eat Fried EGgs</strong>. The change to chord iii and the use of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A chord where a note other than the root is in the bass. Written with a slash, e.g. E/G# means an E chord with G# in the bass.">slash chord</dfn> E/G# at the end prepares the shift back to B major for the link section. The slash chord E/G# means an E chord with G# as the bass note — this is a first inversion (Vb).</p>

This gradual increase in the number of vocal parts thickens the texture and builds energy towards the climax of the chorus. The dynamics also shift to forte (f) in the chorus, compared with the mezzo-forte (mf) of the verses.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Link Sections — Bars 58–65</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Link 1 and Link 2 are identical. They are both 8 bars long (4 + 4) and are in B major. Their job is to take us from the A major chorus back to B major for the next verse or instrumental section.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The chords are the same as the introduction: <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated phrase in popular music, typically used as an introduction or refrain.">Riff a</dfn> is played twice. The chord pattern for each 4-bar statement is ♮VII (A) – vi (G#m) – ii (C#m) – ii (C#m). Remember the mnemonic: <strong>All Good Cats Chase Cheese</strong>. While the introduction played Riff a three times, the links only play it once through (repeated), so they are shorter.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 3 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Riff a appears in every section of the song except the choruses. It uses syncopation and features the chords ♮VII, vi and ii in B major. In the links, Riff a is stated twice (8 bars total), compared with three times in the introduction.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Verse 2 — Bars 14–39</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Verse 2 is in B major, just like Verse 1, and uses the same chord progressions. However, it is shorter: 26 bars (9 + 9 + 8) compared with 35 bars (9 + 9 + 9 + 8) in Verse 1. This is because the 9-bar phrase goes round twice instead of three times — one fewer repetition.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The three phrases are:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n19">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 1 — Bars 14–22 (9 bars)</div>
    <h4>“The wild dogs cry out in the night…”</h4><p>“…as they grow restless, longing for some solitary company.”</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 2 — Bars 23–31 (9 bars)</div>
    <h4>“I know that I must do what’s right…”</h4><p>“…sure as Kilimanjaro rises like Olympus above the Serengeti.”</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 3 — Bars 32–39 (8 bars)</div>
    <h4>“I seek to cure what’s deep inside…”</h4><p>“…frightened of this thing that I’ve become.”</p>
  </div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">How Verse 2 Differs from Verse 1</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Although Verse 2 reuses the same harmony as Verse 1, there are four key differences to know:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>1. Fewer phrases:</strong> Verse 2 has three phrases (9 + 9 + 8 = 26 bars) instead of four (9 + 9 + 9 + 8 = 35 bars). This shortens the section by 9 bars.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>2. Melodic change in bar 33:</strong> The singer leaps up to pitches D# and C# on the words “deep in,” which is different from the equivalent point in Verse 1.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>3. Countermelody:</strong> A <dfn class="term" data-def="A secondary melody played or sung alongside the main melody, also called a descant.">countermelody</dfn> (or descant) played on a flute-like instrument is heard across the top of the texture. It changes pitch conjunctly and moves at the same speed as the harmonic rhythm.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>4. More prominent backing vocals:</strong> In the two 9-bar phrases, backing vocals enter in the fifth bar, harmonising on “Ooo” before joining the lead singer with the last word of each phrase. At the start of the second 9-bar phrase, a backing vocalist harmonises initially a 4th higher than the melody, then continues a 3rd higher.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">There is a clear pattern of shortening each time the verse material returns. Verse 1 has three 9-bar phrases plus one 8-bar phrase (35 bars). Verse 2 drops to two 9-bar phrases plus one 8-bar phrase (26 bars). The instrumental section drops further to one 9-bar phrase plus one 8-bar phrase (17 bars). Each time, one 9-bar repetition is removed. This creates a sense of momentum as the song progresses towards the final choruses.</p>

</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Chorus 2 — Bars 40–57</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Chorus 2 is identical to Chorus 1. It is 18 bars long, in A major, and uses the same vi–IV–I–V chord pattern for the first three phrases and the extended 6-bar pattern for the fourth phrase. After Chorus 2, Link 2 returns the music to B major, ready for the instrumental.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n29">The Instrumental — Bars 66–82</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The instrumental section is 17 bars long (9 + 8) and is in B major. It uses the same accompanying chords as the verse, but the vocal melody is almost entirely replaced by an instrumental melody based on <dfn class="term" data-def="The second riff in the introduction, using an ostinato pattern of five notes based on the E major pentatonic scale over a sustained chord ii.">Riff b</dfn>. This is the shortest statement of the verse material, with only one 9-bar phrase before the final 8-bar phrase.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">The instrumental melody can be broken down into four clear sub-sections:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Bars 66–69 (4 bars):</strong> A descending melody using a B major <dfn class="term" data-def="A musical scale with five notes per octave, most commonly notes 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of the major scale.">pentatonic scale</dfn>. The notes fall into groups of three, ending with a triplet.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Bars 70–74 (5 bars):</strong> An ascending and descending melody using the E major scale. This passage has more rhythmic variety than the first half.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Bars 75–77 (3 bars):</strong> An ascending melody, also using the B major pentatonic scale with notes grouped in threes. It ends in a more “pop” style with the use of <dfn class="term" data-def="Additional notes added as decoration that are not essential to the melody or harmony.">grace notes</dfn> and syncopation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Bars 78–82 (5 bars):</strong> The vocal part returns with the second half of the original 8-bar phrase: “Hurry boy, she’s waiting there for you.”</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n36" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 5 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The instrumental melody develops Riff b from the introduction. It uses the B major pentatonic scale and the E major scale, with notes grouped in threes and featuring triplets, grace notes and syncopation. The vocal part only returns at bar 78 for the final five bars of the section.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n37">There are a few important rules for the chords in Africa. All F, C and G chords in the song are sharp minor (e.g. F#m, C#m, G#m). The D chords are also sharp minor 7ths (D#m7) but only in the verse and instrumental sections — in the chorus, D is a major chord. Watch out for slash chords (inversions) such as E/G#: the first letter is the chord name and the second letter is the bass note.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n39">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n40">The chorus is in A major with a vi–IV–I–V chord pattern (F#m–D–A–E). The melody uses only the tonic and leading note, is high in the voice, syncopated and syllabic, and the vocal texture builds from solo to three parts.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n41">Verse 2 is 9 bars shorter than Verse 1 (26 bars vs. 35 bars) and adds a countermelody on a flute-like instrument, more prominent backing vocals, and a melodic change in bar 33 on the words “deep in.”</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">The instrumental (bars 66–82) is the shortest verse-based section at 17 bars. It develops Riff b into a full instrumental melody using the B major pentatonic and E major scales, with the vocal returning only for the final 5 bars.</li></ul>
