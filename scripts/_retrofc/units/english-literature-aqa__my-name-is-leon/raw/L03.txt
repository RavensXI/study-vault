--- DESCRIPTION ---
Leon and Jake are separated by the care system. Leon is placed with foster carer Maureen while Jake is adopted by a white family.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Separation: The System Divides</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Part 2 opens with the event that defines the rest of the novel: Leon and Jake are separated. Jake — white, a baby, and therefore ‘more adoptable’ in the language of the system — is placed with a middle-class adoptive family. Leon is placed with <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who provides temporary care for a child in their own home, appointed by social services.">foster carer</dfn> Maureen. The separation is presented as a bureaucratic decision, made in an office, without Leon’s consent or understanding.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does the system separate Leon and Jake? Name the two main factors.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jake is adopted quickly because he is white and a baby — the two characteristics that made children most ‘placeable’ in the 1980s care system. Leon is older, mixed-race, and emotionally damaged. The system sees Jake as an easy success and Leon as a difficult case.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">De Waal presents the separation without melodrama. Leon is simply told, in careful language, that Jake is going to live with another family. He doesn’t fully understand that this is permanent. The restraint of the prose makes the scene more devastating — Leon’s silence and confusion speak louder than tears would.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Maureen: Warmth and Stability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Maureen is a Jamaican-British woman who has fostered many children. She is warm, practical, and loving — the opposite of the cold institutional world Leon has just experienced. Her home smells of cooking. She feeds Leon properly. She calls him “sweetheart.” She represents the <dfn class="term" data-def="A community or cultural group that provides identity, belonging, and support outside of official institutions.">diaspora community</dfn> — people who understand racial identity and cultural belonging in ways the white care system does not.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Sensory language:</strong> Maureen’s home is presented through warmth, food, and colour. De Waal uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of related words that create a particular impression or atmosphere in a text.">semantic field</dfn> of nourishment — both physical and emotional — to contrast with the deprivation of Leon’s previous life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cultural identity:</strong> Maureen gives Leon something the system cannot — a connection to his Black heritage. She cooks Caribbean food, uses Jamaican dialect, and normalises his mixed-race identity in ways that no social worker has done.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Patience:</strong> Maureen doesn’t push Leon to talk or perform gratitude. She allows him space to grieve. Her parenting style is instinctive and community-based, contrasting with the social workers’ procedural approach.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Limitations:</strong> Despite her warmth, Maureen cannot fix the fundamental problem — Leon wants Jake, and she cannot give him that. This creates an ongoing tension: Leon loves Maureen but cannot let go of his brother.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Leon’s Internal Conflict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Part 2 is dominated by Leon’s struggle between two impulses: settling into Maureen’s home and refusing to accept the loss of Jake. He begins to form attachments — to Maureen, to the garden, to routines — but guilt pulls him back. Accepting Maureen feels like betraying Jake and Carol.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words the internal conflict Leon experiences in Part 2.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>De Waal presents Leon’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A struggle within a character's mind between opposing desires, values, or loyalties.">internal conflict</dfn> through behaviour rather than explicit thought. He acts out, tests boundaries, and oscillates between warmth and withdrawal. His anger is never fully explained to other characters because he lacks the vocabulary to articulate his grief.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Leon’s behaviour in Part 2 includes stealing, lying, and occasional violence. De Waal never presents these as moral failings. Instead, they are shown as the logical responses of a traumatised child trying to regain control over a world that has taken everything from him. The reader is positioned to understand what the system might label as ‘difficult behaviour’ is actually <dfn class="term" data-def="Emotional or behavioural responses to distressing events that may include anxiety, anger, withdrawal, or difficulty trusting others.">trauma response</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what the garden symbolises for Leon, and why it also represents the tension between hope and impermanence in foster care.">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Maureen’s garden becomes a central <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that represents a larger idea or concept beyond its literal meaning in a text.">symbol</dfn> in Part 2. Leon works in the garden, planting seeds and watching things grow. The garden represents nurture, patience, and the possibility of putting down roots. It offers Leon a form of agency — he can care for something and see results.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">However, the garden is also temporary. Leon knows he is in foster care, not adopted. Everything he plants could be taken away, just as Jake was taken. De Waal uses the garden to explore the tension between hope and impermanence that defines the foster care experience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Contact Visits and Diminishing Hope</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Leon is allowed <dfn class="term" data-def="Supervised meetings between a child in care and their birth family, arranged by social services.">contact visits</dfn> with Jake, but these become less frequent over time. Each visit is both joyful and agonising — Leon sees Jake changing, growing, becoming part of another family. The visits are supervised, timed, and controlled by adults. Leon has no power over when he sees his brother or for how long.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">De Waal uses the diminishing visits structurally: as the gaps between contact grow longer, the reader feels Leon’s grip on his brother loosening. The system is slowly, bureaucratically erasing the sibling bond — not through cruelty, but through process.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Jake is adopted because he is white and a baby; Leon is fostered because the system sees him as harder to place.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Maureen provides love, stability, and cultural identity, but cannot replace Leon’s lost family.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Part 2 explores Leon’s internal conflict between settling with Maureen and refusing to let go of Jake.</li>
</ul>
