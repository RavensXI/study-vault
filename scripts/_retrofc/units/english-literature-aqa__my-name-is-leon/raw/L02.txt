--- DESCRIPTION ---
Nine-year-old Leon cares for baby Jake while their mother Carol spirals, setting the stage for the brothers’ separation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Opening: Life with Carol</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with nine-year-old Leon living with his mother Carol and baby half-brother Jake. Leon is mixed-race — his father is Black and absent, his mother is white. Jake is fully white, fathered by a different man. From the first pages, De Waal establishes Leon as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A child who takes on caring responsibilities for a parent or sibling due to the parent's inability to cope.">young carer</dfn> — he feeds Jake, changes nappies, and shields the baby from Carol’s increasingly erratic behaviour.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three things does Leon do for Jake that show he is a young carer?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>De Waal uses Leon’s child perspective to show neglect without naming it. Leon doesn’t understand that his mother is struggling with mental health and substance issues — he simply adapts, taking on adult responsibilities because no one else will.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Carol is not presented as a villain. De Waal shows her as a woman overwhelmed by poverty, isolation, and untreated <dfn class="term" data-def="Conditions affecting a person's thinking, feeling, mood, or behaviour, such as depression, anxiety, or psychosis.">mental health problems</dfn>. She loves her children but cannot care for them. Her decline is shown through Leon’s eyes: unwashed dishes, curtains drawn all day, Carol sleeping while Jake cries. The reader understands what Leon cannot — that this household is heading toward crisis.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Leon’s Fierce Protection of Jake</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The central relationship in Part 1 is between Leon and Jake. Leon’s love for his brother is absolute and physical — he holds Jake, whispers to him, makes him laugh. De Waal establishes this bond so powerfully that its later destruction carries enormous emotional weight.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe the four ways De Waal establishes the bond between Leon and Jake — physical closeness, responsibility, promises, and identity.">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How De Waal Presents the Bond</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Physical closeness:</strong> Leon is constantly holding, carrying, or lying beside Jake. De Waal uses tactile language — “warm,” “soft,” “tucked in” — to show love expressed through touch rather than words.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Responsibility:</strong> Leon takes on a parental role. He knows Jake’s feeding schedule, recognises different cries, and instinctively prioritises Jake’s needs above his own.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Promises:</strong> Leon repeatedly promises Jake that he will look after him. These promises become tragic as the narrative progresses and Leon loses the power to keep them.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Leon’s identity:</strong> Being Jake’s brother is central to who Leon is. Without Jake, Leon loses his sense of purpose and belonging.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Carol’s Deterioration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">De Waal charts Carol’s decline through a series of increasingly alarming scenes, all filtered through Leon’s limited understanding. Carol stops cooking. She sleeps for days. She brings home men who frighten Leon. She disappears, leaving the children alone. The prose becomes fragmented and anxious, mirroring Leon’s growing fear.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two ways De Waal shows Carol's decline through Leon's perspective rather than telling the reader directly.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>De Waal never explicitly states Carol’s diagnosis. Instead, she shows the <em>effects</em> of mental illness and substance abuse through a child’s confused perspective. This technique creates dramatic irony — the reader sees what Leon cannot.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation in which the audience or reader knows more than the characters, creating tension or pathos.">dramatic irony</dfn> is central to De Waal’s method. When Leon describes Carol being “tired” or having “medicine,” the adult reader recognises depression, substance use, or both. This gap between Leon’s innocent narration and the reader’s understanding generates both empathy and anguish.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Social Services Intervene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The crisis point arrives when Carol is found unable to care for the children. <dfn class="term" data-def="Government departments responsible for protecting vulnerable people, especially children at risk of harm or neglect.">Social services</dfn> intervene. Leon and Jake are taken into care together — initially to a temporary placement. Leon is told this is temporary, that he will go home soon. De Waal shows how the system speaks in reassuring language that masks permanent decisions being made behind closed doors.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how De Waal contrasts Leon’s warm, physical language with the cold bureaucratic language of social workers. Why does this contrast matter?">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Language and Power in Part 1</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">De Waal contrasts the warm, physical language of Leon’s inner world with the cold, bureaucratic language of social workers. Words like “placement,” “contact,” and “permanency” are used by adults to describe devastating changes in Leon’s life. He doesn’t understand these words, but the reader does.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">This contrast highlights the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imbalance in control or influence between individuals or groups, often determined by age, race, or social position.">power imbalance</dfn> between Leon and the adults who control his fate. He has no voice in the decisions being made about him. The child’s perspective forces the reader to experience this powerlessness directly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">Part 1 ends with the brothers still together but the reader sensing that separation is coming. De Waal builds <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of anxious anticipation about what will happen next, created by hints and tension in the narrative.">suspense</dfn> through small, ominous details: social workers whispering, meetings Leon isn’t told about, adults exchanging looks over his head.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Part 1 establishes Leon as a young carer who protects Jake while Carol’s mental health deteriorates.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">De Waal uses dramatic irony — the reader understands neglect and illness that Leon cannot name.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The bond between Leon and Jake is established as the emotional core that the novel will later destroy.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">When analysing Part 1, focus on De Waal’s use of the <strong>child narrator</strong> as a method. The gap between what Leon understands and what the reader understands is the source of the novel’s emotional power. Always link this technique to effect: “De Waal uses Leon’s limited perspective to generate pathos because the reader can see the approaching separation that Leon cannot.”</p>
