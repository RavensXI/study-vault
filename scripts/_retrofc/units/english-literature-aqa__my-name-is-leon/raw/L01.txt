--- DESCRIPTION ---

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Britain in 1981</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>My Name is Leon</em> by Kit de Waal is set in 1981 Birmingham, during a period of intense social upheaval. Margaret Thatcher’s Conservative government had been in power since 1979, implementing policies of economic <dfn class="term" data-def="The policy of reducing government spending on public services and welfare.">austerity</dfn> that hit working-class and Black communities hardest. Unemployment was soaring, particularly in industrial cities like Birmingham, and racial tensions were at breaking point.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three social factors created the backdrop for the 1981 riots?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The novel is set against the real Handsworth riots of July 1981 — sparked by aggressive policing, racial discrimination, and mass unemployment in Black communities.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavy-handed police operation that used 'stop and search' powers disproportionately against Black people in Britain.">sus laws</dfn> (the ‘suspected person’ laws) allowed police to stop and search anyone they suspected of criminal intent. In practice, this meant young Black men were routinely harassed. The Brixton riots erupted in April 1981, followed by Handsworth in July — both fuelled by the same toxic combination of poverty, racism, and police brutality.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Race Relations in 1980s Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The 1980s was a decade when overt <dfn class="term" data-def="Prejudice or discrimination directed against a person or group based on their racial or ethnic background.">racism</dfn> was commonplace and largely unchallenged by institutions. The National Front had marched openly through cities in the late 1970s. Black and mixed-race families faced discrimination in housing, employment, education, and — crucially for this novel — the care system.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Institutional Racism in the Care System</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">In the 1980s, the care system operated with what we would now recognise as <dfn class="term" data-def="Racism embedded within the policies, procedures, and culture of an organisation, rather than just individual prejudice.">institutional racism</dfn>. White babies were considered more ‘adoptable’ because there were more white families seeking to adopt. Black and mixed-race children were often left in long-term foster care rather than placed with permanent families.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">De Waal draws on real social work practices of the era. The separation of Leon and Jake in the novel reflects documented cases where siblings of different races were split up because the system prioritised racial matching — or, more accurately, prioritised finding homes for white children while failing Black ones.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal and social system responsible for the care of children who cannot safely remain with their parents.">care system</dfn> at this time was also underfunded, with overworked social workers managing enormous caseloads. Children’s emotional needs were often secondary to bureaucratic processes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Handsworth Riots</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">On 10 July 1981, tensions in Handsworth, Birmingham, exploded into violence. Police had conducted aggressive raids on Black-owned businesses and homes. Young people fought back. Cars were burned, shops were looted, and over 100 people were arrested. The riots lasted two days and caused widespread damage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three causes of the Handsworth riots that De Waal weaves into the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>De Waal uses the riots not just as a backdrop but as a turning point in the plot. They represent the moment when suppressed rage — both communal and personal — finally erupts. Leon’s anger at losing Jake mirrors the community’s anger at systemic injustice.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain why De Waal’s retrospective viewpoint (writing in 2016 about 1981) strengthens the novel’s social criticism.">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Context Matters for Analysis</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">De Waal wrote this novel in 2016, looking back at 1981. This retrospective viewpoint allows her to show readers how structural racism operated — things that were invisible to many white people at the time are made starkly visible through Leon’s child perspective. The reader can see what Leon cannot fully understand: that the system is not merely failing him, but is designed in ways that disadvantage him because of his race.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Understanding the historical context is essential for literary analysis. When De Waal describes social workers making decisions about Leon and Jake, she is not just telling a fictional story — she is dramatising a real pattern of <dfn class="term" data-def="Treating people differently based on characteristics such as race, resulting in unequal outcomes.">discrimination</dfn> that affected thousands of real children.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Kit de Waal’s Background</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Kit de Waal (born 1960) grew up in Birmingham as the daughter of an Irish mother and a Kittitian (Caribbean) father. She worked for fifteen years as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal professional who represents clients, particularly vulnerable people such as children in care proceedings.">magistrates’ court adviser</dfn> and in criminal law, specialising in cases involving children in care. Her professional experience gives the novel its unflinching accuracy about how the care system operates — and fails.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">De Waal has spoken openly about how the novel draws on both her professional knowledge and her personal experience of growing up mixed-race in 1970s and 1980s Britain. The dedication reads: “For all the brothers and sisters who were separated.” This signals that the story, while fictional, represents a widespread reality.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The novel is set in 1981 Birmingham during a period of mass unemployment, racial tension, and the Handsworth riots.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The care system of the 1980s was institutionally racist — white babies were prioritised for adoption while Black and mixed-race children were left in long-term foster care.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">De Waal draws on her professional experience in family law to create an authentic portrayal of how the system separates siblings.</li>
</ul>
