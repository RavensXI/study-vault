<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pip’s Moral Recovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the shock of Magwitch’s revelation, Pip begins a slow moral transformation. He stops seeing Magwitch as a class category and starts seeing him as a human being. He learns Magwitch’s tragic life story — orphaned, starved, criminalised from childhood — and feels “a new affection” for the old convict. This shift is the novel’s emotional turning point: Pip’s recovery begins when he stops judging people by their class and starts judging them by their character.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Pip’s first act of genuine selflessness is his plan to help Magwitch escape England. He enlists Herbert Pocket’s help to row Magwitch down the Thames to a ship bound for Europe. This is not about money or status — Pip risks his own freedom to save the man he once despised. Dickens shows that real gentlemanliness is not about manners or wealth; it is about moral courage and compassion.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what triggers Pip’s moral recovery, and what is his first selfless act?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pip’s moral recovery begins when he sees Magwitch as a person rather than a class category. His plan to help Magwitch escape — risking his own freedom — is his first genuinely selfless act, proving that true gentlemanliness is about compassion, not status.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Escape Attempt</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Pip, Herbert, and Startop row Magwitch down the Thames in Chapter 54. The escape fails. Compeyson — Magwitch’s old enemy — has informed on them. As the customs boat bears down, Magwitch and Compeyson struggle in the water. Compeyson drowns. Magwitch is recaptured, badly injured, and sentenced to death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The escape sequence is Dickens at his most cinematic. The river journey, conducted in whispers and tension, contrasts with the violent climax. Structurally, the Thames functions as both physical escape route and <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary device where a physical journey represents a character’s internal emotional or moral journey.">symbolic journey</dfn>: Pip is attempting to free Magwitch from the system that has always oppressed him, just as he himself is finally breaking free from the class prejudice that has oppressed <em>his</em> moral development.</p>

He gives the dying man the only comfort he can — the knowledge that his daughter survived.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Joe’s Return and Pip’s Illness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">After Magwitch’s death, Pip falls desperately ill with fever and debt. He is arrested and imprisoned. In his delirium, he keeps calling for Joe — and Joe comes. Without reproach, without a word of recrimination, Joe nurses Pip back to health. He pays Pip’s debts. He sits by his bedside day and night. When Pip recovers, Joe quietly slips away before Pip can thank him properly, leaving only a note.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This scene mirrors the novel’s opening: once again, Pip is helpless and Joe is his rescuer. But now Pip understands what Joe’s love means. He has spent the entire novel trying to become a gentleman — and the most genuinely gentle man in his life was there all along, at the forge, asking nothing in return. Joe’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Love or kindness given without expectation of reward. Joe’s love for Pip is the novel’s purest example.">unconditional love</dfn> is the moral standard against which all other characters are measured.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: how does Joe’s behaviour when Pip is ill demonstrate the novel’s definition of true gentlemanliness?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Joe nurses Pip without reproach, pays his debts, and leaves without seeking thanks. His unconditional love — consistent from the first chapter to the last — is the novel’s definition of true gentlemanliness: moral worth that exists entirely independent of wealth or class.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Two Endings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Dickens originally wrote a bleak ending in which Pip encounters Estella briefly in London. She has remarried after Drummle’s death, and they part as strangers. But his friend Bulwer-Lytton persuaded him to revise it. In the published ending, Pip returns to Satis House (now demolished) and finds Estella there. She has been “bent and broken” by suffering, and the final line is deliberately ambiguous: “I saw no shadow of another parting from her.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A quality of having more than one possible interpretation. The final line of Great Expectations can be read as either a happy reunion or a resigned acceptance.">ambiguity</dfn> of the revised ending is crucial. It can be read as hopeful (they stay together) or melancholy (Pip’s delusion continues). Dickens may have intended both readings simultaneously. The original ending is arguably more consistent with the novel’s themes: Pip’s expectations were always misguided, and a fairy-tale union with Estella would undermine that message.</p>

This final humbling is Dickens’s last lesson for Pip: he cannot simply ‘claim’ the good people he neglected. Joe and Biddy have found happiness together without him. Pip must accept the consequences of his choices. He goes abroad with Herbert to work honestly in business — finally earning his way rather than living off someone else’s money.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Pip’s moral recovery begins when he sees Magwitch as a person. Helping him escape is Pip’s first selfless act.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Joe’s unconditional love — nursing Pip, paying debts, leaving without thanks — defines true gentlemanliness.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The two endings offer different conclusions: the original is bleaker but more thematically consistent; the revised is deliberately ambiguous.</li></ul>
