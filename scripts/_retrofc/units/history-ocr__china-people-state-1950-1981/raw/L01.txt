--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the PRC consolidated power through land reform, mass campaigns and political terror.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Proclaiming the People's Republic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">On 1 October 1949, <dfn class="term" data-def="The founder and first Chairman of the People's Republic of China, who led the Communist Party to victory in the civil war.">Mao Zedong</dfn> stood on Tiananmen Gate and proclaimed the People's Republic of China (PRC). The <dfn class="term" data-def="The governing party of China from 1949, committed to Marxist-Leninist ideology and led by Mao Zedong.">Chinese Communist Party (CCP)</dfn> had defeated Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist forces after a brutal civil war. The Nationalists retreated to Taiwan. Mao inherited a country exhausted by decades of war, with widespread poverty, regional warlords still influential, and a largely illiterate peasant population. Consolidating power meant dismantling the old social order at speed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what did the 1950 Agrarian Reform Law do, and which two groups were its main targets?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1950 Agrarian Reform Law redistributed land from landlords to peasants. By 1952, around 300 million peasants had received land. Landlords and rich peasants faced <dfn class="term" data-def="Public meetings organised by the CCP at which people were forced to confess crimes or were accused by neighbours — a key tool of political intimidation.">struggle sessions</dfn>, and scholars estimate between one and three million were executed during the land reform.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Land Reform and Its Reception</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Agrarian Reform Law of 1950 was the Party's first major social transformation. Historically, China's countryside was dominated by landlords who rented land to tenant farmers. The CCP organised village committees to classify inhabitants: poor peasants, middle peasants, rich peasants, and landlords. Land and property were seized from the upper categories and distributed below. For many poor peasants this represented a genuine, life-changing improvement. Local power shifted dramatically.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The reception of land reform was, however, mixed. In many regions, poor peasants enthusiastically participated in struggle sessions, denouncing landlords in public meetings. Yet historians such as Frank Dikötter note that violence varied enormously by locality: some villages conducted peaceful transfers, while in others the campaigns became instruments of private score-settling. The Party's own records acknowledged excesses. Presenting land reform as uniformly welcomed masks a more complicated local reality.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Running parallel to land reform from 1950 to 1953, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A CCP mass campaign from 1950–53 that targeted former Nationalist officials, landlords, religious leaders and anyone deemed hostile to Communist rule.">Suppression of Counter-Revolutionaries Campaign</dfn> aimed to eliminate organised opposition. Targets included former Nationalist officials, Triad gang members, religious leaders suspected of foreign ties, and anyone labelled an enemy of the state. The campaign used public trials and executions &mdash; often carried out in front of large crowds &mdash; as instruments of mass political education. The message was clear: the old order was finished and resistance was fatal. Western scholars estimate between one and two million executions during this campaign, though precise figures remain debated.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Three-Anti and Five-Anti Campaigns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In 1951&ndash;52, the Party launched two interlocking campaigns targeting urban China. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1951 campaign targeting corruption, waste and obstruction within the Party itself and state organisations.">Three-Anti Campaign</dfn> (against corruption, waste, and obstruction) focused on Party and government officials. Shortly afterwards, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1952 campaign targeting the urban bourgeoisie, accusing businessmen of bribery, tax evasion, theft of state property, fraud and leaking state economic secrets.">Five-Anti Campaign</dfn> turned on the urban bourgeoisie &mdash; merchants, industrialists, and businesspeople &mdash; accused of five crimes including bribery and tax fraud.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">These campaigns had two overlapping purposes. First, they were genuine drives against corruption in a Party that had rapidly expanded and absorbed many opportunists. Second, they were political tools to subordinate the urban middle class and private capital to Party control. Businesses were fined, property was seized, and prominent capitalists were publicly humiliated. The campaigns effectively ended independent urban capitalism before the formal nationalisation of industry in 1956.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: what were the Three-Anti and Five-Anti Campaigns targeting, and who were their respective victims?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Three-Anti Campaign (1951) targeted Party officials accused of corruption, waste and obstruction. The Five-Anti Campaign (1952) targeted private businesspeople. Together they extended Party control into both the state apparatus and the urban economy.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">In October 1950, the People's Liberation Army entered Tibet. The PRC described this as &ldquo;peaceful liberation&rdquo; from feudal theocracy; Tibetan and many Western accounts describe it as an invasion ending Tibetan autonomy. A Seventeen Point Agreement was signed in 1951, formally incorporating Tibet into the PRC while nominally preserving the Dalai Lama's authority. In practice, CCP control tightened steadily. The annexation of Tibet illustrates how consolidation extended beyond the Chinese heartland to ethnic minority regions, using a mix of military force, negotiated agreements, and political assimilation. The purge of <dfn class="term" data-def="A senior CCP leader in Manchuria who was expelled from the Party in 1954 after attempting to build an independent power base — one of the first high-profile purges within the Party itself.">Gao Gang</dfn> in 1954 further demonstrated that the Party would not tolerate regional power bases, even from within its own ranks.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">How Effective Was the Consolidation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">By 1953 the CCP had achieved rapid, sweeping control across most of China. The old landowning class had been physically destroyed or politically neutralised. Urban capitalism was subordinated to the state. Regional challenges &mdash; including a high-profile internal purge of Gao Gang &mdash; had been suppressed. The Korean War (1950&ndash;53), in which Chinese &ldquo;volunteers&rdquo; fought American-led UN forces, also served domestic purposes: it generated nationalist solidarity and justified the continued mobilisation of Chinese society under wartime discipline.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">However, consolidation came at significant human cost. Historians including Roderick MacFarquhar estimate that mass campaigns and land reform together resulted in between one and three million executions, alongside widespread imprisonment and forced labour. The Party's own socialist construction was partly built on the suppression of dissent, not simply on popular enthusiasm. Students should weigh both the genuine social changes achieved and the political violence through which they were delivered.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The CCP used overlapping campaigns &mdash; land reform, Three-Anti, Five-Anti, Suppression of Counter-Revolutionaries &mdash; to simultaneously transform rural society and neutralise urban opposition between 1950 and 1953.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Consolidation combined genuine social change (redistribution of land to peasants) with mass political violence; scholars estimate between one and three million executions during the land reform and counter-revolutionary campaigns.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Regional consolidation, including the annexation of Tibet and the purge of Gao Gang in 1954, showed that the Party would not tolerate independence either at China's periphery or within its own leadership.</li>
</ul>
