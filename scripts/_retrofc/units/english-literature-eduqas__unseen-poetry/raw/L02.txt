--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to analyse metaphor, simile, personification, and other language techniques in poetry.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Language Analysis: The Core Skill</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The most important skill for unseen poetry is the ability to analyse <dfn class="term" data-def="The choice and use of words and phrases to create specific effects in a text.">language</dfn>. This means going beyond identifying techniques to explaining <em>why</em> a poet chose a particular word or image and <em>what effect</em> it creates. Every word in a poem is chosen deliberately — poems are compressed texts where every syllable carries weight. Your job is to unpack that compression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Start with the most striking language feature. Ask three questions: (1) What is the literal meaning? (2) What are the <dfn class="term" data-def="The associations, ideas, or feelings that a word evokes beyond its literal dictionary meaning.">connotations</dfn>? (3) What is the effect on the reader? For example, if a poet writes “the sun bled into the horizon,” the literal meaning is a sunset. The connotation of “bled” includes pain, injury, and loss. The effect is to transform a beautiful scene into something wounded, suggesting the speaker sees beauty as inseparable from suffering.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the three-question method for analysing a language choice, with an example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Always ask three questions: (1) What is the literal meaning? (2) What are the connotations? (3) What is the effect on the reader? This three-step method turns simple identification into genuine analysis. Practise it with every quotation you select.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Key Language Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A direct comparison between two unlike things, stating one IS the other (e.g. ‘love is a battlefield’).">Metaphor</dfn>:</strong> Creates a direct identification between two things. When a poet writes “my heart is a locked room,” they present emotions as inaccessible and hidden. Analyse what the comparison reveals: locked rooms suggest secrets, isolation, and the need for a key.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison using ‘like’ or ‘as’ (e.g. ‘grief fell like rain’).">Simile</dfn>:</strong> Creates an explicit comparison. “Her voice was like a knife” suggests sharpness, precision, and the capacity to wound. Similes are slightly more distanced than metaphors because they acknowledge the comparison rather than asserting identity.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Giving human qualities to non-human things (e.g. ‘the wind whispered’).">Personification</dfn>:</strong> Gives human qualities to nature or objects. “The trees reached out their arms” creates a sense of nature as a living, welcoming presence. Personification often reveals the speaker’s emotional state: they project their feelings onto the landscape.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Pathetic fallacy:</strong> Weather or landscape reflecting emotion. A storm during a character’s anger, or sunshine during joy. In poetry, the natural world often mirrors the speaker’s inner state.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Sensory imagery:</strong> Appeals to sight (visual), sound (auditory), touch (tactile), smell (olfactory), or taste (gustatory). The best poets engage multiple senses to create a vivid experience. If a poem describes “the sharp scent of rain on warm earth,” analyse why “sharp” makes the smell almost physical.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Word-Level Analysis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The highest-level analysis focuses on individual word choices. Consider: Why did the poet choose <em>this</em> word and not a synonym? What are its connotations? What sound does it make? <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of harsh, discordant sounds in language to create a jarring, uncomfortable effect.">Cacophony</dfn> (harsh sounds like “crack,” “grind,” “scraped”) creates discomfort. <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of smooth, pleasant sounds in language to create a harmonious, soothing effect.">Euphony</dfn> (smooth sounds like “murmur,” “melody,” “flow”) creates calm. Sound and meaning work together.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Consider the line: “The city swallowed him whole.” <strong>Technique:</strong> personification and metaphor. <strong>Literal meaning:</strong> the speaker disappears into the city. <strong>Connotations:</strong> “swallowed” suggests consumption, digestion, destruction — the city as a predator, the person as prey. “Whole” suggests completeness: nothing of the person remains. <strong>Effect:</strong> the reader feels the city as threatening and all-consuming, creating sympathy for the vulnerable individual against the overwhelming urban landscape.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">This is the level of detail the examiner wants. Notice how one short quotation generates a full paragraph of analysis. You do not need many quotations — you need <em>depth</em> on the ones you choose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: take the phrase ‘silence roared in my ears’ and analyse it using the three-question method.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Quality over quantity. Three well-analysed quotations will earn more marks than ten quotations that are merely identified. Select the most striking language choice, quote it precisely, and analyse it in depth: technique, connotation, effect. This is what separates Band 5 responses from lower bands.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Use the three-question method for every quotation: literal meaning, connotations, effect on the reader.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Depth beats quantity: three well-analysed quotations will outscore ten briefly mentioned ones.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Word-level analysis (why <em>this</em> word and not a synonym?) is the skill that earns the highest marks.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When you select a quotation to analyse, keep it short (a phrase, not a whole stanza). Short quotations are easier to analyse in detail. Embed them in your sentences: “The verb ‘shattered’ suggests sudden, violent destruction, conveying the speaker’s sense that their world has been irreparably broken.” This embedded approach shows confidence and earns AO1 + AO2 marks simultaneously.</p>
