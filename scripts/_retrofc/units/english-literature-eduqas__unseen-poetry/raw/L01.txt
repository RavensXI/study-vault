--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Unseen Poetry?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the Eduqas Component 1 Section B exam, you will face two poems you have never seen before. There is no way to predict which poems will appear, so you cannot revise specific texts. Instead, you revise <em>skills</em>: the ability to analyse language, identify techniques, explore meaning, and compare poems under timed conditions. This unit teaches you those skills systematically.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The section is worth <strong>20 marks</strong> in total. Part (i) asks you to analyse <em>one</em> poem (worth approximately 12 marks), and Part (ii) asks you to compare that poem with a <em>second</em> poem (worth approximately 8 marks). You assess AO1 (informed response with textual references) and AO2 (analysis of language, form, and structure). There is no AO3 (context) in unseen poetry because you have no background information about the poets.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the mark split and AOs for the unseen poetry section from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eduqas Component 1 Section B: Part (i) = analyse one poem (~12 marks, AO1 + AO2). Part (ii) = compare with a second poem (~8 marks, AO1 + AO2). No AO3. Total = 20 marks. Spend approximately 15 minutes on Part (i) and 10 minutes on Part (ii).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Your Step-by-Step Approach</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When you first see the poem, do not panic. Read it through <strong>three times</strong> before writing anything:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>First read:</strong> Get the gist. What is the poem about on the surface? Who is speaking? What is the situation? <strong>Second read:</strong> Look for <dfn class="term" data-def="The methods a poet uses to create meaning, including imagery, metaphor, rhyme, rhythm, structure, and tone.">techniques</dfn> — imagery, metaphor, simile, personification, rhyme, rhythm, enjambment. Annotate the poem. <strong>Third read:</strong> Consider the poet’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The reason or intention behind a text — what the writer wants the reader to think, feel, or understand.">purpose</dfn>. What is the deeper meaning? What effect does the poet want to create? How does the form support the content?</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Common Mistakes to Avoid</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Mistake 1: Feature-spotting without analysis.</strong> Do not write “The poet uses a metaphor” and stop. Always explain the <em>effect</em>: what does the metaphor make the reader think or feel? <strong>Mistake 2: Retelling the poem.</strong> Do not paraphrase what happens. The examiner has read the poem — they want to see your analysis of <em>how</em> it works, not <em>what</em> it says. <strong>Mistake 3: Ignoring structure.</strong> Many students focus only on individual words and miss how the poem is built: stanza breaks, line length, enjambment, the relationship between the opening and the ending.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Mistake 4: Writing too much on Part (i) and running out of time for Part (ii).</strong> The comparison is worth 8 marks — almost half the section. Budget your time carefully.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">How to Annotate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Use your three reads to annotate the poem directly. Circle or underline key words and phrases. In the margins, note: <strong>L</strong> = language technique (metaphor, simile, personification), <strong>S</strong> = structural feature (enjambment, caesura, stanza break), <strong>T</strong> = tone (angry, reflective, nostalgic, bitter). At the bottom of the poem, write one sentence summarising the poet’s purpose. These annotations become your essay plan.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What the Examiner Is Looking For</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The Eduqas mark scheme rewards: <strong>Band 5</strong> — perceptive, detailed analysis of language and structure with convincing personal response. <strong>Band 4</strong> — thorough analysis with well-supported interpretation. <strong>Band 3</strong> — clear understanding with some analytical detail. The difference between bands is the depth of your analysis: Band 5 responses explore <em>why</em> a technique works, not just <em>what</em> it is.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A Band 5 response might say: “The enjambment between lines 3 and 4 forces the reader to continue without pause, mirroring the speaker’s breathless urgency and suggesting that their emotions overflow the constraints of the line.” A Band 2 response would say: “The poet uses enjambment.” The difference is explanation of effect.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the three-read strategy from memory: what do you look for on each read?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three reads: (1) gist — what is the surface meaning, who speaks, what is the situation; (2) techniques — annotate imagery, metaphor, structure, rhythm; (3) purpose — what is the deeper meaning and how does form support content. These three reads should take 3–4 minutes and form the basis of your response.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Unseen poetry tests skills, not knowledge — you revise the ability to analyse language, structure, and meaning under timed conditions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Read the poem three times before writing: gist, techniques, purpose. Annotate in the margins. Your annotations become your plan.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The key to high marks is explaining the <em>effect</em> of techniques, not just naming them. Every point must answer: what does this make the reader think or feel?</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">Always explain the <em>effect</em> of a technique, not just its name. “The poet uses a simile” earns minimal marks. “The simile comparing grief to ‘a stone in my chest’ conveys the physical weight of loss, suggesting it is something the speaker carries constantly” earns full AO2 marks. Effect is everything.</p>
