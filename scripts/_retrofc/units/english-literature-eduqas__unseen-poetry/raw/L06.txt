--- DESCRIPTION ---
Exam timing, planning under pressure, and strategies for maximising marks in 25 minutes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Timing: Your 25-Minute Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The unseen poetry section should take approximately <strong>25 minutes</strong> in total. Poor timing is the most common reason students underperform: they spend too long on Part (i) and rush Part (ii), or they spend too long reading and don’t leave enough time to write. A clear time plan prevents this:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Minutes 0–3:</strong> Read Poem 1 three times and annotate. <strong>Minutes 3–15:</strong> Write Part (i) — 3 developed paragraphs on Poem 1. <strong>Minutes 15–18:</strong> Read Poem 2 and identify comparison points. <strong>Minutes 18–25:</strong> Write Part (ii) — 2 developed paragraphs comparing both poems.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the timing breakdown from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Time allocation: 3 minutes reading/annotating Poem 1, 12 minutes writing Part (i), 3 minutes reading/annotating Poem 2, 7 minutes writing Part (ii). Stick to this plan rigidly in practice and it becomes automatic in the exam. The number one timing mistake is spending too long on Part (i).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Planning Under Pressure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">You do not have time for a full written plan. Your annotations <em>are</em> your plan. During your three reads, mark: the most striking image (this becomes paragraph 1), the most important structural feature (paragraph 2), and the overall theme/tone (your opening and closing sentences). For Part (ii), mark 2–3 comparison points in the margins of Poem 2.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Part (i): What 3 Paragraphs Look Like</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Paragraph 1:</strong> Open with a thematic statement. Analyse the most striking language feature (imagery, metaphor, word choice). Quotation + connotation + effect. <strong>Paragraph 2:</strong> Analyse a structural feature (enjambment, volta, stanza pattern). Integrate with another language observation. <strong>Paragraph 3:</strong> Analyse the ending and how it resolves or complicates the theme. Link back to your opening statement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">This is not a rigid formula — adapt it to the poem. But having a default structure prevents you from freezing under exam pressure. Three paragraphs with depth will outscore five paragraphs of feature-spotting.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Maximising Marks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Band 5 requires “perceptive, detailed analysis.” This means: (1) choosing unexpected or subtle features to analyse, not just the obvious ones; (2) explaining connotations and effects, not just identifying techniques; (3) showing how language and structure work <em>together</em>; (4) offering a personal response that goes beyond generic statements.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What to Do When You’re Stuck</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">If you cannot identify a technique, focus on <strong>word choice</strong>. Pick any word that stands out and ask: why did the poet choose THIS word? What are its connotations? What does it make you feel? You do not need to name a technique to analyse language effectively. “The word ‘shattered’ connotes sudden, violent destruction” is excellent analysis even without naming it as a metaphor.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">If you cannot understand the poem, focus on <strong>tone</strong>. Is the speaker happy, sad, angry, confused, nostalgic? How do you know? What words or images create that tone? Tone is always analysable, even when meaning is unclear.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write a default three-paragraph plan for Part (i) from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Default Part (i) structure: Paragraph 1 (thematic statement + key image analysis), Paragraph 2 (structural feature + second language point), Paragraph 3 (ending analysis + link to theme). Default Part (ii) structure: Paragraph 1 (compare a technique from each poem), Paragraph 2 (compare tone, structure, or ending). Having a default removes panic.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Stick to the 3/12/3/7 minute split: 3 minutes reading Poem 1, 12 minutes writing Part (i), 3 minutes reading Poem 2, 7 minutes writing Part (ii).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Your annotations are your plan — mark the most striking image, the key structural feature, and the overall theme during your three reads.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">If stuck, focus on word choice and tone — you can always analyse why a specific word was chosen and what feeling it creates.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n14">If you find yourself running out of time on Part (ii), write one strong comparative paragraph rather than two weak ones. A single paragraph that makes a precise comparison with evidence from both poems will earn more marks than two paragraphs of vague observation. Quality always beats quantity in unseen poetry.</p>
