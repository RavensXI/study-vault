--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to analyse form and structure: stanzas, enjambment, caesura, rhyme, rhythm, and volta.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Structure Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Many students focus entirely on language and ignore <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a poem is organised and arranged, including line length, stanza breaks, enjambment, and the relationship between beginning and ending.">structure</dfn>. This is a missed opportunity. Structure is how a poem is <em>built</em>, and it creates meaning just as powerfully as individual words. A poem that breaks into short, sharp lines feels different from one that flows in long, unbroken sentences — even if both say the same thing. Structure is the skeleton; language is the flesh.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The particular arrangement or pattern of a poem, including its type (sonnet, free verse, ballad) and how its elements are organised.">Form</dfn> refers to the overall type and pattern of a poem: is it a sonnet, a ballad, free verse? Does it have a regular rhyme scheme or not? Structure refers to how elements are arranged within the poem: where are the stanza breaks? How do line lengths change? Where is the turning point? Both form and structure carry meaning.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the difference between form and structure, with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Form = the overall type and pattern (sonnet, free verse, ballad). Structure = how elements are arranged within the poem (stanza breaks, line lengths, turning points). Both create meaning. A sonnet’s 14-line constraint suggests discipline; free verse’s lack of pattern suggests freedom or disorder. Always comment on <em>why</em> the poet chose this form.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Key Structural Features</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="When a sentence or phrase runs over from one line to the next without punctuation, creating a sense of flow or urgency.">Enjambment</dfn>:</strong> A sentence continues past the end of a line without punctuation. Effect: creates momentum, urgency, or overflow. It can suggest that emotions are too strong to be contained within a single line. <strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A pause within a line of poetry, usually created by punctuation such as a comma, full stop, or dash.">Caesura</dfn>:</strong> A pause within a line, created by punctuation. Effect: creates hesitation, emphasis, or a sudden shift in tone. A caesura can make the reader stop and reflect on what they have just read.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Stanza breaks:</strong> Gaps between stanzas can represent time passing, a shift in perspective, or a change in mood. If a poem about a relationship has two stanzas — one happy, one sad — the stanza break marks the moment of change. <strong>Line length:</strong> Short lines create a sense of control, tension, or abruptness. Long lines suggest expansiveness, freedom, or overwhelming emotion.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A turn or shift in a poem, usually in argument, mood, or perspective, often occurring at a specific structural point.">Volta</dfn>:</strong> Italian for “turn.” A volta is a shift in the poem’s argument, mood, or perspective. In a sonnet, the volta traditionally occurs between the octave (first 8 lines) and the sestet (last 6). In other poems, look for a word like “but,” “yet,” or “however” signalling a change. The volta is often the poem’s most important moment: it is where the meaning deepens or reverses.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Rhyme and Rhythm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Rhyme:</strong> Regular rhyme creates a sense of order, harmony, or inevitability. Broken or <dfn class="term" data-def="Words that almost rhyme but not quite (e.g. ‘love’/‘move’), creating a sense of discord or unease.">half-rhyme</dfn> creates unease or discord. If a poem about a happy memory uses full rhyme that breaks down in the final stanza, the structural change mirrors the emotional shift from happiness to loss.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Rhythm:</strong> Regular rhythm (iambic pentameter, for example) creates a steady, controlled pace. Disrupted rhythm creates tension or confusion. Listen to the poem in your head — where does it speed up or slow down? These changes are deliberate choices that guide the reader’s experience.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How to Write About Structure in the Exam</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Do not write about structure in a separate paragraph. Integrate structural analysis into your language points. For example: “The enjambment between ‘I wanted to say’ and ‘everything’ forces the reader forward without pause, mirroring the speaker’s desperate urgency. The word ‘everything’ sits alone at the start of the next line, giving it emphasis and weight.” This combines structural analysis (enjambment), language analysis (‘everything’), and effect (urgency, weight).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Also consider the relationship between the poem’s opening and ending. Does the poem come full circle? Does it resolve or remain open? Does the final line answer the opening question or complicate it? The ending of a poem is always significant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and define enjambment, caesura, and volta, giving one example of how each creates meaning.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three most useful structural terms are enjambment (momentum/overflow), caesura (pause/emphasis), and volta (turning point/shift). Learn to identify all three and always explain their <em>effect</em>: what does the structure make the reader experience? Structure analysis integrated with language analysis earns the highest marks.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Structure is as important as language: enjambment, caesura, stanza breaks, and volta all create meaning and should be analysed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Integrate structural analysis into language paragraphs rather than treating them separately — show how form and content work together.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Always comment on the opening, the ending, and any turning points — these structural moments carry the poem’s deepest meaning.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">Always comment on the opening and the ending of the poem. The opening establishes the situation; the ending resolves, complicates, or subverts it. If the ending echoes the opening, discuss circular structure. If it contradicts the opening, discuss how the poem’s argument has shifted. These structural observations show the examiner you understand the poem as a whole, not just as isolated quotations.</p>
