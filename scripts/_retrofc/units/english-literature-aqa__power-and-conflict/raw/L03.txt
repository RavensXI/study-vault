<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Tennyson’s ‘The Charge of the Light Brigade’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Alfred, Lord Tennyson (1809–1892), Poet Laureate during the Victorian era who wrote patriotic and commemorative poetry, including works responding to the Crimean War.">Alfred, Lord Tennyson</dfn> wrote this poem in 1854, shortly after reading a newspaper report about the disastrous cavalry charge at the Battle of Balaclava during the Crimean War. Due to a miscommunication in orders, 600 British soldiers on horseback charged directly into Russian cannons. Around 110 were killed, 160 wounded, and 375 horses destroyed. Tennyson wrote the poem in minutes, driven by admiration for the soldiers’ obedience and courage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Tennyson's official role, what newspaper published the poem, and which word he used to describe the commanders' mistake.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>As <strong>Poet Laureate</strong>, Tennyson had an official role to write for the nation. His poem commemorates the bravery of ordinary soldiers while subtly criticising the commanders whose “blunder” caused the disaster. The poem was published in <em>The Examiner</em> newspaper and was widely read by the Victorian public.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>“Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward”</strong> — The poem opens with driving <dfn class="term" data-def="A metrical foot consisting of one stressed syllable followed by two unstressed syllables, creating a galloping rhythm.">dactylic</dfn> rhythm that mimics the galloping of horses. The repetition creates a sense of unstoppable momentum — the soldiers cannot turn back. A league is approximately three miles; the repetition emphasises the vast distance they must cover under fire.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>“Into the valley of Death”</strong> — An allusion to Psalm 23 (“the valley of the shadow of death”). The capitalisation of “Death” personifies it as a force waiting for them. The valley is also literal — the soldiers rode into a valley surrounded by cannon on three sides.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>“Some one had blunder’d”</strong> — Tennyson acknowledges the catastrophic military error, but does not name the person responsible. The vague “Some one” deflects blame from any individual, keeping the focus on the soldiers’ bravery rather than the leaders’ incompetence.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>“Theirs not to make reply, / Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die”</strong> — The triple repetition of “Theirs” emphasises the soldiers’ unquestioning obedience. The rhyme of “why” and “die” links questioning with death — obedience is their only option. This can be read as both admiration for their duty and a quiet criticism of a system that demands blind obedience.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>“Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them”</strong> — The repetition surrounds the reader just as the cannons surround the soldiers. The three directions create a claustrophobic trap. “Volley’d and thunder’d” uses onomatopoeia to bring the sounds of battle to life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>“Honour the charge they made! / Honour the Light Brigade”</strong> — The imperative “Honour” is a direct instruction to the reader. Tennyson commands us to remember and respect these men, turning the poem into a memorial.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Ted Hughes’s ‘Bayonet Charge’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Ted Hughes (1930–1998), Poet Laureate known for his raw, visceral poetry about nature, violence, and the human condition. His father survived the Gallipoli campaign in World War I.">Ted Hughes</dfn> wrote ‘Bayonet Charge’ from the imagined perspective of a World War I soldier mid-attack. Unlike Tennyson, Hughes does not celebrate or commemorate — he exposes the terror, confusion, and absurdity of combat. Hughes’s father survived the Gallipoli campaign, and this personal connection shapes the poem’s raw intensity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the Latin term Hughes uses by starting in the middle of action, and the specific opening word of Bayonet Charge.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hughes begins the poem <em>in medias res</em> (in the middle of the action) with the word “Suddenly.” There is no buildup, no context, no patriotic framing — the reader is thrown into the chaos just as the soldier is, reflecting the disorientation of combat.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Quotations from ‘Bayonet Charge’</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>“Suddenly he awoke and was running”</strong> — The abrupt opening disorients the reader. “Awoke” suggests the soldier has been in a trance-like state; the violence has shocked him into consciousness. The present continuous “was running” creates immediacy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>“raw-seamed hot khaki, his sweat heavy”</strong> — The compound adjectives and tactile imagery make the reader feel the soldier’s physical discomfort. “Raw-seamed” suggests the uniform is chafing his skin, reducing the heroic soldier to a suffering body.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>“King, honour, human dignity, etcetera / Dropped like luxuries”</strong> — The dismissive “etcetera” is devastating. The abstract concepts that supposedly justify war (King, honour, dignity) are treated as meaningless afterthoughts. “Dropped like luxuries” suggests they are discarded under the pressure of survival — in the moment of combat, ideology is worthless.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>“a yellow hare that rolled like a flame”</strong> — The simile compares the injured hare to a flame, linking nature with the violence of war. The hare’s suffering mirrors the soldier’s — both are innocent victims caught in destruction they don’t understand. The image is both beautiful and horrifying.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Comparing the Two Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Attitude to war:</strong> Tennyson commemorates and honours the soldiers; Hughes strips away all heroism and glory. Tennyson’s repetitive, galloping rhythm creates energy and momentum; Hughes’s irregular, fractured lines mirror the soldier’s confusion. Tennyson’s soldiers are a collective (“the six hundred”); Hughes focuses on a single, anonymous individual.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Use of form:</strong> Tennyson uses regular stanzas and a driving rhythm; Hughes uses three stanzas of unequal length with enjambment that creates a breathless, stumbling pace. Both poets use sound devices, but for opposite effects: Tennyson’s onomatopoeia (“thunder’d”) creates dramatic spectacle, while Hughes’s harsh consonants (“raw-seamed hot khaki”) create physical discomfort.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Context:</strong> Tennyson wrote as Poet Laureate with a patriotic duty; Hughes wrote from a twentieth-century perspective shaped by two world wars and a more critical attitude towards military authority.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Tennyson uses galloping dactylic rhythm, repetition, and imperatives to commemorate the Light Brigade’s bravery while subtly acknowledging the “blunder” that caused the disaster.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Hughes uses <em>in medias res</em>, fractured enjambment, and the dismissive “etcetera” to strip away all heroism and expose the raw terror of combat.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Comparing these poems is excellent practice for the exam — they share the theme of conflict but present completely opposing attitudes to war.</li></ul>
