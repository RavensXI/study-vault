<h2 data-narration-id="n1">William Blake’s ‘London’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="William Blake (1757–1827), a Romantic poet, artist, and visionary who fiercely criticised social injustice, industrialisation, and institutional oppression.">William Blake</dfn> wrote ‘London’ in 1794 as part of his collection <em>Songs of Experience</em>. The poem is a scathing attack on the institutions Blake saw as responsible for human suffering: the Church, the monarchy, and the government. Blake walked through the streets of London and what he saw — poverty, child labour, prostitution — shaped this angry, despairing poem.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall when Blake wrote London: which revolution had happened 5 years earlier and what industrial process created poor living co...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Blake wrote during the early Industrial Revolution, when rapid urbanisation had created terrible living conditions for the poor. The poem was also written five years after the French Revolution (1789), which Blake initially supported as a symbol of people overthrowing oppressive power.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>“I wander thro’ each charter’d street, / Near where the charter’d Thames does flow”</strong> — The repetition of <dfn class="term" data-def="Controlled by royal charter or legal document. Blake uses it ironically to suggest that even the streets and river are owned and restricted by those in power.">charter’d</dfn> is deeply ironic. A charter is meant to grant freedoms, but Blake uses the word to suggest everything is owned, controlled, and restricted. Even the Thames — a natural river — has been claimed by those in power.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>“Marks of weakness, marks of woe”</strong> — The repetition of “marks” suggests both physical signs of suffering and the metaphorical “marks” branded onto people by an oppressive system. The noun “woe” is absolute and inescapable.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>“mind-forg’d manacles”</strong> — This powerful metaphor suggests that people are mentally imprisoned. The “manacles” are not physical chains but psychological ones — the people have been conditioned to accept their oppression. The compound adjective “mind-forg’d” implies the chains are both created by and locked within the mind.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>“every black’ning Church appalls”</strong> — “Black’ning” refers literally to soot from factories staining church walls, but also metaphorically to the moral corruption of the Church. The verb “appalls” carries a double meaning: to shock, and to cast a “pall” (a funeral cloth) over the institution. Blake accuses the Church of being complicit in suffering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>“the hapless Soldier’s sigh / Runs in blood down Palace walls”</strong> — The synaesthesia of a sigh turning into blood on walls creates a horrifying image. The “Palace” (capitalised to represent the monarchy) is stained by the blood of soldiers sent to die in wars. Blake blames the ruling class directly.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>“the youthful Harlot’s curse / Blasts the new-born Infant’s tear”</strong> — The final stanza is the most disturbing. The young prostitute’s suffering (a “curse” — both swearing and disease) destroys even the innocence of a newborn. The verb “Blasts” is violent and explosive. Blake shows that poverty corrupts everything, even the next generation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Structure and Form of ‘London’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The poem has four quatrains with an ABAB rhyme scheme, creating a steady, relentless rhythm like the speaker’s footsteps through the streets. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of words beginning with the same sound in close succession, creating emphasis or a particular effect.">anaphora</dfn> of “In every” in stanza two hammers home the universality of suffering — no one is exempt. The poem moves from observation (stanza 1) to analysis (stanzas 2-3) to a devastating conclusion (stanza 4), building in intensity.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n12">John Agard’s ‘Checking Out Me History’</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="John Agard (born 1949), a Guyanese-British poet who uses Caribbean dialect and oral tradition to challenge Western cultural dominance and celebrate hidden histories.">John Agard</dfn> wrote this poem to protest the Eurocentric education system he experienced. The poem alternates between mocking the “history” he was taught in British schools (Guy Fawkes, Dick Whittington, Lord Nelson) and celebrating the Black and Caribbean historical figures who were deliberately excluded from the curriculum.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Agard's specific dialect choice: what Caribbean language feature does he use and what phrase exemplifies this throughout ...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Agard uses <strong>Caribbean Creole dialect</strong> (“Dem tell me”) throughout the poem. This is a deliberate political choice — it asserts his Caribbean identity in a poem about cultural erasure and challenges the dominance of Standard English in education and literature.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>“Dem tell me / Dem tell me / Wha dem want to tell me”</strong> — The angry refrain uses repetition to emphasise how controlled the education was. “Dem” represents the white British establishment. The Caribbean dialect is itself an act of resistance against the language imposed by colonisers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>“Toussaint / a slave / with a vision / lick back / Napoleon / battalion”</strong> — The short, punchy lines about <dfn class="term" data-def="Toussaint Louverture (1743–1803), who led the Haitian Revolution, the only successful slave revolt in history, defeating Napoleon’s forces.">Toussaint Louverture</dfn> create a powerful, rhythmic energy. The verb “lick back” (Caribbean dialect for “fought back”) is forceful and physical. The contrast between “a slave” and defeating “Napoleon / battalion” highlights the extraordinary achievement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>“a healing star / among the wounded / a yellow sunrise / to the dying”</strong> — Mary Seacole is described using natural imagery: “star,” “sunrise.” This elevates her to something almost divine, contrasting with the mundane British figures (Dick Whittington and his cat). The metaphors suggest she brought hope and life to the hopeless.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>“But now I checking out me own history / I carving out me identity”</strong> — The shift to the present tense shows the speaker taking control. “Carving” suggests active, physical effort — reclaiming identity is hard work, not passive. The rhyme of “history” and “identity” links the two concepts: understanding your history is essential to knowing who you are.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Comparison: Blake and Agard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Both poets use their poems as acts of political protest. Blake attacks the Church, monarchy, and government for causing suffering in Georgian London; Agard attacks the education system for erasing Black history and identity. Both use <strong>repetition</strong> as a structural device (Blake’s “In every”; Agard’s “Dem tell me”) to build a sense of relentless injustice. However, while Blake’s tone is despairing and the poem ends with no hope, Agard’s poem ends with empowerment: “I carving out me identity.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Both poets challenge who holds power over narratives. Blake shows how institutions control people’s lives; Agard shows how institutions control people’s understanding of history. Both suggest that true freedom requires breaking free from these controls.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Blake’s ‘London’ is a furious attack on institutional oppression, using “mind-forg’d manacles” to suggest people are psychologically as well as physically trapped.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Agard’s ‘Checking Out Me History’ protests the erasure of Black history in British education, using Caribbean dialect and contrasting structures to assert cultural identity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Both poems challenge power structures, but Blake’s ends in despair while Agard’s ends with empowerment and reclamation.</li></ul>
