<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Comparison Task</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your anthology comparison will give you one named poem from the cluster and ask you to compare it with another poem of your choice on a shared theme. The task rewards candidates who can hold two texts in mind at once and draw precise, insightful links between them. Plan your time so you finish with a few minutes spare for checking — planning, writing and proofreading are all part of a strong response. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact wording and timing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what three skills do markers reward most across every paragraph of a poetry comparison?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Strong comparative essays balance three skills in every paragraph: a <strong>personal, argued response</strong> anchored in precise quotations; <strong>close analysis of the poets’ methods</strong> (language, structure, form); and <strong>contextual understanding</strong> woven in rather than bolted on. Accurate written expression also matters — slips in spelling and grammar make analysis harder to follow.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Choosing Your Second Poem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Your choice of second poem is crucial. Choose a poem that allows you to make <strong>meaningful comparisons</strong>, not just surface-level connections. The best choices will share a theme but present it <em>differently</em> — through contrasting attitudes, forms, or contexts. For example, if given ‘Remains’ (guilt and PTSD), comparing with ‘War Photographer’ (witnessing trauma) allows you to compare two different relationships with conflict.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Planning Your Response</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Spend a few minutes planning before you write. Use the <dfn class="term" data-def="A planning technique for poetry comparison: identify 3 points of comparison, each with a quotation from both poems, and note the method and context for each.">3-point plan</dfn>: identify three points of comparison, each with a quotation from both poems and a note on the method used. A strong plan might look like this:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name: the three comparison points for conflict effects, including the specific quotes like 'bloody life in my bloody hands' and ...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Example plan</strong> (theme: effects of conflict):<br>
  1. <em>How conflict is experienced</em> — “probably armed, possibly not” (Remains, moral ambiguity) vs “spools of suffering” (War Photographer, witnessing)<br>
  2. <em>Psychological aftermath</em> — “his bloody life in my bloody hands” (Remains, guilt/PTSD) vs “half-formed ghost” (WP, haunting)<br>
  3. <em>Form and structure</em> — collapsing stanzas (Remains, breakdown) vs controlled quatrains (WP, imposed order)</p>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Writing a Comparative Paragraph</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Each paragraph should follow the <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure for comparative paragraphs: Point (comparative statement), Evidence (quotation from poem A), Analysis (method + effect), Link (quotation from poem B with comparison), Context (relevant background for both poems).">PEALC structure</dfn>: Point (a comparative statement), Evidence (quotation from poem A), Analysis (method and effect), Link (quotation from poem B with comparison), Context (relevant background). Crucially, you must <strong>compare throughout the paragraph</strong>, not write about one poem and then the other.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Model Comparative Paragraph</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Model:</strong> Both poets present speakers who are haunted by the violence they have witnessed, though they respond to it differently. In ‘Remains’, Armitage uses the present tense — “he bursts again through the doors of the bank” — to show the killing replaying involuntarily, a hallmark of PTSD that reflects the psychological toll on soldiers returning from Iraq. Similarly, Duffy’s photographer sees a “half-formed ghost” as the image develops — a metaphor that works both literally (the developing photograph) and figuratively (the dead man haunting him). However, while Armitage’s soldier is consumed by guilt (“his bloody life in my bloody hands”), Duffy’s photographer is consumed by the <em>futility</em> of documenting suffering that the public ignores: “eyeballs prick with tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers.” This bitter irony reflects Duffy’s anger at a society that treats real human suffering as a momentary inconvenience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Writing about one poem then the other:</strong> This is the most common mistake. You must compare <em>within</em> each paragraph, using connectives like “similarly,” “in contrast,” “whereas,” and “however.” Essays that treat the poems in separate halves rarely reach the top bands, however strong the individual analysis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Feature-spotting without analysis:</strong> Naming a technique (e.g. “Shelley uses alliteration”) is not enough. You must explain the <em>effect</em> of that technique and link it to the poet’s purpose. Ask: <em>Why</em> did the poet choose this word/technique? What does it make the reader feel or think?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Bolting on context:</strong> Context should be woven into your analysis, not added as a separate sentence. Instead of “Blake wrote during the Industrial Revolution,” write: “Blake’s use of ‘charter’d’ reflects the commodification of public space during the Industrial Revolution, when even the Thames was controlled by commercial interests.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>For similarities:</strong> Similarly, Likewise, In the same way, Both poets, This is echoed in, [Poet B] also</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>For differences:</strong> In contrast, Whereas, However, Conversely, Unlike [Poet A], While [Poet A]… [Poet B]</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>For nuanced comparison:</strong> Although both poets… they differ in, While the theme is shared, the methods contrast, Where [Poet A] uses… [Poet B] instead employs</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">The comparison question requires you to compare two poems throughout your response — never write about one poem and then the other.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Choose your second poem carefully: the best comparisons involve poems that share a theme but present it differently through contrasting methods and contexts.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Use the PEALC structure (Point, Evidence, Analysis, Link, Context) to ensure every paragraph addresses all three Assessment Objectives.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Aim for <strong>three comparative paragraphs</strong> in 45 minutes (5 minutes planning, 35 minutes writing, 5 minutes checking). Each paragraph should discuss <em>both poems together</em>. The strongest responses will compare not just what the poems say (AO1) but <em>how</em> they say it (AO2 — language, structure, form) and <em>why</em> (AO3 — context). Remember: you choose the second poem, so pick one you know well and can analyse in detail.</p>
