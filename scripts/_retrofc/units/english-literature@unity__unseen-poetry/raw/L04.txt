How to approach the 8-mark comparison question, linking methods, themes, and effects across two poems.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Understanding Question 2</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the 24-mark single-poem analysis, the exam gives you a second unseen poem and asks you to <dfn class="term" data-def="Identifying and analysing the similarities and differences between two poems in terms of their methods, themes, and effects on the reader.">compare</dfn> the two poems. Question 2 is worth 8 marks and should take about 15 minutes: 5 minutes reading and annotating the new poem, and 10 minutes writing one detailed comparison paragraph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Many students lose marks on this question by writing about each poem separately. The question is asking for a <strong>genuinely comparative</strong> response — one where you move back and forth between the poems, drawing connections and contrasts in the same sentence or paragraph.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover the mark scheme and recall: Question 2 has 3 levels, with Level 3 (7-8 marks) requiring you to compare poets' use of form, language and struc...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Question 2 is marked out of 8 on three levels. Level 3 (7–8 marks) requires you to “identify, explore and compare how the poets use form, language and/or structure” and “compare how the poets use these techniques to affect, manipulate or influence the reader.” The key word is <strong>compare</strong> — not analyse separately.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The PEECPEED Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For the 8-mark comparison, use the <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparative paragraph structure: Point, Evidence, Explore (Poem 1), Connective, Point, Evidence, Explore (Poem 2), Develop — designed for linking two texts.">PEECPEED</dfn> framework. This structure keeps you focused on comparison throughout:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>P</strong> — Make a point about Poem 1 that answers the question. <strong>E</strong> — Provide evidence (an embedded quotation from Poem 1). <strong>E</strong> — Explore the technique and its effect. <strong>C</strong> — Use a <dfn class="term" data-def="Words or phrases that signal comparison between texts — showing similarities (similarly, likewise, correspondingly) or differences (however, conversely, in contrast, whereas).">comparison connective</dfn> to link to Poem 2. <strong>P</strong> — Make a connected point about Poem 2. <strong>E</strong> — Provide evidence from Poem 2. <strong>E</strong> — Explore the technique and its effect. <strong>D</strong> — Develop by reflecting on what the comparison reveals about the poets’ different attitudes or purposes.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparison Connectives to Use</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>For similarities:</strong> Similarly, Likewise, Correspondingly, In the same way, Both poets, This is echoed in. <strong>For differences:</strong> However, Conversely, In contrast, Whereas, On the other hand, Unlike, While, This differs from. These connectives show that you are genuinely comparing, not just analysing two poems back-to-back.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">What to Compare</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The question will usually tell you the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central ideas, subjects, or messages explored in a poem — such as love, death, nature, conflict, identity, power, or loss.">theme</dfn> or focus for comparison (e.g. “feelings towards loss and death”). Within that theme, you should compare three things:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>1. Methods:</strong> How do the poets use language and/or structure differently? One poet may use personification while the other uses an extended metaphor. One may use regular rhyme while the other uses free verse. <strong>2. Attitudes:</strong> Do the poets feel the same way about the theme, or differently? One may accept death peacefully while the other resists it. <strong>3. Effects on the reader:</strong> Do the poems make you feel the same emotion, or contrasting ones? One may create sadness while the other creates unease.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close your notes and remember: you have exactly 10 minutes to write one detailed PEECPEED paragraph with embedded quotations from both poems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You only have 10 minutes to write, so aim for <strong>one detailed comparison paragraph</strong>. A single well-developed PEECPEED paragraph with embedded quotations from both poems, specific technique analysis, and clear comparison connectives will score higher than two separate analyses of each poem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Worked Example: Comparing Two Poems About Loss</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Consider this question: <em>“Both poems discuss nature, loss, and death. Compare the ways that poets present their feelings towards loss and death in ‘Pictures of Memory’ and ‘The Last Rose of Summer.’”</em> [8 Marks]</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Model Comparison Paragraph</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In ‘Pictures of Memory,’ Cary uses metaphor to present strong feelings of positivity towards death. The “gates of light” metaphor could be alluding to the gates of heaven, suggesting the poet believes her brother is now at peace in a better place. The word “light” has warm, comforting connotations, indicating the speaker has reached a state of acceptance. This is further supported by references to nature throughout, revealing a tone of peace and purity. <strong>Similarly</strong>, in ‘The Last Rose of Summer,’ Moore also uses metaphor to convey strong feelings towards death; <strong>however</strong>, these feelings are less positive and accepting. The “last rose of Summer” symbolises an elderly person waiting to die long after their friends have passed. “Summer” symbolises beauty and vitality, while “rose” has connotations of love and romance. Yet the semantic field of loss in “last… alone… gone… pine” outlines the narrator’s negative emotions. <strong>Unlike</strong> Cary, Moore presents someone struggling to come to terms with loss rather than finding peace in it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Notice how the model paragraph moves between the two poems using connectives (“Similarly,” “however,” “Unlike”). It identifies a shared method (metaphor) but explores how the poets use it differently to create contrasting attitudes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Planning Your Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the 5 minutes you have for reading and annotating the second poem, use a quick comparison grid:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Quick Comparison Grid Method</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Draw a quick table with three columns: <strong>Poem 1 | Both / Difference | Poem 2</strong>. Under each poem, jot down: (1) a key quotation, (2) the main technique, (3) the tone/attitude. In the middle column, note whether these are similar or different. This takes 2–3 minutes and gives you a clear plan for your paragraph. For example: Poem 1: “gates of light” / metaphor / accepting of death. Poem 2: “last rose of Summer” / metaphor / struggling with loss. Middle: Both use metaphor but contrasting attitudes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Common Comparison Mistakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Writing about poems separately:</strong> The most common mistake. If you write one paragraph about Poem 1 and another about Poem 2, you are <em>analysing</em>, not <em>comparing</em>. That caps your mark at Level 1 (1–2 marks). <strong>Comparing content instead of methods:</strong> Saying “both poems are about death” is not analysis. You must compare <em>how</em> the poets present the theme — their techniques, word choices, structures, and effects. <strong>Ignoring the second poem:</strong> Some students spend too long on the first poem and barely reference the second. Aim for roughly equal coverage of both.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Finding Comparisons Quickly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">When you first read the second poem, look for these quick comparison points: <strong>Tone:</strong> Is it the same or different from Poem 1? <strong>Imagery type:</strong> Do both poets use nature imagery, or does one use something else? <strong>Structure:</strong> Are the stanza patterns similar or contrasting? <strong>Speaker’s attitude:</strong> Does the speaker in Poem 2 feel the same way as in Poem 1, or are they at a different emotional stage? <strong>Overall message:</strong> Do the poems reach the same conclusion about the theme?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this tip and recall the comparison sentence structure: 'Both poets use [technique], however while Poet A uses it to suggest [X], Poet B uses ...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You can compare both similarities AND differences in the same paragraph. In fact, this is the most effective approach: “Both poets use [technique], <strong>however</strong> while Poet A uses it to suggest [X], Poet B uses it to create a contrasting sense of [Y].” This single sentence is already a strong comparison.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Timing and Exam Strategy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Section C of Paper 2 gives you 45 minutes total for both questions. Use the time strategically: <strong>Question 1 (24 marks):</strong> 10 minutes annotating, 20 minutes writing. <strong>Question 2 (8 marks):</strong> 5 minutes annotating the second poem and planning your comparison, 10 minutes writing. Do not spend longer than 30 minutes on Question 1 — Question 2 is worth a third of Section C’s marks and students routinely underperform on it simply by running out of time.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n29">Question 2 is worth 8 marks and demands genuine comparison — move between poems using connectives like “similarly,” “however,” and “in contrast.”</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">Compare methods (techniques), attitudes (what the poets think/feel), and effects (how the reader responds) — not just content or themes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Use the PEECPEED structure and a quick comparison grid to plan your paragraph in under 3 minutes, leaving 10 minutes for focused, comparative writing.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">For Question 2, a good answer shows <strong>genuine comparison</strong>, not two separate analyses. Every sentence should reference both poems or use a comparison connective. If your paragraph does not contain words like “similarly,” “however,” “whereas,” or “in contrast,” you are probably not comparing. One well-structured PEECPEED paragraph with clear connectives, embedded quotations from both poems, and method-level analysis will comfortably reach Level 3 (7–8 marks).</p>
