<h2 data-narration-id="n1">First Impressions: The Novel’s Original Title</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Austen originally titled the novel <em>First Impressions</em>, and Chapters 1–6 show why. Every major relationship in the book is shaped by snap judgements made at the Meryton assembly and in its immediate aftermath. Darcy judges Elizabeth as “tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me”; Elizabeth judges Darcy as proud and disagreeable; Mrs Bennet judges Bingley as the perfect son-in-law purely because of his £5,000 a year. Austen builds the entire novel around the question of whether people can move beyond these first impressions — and at what cost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Meryton assembly in Chapter 3 functions as the novel’s inciting incident. It is a public event where the whole neighbourhood gathers, and where reputations are made and destroyed in a single evening. Bingley is immediately popular: “he was lively and unreserved, danced every dance, was angry that the ball closed so early.” Darcy, by contrast, “danced only once with Mrs Hurst and once with Miss Bingley, declined being introduced to any other lady, and spent the rest of the evening in walking about the room.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall Darcy’s exact insult about Elizabeth and explain why it matters for the plot.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Darcy’s insult — “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” — is overheard by Elizabeth and shapes her <dfn class="term" data-def="A preconceived opinion formed without sufficient evidence or reason, leading to unfair judgement.">prejudice</dfn> against him for much of the novel. This single sentence drives the central conflict: Elizabeth’s wounded pride prevents her from seeing Darcy’s true character until his letter forces her to re-examine her judgement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Bingley and Darcy: A Study in Contrasts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Austen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who contrasts with another character, highlighting their differences.">foil</dfn> characters to sharpen her themes. Bingley is open, warm, and socially generous; Darcy is reserved, proud, and socially awkward. The neighbourhood quickly decides that Bingley is “quite young, wonderfully handsome, extremely agreeable” while Darcy is “the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world.” These instant verdicts prove unreliable: Bingley’s amiability makes him easily influenced by others (particularly his sisters and Darcy), while Darcy’s apparent pride conceals genuine integrity.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Assembly as Social Theatre</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Dancing at a Regency assembly was not simply entertainment — it was a public display of social worth. To dance with someone was to signal interest; to refuse was an insult. Darcy’s refusal to dance with anyone beyond his own party marks him as antisocial. When Bingley urges him to dance with Elizabeth and Darcy refuses within earshot, the insult is amplified by its public setting. Elizabeth’s response — she retells the story “with great spirit among her friends” — shows her using humour as a defence mechanism, but also reveals that the wound is real.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Austen’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique where the narrator’s voice blends with a character’s thoughts, presenting the character’s perspective in the third person.">free indirect discourse</dfn> means we experience the assembly partly through the neighbourhood’s collective consciousness. Phrases like “he was discovered to be proud; to be above his company” show how quickly gossip solidifies into accepted truth. Austen questions whether public opinion is ever reliable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Elizabeth’s Wit and Independence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Elizabeth stands apart from the other female characters because she responds to Darcy’s insult not with tears or submission but with laughter. Her ability to laugh at herself and at Darcy is central to her appeal. In Chapter 6, she tells Charlotte: “I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine.” This line is crucial — it shows that Elizabeth’s “prejudice” against Darcy is rooted in her own wounded pride. The novel’s title refers to both characters: Darcy’s pride and Elizabeth’s prejudice, but also Darcy’s prejudice against her family and Elizabeth’s pride in her own judgement.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jane and Bingley: The Conventional Romance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Jane and Bingley’s instant mutual attraction at the assembly represents the conventional romance plot — two good-natured people who fall in love at first sight. But Austen complicates this: Jane’s reserved manner means Bingley cannot be sure of her feelings, and Charlotte warns Elizabeth that Jane must “show more affection than she feels.” Jane’s goodness becomes a vulnerability. She is too trusting to suspect Bingley’s sisters of sabotaging the relationship, and too modest to pursue him when he leaves Netherfield.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The Jane–Bingley subplot serves as a mirror to Elizabeth–Darcy. Where Jane is passive and trusting, Elizabeth is active and sceptical. Where Bingley is easily led, Darcy is fiercely independent. Austen uses the parallel to argue that a successful marriage requires both feeling <em>and</em> judgement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Elizabeth’s line about forgiving Darcy’s pride and explain the double meaning.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth’s line “I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine” reveals that the novel’s title applies to both protagonists. Elizabeth has pride (in her judgement) and prejudice (against Darcy); Darcy has pride (in his status) and prejudice (against her family’s lack of connections). Both must overcome their flaws to find happiness.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why First Impressions Matter for the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Eduqas extract question may come from these early chapters. You need to be able to analyse Austen’s language closely (AO1 and AO2) while also connecting the extract to the rest of the novel. If given an extract from the assembly, discuss how the first impressions formed here are overturned by the end of the novel — Darcy proves honourable and generous, while Wickham (who makes a charming first impression later) proves dishonest and predatory. This pattern of reversal is the novel’s central structural device.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The Meryton assembly establishes the central conflict: Darcy’s public insult of Elizabeth creates a prejudice that drives the plot until his letter forces her to re-examine her judgement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Bingley and Darcy function as foils — Bingley’s openness and Darcy’s reserve lead the neighbourhood (and the reader) to make the same snap judgements the novel ultimately critiques.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Elizabeth’s line about forgiving Darcy’s pride reveals that both protagonists suffer from the flaws named in the title — pride and prejudice are intertwined.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When analysing an extract from the early chapters, always link forward to how first impressions change. Show the examiner you know the whole novel by connecting the extract to later events — for example, Darcy’s insult at the assembly versus his generous actions at Pemberley. This demonstrates AO1 (whole-text knowledge) alongside your close reading.</p>
