<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme: Pride and Prejudice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel’s title names its two central flaws. <dfn class="term" data-def="An excessively high opinion of oneself, particularly regarding one’s social status, abilities, or worth.">Pride</dfn> is most obviously associated with Darcy — his belief that his wealth and status entitle him to look down on Elizabeth’s family. But Elizabeth is also proud: proud of her judgement, proud of her independence, and wounded in her pride by Darcy’s insult. <dfn class="term" data-def="Preconceived opinion not based on reason or experience, leading to unfair treatment of a person or group.">Prejudice</dfn> is most obviously Elizabeth’s — she prejudges Darcy based on his behaviour at the assembly and Wickham’s lies. But Darcy is also prejudiced: against the Bennet family’s social connections and lack of wealth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Austen’s genius is to show that pride and prejudice are <em>intertwined</em>. Elizabeth’s prejudice against Darcy is rooted in her wounded pride. Darcy’s pride in his class creates prejudice against Elizabeth’s family. Both characters must recognise and overcome both flaws to achieve happiness. Mary Bennet offers a neat definition: “Pride relates more to our opinion of ourselves, vanity to what we would have others think of us.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain how pride and prejudice apply to BOTH Elizabeth and Darcy, not just one each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both protagonists suffer from both pride and prejudice. Darcy’s class pride creates prejudice against the Bennets; Elizabeth’s intellectual pride creates prejudice against Darcy. The novel argues that self-knowledge is the cure: “Till this moment I never knew myself” marks Elizabeth’s recognition, while Darcy’s changed behaviour at Pemberley proves his.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme: Marriage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Austen presents marriage as the defining event in a woman’s life — not because it should be, but because Regency society made it so. The novel offers four models: Elizabeth and Darcy (love plus mutual respect), Jane and Bingley (affection and goodness), Charlotte and Collins (pragmatic security), and Lydia and Wickham (physical attraction without substance). Austen does not condemn Charlotte’s choice — she understands the impossible position women faced — but she clearly argues that the best marriage combines feeling with judgement.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

    <span>Theme: Appearance versus Reality</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Almost every character in the novel is not what they first appear. Darcy seems proud but is generous; Wickham seems charming but is dishonest; Caroline Bingley seems refined but is a snob concealing her trade origins; Charlotte seems foolish for marrying Collins but is actually making the most rational decision available to her. The novel repeatedly asks: can we trust what we see? Austen’s answer is no — true understanding requires time, evidence, and the humility to revise first impressions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">This theme connects directly to the narrative technique. Austen’s free indirect discourse means the reader experiences the story through Elizabeth’s perspective for much of the novel, and Elizabeth’s perceptions are wrong. The reader is deceived alongside the protagonist, making the moment of revelation (“Till this moment I never knew myself”) a shared experience. Austen implicates the reader in Elizabeth’s failure of judgement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Theme: Class and Social Mobility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Austen neither endorses nor entirely rejects the class system. She mocks its absurdities (Lady Catherine’s pomposity, Collins’s servility) but also acknowledges that characters like Darcy take their social responsibilities seriously. Her argument is that <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that a person’s worth should be measured by their character and behaviour, not their birth or wealth.">meritocracy</dfn> — judging people by character rather than birth — produces better outcomes than rigid class boundaries. The Gardiners prove this: they are the most admirable adults in the novel despite living in unfashionable Cheapside.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Most 19th-century prose questions give you a printed extract and ask you to write about the extract <em>and</em> the novel as a whole. You will be assessed against the standard GCSE English Literature assessment objectives: AO1 (informed personal response with textual references), AO2 (analysis of language, form and structure), AO3 (understanding of context), and AO4 (accurate written expression). Check your exam board’s specimen paper for exact mark weightings and timings.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Structure your response:</strong> Begin by addressing the extract closely (AO1 + AO2), then broaden to the wider novel (AO1 + AO3). Do not write a separate “context paragraph” — embed contextual understanding within your analysis. Aim for 4–5 developed paragraphs, each containing a point, evidence (quotation), analysis of language or technique, and a contextual link. Use the extract as your anchor but show you know the whole text.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the four assessment objectives and what each requires.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>GCSE English Literature responses are assessed against four AOs: AO1 (informed personal response with textual references), AO2 (analysis of language, form, and structure), AO3 (understanding of contexts), and AO4 (accurate spelling, punctuation, and grammar). Top-band responses embed all four within every paragraph rather than treating them as separate tasks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Quotation Bank</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Memorise these key quotations and practise linking each to a theme, a character, and a technique:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">“It is a truth universally acknowledged” — irony, marriage as economic transaction. “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” — Darcy’s pride, first impressions. “I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine” — Elizabeth’s wounded pride. “Till this moment I never knew myself” — anagnorisis, self-knowledge. “I am not romantic, you know; I never was” — Charlotte’s pragmatism. “Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?” — Lady Catherine, class prejudice. “I was in the middle before I knew that I had begun” — Darcy’s transformation.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Pride and prejudice are intertwined flaws affecting both protagonists — self-knowledge is the cure, and the novel traces both characters’ journey toward it.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The four marriages form a structural argument about what makes a successful relationship: love, judgement, and mutual respect.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">For the Eduqas exam, embed AO1–AO4 within every paragraph, begin with close extract analysis, then broaden to the wider novel with contextual connections.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For the 40-mark Eduqas question, aim for 4–5 paragraphs. Start with the extract (close analysis of 2–3 quotations with language/technique comments), then broaden to the wider novel (connecting the extract’s themes to other key moments). Embed context throughout — never in a standalone paragraph. End with a brief concluding sentence that answers the question directly.</p>
