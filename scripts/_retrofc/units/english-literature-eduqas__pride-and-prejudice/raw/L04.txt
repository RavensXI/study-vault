--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pemberley: More Than a House</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth visits Pemberley in Chapters 42–43 while touring Derbyshire with her aunt and uncle, the Gardiners. The visit is a turning point because Pemberley is both a physical place and a <dfn class="term" data-def="An object, place, or idea that represents something beyond its literal meaning.">symbol</dfn> of Darcy’s true character. Elizabeth initially refuses to visit, fearing an awkward encounter, but agrees when she learns Darcy is away. Her reaction to the house reveals how much her feelings have already shifted since reading his letter.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Austen describes Pemberley with unusual detail: it is “a large, handsome stone building, standing well on rising ground, and backed by a ridge of high woody hills.” The estate is beautiful but not ostentatious — its grounds have been “neither formally nor falsely adorned.” This description mirrors Darcy himself: impressive but not showy, with substance rather than superficial display. Elizabeth thinks: “And of this place I might have been mistress!” Austen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique where a character’s thoughts are presented indirectly through the narrator’s voice.">free indirect discourse</dfn> to let us hear Elizabeth’s regret without her having to voice it directly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain three ways Pemberley reflects Darcy’s true character.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pemberley symbolises Darcy’s real nature. Its grounds are “neither formally nor falsely adorned” — natural, tasteful, unpretentious. The housekeeper Mrs Reynolds describes Darcy as “the best landlord, and the best master” who has never had “a cross word” from her. Elizabeth is forced to reconcile this private Darcy with the public one she judged at Meryton.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mrs Reynolds and the Private Darcy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The housekeeper Mrs Reynolds provides crucial testimony about Darcy’s character. She has known him “ever since he was four years old” and declares him “the best landlord, and the best master, that ever lived.” She adds: “I have never had a cross word from him in my life.” This is significant because servants see their masters without social masks. Mrs Reynolds’s praise carries more weight than any public performance because it comes from daily private observation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Mr and Mrs Gardiner are Elizabeth’s uncle and aunt who live in Cheapside, London — a commercial area that Caroline Bingley sneers at. Yet the Gardiners are the most sensible, refined, and morally admirable adults in the novel. They are intelligent, tasteful, and genuinely caring. When Darcy meets them at Pemberley, he treats them with courtesy and respect, and later involves Mr Gardiner in resolving Lydia’s elopement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Austen uses the Gardiners to demolish the class prejudice that Caroline Bingley and Lady Catherine represent. If worth were measured by character rather than address, the Gardiners would outrank almost everyone in the novel. Elizabeth herself acknowledges this: she credits them as the people who brought her and Darcy together. They are Austen’s quiet argument that <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of being socially refined, ethical, and well-mannered, regardless of birth or wealth.">gentility</dfn> is about behaviour, not birth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Darcy’s Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When Darcy unexpectedly returns to Pemberley and finds Elizabeth there, his behaviour is completely changed. He is “eager to introduce his sister” to Elizabeth — a gesture that would only make sense if he still loved her and wanted her approval. He is polite, attentive, and warm to the Gardiners. This is the Darcy that Elizabeth could not see at the assembly because his reserve and social awkwardness masked his true nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Darcy’s change is not a sudden conversion but a gradual growth. His letter showed intellectual honesty; his behaviour at Pemberley shows emotional generosity. Elizabeth’s accusation that he did not behave in a “gentleman-like manner” wounded him profoundly, and he has worked to become worthy of her respect. When Elizabeth later asks when he began to love her, he replies: “I was in the middle before I knew that I had begun.” This honest, unguarded confession is the opposite of his first stiff proposal.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Elizabeth’s Changed Perceptions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">At Pemberley, Elizabeth sees Darcy through new eyes. The letter began her re-evaluation; Pemberley completes it. She notices that his pride has softened into quiet dignity, that his reserve was never coldness but shyness, and that his wealth is used responsibly rather than displayed arrogantly. Her thought — “And of this place I might have been mistress!” — is not mercenary. Austen is showing that Elizabeth now values the substance behind the wealth: Darcy’s character, taste, and moral seriousness.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Crucially, Elizabeth’s changed feelings develop <em>before</em> she learns of Darcy’s role in saving Lydia. Her love is based on revised judgement, not gratitude. Austen is careful to show that Elizabeth’s transformation is intellectual and moral, not merely emotional.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write from memory what Darcy says about when he began to love Elizabeth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Darcy’s later confession — “I was in the middle before I knew that I had begun” — contrasts directly with his first proposal’s arrogant focus on Elizabeth’s “inferiority.” His growth from proud aristocrat to honest lover mirrors Elizabeth’s growth from prejudiced judge to self-aware woman. Both must change to deserve each other.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Pemberley symbolises Darcy’s true character: tasteful, substantial, and unpretentious. Elizabeth’s response to the house mirrors her growing respect for its owner.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The Gardiners prove that gentility is about behaviour, not birth — Austen uses them to dismantle the class prejudice of characters like Caroline Bingley and Lady Catherine.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Both Darcy and Elizabeth undergo genuine moral growth: Darcy learns humility from Elizabeth’s rejection, and Elizabeth learns self-awareness from Darcy’s letter.</li></ul>
