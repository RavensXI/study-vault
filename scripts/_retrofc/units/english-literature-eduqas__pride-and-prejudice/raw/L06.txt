Elizabeth, Darcy, and the supporting cast: character analysis with key quotations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Elizabeth Bennet: Wit, Pride, and Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth is Austen’s most celebrated <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character in a story, whose journey and development drive the narrative.">protagonist</dfn>. She is intelligent, witty, and morally courageous — but she is also proud of her own judgement and capable of serious misjudgement. Her father calls her the most sensible of his daughters, and Darcy is attracted to “the liveliness of your mind.” Elizabeth’s defining quality is her refusal to be intimidated by wealth or status: she stands up to Darcy, Lady Catherine, and Collins with equal composure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">However, Elizabeth’s confidence in her own perception becomes her flaw. She believes Wickham because his story confirms her prejudice against Darcy. She dismisses Jane’s generosity and Charlotte’s pragmatism because neither matches her own romantic idealism. Her growth comes when she recognises that “Till this moment I never knew myself” — that her judgement is fallible and her pride has blinded her just as much as Darcy’s pride blinded him.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall three quotations that reveal Elizabeth’s character at different points in the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth’s key quotations: “I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine” (wounded pride), “Till this moment I never knew myself” (self-recognition), and her defiance of Lady Catherine: “I am only resolved to act in that manner, which will, in my own opinion, constitute my happiness.” These three moments trace her arc from prejudice through humility to principled independence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mr Darcy: Pride, Reserve, and Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Darcy is initially presented through the neighbourhood’s hostile verdict: “the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world.” He appears cold, aloof, and class-conscious. His first proposal confirms this: he focuses on Elizabeth’s “inferiority” and “degradation” rather than his love. But the novel gradually reveals that Darcy’s reserve masks genuine feeling, and his pride is partly justified — he <em>is</em> responsible, generous, and morally serious. Mrs Reynolds’s testimony, the Pemberley visit, and his secret rescue of Lydia reveal the private man behind the public mask.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Mr Bennet is witty and intelligent — Elizabeth clearly inherits her sharpness from him — but he is also negligent. He married Mrs Bennet for her beauty and has spent twenty years retreating into his library to avoid her. He allows Lydia to go to Brighton despite Elizabeth’s warnings, and his response to the elopement is passive: “Let me once in my life feel how much I have been to blame.” The confession is sincere but brief. Mr Bennet represents the danger of intelligence without responsibility — he sees the world clearly but refuses to act on what he sees.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Austen uses Mr Bennet as a cautionary example of what happens when a marriage is based on physical attraction alone. His witty detachment, which seems charming at first, is revealed as a form of moral laziness that endangers his daughters.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Supporting Cast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Mrs Bennet</strong> is Austen’s most comic creation: loud, embarrassing, and obsessed with marriage. Yet her behaviour is driven by genuine fear of poverty under entailment. She is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who does not undergo significant change or development in a story.">static character</dfn> — she does not grow or change — but she serves as a mirror reflecting the society that created her.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Wickham</strong> is the novel’s villain, but he is also Austen’s most complex warning about charm. He is everything Darcy is not on the surface — sociable, flattering, attentive — but beneath the surface he is dishonest, predatory, and mercenary. He represents the danger of valuing appearance over substance.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jane, Lydia, and the Bennet Sisters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Jane</strong> is Elizabeth’s foil: gentle, trusting, and inclined to see the best in everyone. Her goodness is genuine but also a weakness — she is too trusting to suspect Caroline Bingley’s manipulation. <strong>Lydia</strong> is impulsive, vain, and oblivious to consequences. Her elopement with Wickham is the result of poor parenting (Mr Bennet’s neglect, Mrs Bennet’s indulgence) and shows what happens when a young woman is given freedom without education or moral guidance.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Mary</strong> and <strong>Kitty</strong> are lightly sketched. Mary is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A representation of a widely held but oversimplified idea of a particular type of person.">caricature</dfn> of the earnest intellectual who lacks genuine understanding. Kitty is easily led and follows Lydia’s example. Together, the five sisters represent a spectrum of female responses to the limited options available to Regency women.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name five key characters and one quotation or detail that defines each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Austen’s characters are defined by their relationship to the central theme of judgement. Elizabeth judges too quickly; Darcy judges by class; Jane judges too generously; Mr Bennet judges accurately but refuses to act; Mrs Bennet judges entirely by wealth. Every character embodies a different failure or success of perception.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Elizabeth’s arc moves from confident misjudgement through humiliating self-recognition to principled independence — she is Austen’s model of a mind willing to learn.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Darcy’s transformation from public pride to private generosity is proven by actions (the rescue) rather than words — Austen values substance over performance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Every character in the novel embodies a different relationship to judgement, making the entire cast a thematic exploration of how we see others and ourselves.</li></ul>
