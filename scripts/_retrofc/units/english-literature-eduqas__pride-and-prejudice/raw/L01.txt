--- DESCRIPTION ---
Regency society, class, marriage, and the world Austen wrote Pride and Prejudice for.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The World of Pride and Prejudice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane Austen published <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> in 1813, during the <dfn class="term" data-def="The period 1811–1820 when George, Prince of Wales, ruled as regent because King George III was deemed unfit to govern.">Regency period</dfn>. The novel is set among the landed gentry of rural England — families who did not work for a living but lived on inherited wealth and rental income. Understanding this world is essential for the Eduqas exam because Austen’s social comedy only makes sense when you grasp the rigid class system and marriage market that governed her characters’ lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For women in Austen’s time, marriage was not primarily about love — it was an economic necessity. Under the laws of <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal rule that restricted inheritance of property to male heirs, meaning daughters could not inherit their father’s estate.">entailment</dfn>, property typically passed to the nearest male relative. The Bennet estate is entailed to Mr Collins, meaning that when Mr Bennet dies, his wife and five daughters will be left with almost nothing. Mrs Bennet’s obsession with marrying her daughters well is therefore not simply comic — it is a matter of survival.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three reasons why marriage was an economic necessity for Regency women.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under entailment, the Bennet estate will pass to Mr Collins when Mr Bennet dies. Mrs Bennet’s determination to marry her daughters to wealthy men is driven by genuine financial desperation, not mere social climbing. Without good marriages, the Bennet women face poverty.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Class and Social Hierarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Regency society was divided into strict ranks. At the top sat the aristocracy, below them the <dfn class="term" data-def="Wealthy families who owned land and lived on its income without needing to work, sitting below the aristocracy but above the professional and working classes.">landed gentry</dfn> (like Mr Darcy, with his estate at Pemberley and £10,000 a year), and below them the professional classes and tradespeople. Mr Bingley’s wealth comes from trade — his family made their fortune in the north of England — and Caroline Bingley’s snobbery towards the Bennets is partly an attempt to distance herself from her own commercial origins.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Austen uses Darcy’s initial pride and his aunt Lady Catherine de Bourgh’s outrage at Elizabeth’s “inferiority” to expose the absurdity of judging people by birth rather than character. Lady Catherine tells Elizabeth: “Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?” This language of contamination reveals how the upper classes viewed marriage across class lines as a form of social disease.</p>

Their education was limited to “accomplishments” — music, drawing, needlework, and modern languages — designed to make them attractive to potential husbands. Caroline Bingley lists these accomplishments in Chapter 8, and Darcy adds that a woman must also have “something more substantial, in the improvement of her mind by extensive reading.” Elizabeth embodies this intellectual depth, which is precisely what distinguishes her from the other women in the novel.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Charlotte Lucas, aged twenty-seven and with no fortune, accepts Mr Collins’s proposal despite finding him ridiculous. She tells Elizabeth: “I am not romantic, you know; I never was. I ask only a comfortable home.” Charlotte’s pragmatic choice shows the reality facing women without beauty, wealth, or youth — she cannot afford Elizabeth’s idealism.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Austen’s Satirical Purpose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Austen was not simply describing her society — she was <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of humour, irony, or exaggeration to criticise people’s stupidity or vices, particularly in the context of social behaviour.">satirising</dfn> it. The novel’s famous opening line — “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife” — is deeply ironic. The “universal truth” is really Mrs Bennet’s desperate assumption, not an actual fact. Austen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of irony where the narrator’s words carry a different or opposite meaning to what is literally stated, creating humour or criticism.">narrative irony</dfn> throughout the novel to expose the gap between how characters see themselves and how the reader sees them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The small community around Meryton functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small place or community that represents the characteristics of something much larger.">microcosm</dfn> of Regency society. Balls, dinners, and social calls are the settings where marriages are negotiated. The Meryton assembly in Chapter 3 is a marketplace: Mr Bingley’s £5,000 a year and Darcy’s £10,000 are discussed before any character trait. Austen shows how a person’s worth is measured in pounds, not personality.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The militia officers stationed in Meryton (including Wickham) represent danger and temptation for young women. Lydia’s elopement with Wickham later in the novel shows the consequences when the marriage market breaks down — a woman’s reputation, once lost, threatens the entire family’s social standing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the novel’s opening line from memory, then explain what makes it ironic.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The opening line of <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> is a masterclass in narrative irony. “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife” actually reflects the desires of mothers like Mrs Bennet, not the man himself. Austen’s ironic tone is established from the very first sentence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Connecting Context to the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In the Eduqas Component 2 Section B exam, you will be given a printed extract and asked to write about both the extract and the wider text. AO3 (context) carries marks, so you must embed contextual understanding within your analysis. Do not write a separate paragraph on “context” — instead, weave it into your points about language and themes. For example, when discussing Charlotte’s acceptance of Collins, link it to the limited options available to women without fortune, showing how Austen uses this subplot to critique the marriage market.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The Bennet estate is entailed to Mr Collins, making marriage an economic necessity for the Bennet sisters — Mrs Bennet’s desperation is driven by real financial danger.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Austen satirises the Regency marriage market and class snobbery through irony, using characters like Lady Catherine, Charlotte Lucas, and Caroline Bingley to expose social hypocrisy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The novel’s opening line establishes Austen’s ironic narrative voice, signalling from the start that things are not always what they seem.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">In the Eduqas exam, context (AO3) must be woven into analysis, not bolted on. Instead of writing “In the Regency period, women could not inherit property,” link it directly to the text: “Charlotte’s acceptance of Collins reflects the reality facing women under entailment — Austen exposes a society where a comfortable home matters more than love.” Always connect context to a specific character, quotation, or scene.</p>
