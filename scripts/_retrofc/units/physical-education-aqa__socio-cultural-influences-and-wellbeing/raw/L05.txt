<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Feedback Matters in Sport</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every time a gymnast sticks a landing, a coach calls a correction, or a tennis player hears the crowd react to a shot, information is flowing. That information &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Information received about the outcome or quality of a movement or performance, used to improve future actions.">feedback</dfn> &mdash; is at the heart of how athletes learn, refine skills and progress. Without it, performers would repeat errors indefinitely. With the right type of feedback, delivered at the right moment, improvement accelerates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The specification organises feedback into three overlapping categories: its emotional tone (positive or negative), its source (intrinsic or extrinsic), and what it tells you (knowledge of results or knowledge of performance). A single piece of feedback can belong to more than one category simultaneously &mdash; for instance, a coach saying &ldquo;that was an excellent drive, but your follow-through was cut short&rdquo; is extrinsic, partly positive, partly negative, and contains knowledge of performance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Positive and Negative Feedback</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback that tells a performer what they did correctly, reinforcing the action so it is repeated.">Positive feedback</dfn> confirms that an action was performed correctly. It reinforces the movement pattern in the performer&rsquo;s memory, making it more likely to be repeated. A coach saying &ldquo;great shoulder rotation on that serve&rdquo; or a crowd applauding a clean strike are both positive feedback. For beginners especially, positive feedback is powerful &mdash; it builds confidence and keeps motivation high at a stage when errors are frequent and self-belief is fragile.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback that identifies an error or weakness in performance, pointing out what needs to change.">Negative feedback</dfn> highlights what went wrong and, ideally, what should be done differently. The term &ldquo;negative&rdquo; does not mean unkind or demoralising &mdash; it simply means the feedback signals a mismatch between what was intended and what was produced. A rugby coach who tells a player &ldquo;your tackling angle is too narrow, step out wider next time&rdquo; is giving negative feedback that is precise and constructive. Elite performers often seek and welcome negative feedback because they are confident enough to use it as a tool rather than a threat.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: for beginners, which type of feedback is most important and why? Then do the same for elite performers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Positive feedback is especially important for beginners because it builds confidence and reinforces correct movements. Negative feedback becomes more valuable for elite performers, who need precise detail on how to refine already high-level technique.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Intrinsic and Extrinsic Feedback</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback that comes from within the performer, through the senses &mdash; touch, proprioception, sight and sound.">Intrinsic feedback</dfn> (also called internal feedback) comes from the performer&rsquo;s own body. It travels through sensory channels: proprioception (the sense of where your limbs are in space), vision, touch and hearing. A gymnast knows her balance beam routine felt fluent because her proprioceptive system reports smooth weight transfer and correctly timed muscle contractions. A cricketer senses from the impact vibration through the bat handle whether the ball was struck from the middle of the bat. Intrinsic feedback is instantaneous and continuous &mdash; it arrives during and immediately after every movement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">For intrinsic feedback to be useful, the performer must have the experience and body awareness to interpret the sensations correctly. Beginners often struggle to do this &mdash; they may feel that a movement went well when it did not, because they lack the kinaesthetic reference point of what &ldquo;correct&rdquo; feels like. Elite athletes, by contrast, are highly attuned to intrinsic feedback: Mo Farah described being able to sense the precise moment his running economy started to drop in the final 200&nbsp;m of a 5,000&nbsp;m race.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback that comes from an outside source &mdash; a coach, a teammate, a video replay, or the scoreboard.">Extrinsic feedback</dfn> (external feedback) originates outside the performer. Sources include coaches and teachers, peers, video analysis, heart rate monitors, scoreboards and the crowd. Because it comes from outside, extrinsic feedback can fill the gaps that intrinsic feedback misses &mdash; for example, a sprinter cannot feel the exact angle of her block start, but her coach can see it on a camera and give precise corrections.</p>

Beginners lack the kinaesthetic memory to judge &ldquo;correct&rdquo; feeling &mdash; a novice swimmer may feel that her arm entry is clean when it is actually crossed over the midline. This is precisely why extrinsic feedback from a coach or video is so critical in early learning: it provides a reliable external reference point until the performer builds a more accurate internal model.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Knowledge of Results and Knowledge of Performance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback about the outcome of a movement &mdash; the score, distance, time or result achieved.">Knowledge of results</dfn> (KR) tells the performer what the outcome was: the shot went wide, the javelin landed at 42 metres, the race was completed in 13.8 seconds. It is always extrinsic because the result is separate from the movement itself. KR is easy to understand and motivating &mdash; seeing a time improve from 14.2&nbsp;s to 13.8&nbsp;s gives a clear signal that training is working &mdash; but it tells the performer nothing about why the result was what it was, or how to change it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Feedback about the quality of the movement itself &mdash; the technique, timing or mechanics that produced the result.">Knowledge of performance</dfn> (KP) focuses on the quality of the movement: the hips rotated too early, the release angle was too flat, the elbow position was correct. KP is richer than KR and more directly actionable because it tells the performer what to change. A coach watching a discus thrower and saying &ldquo;your pivot foot twisted before your non-throwing arm swept across &mdash; you lost rotation&rdquo; is giving knowledge of performance. Elite performers typically benefit more from KP because their outcomes are already competitive and fine technical detail is what separates them from rivals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words: what is the difference between knowledge of results and knowledge of performance? Give one sporting example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Knowledge of results (KR) = the outcome (time, score, distance). Knowledge of performance (KP) = the quality of the technique. KR motivates; KP teaches. Elite performers need both, but KP is the more powerful learning tool.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Choosing the Right Feedback for the Performer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The effectiveness of feedback depends on the performer&rsquo;s stage of learning. Beginners benefit most from positive feedback (to build confidence), extrinsic feedback (because intrinsic awareness is unreliable), and knowledge of results (which is simple to understand). Overloading a beginner with detailed KP can cause confusion and undermine motivation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Elite performers, on the other hand, respond best to specific negative and positive feedback on technique (KP), as well as their own well-developed intrinsic feedback. They also benefit from extrinsic sources such as video analysis and data from performance monitoring equipment. The key principle is that feedback should always be actionable &mdash; vague comments such as &ldquo;try harder&rdquo; or &ldquo;that was good&rdquo; without further detail have little learning value regardless of the performer&rsquo;s level.</p>

Intrinsic feedback feeds into the system through the sensory display (sight, touch, proprioception). Extrinsic feedback arrives as a new input &mdash; the coach&rsquo;s voice or the video replay enters the input stage alongside environmental information from the display. Both types then influence decision making in the next attempt, helping the performer select a more appropriate motor response from long-term memory. This loop is why consistent, timely feedback speeds up skill acquisition.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Feedback has three dimensions: emotional tone (positive or negative), source (intrinsic or extrinsic), and what it tells you (knowledge of results or knowledge of performance). These categories overlap &mdash; a single piece of feedback can fit all three simultaneously.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Beginners benefit most from positive, extrinsic feedback and simple knowledge of results; elite performers gain more from specific, technique-focused knowledge of performance and their own well-developed intrinsic feedback.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Feedback must be actionable to have learning value &mdash; vague praise or criticism does not help performers identify what to change or repeat.</li>
</ul>
