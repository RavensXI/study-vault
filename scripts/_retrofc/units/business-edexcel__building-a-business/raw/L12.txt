--- DESCRIPTION ---
Use financial, marketing and market data to support business decisions, and understand data limitations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Businesses Use Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every growing business generates and receives large amounts of data. Sales figures, customer feedback scores, market research surveys, competitor price lists &mdash; all of this can inform better decisions. The challenge is knowing which data to trust, how to read it accurately, and what it cannot tell you.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Business data falls into two broad categories. <dfn class="term" data-def="Data expressed as numbers that can be measured and compared.">Quantitative data</dfn> uses numbers &mdash; sales volumes, profit margins, market share percentages. <dfn class="term" data-def="Data based on opinions, feelings or descriptions that cannot be easily measured in numbers.">Qualitative data</dfn> captures opinions, descriptions and feelings &mdash; customer reviews, focus group transcripts, staff feedback. Both types have value; relying solely on one risks missing important information.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Types of Business Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The specification identifies four main categories of data a business uses to understand performance and inform decisions:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Financial data</strong> &mdash; profit and loss figures, cash flow statements, balance sheet extracts, break-even analysis. Used to assess profitability, liquidity and financial health.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Marketing data</strong> &mdash; customer satisfaction scores, conversion rates, advertising response rates, social media engagement metrics. Used to assess how well marketing is reaching and influencing customers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Market data</strong> &mdash; total market size, sales trends, competitor market share, growth rates. Used to assess a business&rsquo;s position within its industry and spot opportunities or threats.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Data from graphs and charts</strong> &mdash; bar charts, line graphs, pie charts and scatter diagrams present numerical data visually, making trends and comparisons easier to identify quickly.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the four types of business data. For each, write one example of the specific piece of data it might include.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="A business's sales as a percentage of total sales in the market.">Market share</dfn> is one of the most important market data measures. It shows not just how well a business is performing in absolute terms, but how it is performing <strong>relative to competitors</strong>. A business can increase its revenue while its market share falls &mdash; meaning rivals are growing faster.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Reading Graphs and Charts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When interpreting a graph or chart in an exam question, a systematic approach prevents simple errors:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13">Read the title and axis labels carefully before drawing any conclusions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14">Check the units on each axis (thousands, millions, percentages, indices).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15">Identify the trend (rising, falling, stable, fluctuating) before stating specific values.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">Note any anomalies &mdash; sudden spikes or dips &mdash; and consider what business event might explain them.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Calculating Market Share from Data</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Market share is calculated as: <strong>(Business&rsquo;s sales &divide; Total market sales) &times; 100</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Example: Inkwell Stationers has annual sales of &pound;2.4 million. The total UK stationery market is worth &pound;30 million. Market share = (&pound;2.4m &divide; &pound;30m) &times; 100 = <strong>8.00%</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">A rising market share means the business is growing faster than its market. A falling market share is a warning signal even if total sales are rising &mdash; competitors are gaining ground. This distinction is important when using market data to justify business decisions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Using Data to Support Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Data is most valuable when it informs specific decisions. A falling net profit margin might prompt a business to review supplier costs. A declining market share may lead to a marketing campaign or product development. An improving customer satisfaction score may justify a price increase.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">However, data should be used to <em>support</em> decisions rather than to make them automatically. Good business judgement combines data with context, experience and knowledge of the specific business&rsquo;s circumstances. Two businesses can look at the same market data and make completely different, equally rational decisions based on their different strategies, resources and risk appetites.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two limitations of financial data for making business decisions. Think about what numbers cannot tell you about a business.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Financial data is historical &mdash; it shows what <strong>has happened</strong>, not what will happen. A business with strong past profits can still fail if market conditions change rapidly. This is one reason why data must always be interpreted in the context of the wider business environment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Limitations of Business Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Understanding what data <em>cannot</em> tell you is just as important as knowing how to read it. Key limitations include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Historical data is backward-looking.</strong> Financial statements show last year&rsquo;s performance. A business making decisions based only on past data may miss new market trends or emerging threats.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Data can be misleading without context.</strong> A 10% rise in sales revenue looks positive, but not if it cost 25% more to achieve. Raw figures without comparison or context lead to poor decisions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Quantitative data misses qualitative insight.</strong> A sales report cannot tell you <em>why</em> customers are buying less, or what they really think about the product. Qualitative research is needed to fill this gap.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Data accuracy depends on how it was collected.</strong> Sample sizes, survey bias, accounting policies and data recording errors all affect reliability. Decisions based on flawed data can be worse than decisions made with no data at all.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Market data may not reflect the specific business&rsquo;s segment.</strong> National market size figures may not accurately represent the niche or region the business operates in.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: More Data Always Means Better Decisions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Students often assume that a business with more data will make better decisions. In practice, too much data without the ability to filter, interpret and apply it can lead to &ldquo;analysis paralysis&rdquo; &mdash; delayed decisions while more information is gathered. The quality, relevance and timeliness of data matter more than the quantity. A small business that acts quickly on one reliable signal may outperform a large business drowning in dashboards it cannot interpret. In extended responses, always question whether the data available is <em>sufficient and appropriate</em> for the decision being made.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Business data comes in four main types: financial, marketing, market and graphical. Quantitative data uses numbers; qualitative data captures opinions &mdash; both are needed for well-rounded decisions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Market share = (business sales &divide; total market sales) &times; 100. A rising market share shows the business is outgrowing its market; a falling share is a warning signal even when total sales are rising.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Data has real limitations: it is historical, can mislead without context, and quantitative data cannot explain the 'why' behind trends. Strong business analysis acknowledges what the data cannot tell you.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">For &ldquo;Identify from Data&rdquo; (1 mark), read from the graph or table directly and state only what you are asked. Do not interpret or add context &mdash; a single accurate reading is all that is required. For &ldquo;Evaluate a Statement&rdquo; (12 marks) about data, your conclusion must introduce new evaluative thinking: identify the specific factor (data type, business context, reliability of the source) that determines whether the statement holds. Writing &ldquo;it depends&rdquo; earns nothing &mdash; but &ldquo;it depends on whether the data is current, because historical financial figures cannot capture a sudden shift in consumer behaviour&rdquo; earns evaluation marks. Make your criteria explicit.</p>
