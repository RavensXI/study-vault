Design mix, life-cycle phases and extension strategies that keep products competitive.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Makes a Product Stand Out?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a business develops a product or service, it must balance three competing factors. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of function, aesthetics and cost that a business uses to design and position a product.">design mix</dfn> captures this balance: every product sits somewhere in the triangle of function, aesthetics and cost. Function refers to whether the product does its job well. Aesthetics covers how it looks, feels and is experienced by the user. Cost relates to the expense of producing it and, by extension, the price charged. A product does not have to score equally on all three &mdash; a budget supermarket stacks of baked beans focuses almost entirely on low cost, while a luxury perfume brand leans heavily on aesthetics and function. The skill is in understanding which factor matters most to the target market.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Differentiation is the reason design matters. <dfn class="term" data-def="Making a product or service distinct from competitors&rsquo; offerings in a way that is valued by customers.">Differentiation</dfn> gives customers a reason to choose one product over another. It can be achieved through design, brand identity, unique features, superior materials, or outstanding service. A business that fails to differentiate risks competing purely on price &mdash; a race to the bottom that squeezes profit margins.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three elements of the design mix? Give one example of a product that prioritises each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The design mix has three elements: <strong>function</strong> (does it work?), <strong>aesthetics</strong> (does it look and feel right?) and <strong>cost</strong> (is it affordable to produce?). Businesses adjust the balance depending on their target market and competitive strategy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Product Life Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">No product sells at the same rate forever. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A model showing the stages a product passes through from launch to withdrawal: introduction, growth, maturity and decline.">product life cycle</dfn> maps four phases that most products move through over time. Understanding which phase a product is in helps a business decide where to invest marketing spend, when to cut prices, and when to develop something new.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Phase 1: Introduction</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">At introduction, a product has just been launched. Sales are low because customers do not yet know it exists. The business spends heavily on promotion to build awareness. Costs are high relative to revenue, so the product is usually making a loss at this stage. Businesses use this phase to test the market and gather customer feedback. Not all products survive introduction &mdash; many are withdrawn quickly if demand fails to materialise.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Phases 2 &amp; 3: Growth and Maturity</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">During the growth phase, sales rise rapidly as more customers become aware of the product. Competitors begin to take notice and may launch similar products. The business is now making a profit and may widen distribution or add product variants. Promotional spend may shift from awareness-building to brand-reinforcing campaigns.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Maturity is the most profitable phase. Sales have peaked and are stable. Most customers in the target market have tried the product. Competition is fierce, so businesses compete on price, quality improvements, and promotional activity. Profit per unit may begin to fall as the market becomes saturated. This is the phase where many well-known brands sit for years &mdash; think of branded breakfast cereals or popular fizzy drinks that have been on shelves for decades.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Phase 4: Decline and Withdrawal</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Decline occurs when sales fall consistently. This may be caused by changing consumer tastes, new technology making the product obsolete, or more attractive competitor products entering the market. Sales and profits fall. A business must decide whether to withdraw the product, reduce its costs to maintain a small loyal customer base, or invest in an extension strategy to revive sales.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the four phases of the product life cycle in order, noting whether a business is typically making a profit or loss at each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four phases of the product life cycle are: <strong>introduction</strong> (high costs, low sales, often a loss), <strong>growth</strong> (rising sales, rising profit), <strong>maturity</strong> (peak sales, maximum profit, heavy competition) and <strong>decline</strong> (falling sales and profit). Cash flow varies significantly across the cycle.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Extension Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Businesses rarely want a product to reach the withdrawal stage. <dfn class="term" data-def="Actions taken by a business to extend the life of a product and prevent or slow the decline phase.">Extension strategies</dfn> are used to boost sales once a product enters maturity or starts to decline. Common approaches include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>New markets</strong> &mdash; selling to a different customer segment or entering overseas markets. A UK clothing brand might begin selling in Europe or Asia.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Product modification</strong> &mdash; updating the product with new features, improved materials or a refreshed design. A smartphone manufacturer regularly updates its flagship handset rather than launching an entirely new product.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Promotional campaigns</strong> &mdash; relaunching with new advertising or a celebrity endorsement to attract a fresh audience or re-engage lapsed customers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Price reductions</strong> &mdash; a lower price attracts price-sensitive customers who were not willing to pay the original price, expanding the market.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Extension strategies cost money, so a business must judge whether the potential increase in revenue justifies the investment. If the product is genuinely becoming obsolete &mdash; for instance, a DVD player in a streaming era &mdash; even a large marketing budget may not revive demand. However, for brands with genuine customer loyalty, a well-timed extension strategy can add years of profitable sales.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Why Differentiation Is the Goal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Differentiation underpins every stage of the product life cycle. At introduction, a clearly differentiated product cuts through noise and attracts early adopters. During maturity, differentiation defends market share against cheaper competitors. After a successful extension strategy, differentiation helps justify a price premium rather than competing purely on cost. Businesses that treat differentiation as an afterthought tend to find themselves fighting price wars that erode profit margins and damage the brand.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The design mix (function, aesthetics, cost) helps a business position its product. Differentiating a product gives customers a reason to choose it over competitors and can justify a higher price.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The product life cycle has four phases &mdash; introduction, growth, maturity and decline &mdash; each with different sales levels, profitability and marketing priorities.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Extension strategies (new markets, product updates, promotions, price cuts) can delay decline, but only if the investment is justified by the likely increase in revenue.</li>
</ul>
