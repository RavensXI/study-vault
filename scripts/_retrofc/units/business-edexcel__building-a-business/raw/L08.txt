--- DESCRIPTION ---
Job, batch and flow production, and how technology shapes cost, quality and flexibility.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Purpose of Business Operations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business exists to create something of value &mdash; a physical good, a service, or a combination of both. The operations function manages how that creation happens. Two core goals drive operational decisions: keeping <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of output produced per worker or per machine in a given period of time.">productivity</dfn> high and keeping costs low. A business that achieves both can offer competitive prices while still making a healthy profit. One that fails at either will either overspend on production or undercharge customers and erode its margins.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Choosing the right <dfn class="term" data-def="The method a business uses to organise and carry out the making of its goods or delivery of its services.">production process</dfn> is fundamental. The three main types are job production, batch production and flow production. Each suits different products, volumes and customer requirements.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three types of production process? Write one example product for each that you could use in an exam answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three production processes are <strong>job production</strong> (one unique item at a time), <strong>batch production</strong> (groups of identical items at a time) and <strong>flow production</strong> (continuous, large-scale, automated production). Choosing the right process directly affects costs, quality, flexibility and the ability to charge competitive prices.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Job Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Making a single, unique product to a customer&rsquo;s specific requirements.">Job production</dfn> involves making one item at a time, tailored to an individual customer&rsquo;s exact requirements. A bespoke suit, a wedding cake, a custom-built kitchen or a handmade piece of jewellery are all examples. The product is unique, and skilled workers typically complete most or all of the work. This gives the customer exactly what they want, and the business can charge a premium price for the customisation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The disadvantage is cost. Because each item is made individually, there are no economies of scale. Labour is intensive and time-consuming. Job production is therefore unsuitable for mass-market products where customers expect low prices and fast delivery. It works best in niche or premium markets where uniqueness and quality justify the higher cost.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Batch Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Making a group of identical products at the same time, then switching machinery and staff to make a different group.">Batch production</dfn> makes a set of similar items together, then the production line is reconfigured to make a different product. A bakery making 200 white loaves and then switching to 200 brown loaves is using batch production. So is a clothing manufacturer producing a run of medium-size T-shirts before moving to large.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Batch production is more efficient than job production because the setup cost (time, resources, materials) is spread across many units. It also offers flexibility &mdash; the same equipment can make a range of products. However, between batches, machinery may sit idle, and the business must hold stock of part-finished goods while waiting for the next stage. Batch production suits medium-volume markets where product variety is needed but each variant does not justify a dedicated production line.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Continuous, large-scale production of identical products using a moving production line, typically highly automated.">Flow production</dfn> involves a continuous, moving production line where large volumes of identical items are produced without stopping. Car assembly plants, beverage bottling lines and consumer electronics factories use this approach. Flow production delivers the lowest cost per unit because the setup costs and machinery investments are spread across millions of units.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">However, flow production is inflexible. Reconfiguring a production line for a different product takes time and money. It also requires large initial capital investment in machinery and equipment. Flow production is most effective when demand is high, consistent and predictable, and when the product specification rarely changes. A shift in consumer taste can leave a flow-production business with expensive machinery geared to a product nobody wants.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The correct process depends on four factors: the volume of output needed, the degree of customisation required by customers, the target cost per unit, and the level of flexibility the market demands. Job production offers maximum flexibility and customisation at the highest cost per unit. Flow production offers minimum flexibility and customisation at the lowest cost per unit. Batch production sits in between. Most businesses in competitive markets aim to move along the spectrum from job to batch as they grow, and from batch to flow as volume increases further &mdash; because lower costs enable lower prices and greater market share.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Technology in Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Technology has transformed all three production processes. Automation &mdash; using machines and computer systems to perform tasks previously done by workers &mdash; can cut labour costs, increase output speed and reduce errors. CAM (computer-aided manufacture) and robotics in car plants are a familiar example. 3D printing enables small businesses to produce customised items at lower cost than traditional job production methods.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">However, technology in production involves a trade-off between four variables: <strong>cost</strong>, <strong>productivity</strong>, <strong>quality</strong> and <strong>flexibility</strong>. Investing in automation reduces labour costs (lowers cost) and speeds up output (raises productivity), but typically reduces flexibility (machines are configured for specific tasks) and may reduce quality for complex or delicate products that benefit from human skill and judgement.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write in your own words how technology creates a trade-off between cost, productivity, quality and flexibility. Use a specific production example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Technology investment in production creates a four-way trade-off: cost, productivity, quality and flexibility. Automation typically reduces cost and raises productivity but reduces flexibility. High-skill or variable products may see quality fall if human judgment is replaced too quickly. Businesses must balance all four outcomes &mdash; not simply pursue the cheapest option.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Connecting Operations to Competitive Advantage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Efficient operations allow a business to offer competitive prices &mdash; either by charging less than rivals (cost leadership) or by producing higher-quality goods at comparable prices (differentiation). A business using flow production for a mass-market product can undercut job-production competitors. A business using skilled job production can charge a premium that volume producers cannot match. The production process is therefore not just a technical choice &mdash; it is a strategic one that shapes where the business sits in its market.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The three production processes are job (unique, high-cost, high-skill), batch (groups of similar items, flexible, medium-cost) and flow (continuous, automated, low-cost per unit but inflexible). The right choice depends on volume, customisation, cost targets and market flexibility needs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Technology investment creates a four-way trade-off between cost, productivity, quality and flexibility. Automation lowers cost and raises productivity but typically reduces flexibility and may affect quality.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Choosing a production process is a strategic decision: efficient operations enable competitive prices and stronger profit margins, directly shaping a business&rsquo;s competitive position.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">For a <strong>6-mark Analyse</strong> question on production processes, the question will give you a business context. Identify the most suitable production process and analyse why it suits the business &mdash; refer to the volume of output, the level of customisation needed, and the cost implications. A common error is simply defining job, batch and flow production without applying them to the specific context. For a <strong>9-mark Discuss</strong> on technology in production, explore both sides: increased productivity and lower costs are advantages, but reduced flexibility and high capital investment are genuine drawbacks. Examiners want to see analysis that acknowledges trade-offs, not just a list of benefits.</p>
