--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses recruit the right people and why training boosts retention.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Recruitment Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business needs the right people in the right roles. Poor recruitment is expensive: advertising costs money, interviewing takes management time, and if the wrong person is hired, the business faces the cost of retraining or starting the process again. Getting recruitment right the first time saves time and money and improves the performance of the whole team.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Recruitment begins with a clear understanding of what the job involves and what kind of person would do it well. Two key documents make this explicit before a single application arrives.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Recruitment Documents</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A document that describes the tasks, duties and responsibilities of a job role.">job description</dfn> sets out what the job involves: the job title, the department, who the employee will report to, the main duties, and the expected working hours. It tells applicants exactly what they would be doing if appointed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Alongside the job description, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A document that describes the skills, qualifications, experience and personal qualities required of a successful candidate.">person specification</dfn> details what the ideal candidate looks like. It lists essential requirements &mdash; the minimum a candidate must have to be considered &mdash; and desirable ones, which are advantages but not deal-breakers. For example, a person specification for an accounts assistant might list &ldquo;GCSE Maths grade 5+&rdquo; as essential and &ldquo;experience with accounting software&rdquo; as desirable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Applicants respond using one of two routes. A standard <dfn class="term" data-def="A form provided by the employer for applicants to fill in with personal details, qualifications and experience.">application form</dfn> allows the employer to collect information in a consistent format, making it easier to compare candidates fairly. Alternatively, some employers accept a <dfn class="term" data-def="A document prepared by the applicant summarising their personal details, education, qualifications and work experience.">CV</dfn> (curriculum vitae), which the applicant prepares themselves. CVs give candidates more freedom to present their strengths but make direct comparison harder for employers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the difference between a job description and a person specification in your own words. Check: which document describes the job? Which describes the ideal person?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A job description describes the role; a person specification describes the ideal candidate. Both are written before recruitment begins and guide every stage of the selection process, including shortlisting and interview questions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Job Roles and Responsibilities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Businesses are structured around different levels of job role, each with distinct responsibilities. <dfn class="term" data-def="Senior leaders responsible for the strategic direction and overall performance of the business, accountable to shareholders.">Directors</dfn> sit at the top and set the strategy and vision of the company. Below them, senior managers translate that strategy into operational plans and lead major departments. <dfn class="term" data-def="Middle-level managers who oversee teams of operational staff, monitor day-to-day performance, and act as a link between senior management and front-line employees.">Supervisors</dfn> or team leaders manage front-line staff directly, monitoring output and resolving day-to-day issues. Operational and support staff carry out the practical work that delivers the product or service to customers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Understanding these layers is important when recruiting: a vacancy at supervisor level requires a very different person specification from one at director level. Seniority determines pay, authority, and the expectations placed on the employee.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Internal and External Recruitment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Once documents are prepared, the business decides whether to recruit internally or externally. <dfn class="term" data-def="Filling a vacancy by promoting or moving an existing employee within the business.">Internal recruitment</dfn> promotes or transfers someone already working for the business. It is faster and cheaper than advertising externally, the candidate already understands the company&rsquo;s culture, and it motivates other staff by showing that progression is possible. The disadvantage is that it leaves a gap elsewhere in the business and brings no new ideas or skills from outside.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Filling a vacancy by advertising to people outside the business.">External recruitment</dfn> advertises the role to the public or through agencies, job boards, university careers fairs, or social media. It brings in fresh perspectives and new skills, and it is the only option when the business needs expertise it does not currently have. However, it is more expensive and time-consuming, and the employer takes on more risk because they know less about the new person than they would about an internal candidate.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">The best method depends on the nature of the vacancy and the business&rsquo;s situation. A rapidly growing technology business entering a new market might need external specialist knowledge that no existing employee can provide. By contrast, a stable business filling a supervisory role among experienced staff might get better results &mdash; and save significant cost &mdash; by promoting from within.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">In practice, many businesses use both. They advertise internally first to give employees the opportunity to progress, then open the search externally if no internal candidate is strong enough. This balances motivation and cost against the need to bring in new capability.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Write two advantages of internal recruitment and two of external recruitment without looking. Then check whether you captured the financial and cultural angles for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Internal recruitment is cheaper and faster; external recruitment brings fresh skills and ideas. The choice depends on whether the business needs continuity or new capability, and how urgently the vacancy needs to be filled.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Training and Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Recruiting well is only the first step. Businesses that invest in their employees through training and development see higher productivity, lower turnover, and a more skilled workforce. Training can take many forms depending on the employee&rsquo;s role, the business&rsquo;s budget, and the knowledge that needs to be transferred.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Structured, planned training delivered by an employer or provider, often in a classroom or dedicated training session.">Formal training</dfn> is planned and organised: it might be an off-the-job course, a workshop, or a qualification programme. A new receptionist attending a customer service course is an example of formal training. <dfn class="term" data-def="Unstructured learning that happens naturally through day-to-day work, such as learning by watching colleagues or asking questions.">Informal training</dfn> happens on the job without a structured programme &mdash; a junior employee watching an experienced colleague handle a complaint, for instance. Informal training is low-cost but less consistent in quality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Beyond initial training, businesses use ongoing learning to keep staff up to date. <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular meeting between a manager and an employee to review progress against targets and discuss development needs.">Performance reviews</dfn> are structured meetings in which manager and employee discuss targets, achievements, and development goals. They give employees clear feedback and allow the business to identify skill gaps before they become problems. Target-setting links individual employee goals to the wider business plan, providing direction and motivation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">There is a strong link between training and <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a business to keep its employees over time, reducing costly staff turnover.">retention</dfn>. Employees who feel they are developing their skills are more likely to stay with the business because they see a future there. High retention matters because replacing an employee costs money &mdash; advertising, interviewing, and induction all take time and resources &mdash; and experienced employees are more productive than new ones still learning the role.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Businesses also retrain employees when they introduce new technology. A manufacturing company upgrading its production line will need to train staff to use the new equipment safely and efficiently. Without retraining, productivity gains from the new technology may not be realised. This makes training a strategic investment rather than just an administrative task.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">A job description details the role and its duties; a person specification details the qualities the ideal candidate must have. Both documents guide the recruitment process before a single application is received.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Internal recruitment is faster and cheaper and motivates existing staff; external recruitment brings new skills and perspectives but costs more. The right choice depends on the vacancy, the urgency, and the skills available internally.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Training &mdash; whether formal, informal, or through performance reviews &mdash; raises productivity, builds motivation, and improves retention by showing employees the business is invested in their future.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">On a 6-mark <strong>Explain</strong> question about recruitment or training, the examiner marks only your best two points &mdash; so write two clear, well-developed points rather than trying to squeeze in three or four. A common error is to name a method (e.g. &ldquo;external recruitment&rdquo;) without explaining <em>why</em> it benefits the business or what its trade-off is. Each point should follow this pattern: state the method &rarr; explain the mechanism &rarr; explain the impact on the business. For 9-mark <strong>Analyse in Context</strong> questions, embedding specific details from the scenario is what separates Mastering answers from Developing ones. Naming the business or its sector once is not enough &mdash; you must weave contextual details (location, size, type of vacancy) into your analysis at least twice to score in the upper tier.</p>
