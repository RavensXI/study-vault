--- DESCRIPTION ---
Working accurately with tolerances, selecting materials, and minimising waste through efficient cutting and marking out.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Selecting Materials & Components</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Choosing the right material for a prototype is one of the most important decisions a designer makes. The specification requires you to consider three key factors when selecting materials and components: <strong>functional need</strong>, <strong>cost</strong>, and <strong>availability</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The performance requirements a material must meet, such as strength, flexibility, heat resistance, or electrical conductivity.">Functional need</dfn> comes first — the material must be suitable for the job. A structural component needs strength and rigidity, while a handle needs good grip and comfort. Cost matters because designers work within budgets, and availability determines whether the material can be sourced in the right <dfn class="term" data-def="The standard sizes and forms in which materials are commercially sold, such as sheets, bars, tubes, or rolls.">stock forms</dfn> and quantities.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three factors must a designer consider when selecting materials according to the AQA spec?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Material selection is based on three factors: functional need (does the material perform the required task?), cost (is it within budget?), and availability (can it be sourced in appropriate stock forms?).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Understanding Tolerances</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The acceptable range of variation in a dimension or value, expressed as a plus/minus figure (e.g., ±2 mm).">tolerance</dfn> is the acceptable range of variation in a measurement. No manufacturing process is perfectly precise, so tolerances define how much a dimension can vary and still produce a functional product. the specification uses the example of ±2 mm for general workshop tolerances.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Tolerances Across Material Categories</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Different materials and processes require different tolerances:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Timber</strong> — general tolerance of ±2 mm for hand-cut joints. CNC-cut timber can achieve much tighter tolerances.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Metals</strong> — precision engineering may require tolerances of ±0.1 mm or tighter, especially for components that must fit together exactly.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Textiles</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="Extra fabric added beyond the stitching line to allow for sewing, prevent fraying, and give room for alteration.">seam allowances</dfn> (typically 10–15 mm) act as a form of tolerance, ensuring there is enough material for stitching without the garment being too tight or too loose.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Electronics</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="The acceptable range of variation in a resistor's actual value compared to its stated value, typically expressed as a percentage (e.g., ±5%).">resistor tolerances</dfn> are given as a percentage (e.g., ±5% for a standard carbon film resistor), indicated by a coloured band on the component.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain what a tolerance of ±2 mm means and give one reason why tolerances are important in manufacturing.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A tolerance of ±2 mm means a component can be up to 2 mm larger or smaller than the specified dimension and still be acceptable. Tolerances ensure parts fit together correctly and the product functions as intended.</p>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Material Management — Minimising Waste</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The specification emphasises the importance of planning the cutting and shaping of materials to minimise waste. This is both an economic consideration (wasted material is wasted money) and an environmental one (less waste means a smaller environmental footprint).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Nesting & Efficient Cutting</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Arranging parts as closely together as possible on a sheet of material before cutting, to minimise waste.">Nesting</dfn> is the practice of arranging shapes as closely together as possible on a sheet of material before cutting. CNC machines and laser cutters can use software to optimise nesting automatically. Designers must also account for material removed by the cutting method — a saw blade removes a <dfn class="term" data-def="The width of material removed by a cutting tool such as a saw blade, which must be accounted for when measuring and cutting.">kerf</dfn> width of material, and this must be factored into measurements.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">For textiles, pattern pieces are laid out on fabric to minimise offcuts. The grain direction of the fabric must also be considered, as cutting across the grain can affect the way the garment hangs and stretches.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Marking Out Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Accurate marking out is essential for working within tolerances. The specification requires understanding of <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed point, line, or surface from which all other measurements are taken, ensuring consistency and accuracy.">reference datum points</dfn>, coordinates, templates, jigs, and patterns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A <strong>datum point</strong> is a fixed reference from which all other measurements are taken. Using a single datum reduces cumulative errors. <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that holds a workpiece in position and guides a cutting tool, ensuring identical parts are produced repeatedly.">Jigs</dfn> hold workpieces in position and guide cutting tools, ensuring repeatability when making multiple identical parts. Templates and patterns are particularly important in textile work, where paper patterns are pinned to fabric before cutting.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Calculating Material Requirements</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Designers calculate material requirements using surface area and volume. For flat materials (sheet metal, plywood, fabric), the total area of all parts plus waste allowance determines how much material to order. For 3D products, volume calculations help estimate material mass and cost. All calculations should use <dfn class="term" data-def="The International System of Units, using metres, kilograms, seconds, and their multiples as the standard measurement system.">SI units</dfn> (millimetres for dimensions, grams or kilograms for mass).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what nesting is and why it is important in material management.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nesting arranges parts as closely together as possible on a material sheet to minimise waste. Combined with accurate marking out from a datum point, it ensures efficient use of materials and reduces both cost and environmental impact.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Material selection considers functional need, cost, and availability; components must be sourced in appropriate commercial stock forms.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Tolerances define the acceptable range of variation in dimensions (e.g., &plusmn;2 mm) and differ across material categories.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Material management includes nesting, kerf allowance, datum-based marking out, and calculating material requirements in SI units to minimise waste.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">Tolerance questions often ask you to explain why tolerances are <strong>applied</strong> rather than just what they are. Always link your answer to the function of the product: “If the tolerance is exceeded, the two components will not fit together, and the mechanism will jam.” Showing the <strong>consequence</strong> of exceeding a tolerance demonstrates deeper understanding.</p>
