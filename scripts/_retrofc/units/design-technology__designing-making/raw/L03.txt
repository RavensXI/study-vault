<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Learning from Design History</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The specification requires you to investigate, analyse, and evaluate the work of past and present designers and companies. Understanding how others have solved design problems helps you develop your own design thinking. You must study at least two designers and two companies from the specification list.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Influential Designers</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>William Morris (1834–1896) — Textiles & Pattern Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A 19th-century British designer, writer, and activist who championed traditional craftsmanship and founded the Arts and Crafts movement.">William Morris</dfn> was a leading figure in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A late 19th-century design movement that valued handcraftsmanship, natural materials, and honest construction over mass-produced industrial goods.">Arts and Crafts movement</dfn>. He believed that mass production led to poor quality and that beauty should be part of everyday objects. His textile and wallpaper designs featured intricate, nature-inspired patterns — flowers, birds, and trailing vines — created using traditional hand-printing techniques. Morris’s philosophy that “nothing should be in your houses that you do not know to be useful or believe to be beautiful” still influences design today.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Morris founded Morris & Co. to produce handcrafted furnishings, stained glass, and textiles. His work demonstrates how a designer’s values — in this case, a rejection of industrial mass production — shape both the aesthetic and the manufacturing process of their products.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Philippe Starck (b. 1949) — Product & Furniture Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A French industrial designer known for combining playful, sculptural forms with mass production, making high-quality design accessible to wider audiences.">Philippe Starck</dfn> is one of the most prolific designers of the modern era. His approach is almost the opposite of Morris’s — he embraces mass production and believes good design should be affordable and accessible to everyone. His Juicy Salif citrus squeezer for Alessi (1990) is a famous example: a sculptural, aluminium spider-like form that functions as a lemon press but is equally seen as an art object.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Starck works across furniture, interiors, consumer electronics, and even architecture. His Louis Ghost Chair (2002), made from transparent <dfn class="term" data-def="A clear thermoplastic polymer that can be injection-moulded into complex shapes, often used as a lightweight alternative to glass.">polycarbonate</dfn>, reinterprets a classic 18th-century silhouette using modern materials and manufacturing. Starck demonstrates how designers use new materials and technologies to reimagine traditional forms.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Compare the design philosophies of William Morris and Philippe Starck — how do they differ on mass production?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>William Morris rejected mass production and valued handcraftsmanship. Philippe Starck embraces it, believing good design should be affordable and accessible. Both have shaped how we think about the relationship between design, manufacturing, and the consumer.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Zaha Hadid (1950–2016) — Architecture & Product Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Zaha Hadid was an Iraqi-British architect known for her dramatic, flowing, curvilinear structures that challenged conventional building design. She was the first woman to win the Pritzker Architecture Prize (2004). Her buildings — such as the London Aquatics Centre and the Heydar Aliyev Centre in Baku — use reinforced concrete and parametric design software to create forms that seem to defy gravity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Hadid also designed products including furniture, jewellery, and shoes, always characterised by the same fluid, dynamic lines. Her work shows how <dfn class="term" data-def="A design process that uses algorithms and computational rules to generate complex, organic forms that would be difficult to create by hand.">parametric design</dfn> and advanced manufacturing (such as CNC milling and 3D printing) allow designers to realise forms that were previously impossible.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sir Alec Issigonis (1906–1988) — Automotive Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Sir Alec Issigonis was a British-Greek automotive engineer best known for designing the original Mini (1959). The Mini was a response to the Suez fuel crisis — a social and economic challenge that demanded a small, fuel-efficient, affordable car. Issigonis placed the engine sideways (transverse) and used front-wheel drive to maximise interior space in a tiny body. His innovative use of space shows how constraints can drive creative engineering solutions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Influential Companies</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Dyson — Innovation Through Engineering</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A British technology company founded by Sir James Dyson, known for its bagless cyclone vacuum cleaners, bladeless fans, and iterative prototyping approach.">Dyson</dfn> is a British technology company that built its reputation on the bagless cyclone vacuum cleaner. Founder James Dyson famously created 5,127 prototypes before perfecting the design. This exemplifies <dfn class="term" data-def="A design process in which a product is repeatedly tested, evaluated, and refined in cycles until the desired outcome is achieved.">iterative design</dfn> — a cycle of testing, evaluating, and improving. Dyson products are characterised by engineering innovation, distinctive aesthetics, and premium pricing. The company invests heavily in research and development, holding thousands of patents.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Apple — User-Centred Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="An American technology company known for its integrated hardware-software design approach, minimalist aesthetics, and focus on user experience.">Apple</dfn> is famous for its design-led approach, where the user experience drives every decision. Under the influence of former Chief Design Officer Jony Ive, Apple products became known for minimalist aesthetics, precision-machined aluminium bodies, and intuitive interfaces. The company demonstrates how material choice, manufacturing precision, and consistent branding create a premium product identity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Apple’s approach to <dfn class="term" data-def="A design approach that prioritises the needs, wants, and limitations of the end user at every stage of the design process.">user-centred design</dfn> means products are tested extensively with real users. Features are removed rather than added — simplicity is a core principle. This contrasts with companies that compete on features, showing there are multiple valid approaches to product design.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and name two companies from the AQA list, then describe one key characteristic of each company's design approach.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dyson demonstrates iterative design through extensive prototyping and engineering innovation. Apple demonstrates user-centred design through minimalist aesthetics and intuitive interfaces. Both invest heavily in R&D to maintain their market position.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Material Categories in Design</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">the specification lists six material categories that designers work with. Each designer and company above uses different materials depending on the product type:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Papers and boards</strong> — used in packaging design, prototyping, and graphic products.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Timber-based materials</strong> — hardwoods, softwoods, and manufactured boards used in furniture and construction (Morris).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Metal-based materials</strong> — ferrous and non-ferrous metals used in engineering and product design (Starck’s Juicy Salif uses cast aluminium).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Polymers</strong> — thermoplastics and thermosets used in consumer products (Starck’s Ghost Chair uses polycarbonate; Dyson uses ABS and polypropylene).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Textile-based materials</strong> — natural and synthetic fabrics used in fashion, interiors, and technical products (Morris’s printed fabrics).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Electronic and mechanical systems</strong> — circuits, motors, sensors, and mechanisms used in smart products (Dyson’s digital motors; Apple’s integrated electronics).</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list all six material categories from the specification from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six AQA material categories are: papers and boards, timber-based materials, metal-based materials, polymers, textile-based materials, and electronic and mechanical systems. You must study at least one category in depth for your NEA.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">William Morris championed handcraftsmanship and natural forms; Philippe Starck embraces mass production to make design accessible; Zaha Hadid used parametric design to create fluid architectural forms; Issigonis showed how constraints drive innovation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Dyson exemplifies iterative design through engineering and prototyping; Apple demonstrates user-centred design through simplicity and material quality.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">The six material categories (papers/boards, timber, metals, polymers, textiles, electronic/mechanical systems) span the full range of design and technology.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">Questions about designers and companies often ask you to “analyse” or “evaluate” their work. This means going beyond description — explain <strong>why</strong> their design choices were effective and how their work could <strong>inform your own designing</strong>. For example, don’t just say Starck used polycarbonate — explain that it allowed mass production of a complex form at lower cost than traditional materials.</p>
