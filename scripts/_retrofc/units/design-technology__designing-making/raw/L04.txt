<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Design Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The specification requires you to understand how different strategies help designers generate creative ideas and develop them into successful products. A strategy is a planned approach to solving a design problem — choosing the right one depends on the nature of the brief, the client, and the context.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Collaboration</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="A design strategy in which multiple people with different skills and perspectives work together to solve a design problem.">Collaboration</dfn> involves working with others — other designers, engineers, clients, or end users — to develop ideas. It brings together different skills and viewpoints, often producing solutions that no individual would have reached alone. In industry, product design teams typically include specialists in materials, electronics, marketing, and manufacturing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>User-Centred Design</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A design strategy that places the end user at the centre of every decision, using research and testing to ensure the product meets real needs.">User-centred design</dfn> places the end user at the heart of every decision. The designer gathers data through interviews, observation, and usability testing, then designs specifically around those findings. This reduces the risk of creating something that looks good but fails in practice. Apple’s product design is a well-known example of this approach.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Systems Approach</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A design strategy that breaks a complex product into sub-systems (input, process, output) and designs each part separately before integrating them.">systems approach</dfn> breaks a complex product down into manageable sub-systems — typically <strong>input</strong>, <strong>process</strong>, and <strong>output</strong>. Each sub-system is designed and tested separately, then integrated. This is common in electronic and mechanical products. For example, an automatic hand dryer has an input (motion sensor), a process (control circuit), and outputs (heater element and fan motor).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the five design strategies named in The spec from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five design strategies named in the specification are: collaboration, user-centred design, a systems approach, iterative design, and avoiding design fixation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Iterative Design & Avoiding Design Fixation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A design process in which a product is repeatedly developed through cycles of designing, making, testing, and evaluating.">Iterative design</dfn> involves repeated cycles of designing, making, testing, and evaluating. Rather than trying to produce a perfect solution first time, the designer creates quick prototypes, tests them, learns from the results, and refines the design. Each cycle gets closer to the final solution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A cognitive bias in which a designer becomes too attached to an early idea, limiting their ability to explore alternative solutions.">Design fixation</dfn> occurs when a designer becomes too attached to an early idea and stops exploring alternatives. To avoid it, designers use techniques such as brainstorming, mind mapping, SCAMPER (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse), and looking at the problem from different perspectives.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Communicating Design Ideas</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">AQA requires you to develop, communicate, record, and justify your design ideas using a range of techniques. The method you choose depends on the audience and purpose.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Drawing Techniques</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Freehand sketching</strong> is used in the early stages to quickly explore ideas. Sketches are fast, informal, and often annotated with notes explaining materials, dimensions, or functions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A 3D drawing method where vertical lines remain vertical and horizontal lines are drawn at 30 degrees, with all measurements kept to scale.">Isometric drawing</dfn> shows a 3D view of an object with all three axes visible. Measurements are to scale, making it useful for communicating shape and proportion. <dfn class="term" data-def="A drawing method that shows how objects appear to the human eye, with parallel lines converging at vanishing points on the horizon.">Perspective drawing</dfn> creates a more realistic view by using vanishing points, making objects appear to recede into the distance.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A formal engineering drawing system that shows front, side, and plan views of an object, projected at right angles using standardised conventions.">Third-angle orthographic projection</dfn> is a formal engineering drawing showing front, side, and plan views. It uses conventions such as dimension lines, centre lines, and hidden detail (dashed lines), all drawn to scale. This is the standard format for working drawings that go to a manufacturer.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Other Communication Methods</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Exploded diagrams</strong> show how parts fit together by separating them along an axis, revealing assembly order and constructional detail. <strong>System and schematic diagrams</strong> show the logical relationships between components — circuit diagrams are a common example.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Computer-based tools</strong> include CAD (Computer Aided Design) software such as SolidWorks, Fusion 360, or 2D Design. These allow precise modelling, rendering, and even simulation of how a product will behave. <dfn class="term" data-def="A prediction made through calculation or computer simulation to test how a design will perform before a physical prototype is built.">Mathematical modelling</dfn> uses calculations or simulations to test performance before building a physical prototype.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Physical modelling</strong> involves making 3D models from card, foam, fabric (toile), or breadboard circuits. Models help test form, proportion, and ergonomics in ways that drawings cannot.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name four different drawing or modelling techniques and explain when each would be most useful.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Communication methods range from quick freehand sketches (idea generation) to formal third-angle orthographic projections (manufacture). The choice depends on the stage of the design process and the audience.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The five AQA design strategies are collaboration, user-centred design, systems approach, iterative design, and avoiding design fixation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Iterative design uses repeated cycles of designing, making, testing, and evaluating to refine a product.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Communication methods range from freehand sketches and isometric drawings (early stages) to third-angle orthographic projection and CAD models (manufacture-ready).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When asked about design strategies, don’t just define them — explain <strong>when and why</strong> each one would be used. For communication questions, identify the <strong>purpose</strong> (exploring ideas vs. giving to a manufacturer) and match it to the <strong>appropriate technique</strong> (freehand sketch vs. orthographic projection).</p>
