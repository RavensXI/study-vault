--- DESCRIPTION ---
How designers use primary and secondary research to identify user needs and write effective design briefs.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Research Matters in Design</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every successful product begins with research. Before a designer picks up a pencil or opens CAD software, they need to understand <em>who</em> they are designing for, <em>what</em> problem they are solving, and <em>what already exists</em> on the market. You need to understand how <dfn class="term" data-def="Information collected first-hand by the designer through interviews, surveys, observations or testing.">primary data</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Information gathered from existing sources such as books, websites, databases or published research.">secondary data</dfn> are used to inform the design process.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is the difference between primary and secondary data? Give one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Primary data is gathered first-hand (surveys, interviews, testing), while secondary data comes from existing sources (books, websites, databases). Designers use both to build a complete picture of user needs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Primary Research Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Primary research gives designers direct, up-to-date information about their target users.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Market Research & Interviews</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of gathering information about consumer preferences and market trends to inform design decisions.">Market research</dfn> involves collecting data about consumer preferences, buying habits, and existing products. Designers use questionnaires, online surveys, and face-to-face interviews to gather opinions directly from potential users. Interviews are particularly useful because the designer can ask follow-up questions and observe body language, giving richer data than a written survey alone.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">When analysing questionnaire responses, designers create <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of organising survey data by counting how often each response appears.">frequency tables</dfn> to identify patterns. For example, if 80% of respondents say they want a lightweight product, that becomes a key specification point.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Ergonomics & Anthropometric Data</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The study of how people interact with products and environments, aiming to improve comfort, safety, and efficiency.">Ergonomics</dfn> is the study of how people interact with products. Designers consider factors like grip size, viewing angles, and reach distances to ensure products are comfortable and safe to use. <dfn class="term" data-def="Body measurement data collected from large populations, used to design products that fit the majority of users.">Anthropometric data</dfn> provides body measurements from large population samples — heights, hand spans, sitting heights — that help designers size their products appropriately.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Values that divide a population into 100 equal groups based on a measured characteristic, used to determine the range of sizes a product should accommodate.">Percentiles</dfn> divide the population into 100 groups by measurement. Most products are designed for the 5th to 95th percentile, meaning they suit 90% of the population. A chair designed only for the 50th percentile (average) would be uncomfortable for both shorter and taller users.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Focus Groups & Product Analysis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A small group of target users brought together to discuss and give feedback on a product or design concept.">Focus groups</dfn> bring together a small group of target users to discuss a design concept or existing product. The designer facilitates the discussion and records responses. This qualitative data reveals attitudes and feelings that surveys often miss. Product analysis involves systematically evaluating existing products against criteria such as function, aesthetics, cost, materials, and environmental impact.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three types of primary research a designer might use, and explain why each is useful.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Product analysis helps designers identify strengths and weaknesses in existing solutions. By comparing products using a comparative chart of performance criteria, designers can spot gaps in the market and opportunities for improvement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Secondary Research Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Secondary research uses information that already exists. This includes published books, academic journals, manufacturer datasheets, government statistics, and online databases. It is quicker and cheaper than primary research but may be out of date or not specific to the designer’s exact needs. The best approach combines both types — secondary research to build background knowledge, then primary research to fill gaps.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Writing a Design Brief & Specification</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Once research is complete, the designer writes a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short statement outlining the design problem and the intention to solve it.">design brief</dfn> — a concise statement of the problem and the proposed solution. This is followed by a detailed <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of specific, measurable criteria that the final product must meet.">design specification</dfn> listing measurable criteria the product must meet, such as dimensions, materials, weight limits, target cost, and user requirements.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A good specification is specific and measurable. “The product should be lightweight” is vague. “The product should weigh no more than 500 g” is measurable and testable. Designers may also modify their brief as research reveals new information — this is a normal part of the iterative design process.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three differences between a design brief and a design specification.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A design brief outlines the problem in broad terms. A design specification lists precise, measurable requirements. Both may be revised as the project develops.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Primary data (surveys, interviews, ergonomic testing) gives direct, current information; secondary data (books, databases) provides background context quickly and cheaply.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Anthropometric data and percentiles help designers create products that fit the widest range of users.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">A design brief states the problem; a design specification lists measurable success criteria — both may be modified as the project evolves.</li>
</ul>

Always link your answer to the <strong>type of data</strong> it produces and <strong>how that data improves the final product</strong>. For example, “Anthropometric data allows the designer to size the handle for the 5th to 95th percentile, ensuring it is comfortable for 90% of users.”</p>
