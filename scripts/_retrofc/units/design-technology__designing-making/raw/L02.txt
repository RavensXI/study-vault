--- DESCRIPTION ---
How environmental, social and economic factors create both opportunities and constraints for designers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Design in Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Designers do not work in isolation. Every product they create exists within a web of environmental, social, and economic pressures. The specification requires you to understand how these challenges present both <strong>opportunities</strong> and <strong>constraints</strong> that shape the design and manufacturing process.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Environmental Challenges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The environmental impact of products — from raw material extraction through manufacture, use, and disposal — is one of the biggest challenges facing modern designers. The specification highlights three key areas.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What are the three environmental challenges named in the AQA spec, and how does each affect design decisions?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The spec names three environmental challenges: deforestation, increased carbon dioxide levels leading to potential global warming, and the need for fair trade. Each of these creates constraints on material choice and manufacturing methods.</p>
</div>

For designers working with timber, this means choosing wood from <dfn class="term" data-def="Forests managed so that trees are replanted at the same rate they are harvested, ensuring a continuous supply without depleting the resource.">sustainably managed forests</dfn> certified by organisations like the FSC (Forest Stewardship Council). <h2 data-narration-id="n8">Social Challenges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Social factors influence what people want and need from products. Designers must consider cultural values, ethical concerns, user demographics, and changing lifestyles. An aging population, for example, creates demand for products with larger controls, clearer displays, and easier grip — turning a social challenge into a design opportunity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Design that considers the full range of human diversity, including ability, language, culture, gender, and age, so products can be used by as many people as possible.">Inclusive design</dfn> aims to make products usable by the widest possible range of people, regardless of age, ability, or background. Products designed inclusively — such as lever-style door handles instead of round knobs — often benefit everyone, not just those with specific needs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three social factors that influence product design, and give one example of how each creates a design opportunity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Social challenges such as an aging population, changing cultural trends, and ethical consumer demand create design opportunities. Inclusive design responds to these by making products accessible to the widest range of users.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Economic Challenges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Economic factors shape what materials a designer can afford, where manufacturing takes place, and what price consumers will pay. Key economic challenges include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Material costs</strong> — fluctuating raw material prices force designers to find cost-effective alternatives or reduce material usage.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Labour costs</strong> — manufacturing in countries with lower wages reduces costs but raises ethical concerns about working conditions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Market competition</strong> — designers must balance quality and features against a price point that competes with existing products.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Need for Fair Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="A trading partnership that seeks greater equity in international trade by offering better terms and working conditions to producers in developing countries.">Fair trade</dfn> ensures that producers in developing countries receive fair payment and work in safe conditions. Designers selecting materials — such as cotton for textiles or cocoa butter for cosmetic packaging — should consider whether supply chains meet fair trade standards. While fair trade materials may cost more, they appeal to ethically conscious consumers and reduce exploitation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The 6 Rs of Sustainability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Designers use the <dfn class="term" data-def="Six principles — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Rethink, Repair — that guide sustainable design decisions.">6 Rs</dfn> framework to minimise environmental impact: <strong>Reduce</strong> material and energy use, <strong>Reuse</strong> components or products, <strong>Recycle</strong> materials at end of life, <strong>Refuse</strong> harmful or unnecessary materials, <strong>Rethink</strong> whether the product is needed or could work differently, and <strong>Repair</strong> rather than replace. Applying these principles from the earliest design stages leads to more sustainable products.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and write out all 6 Rs of sustainability from memory, with a brief explanation of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 6 Rs — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Rethink, Repair — guide sustainable design. Applying them from the start of the design process helps minimise environmental impact throughout a product’s life cycle.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Environmental challenges (deforestation, CO<sub>2</sub> emissions, fair trade) constrain material and manufacturing choices but also drive innovation in sustainable design.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Social challenges such as aging populations and changing lifestyles create opportunities for inclusive and accessible design.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The 6 Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Rethink, Repair) provide a framework for sustainable design decisions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">When answering questions about environmental, social, or economic challenges, always link the challenge to a <strong>specific design decision</strong>. Don’t just say “deforestation is bad” — explain how it <strong>constrains material choice</strong> (e.g., “the designer would select FSC-certified timber to avoid contributing to deforestation”). The best answers connect the challenge to the opportunity it creates.</p>
