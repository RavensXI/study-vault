--- DESCRIPTION ---
How designers develop, test, and evaluate prototypes to meet client needs and demonstrate innovation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Prototype?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A working model or test version of a product, used to evaluate form, function, and fitness for purpose before final manufacture.">prototype</dfn> is a working model or test version of a product. In AQA Design & Technology, the term can describe either a physical product or a system (such as an electronic circuit). Prototypes allow designers to test ideas in the real world, identify problems, and refine their designs before committing to full-scale manufacture.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Developing Prototypes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The specification states that prototypes must be developed in response to client wants and needs. A successful prototype should satisfy the requirements of the design brief, respond to what the client has asked for, demonstrate <dfn class="term" data-def="The introduction of new or significantly improved products, processes, or methods that solve a problem in a novel way.">innovation</dfn>, be functional, consider aesthetics, and be potentially <dfn class="term" data-def="Having the qualities needed for a product to be successfully sold to consumers, including desirability, competitive pricing, and meeting a genuine need.">marketable</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: List the six criteria a prototype must satisfy according to the AQA specification.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A well-developed prototype must: (1) satisfy the requirements of the brief, (2) respond to client wants and needs, (3) demonstrate innovation, (4) be functional, (5) consider aesthetics, and (6) be potentially marketable.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">Designers use different types of prototype depending on what they need to test:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Appearance models</strong> — show the form, colour, and surface finish of a product but may not be functional. Often made from foam, card, or 3D-printed materials.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Functional prototypes</strong> — work as intended, allowing the designer to test mechanisms, circuits, or moving parts. They may not look like the final product.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Production prototypes</strong> — closely resemble the final product in both appearance and function. Used for final testing and client approval before manufacturing begins.</li>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The fast creation of physical models using technologies such as 3D printing, laser cutting, or CNC machining, allowing quick testing and iteration.">Rapid prototyping</dfn> uses technologies like 3D printing (additive manufacturing), laser cutting, and CNC machining to produce physical models quickly and cheaply. A designer can go from a CAD model to a physical prototype in hours, test it, modify the digital file, and print an improved version the same day. This dramatically speeds up the iterative design process.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Manufacturing processes controlled by computer software, including 3D printing, CNC milling, and laser cutting, which allow precise and repeatable production.">CAM (Computer Aided Manufacture)</dfn> uses CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machines that read digital design files and cut, shape, or build components with high precision and repeatability. In combination with CAD, this enables a seamless digital workflow from design to manufacture.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Evaluating Prototypes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Evaluation is a continuous process, not just a final step. The specification requires students to evaluate prototypes by reflecting critically on their work, responding to feedback, suggesting modifications, and assessing <dfn class="term" data-def="Whether a product successfully performs the task it was designed for and meets the criteria in the design specification.">fitness for purpose</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the three evaluation skills The spec expects you to demonstrate when evaluating a prototype.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AQA expects you to: (1) reflect critically on your prototype, responding to feedback; (2) suggest modifications to improve it through inception and manufacture; and (3) assess whether the prototype is fit for purpose.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Testing Against the Specification</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The design specification written during the investigation stage becomes the evaluation checklist. Each criterion — dimensions, weight, materials, function, aesthetics — is tested against the prototype. Where the prototype falls short, the designer identifies what went wrong and proposes specific improvements. This testing-and-improvement cycle continues until the product meets all specification requirements or the best possible compromise is reached.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Client & User Feedback</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Effective evaluation includes feedback from the client and target users, not just the designer’s own assessment. This might involve user testing sessions, questionnaires, or interviews. Third-party feedback often reveals issues the designer has overlooked — for example, a handle that feels comfortable to the designer might be too small for users with larger hands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why testing a prototype with real users is more valuable than relying on the designer's own evaluation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Testing with real users reveals problems the designer may not notice. Third-party feedback is essential because the designer is too close to the project to be fully objective.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Prototypes must satisfy the brief, respond to client needs, demonstrate innovation, be functional, consider aesthetics, and be potentially marketable.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Rapid prototyping (3D printing, laser cutting, CNC) accelerates the iterative design cycle by allowing quick physical testing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Evaluation involves testing against the specification, reflecting critically on feedback, suggesting modifications, and assessing fitness for purpose.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">In evaluation questions, always structure your answer around the <strong>specification criteria</strong>. State the criterion, explain how you tested it, give the result, and suggest a modification if the prototype fell short. This shows the examiner you can evaluate systematically, not just offer vague opinions.</p>
