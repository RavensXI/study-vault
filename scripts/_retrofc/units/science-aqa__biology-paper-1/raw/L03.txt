<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Levels of Organisation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The human body is organised into a hierarchy. Individual <dfn class="term" data-def="The basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms.">cells</dfn> with a similar structure and function are grouped together into <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of similar cells working together to carry out a particular function.">tissues</dfn>. Different tissues combine to form <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of different tissues working together to perform a specific function.">organs</dfn>, and organs work together as <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of organs working together to carry out a major bodily function, e.g. the digestive system.">organ systems</dfn>. The digestive system is one of the main organ systems you need to know.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write out the complete hierarchy of organisation from cells to organism in the correct order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The hierarchy of organisation: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. Each level builds on the one below it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Digestive System</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The digestive system is a muscular tube running from the mouth to the anus. Its role is to break large, insoluble food molecules into smaller, soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the blood. Digestion involves both <strong>mechanical</strong> breakdown (teeth, churning in the stomach) and <strong>chemical</strong> breakdown by <dfn class="term" data-def="Biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions in living organisms. They are proteins with a specific active site.">enzymes</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The main digestive enzymes are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Amylase</strong> — produced in the salivary glands and pancreas; breaks down starch into sugars (maltose).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Proteases</strong> — produced in the stomach (pepsin), pancreas and small intestine; break down proteins into amino acids.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Lipases</strong> — produced in the pancreas and small intestine; break down lipids (fats) into fatty acids and glycerol.</li>
</ul>

  <p>Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder, and emulsifies fats in the small intestine. It also neutralises stomach acid to create the alkaline conditions needed by intestinal enzymes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Enzymes and the Lock-and-Key Model</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Enzymes are biological catalysts — they speed up reactions without being used up. Each enzyme has a specific <dfn class="term" data-def="The region of an enzyme where the substrate binds. Its shape is complementary to the substrate, like a lock and key.">active site</dfn> with a unique shape. Only a substrate with the matching shape can fit into the active site, like a key in a lock. This is called the <strong>lock-and-key model</strong>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Enzymes work best at an optimum temperature and pH. If the temperature is too high or the pH is too extreme, the active site changes shape (<dfn class="term" data-def="When an enzyme’s active site permanently changes shape due to high temperature or extreme pH, so the substrate can no longer fit.">denaturation</dfn>) and the substrate can no longer fit. The enzyme stops working.</p>

      <li><strong>Sugars</strong> — Benedict’s reagent heated; turns from blue to green/yellow/orange/red (depending on sugar concentration).</li>
      <li><strong>Proteins</strong> — Biuret reagent turns from blue to purple/lilac.</li>
      <li><strong>Lipids</strong> — Sudan III test; a red layer forms if lipids are present.</li>
    </ul>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The digestive system demonstrates how organs work together as a system. The stomach churns food and adds acid; the pancreas produces enzymes; the liver makes bile; the small intestine absorbs soluble products into the blood; and the large intestine absorbs water.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The body is organised: cells &rarr; tissues &rarr; organs &rarr; organ systems &rarr; organism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Digestive enzymes (amylase, proteases, lipases) break large molecules into smaller soluble ones for absorption.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Enzymes have a specific active site shape (lock-and-key model) and are denatured by high temperature or extreme pH.</li>
</ul>
