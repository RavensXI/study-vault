--- DESCRIPTION ---
How monoclonal antibodies are produced and their uses in medicine and research.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Monoclonal Antibodies?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Identical copies of a single type of antibody, produced from a clone of one B-lymphocyte fused with a tumour cell.">Monoclonal antibodies</dfn> are identical copies of one specific antibody. They are produced from a single clone of cells, so every antibody molecule is exactly the same and binds to the same specific <dfn class="term" data-def="A unique molecule or part of a molecule on the surface of a pathogen that an antibody can bind to.">antigen</dfn>. This makes them extremely useful in medicine and research.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the type of cells created when mouse lymphocytes are fused with tumour cells in monoclonal antibody production.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Monoclonal antibodies are produced by stimulating mouse lymphocytes to make a specific antibody, then fusing those lymphocytes with tumour cells to create <dfn class="term" data-def="A cell formed by fusing a B-lymphocyte with a tumour cell, capable of producing large quantities of a single antibody indefinitely.">hybridoma</dfn> cells that divide indefinitely and produce large quantities of the desired antibody.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Producing Monoclonal Antibodies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The production of monoclonal antibodies is a multi-step process:</p>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n6">A mouse is injected with the target antigen. Its immune system responds by producing <dfn class="term" data-def="White blood cells that produce antibodies as part of the specific immune response.">lymphocytes</dfn> that make antibodies against that antigen.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">The antibody-producing lymphocytes are collected from the mouse’s spleen.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">These lymphocytes are fused with <dfn class="term" data-def="Rapidly dividing cancer cells that are fused with lymphocytes to form hybridoma cells.">tumour cells</dfn> (which divide rapidly and indefinitely) to form hybridoma cells.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">The hybridoma cells are screened to select those that produce the correct antibody.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">Selected hybridoma cells are cloned and grown in large quantities. They produce vast amounts of the identical monoclonal antibody.</li>
</ol>



<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Fuse with Tumour Cells?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Normal lymphocytes die after a short time outside the body. Tumour cells, however, divide uncontrollably and can survive indefinitely in culture. By fusing a lymphocyte (which makes the right antibody) with a tumour cell (which can divide forever), scientists create a hybridoma that combines both properties — it produces the correct antibody <em>and</em> never stops dividing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Uses of Monoclonal Antibodies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Because monoclonal antibodies bind to one specific antigen with very high precision, they have a wide range of applications:</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Pregnancy Testing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Human chorionic gonadotrophin, a hormone found in the urine of pregnant women that pregnancy tests detect using monoclonal antibodies.">HCG</dfn> (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone produced during pregnancy. Pregnancy test sticks contain monoclonal antibodies that bind to HCG in urine. If HCG is present, the antibodies trigger a colour change, producing a positive result.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Diagnosis of Disease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Monoclonal antibodies can detect specific molecules in blood or tissue samples. For example, they can identify the presence of a pathogen or a specific protein linked to a disease. In <strong>cancer diagnosis</strong>, monoclonal antibodies labelled with a radioactive tracer or fluorescent dye can bind to tumour-specific antigens, allowing doctors to locate tumours in the body through imaging.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List three substances that monoclonal antibodies can carry to cancer cells in targeted therapy treatment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In cancer treatment, monoclonal antibodies can be designed to carry a toxic drug, radioactive substance, or chemical directly to cancer cells. This is targeted therapy — it delivers the treatment only to the tumour, reducing damage to healthy tissue compared with chemotherapy.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Monoclonal Antibodies in Research</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Scientists use monoclonal antibodies to locate and identify specific molecules in cells and tissues. They can be tagged with fluorescent dyes, allowing researchers to visualise exactly where a particular protein is within a cell. This technique is invaluable in laboratory research for understanding cell biology and disease mechanisms.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Limitations and Ethical Concerns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Monoclonal antibodies were expected to be more widely used than they currently are. There are limitations:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">They can cause unexpected <strong>side effects</strong> in some patients, which are sometimes more severe than the disease itself.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">They are <strong>expensive</strong> to develop and produce.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">They do not yet produce the miracle cures that were initially hoped for.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n25">There are also ethical concerns about the use of animals (mice) in the production process, as mice must be injected with antigens to stimulate antibody production.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why monoclonal antibody treatment causes less damage to healthy cells compared to chemotherapy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Monoclonal antibody treatment is an example of <dfn class="term" data-def="Medical treatment that targets specific cells or molecules rather than affecting the whole body.">targeted therapy</dfn> because it delivers drugs precisely to diseased cells while leaving healthy cells unharmed.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Monoclonal antibodies are identical antibodies produced by hybridoma cells (lymphocyte fused with a tumour cell).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">They are used in pregnancy tests (detecting HCG), disease diagnosis, cancer treatment (targeted drug delivery), and research.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Limitations include side effects, high cost, and ethical concerns about using mice in production.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---

A common mistake is confusing <strong>hybridoma cells</strong> with normal lymphocytes. Remember: hybridoma = lymphocyte + tumour cell. If asked to evaluate their use, balance benefits (specificity, targeted treatment) against limitations (side effects, cost, ethical concerns about animal use).</p>
