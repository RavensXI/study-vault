--- DESCRIPTION ---
How scientists grow bacteria safely and how pathogens attack plants.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Culturing Microorganisms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Microorganisms such as <dfn class="term" data-def="Single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus and can be grown in laboratory cultures.">bacteria</dfn> can be grown in the laboratory using nutrient media. This allows scientists to study how they grow, test antibiotics, and investigate disease. In the separate biology specification, you need to understand the practical techniques used to culture microorganisms safely and aseptically.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why aseptic technique prevents contamination and what it ensures grows on the culture medium.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique that prevents contamination of cultures by unwanted microorganisms from the environment.">aseptic technique</dfn> is used to prevent contamination of bacterial cultures by unwanted microorganisms from the environment. This ensures that only the microorganism being studied grows on the culture medium.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">To culture bacteria, scientists use either a <dfn class="term" data-def="A jelly-like substance containing nutrients that provides a surface for bacteria to grow on.">nutrient agar</dfn> plate or a nutrient broth. Agar plates provide a solid surface on which individual colonies can be seen, while broth allows bacteria to grow in liquid suspension.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Aseptic Technique Steps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">To prevent contamination, a specific set of precautions must be followed during any work with microorganisms:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">Sterilise all equipment (Petri dishes, inoculating loops) before use — an <dfn class="term" data-def="A metal wire loop used to transfer bacteria, sterilised by passing through a Bunsen flame.">inoculating loop</dfn> is passed through a Bunsen burner flame until it glows red.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">Lids of Petri dishes are secured with tape but not sealed completely, to allow oxygen in but prevent contamination from above.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">Work near a Bunsen flame to create an updraught that carries airborne microorganisms away from the open plates.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">Incubate cultures at a maximum of 25 °C in schools (to reduce the risk of growing pathogens harmful to humans). In industry, cultures can be incubated at higher temperatures, often around 37 °C.</li>
</ul>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">You may be asked to describe how to investigate the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth. Paper discs soaked in different concentrations of antiseptic are placed on an inoculated agar plate. After incubation, clear zones called <dfn class="term" data-def="A clear area around an antibiotic disc on an agar plate where bacteria have been unable to grow.">zones of inhibition</dfn> show where bacteria could not grow.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The area of the zone of inhibition can be calculated using \(A = \pi r^2\), where \(r\) is the radius of the clear zone. A larger zone means the antiseptic is more effective at killing or preventing bacterial growth.</p>

</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Plant Disease</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Plants can be infected by a range of pathogens including viruses, bacteria, fungi, and protists. These diseases reduce crop yields and can devastate agriculture. You need to know specific examples and how to identify diseased plants.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what TMV causes on leaves, how it affects photosynthesis, and the only treatment available.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="A viral disease of tobacco plants that causes a distinctive mosaic pattern of discolouration on leaves, reducing photosynthesis.">Tobacco mosaic virus</dfn> (TMV) causes a mosaic pattern on leaves, reducing the area available for photosynthesis. There is no cure — infected plants must be destroyed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Examples of Plant Diseases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Rose black spot</strong> is a fungal disease that causes purple or black spots on leaves. Affected leaves turn yellow and drop early, reducing the plant’s ability to photosynthesise. It spreads through water and wind and can be treated with fungicides or by removing and destroying affected leaves.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Crown gall disease</strong> is caused by the bacterium <em>Agrobacterium tumefaciens</em>, which inserts its DNA into the plant cells and causes tumour-like growths (galls) on stems and roots.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Plant diseases can be identified by:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Stunted growth</strong> — nutrient deficiency or pathogen infection.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Spots on leaves</strong> — fungal or bacterial infection (e.g. rose black spot).</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Areas of decay or rot</strong> — fungal or bacterial infection.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Discolouration</strong> — viral infection (e.g. TMV mosaic pattern) or mineral deficiency.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Malformed stems or leaves</strong> — viral infection or pest damage.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Gardeners and farmers can also use testing kits, laboratory analysis, or reference material to confirm diagnoses.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Plant Defence Responses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Plants have evolved physical, chemical, and mechanical defences against pathogens and herbivores:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Physical barriers:</strong> Cellulose cell walls, tough waxy cuticles on leaves, and bark on trees prevent pathogen entry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Chemical defences:</strong> Some plants produce <dfn class="term" data-def="Substances produced by plants that are toxic to pathogens or deter herbivores.">antimicrobial substances</dfn> (e.g. mint and witch hazel) or poisons to deter herbivores.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Mechanical adaptations:</strong> Thorns, hairs, and leaves that droop or curl when touched deter herbivores.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the substance plants produce to seal off infected areas and which transport tissue it blocks.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plants cannot move away from threats, so their defences must be structural or chemical. Some plants seal off infected areas by producing callose deposits to block the spread of pathogens through the phloem.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Aseptic technique prevents contamination when culturing microorganisms — sterilise equipment, work near a flame, and incubate at a maximum of 25 °C in schools.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Plants can be infected by viruses (TMV), fungi (rose black spot), bacteria, and protists — symptoms include spots, discolouration, and stunted growth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Plants defend themselves with physical barriers (cell walls, cuticle), chemical defences (antimicrobial compounds), and mechanical adaptations (thorns).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">When asked to calculate the area of a zone of inhibition, always measure the <strong>diameter</strong> first, then halve it to find the radius before applying \(A = \pi r^2\). Show your working clearly and give units (mm²). Exam questions on aseptic technique often ask you to <strong>explain why</strong> each step is necessary — link every step to preventing contamination.</p>
