John’s contested claim, his rivalry with Arthur of Brittany, and his difficult character.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">When Richard I was killed by a crossbow bolt at the siege of Châlus on 6 April 1199, the Angevin Empire he left behind was vast but fragile. Two men could plausibly call themselves his heir. The first was Richard&rsquo;s younger brother John, by then aged 32 and already master of Ireland and large estates in England. The second was a 12-year-old boy: Arthur of Brittany, the son of John&rsquo;s elder dead brother Geoffrey. The choice between uncle and nephew would shape the next decade and end with one of the most controversial events of the reign.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">John&rsquo;s Claim to the Throne</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">John&rsquo;s claim rested on a custom called <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval rule by which a brother of the dead king was preferred to the dead king&rsquo;s underage nephew, on the grounds that an adult ruler was needed.">proximity of blood</dfn>: as the youngest surviving son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, he was a direct adult heir. Arthur&rsquo;s case rested on the rival rule of <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval rule by which a dead son&rsquo;s line should inherit ahead of the dead son&rsquo;s younger brothers; in 1199 it would have favoured Arthur of Brittany.">representation</dfn>: a dead son&rsquo;s line should inherit before his younger brothers. The Angevin lands split. England and Normandy accepted John, swayed by Eleanor of Aquitaine, William Marshal and Hubert Walter, who argued that the realm needed an adult king with a working knowledge of government. Anjou, Maine and Touraine declared for Arthur, who was already being raised by King Philip II of France &mdash; a deliberate move to keep him in French hands as a useful pawn.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">John was crowned at Westminster on 27 May 1199. To secure Normandy he met Philip II at Le Goulet in May 1200 and signed a treaty: John was recognised as Richard&rsquo;s heir on the Continent, but at the price of paying Philip a huge &lsquo;relief&rsquo; (an inheritance fee) of 20,000 marks and ceding territory in the Vexin and Évreux. Contemporaries grumbled that John had bought what Richard had won, and gave him the unflattering nickname &lsquo;John Softsword&rsquo;.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who John&rsquo;s rival claimant was, the rule each man relied on, and which territories accepted Arthur over John in 1199.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>John&rsquo;s claim used proximity of blood (closest adult relative); Arthur of Brittany&rsquo;s used representation (dead son&rsquo;s line). England and Normandy accepted John; Anjou, Maine and Touraine declared for Arthur, who was sheltered by Philip II of France.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">John&rsquo;s Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">John was clever, energetic and far better at the daily grind of administration than his elder brother. The pipe rolls grew thicker every year of his reign as he toured England in person, hearing pleas, checking accounts and badgering sheriffs. But contemporaries also recorded a darker side. Roger of Wendover called him &lsquo;cruel to all men&rsquo;; the chronicler Matthew Paris &mdash; admittedly hostile &mdash; said hell itself was made fouler by his presence. Even allowing for monkish exaggeration, the pattern of distrust ran through everyone who dealt with him: he doubted the loyalty of his barons and they doubted the safety of their persons in his hands.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Three traits show up again and again. First, he was suspicious. He demanded hostages from his great barons &mdash; sons, wives and daughters held at court as a guarantee of good behaviour. Second, he was vindictive. The wife and son of William de Braose, his former close ally, were starved to death in Windsor Castle in 1210. Third, he was personally repellent in a way medieval kings were supposed not to be: he flirted with the wives and daughters of his barons, made bad jokes at their expense, and lacked the easy comradeship of Richard. None of this would have mattered if he had been winning. Once he started losing, it mattered very much.</p>

He inherited a financially exhausted kingdom from Richard&rsquo;s crusade and ransom and squeezed every shilling out of it. The problem was not that he was lazy or stupid &mdash; he was neither &mdash; but that his methods of squeezing revenue were too efficient and too arbitrary, and that his personal style alienated almost everyone he needed to lead.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Rivalry with Arthur of Brittany</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Arthur grew up in the French court and, as a teenager, became a useful weapon for Philip II. In 1202 Philip used the appeal of the disgruntled <dfn class="term" data-def="A powerful Poitevin baronial family whose appeal to Philip II in 1202 over John&rsquo;s seizure of Isabella of Angoul&ecirc;me triggered the legal process that lost John his French lands.">Lusignan family</dfn> &mdash; whose head, Hugh, had been betrothed to Isabella of Angoulême before John snatched her away as his queen &mdash; to summon John to Paris as Duke of Aquitaine. When John refused to attend, Philip declared his French fiefs forfeit and gave Anjou and Brittany to Arthur. The 15-year-old now led a military campaign against his own grandmother, Eleanor of Aquitaine, besieging her at Mirebeau Castle in Poitou in late July 1202.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">John&rsquo;s response was the most decisive military action of his reign. He marched 130 km in 48 hours, surprised the besiegers at dawn on 1 August 1202, and captured Arthur, the leading Lusignans, and around 250 Poitevin knights. It was a stunning victory and could have stabilised his French position for a generation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Murder of Arthur</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Arthur was imprisoned first at Falaise and then at Rouen. After early 1203 he was never seen alive again. The most credible contemporary account &mdash; the Margam Annals &mdash; reports that on 3 April 1203 a drunken John personally killed his nephew, weighted the body with a stone and threw it into the Seine. Other versions have Arthur murdered on John&rsquo;s orders or dying after a botched attempt to blind and castrate him. Either way, the responsibility fell on the king. The political consequences were immediate and catastrophic: Norman, Breton and Poitevin barons who had previously kept their distance from Philip II now switched sides. The Bretons took Arthur&rsquo;s death as a personal blood feud against John. The atrocity dissolved the loyalty that John needed to defend Normandy in the year that followed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: where John captured Arthur, the year and month of his capture, and the date most commonly cited for his death.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>John captured Arthur of Brittany at Mirebeau on 1 August 1202 in a brilliant lightning march. Arthur was murdered at Rouen, probably on 3 April 1203. The killing destroyed John&rsquo;s standing among his French vassals and prepared the ground for the loss of Normandy a year later.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Medieval politics ran on personal trust. A king who would imprison and probably murder his own nephew &mdash; a captured noble protected by the customs of <dfn class="term" data-def="Medieval custom that captured nobles should be ransomed or honourably imprisoned, not killed; killing a nobleman in captivity was considered a grave dishonour.">noble captivity</dfn> &mdash; could not be trusted to keep his word with anyone else. The Bretons rose; the leading Poitevin barons reopened negotiations with Philip; rumours spread that John had personally done the deed. When Philip&rsquo;s forces invaded Normandy in 1203 they met a duchy whose nobility had already decided the king did not deserve their loyalty.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n17">By the spring of 1203, John had crowned himself, bought a peace with Philip, won a brilliant battle at Mirebeau, and then thrown the goodwill away. He had inherited the most successful royal house in Europe and, within four years of accession, had set in motion the chain of events that would lose Normandy and his French inheritance forever. The next lesson examines exactly how that loss unfolded.</p>
