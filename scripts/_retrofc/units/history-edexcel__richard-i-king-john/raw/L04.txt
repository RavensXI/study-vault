--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Richard could not retake Jerusalem, his capture, and the ransom that drained England.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">After the victories at Acre and Arsuf in summer 1191 the road to Jerusalem seemed open. Richard refortified Jaffa and pushed inland twice in late 1191 and early 1192 &mdash; up the road to Beit Nuba, only twelve miles from the Holy City. Both times he turned back. The failure to recapture Jerusalem was not the failure of one battle but of an entire strategic position. Several factors closed in on the king at the same time.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Logistical Problem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jerusalem sat in arid hill country some forty miles from the coast. Even if the crusaders captured it, the city would have to be supplied by mule trains across that distance, through territory dotted with Muslim castles. Saladin had already destroyed walls and water cisterns in towns along the route to deny their use to the crusaders. Richard&rsquo;s council of war &mdash; particularly the local barons of the surviving Latin kingdom &mdash; argued that even a successful assault on Jerusalem would be undone the next campaigning season as soon as the bulk of the western army went home and Saladin counter-attacked the long supply line. Richard accepted the argument with reluctance. He is supposed to have refused to look at Jerusalem from a distance, saying that he was unworthy to see what he could not conquer.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who left the crusade and went home in summer 1191, and why did this damage Richard&rsquo;s position even when he was winning battles?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Philip II of France sailed home in August 1191 shortly after Acre fell. Once back in France, he intrigued with John against Richard&rsquo;s lands, putting permanent pressure on Richard to come home before he could finish the crusade.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Philip II&rsquo;s Departure and the Threat to Normandy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Soon after Acre fell, Philip II announced he was ill and sailed home in August 1191. The departure of the French king was a strategic disaster for Richard. It removed a substantial part of the army; it allowed Philip to plot against Richard&rsquo;s lands in France while Richard himself was thousands of miles away; and it took with it the political legitimacy of a joint royal crusade. By spring 1192 Richard was hearing that John, in concert with Philip, was attempting to seize the Angevin Empire and make himself effective king of England. Every week Richard stayed in the Holy Land was a week that his French inheritance bled. The clock that drove him home had started ticking the moment Philip raised sail.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Internal Christian Division and Saladin&rsquo;s Strategy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Christian camp was split. Conrad of Montferrat and the local barons of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The crusader states established along the eastern Mediterranean coast after the First Crusade; by 1191 reduced to a coastal strip with Jerusalem in Muslim hands.">Latin Kingdom</dfn> wanted Conrad as king of Jerusalem; Richard backed Guy of Lusignan. The dispute was only resolved with Conrad&rsquo;s assassination by the Order of Assassins on 28 April 1192 (a killing rumour blamed, probably unfairly, on Richard) and the elevation of Henry II of Champagne. Even then Christian command was fragile, with Pisans, Genoese, Templars, Hospitallers, French and English barons all pulling in different directions.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Saladin, meanwhile, played a careful game. He was not strong enough to drive Richard out of Acre and Jaffa, but he did not have to be. Wherever Richard pushed, Saladin destroyed wells and cisterns, kept his field army intact, and waited. He understood that Richard&rsquo;s political problems in France would force the king to compromise. The two sides agreed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The September 1192 truce between Richard I and Saladin that ended the Third Crusade, conceding Jerusalem to Saladin in exchange for Christian access to the city for unarmed pilgrims.">Treaty of Jaffa</dfn> on 2 September 1192. Jerusalem stayed in Muslim hands; the Christians kept Acre, Jaffa and the coastal strip; unarmed Christian pilgrims were guaranteed access to the Holy Sepulchre. Richard sailed for home a month later.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Students sometimes argue that the Third Crusade was a Christian success because Richard took Acre, won at Arsuf and signed the Treaty of Jaffa. The strategic test, though, is whether the crusade achieved its stated aim &mdash; the recovery of Jerusalem. It did not. The Treaty of Jaffa kept the Holy City in Muslim hands; the crusader states were reduced to a coastal strip; and Richard&rsquo;s reputation as &lsquo;Lionheart&rsquo; was built on tactical military skill rather than strategic crusading success. The campaign ended not with Richard riding into Jerusalem but with him in chains in an Austrian castle. Top-band answers acknowledge his battlefield achievement while explaining why the crusade failed against the criterion the Pope had set in 1187.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Richard&rsquo;s Return and Capture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Richard left the Holy Land in October 1192. He could not sail north up the western Mediterranean because Philip II controlled the southern French coast and would have arrested him; Spanish coasts were closed by political agreements; the route through Italy was unsafe because of his quarrel with the German emperor Henry VI. He chose to sail north and slip overland through the lands of his enemies. A storm wrecked his ship in the Adriatic in December 1192. Travelling in disguise he was recognised near Vienna and seized by Duke Leopold V of Austria, whose banner Richard had thrown into the moat at Acre. Leopold handed Richard over to Henry VI in March 1193. The Holy Roman Emperor demanded a ransom of 100,000 marks of silver, soon raised to 150,000 &mdash; an extraordinary sum equal to two or three years of total English royal revenue.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: who captured Richard, who held him for ransom, and the size and equivalent of his ransom.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Richard was captured by Duke Leopold V of Austria in December 1192 and handed to Holy Roman Emperor Henry VI in March 1193. The agreed ransom was 150,000 marks of silver &mdash; roughly two to three times the Crown&rsquo;s annual revenue.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Ransom and Its Burden on England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The task of raising 150,000 marks fell on Hubert Walter and Eleanor of Aquitaine. Together they extracted the sum from across the Angevin Empire. The English contribution was punitive: a quarter tax on income and moveables (one of the most aggressive taxes of the medieval period); the entire wool clip of the Cistercian and Gilbertine monasteries; church plate seized from cathedrals; extraordinary tallages on the Jewish community recorded in the archae; a heavy <dfn class="term" data-def="A medieval tax paid by tenants-in-chief in cash in lieu of providing knight service in person; raised under Richard and especially heavily under John.">scutage</dfn> on tenants-in-chief who refused to serve in person; and special levies on towns and royal demesne. The first instalment was paid at Speyer in February 1194 and Richard was released. The remainder of the debt rumbled on for years.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The strain on England was severe. The Crown had already raised the Saladin Tithe in 1188 and squeezed the kingdom on Richard&rsquo;s coronation in 1189. The 1193&ndash;94 ransom on top of that left the Treasury near empty. Inflation rose; baronial resentment hardened; the Cistercian monasteries protested at the loss of their wool clip. The taxes that John inherited &mdash; and his urgent need to keep extracting more &mdash; began here. Richard returned to England briefly in March 1194, was crowned a second time at Winchester to reaffirm his sovereignty, and crossed to Normandy in May 1194 to begin the war with Philip II that would consume the rest of his reign. He never came back. He was killed by a crossbow bolt at the siege of Châlus in Aquitaine on 6 April 1199.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">The ransom shapes everything that follows. It financially exhausted England before John inherited the throne in 1199, leaving him a Treasury that needed continual replenishment. It hardened the use of scutage as a substitute for knight service, a practice John would push to breaking point. It set a precedent for taxing income and moveables that the Angevin Crown would return to in 1207. And it taught the barons that royal demands could escalate without limit, planting one of the seeds of the rebellion that ended at Runnymede.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Richard could not recapture Jerusalem because of logistical limits, Philip II&rsquo;s departure and threat to Normandy, internal Christian division, and Saladin&rsquo;s patient scorched-earth strategy &mdash; the Treaty of Jaffa (September 1192) accepted Muslim control of the city in return for pilgrim access.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Returning home, Richard was wrecked in the Adriatic in December 1192, captured by Duke Leopold of Austria, and handed to Emperor Henry VI for a ransom of 150,000 marks of silver.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Hubert Walter and Eleanor of Aquitaine raised the ransom through a quarter tax, the Cistercian wool clip, church plate and extraordinary tallages &mdash; financially exhausting England and setting the pattern of escalating royal demands that John would inherit.</li></ul>
