--- DESCRIPTION ---
How taxation, the 1212 plot and the disaster at Bouvines in 1214 turned the barons to revolt.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">By 1213 King John had won a remarkable diplomatic victory over Pope Innocent III. He had replaced the Interdict with a papal alliance and looked ready to pour the wealth of the kingdom into one final attempt to recover Normandy. What he had not done was rebuild trust with the men whose money and military service he needed most: the English barons. Three things broke that relationship between 1212 and 1215 &mdash; relentless financial pressure, a baronial plot John never forgave, and the catastrophe of a single July afternoon at Bouvines.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Squeezing the Kingdom for War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The loss of Normandy in 1204 left John with a vast war debt and an obsessive ambition to win it back. Doing so meant raising sums no medieval English king had attempted before, and John squeezed every available source. <dfn class="term" data-def="A money payment by a tenant-in-chief in place of providing knights for royal military service; collected at progressively higher rates by John, especially in 1209 and 1214.">Scutage</dfn> &mdash; the cash payment owed by a baron in place of personal military service &mdash; was levied eleven times in his reign, compared to three times under Richard. The 1214 scutage to fund the Bouvines campaign was charged at three marks per knight&rsquo;s fee, the highest rate of the reign, and was demanded from barons whose families had not crossed the Channel for a generation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Alongside scutage, John exploited every <dfn class="term" data-def="Customary feudal payments owed to the king by his tenants-in-chief on specific occasions, such as inheritance (relief), the marriage of heirs and heiresses, and the wardship of underage heirs.">feudal incident</dfn> the law allowed, and many it did not. Reliefs &mdash; the payment a baron made to inherit his father&rsquo;s lands &mdash; were charged at deliberately punitive rates: William Fitz Alan was charged 10,000 marks for his inheritance, John de Lacy 7,000. Heiresses were sold to the highest bidder; widows were forced to pay enormous &lsquo;fines&rsquo; not to be remarried against their will. Wardships of orphaned heirs were auctioned. The pipe rolls of 1209&ndash;14 record cumulative royal income at levels not matched until the fourteenth century.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many times John levied scutage compared with Richard, the rate of the 1214 scutage, and one example of a punitive baronial relief.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>John levied scutage eleven times between 1199 and 1214, compared with three under Richard I. The 1214 scutage was set at three marks per knight&rsquo;s fee, the highest rate of the reign. Massive feudal &lsquo;fines&rsquo; on inheritances &mdash; 10,000 marks on William Fitz Alan, 7,000 on John de Lacy &mdash; turned ordinary feudal incidents into instruments of royal extortion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Arbitrary Power and the Northerners</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">It was not only the size of the demands but the way they were imposed that infuriated the barons. John ruled by what contemporaries called <dfn class="term" data-def="Government by the unilateral will of the king, without the customary consultation of barons or the established feudal procedures; the central baronial complaint against John&rsquo;s methods.">arbitrary power</dfn> &mdash; the king&rsquo;s personal will, exercised without consultation, often through trusted foreign captains rather than English magnates. He took hostages from the children of barons he distrusted; he kept noblemen waiting at court for months while their estates ran down; he reversed legal judgements he disliked. After 1212 he relied increasingly on mercenary captains such as Falkes de Bre&aacute;ut&eacute; and Engelard de Cigogn&eacute;, whose loyalty was to the wages he paid, not to the customary law of the kingdom.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Resistance gathered first in the north. The barons of Yorkshire, Northumberland and the East Midlands &mdash; the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The Yorkshire and Northumbrian barons who refused to serve overseas or to pay the 1214 scutage; the hard core of the rebellion that produced Magna Carta.">Northerners</dfn> &mdash; held lands too far from the Channel for direct profit from a French campaign. In 1213 they declared they were not obliged to fight in France; in 1214 they refused to pay scutage for an expedition they had not joined. By the spring of 1215 their grievances had spread south, where the leaders included men John had personally provoked: Robert FitzWalter, whose castle John had pulled down, and Eustace de Vesci, whose wife John was rumoured to have insulted.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Modern historians, beginning with W. L. Warren in the 1960s, have stressed that John was an unusually energetic king of England. He travelled the kingdom personally, sat in royal courts, kept detailed pipe-roll records, and experimented with new taxes such as the 1207 thirteenth on movables and income. Royal revenue under John was the highest in real terms before the fourteenth century. The popular image of John as lazy or stupid is a Victorian caricature reinforced by the Robin Hood legend. The barons did not rebel because John was a bad administrator &mdash; they rebelled because he was a good one whose methods were arbitrary, unpredictable, and squeezed them harder than any English king had done before. The competence is genuine; so are the grievances.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Plot of 1212</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In August 1212 John was preparing a campaign in Wales when he uncovered a conspiracy aimed at his life. The leaders, Robert FitzWalter and Eustace de Vesci, planned either to assassinate him during the campaign or to abandon him to the Welsh and offer the throne to a rival. The detail of the plot is obscure &mdash; FitzWalter and de Vesci fled abroad before the king could arrest them &mdash; but its political meaning was unmistakable. Even with the country under Interdict and a French invasion threatened, a section of the baronage now thought rebellion preferable to obedience.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">John cancelled the Welsh campaign, took fresh hostages from the children of the conspirators&rsquo; allies, and tightened his grip on royal castles. He did not, however, pursue and crush the plotters. FitzWalter returned to England in 1213 under the cover of the papal reconciliation and quietly resumed his place &mdash; a leniency John would regret in 1215, when FitzWalter became the rebel barons&rsquo; chosen <em>Marshal of the Army of God</em>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the year of the plot, the two named ringleaders, what they planned, and how they avoided punishment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In August 1212 a baronial plot led by Robert FitzWalter and Eustace de Vesci aimed to kill or replace John during his Welsh campaign. The conspirators escaped abroad, returned under cover of the 1213 papal reconciliation, and emerged as central rebels in 1215.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Bouvines, 27 July 1214</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1214 John had assembled the war he had been preparing for ten years. His plan was a two-pronged invasion of France. He would land in Poitou with English forces and push north; his nephew, the Holy Roman Emperor Otto IV, with the Counts of Flanders and Boulogne, would attack from the north-east. The two armies would crush King Philip II between them and the Angevin lands &mdash; Normandy, Anjou, Maine &mdash; would be restored.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The plan came apart at the village of Bouvines in Flanders on 27 July 1214. John&rsquo;s southern advance had stalled at the siege of La Roche-aux-Moines, where his Poitevin allies refused to fight a pitched battle. In the north, Otto&rsquo;s coalition met Philip&rsquo;s smaller but better-coordinated army on a hot Sunday afternoon. After several hours&rsquo; fighting Philip personally turned the day, the Imperial banner was trampled, Otto fled, and the Counts of Flanders and Boulogne were captured. John, hundreds of miles away, learnt of the disaster while waiting for an ally who would never come.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Bouvines settled the Angevin question for a generation. With Otto deposed and the Flemish counts captives, Philip II was now master of northern France; Normandy could not be recovered by anyone John could afford to hire. For the English barons it meant something more immediate: the colossal cost of the 1214 expedition &mdash; the scutage, the loans from London moneylenders, the fines on the Jewish community &mdash; had bought nothing. The men who had refused the scutage in the first place looked vindicated; the men who had paid it felt cheated. Within months the great northern lords were holding meetings under the cover of pilgrimage, comparing demands, and preparing to confront the king on his return.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Northampton, Lincoln and the March on London</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">John returned to England in October 1214 with the war lost and his treasury empty. Through the winter the Northerners and their southern allies refused another scutage and presented the king with a list of demands based on the coronation charter of Henry I &mdash; a document Stephen Langton had quietly produced from Canterbury archives. By spring 1215 negotiations had collapsed. The rebels mustered first at <strong>Stamford</strong>, then at <strong>Northampton</strong>, where they formally renounced their oaths of <dfn class="term" data-def="The formal oath of loyalty by which a tenant became the &lsquo;man&rsquo; of his lord, owing him military service and obedience.">homage</dfn> on 5 May. They besieged Northampton castle without success and moved north to take the city of <strong>Lincoln</strong>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">The decisive move came on 17 May 1215. While John waited at Windsor, the rebel army was admitted to <strong>London</strong> by sympathetic citizens. The capital&rsquo;s loss was catastrophic for the king. London was the financial heart of the kingdom, the seat of the Exchequer and the gateway to royal credit. Once the rebels held it, John could no longer raise the loans needed to hire mercenaries from Flanders. The barons had not yet won militarily, but they had broken the king&rsquo;s ability to fight back. Within weeks Stephen Langton was mediating, the king was in retreat, and the meadow at Runnymede was being prepared for the seal.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Between 1209 and 1214 John taxed his kingdom harder than any previous English king &mdash; eleven scutages, punitive feudal &lsquo;fines&rsquo; on inheritances, and the 1207 thirteenth on movables &mdash; while ruling by arbitrary personal will rather than baronial counsel.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">The 1212 plot of FitzWalter and de Vesci showed that armed rebellion was now thinkable; the conspirators escaped punishment and returned to lead the rebellion of 1215.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">The defeat of Otto IV at Bouvines on 27 July 1214 ended any chance of recovering Normandy, leaving the costs of the campaign without reward; refusal of scutage, the muster at Northampton and Lincoln, and the rebel entry into London on 17 May 1215 forced John to negotiate at Runnymede.</li></ul>
