--- DESCRIPTION ---
Richard’s mighty castle, John’s failures, and the collapse of Angevin Normandy in 1204.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Normandy mattered. It was the cradle of the dynasty that had ruled England since 1066, the richest of John&rsquo;s French lands, and the strategic gateway between his English kingdom and the rest of his empire. Losing it in 1204 was the single most damaging event of John&rsquo;s reign &mdash; the trigger for the financial extortion that drove his barons to revolt, and a wound the English Crown spent the next two centuries trying to reverse.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Competing Aims in Normandy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Three rulers had different things to gain. Richard I, on his return from crusade, wanted to recover the territory Philip II had grabbed during his absence and to fortify the Norman frontier so that nothing like that could happen again. King John, on his accession in 1199, wanted Philip&rsquo;s recognition that he and not Arthur was the lawful heir, even at heavy cost &mdash; the 20,000-mark relief and ceded territory of the 1200 Treaty of Le Goulet. <dfn class="term" data-def="Capetian king of France 1180&ndash;1223; systematically dismantled the Angevin Empire and is sometimes called &lsquo;Augustus&rsquo; for the lands he added to the French crown.">Philip II of France</dfn> wanted the lot. He had spent two decades probing Angevin weakness and saw the death of Richard, the inexperienced reign of John, and the rival claim of Arthur as the chance to reabsorb Normandy into the French royal demesne.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">From 1199 to 1202 the three positions held in tense balance. Le Goulet bought a formal peace. But the underlying issue &mdash; that John&rsquo;s French lands were technically held in vassalage to Philip &mdash; meant any quarrel could be turned into a legal pretext. The Lusignan appeal in 1202, over John&rsquo;s seizure of Isabella of Angoulême, gave Philip exactly the trigger he needed. Philip&rsquo;s court declared John&rsquo;s French fiefs <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal forfeiture of fiefs by a feudal lord, declared when a vassal failed in the duties owed to him; the legal mechanism by which Philip II declared John&rsquo;s French lands forfeit in 1202.">forfeit</dfn> in spring 1202, and the war for Normandy began.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the three rulers, the year of forfeiture, and the legal mechanism Philip II used to declare John&rsquo;s French lands his.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Norman question split three ways: Richard wanted to recover lost ground; John wanted recognition at almost any price; Philip II wanted to absorb Normandy into the French crown. In 1202 Philip used the Lusignan appeal to declare John&rsquo;s French fiefs forfeit and unleashed the war.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Château Gaillard: Richard&rsquo;s Mighty Castle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1196 Richard I had begun building a fortress on a chalk bluff above the Seine at Les Andelys, twenty-five miles downstream from Rouen. He called it <dfn class="term" data-def="Richard I&rsquo;s great castle on the Seine at Les Andelys, completed in 1198 to block any French advance on Rouen; fell to Philip II in March 1204.">Château Gaillard</dfn> &mdash; &lsquo;saucy castle&rsquo; &mdash; and is supposed to have boasted that he could hold it even if its walls were made of butter. Its design was state of the art: three concentric wards (defensive enclosures), each commanding the next, a deep dry moat cut from the rock, and a great cylindrical keep with sloping walls that no medieval mining technique could undermine. It was completed in 1198 in barely two years &mdash; an astonishing pace for a castle of this size.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The cost was colossal. Contemporary records suggest Richard spent around £11,500 on Château Gaillard alone &mdash; more than he spent on every other English royal castle of his reign combined. To put that in scale, the entire annual revenue of the English Crown was only around £25,000 a year. The castle was meant to do one job: stop any French advance up the Seine to Rouen, the Norman capital. As long as it stood, Normandy was strategically safe. Once it fell, Rouen was naked.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">John&rsquo;s Failures of 1203</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In the year after the murder of Arthur of Brittany, John&rsquo;s position unravelled. Norman, Breton and Poitevin barons defected to Philip; Hubert de Burgh held Chinon for John against odds, but elsewhere fortresses opened their gates without a fight. John&rsquo;s response combined frantic activity with strategic paralysis. He marched troops about Normandy in 1203 without bringing Philip to battle. He refused to commit personally to the relief of Château Gaillard when the siege began in August 1203, despite repeated pleas from his commanders. By December he had abandoned Normandy altogether and crossed back to England, leaving his troops behind.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Students sometimes treat 1204 as the inevitable result of long-standing English versus French rivalry, as if the Channel guaranteed eventual separation. The Angevin Empire of 1199 was actually larger and richer than the French royal domain. Philip II had been losing ground a decade earlier. The loss of Normandy was triggered by very specific events: John&rsquo;s marriage to Isabella of Angoulême, his treatment of the Lusignans, the murder of Arthur, and the fall of the supposedly impregnable Château Gaillard. Different decisions could plausibly have produced a different outcome &mdash; the loss was contingent, not inevitable.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Siege of Château Gaillard, 1203&ndash;1204</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Philip II began the siege of Château Gaillard in August 1203. He cut the Seine with a stockade of boats so that supplies could not reach the town below the castle, then methodically reduced the outer wards over the winter. The defenders &mdash; around 200 knights and sergeants under Roger de Lacy &mdash; held out for six months. Civilian refugees from Les Andelys town were expelled into no-man&rsquo;s-land between the two armies and many died of cold and starvation in the winter of 1203&ndash;04. The outer ward fell on 22 February 1204 when French sappers fired its tower; the middle ward fell soon after, when an enterprising soldier crawled in through a latrine chute. The keep itself fell on 6 March 1204 after de Lacy ran out of food. With Château Gaillard taken, Rouen was open. The Norman capital surrendered in June 1204; by Christmas the duchy was Philip&rsquo;s.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the year Château Gaillard was begun, the year it fell, who its defenders were led by, and the route by which the French entered the middle ward.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Château Gaillard was begun by Richard in 1196 and completed in 1198 at a cost of about &pound;11,500. Philip II besieged it from August 1203; the keep fell on 6 March 1204 after Roger de Lacy&rsquo;s garrison ran out of food. Rouen surrendered in June 1204 and Normandy was lost.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Normandy Fell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Several causes converged. Politically, John&rsquo;s treatment of the Lusignans and the murder of Arthur had drained baronial loyalty in Normandy and the surrounding lands; many lords would not fight for him. Strategically, John refused to commit decisively to the field, perhaps because he could not trust the magnates marching alongside him. Militarily, Philip II concentrated his force on the single decisive point and pushed the siege to its conclusion. Financially, Richard&rsquo;s ransom and crusade had drained the Angevin treasury; John could not match Philip&rsquo;s spending. And technically, even Château Gaillard could not hold against a determined siege when there was no relief from outside.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The loss reshaped John&rsquo;s reign. Norman baronial families who had held lands on both sides of the Channel had to choose which king to obey, splintering long-established estates. John lost a vast chunk of his revenue with the duchy. To recover Normandy he would have to launch an invasion, and to fund the invasion he would have to squeeze England dry &mdash; through scutage, the 1207 tax on income and moveables, ferocious feudal incidents, and tallages on the Jewish community. The financial pressure that produced Magna Carta in 1215 began with the loss of 1204.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">By the end of 1204 the duchy from which the conquerors of England had launched their fleet in 1066 was a French province. The English kings would call themselves Dukes of Normandy in their titles for centuries afterwards, but the political reality had changed. The next chapter of John&rsquo;s reign was about how he tried to claw back the loss &mdash; and about the price his English subjects paid for the attempt.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Richard, John and Philip II had competing aims in Normandy &mdash; Richard wanted to fortify it, John wanted recognition at almost any price, Philip wanted to absorb it; Philip&rsquo;s 1202 declaration of forfeiture turned the rivalry into open war.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Château Gaillard was the most expensive castle of Richard&rsquo;s reign, completed in 1198 to block any French advance on Rouen; its fall on 6 March 1204 after a six-month siege opened the Norman capital.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">The loss of Normandy in 1204 was caused by John&rsquo;s strategic indecision, the political damage of Arthur&rsquo;s murder, Angevin financial exhaustion and Philip II&rsquo;s concentrated military pressure &mdash; and it created the financial squeeze that drove towards Magna Carta.</li></ul>
