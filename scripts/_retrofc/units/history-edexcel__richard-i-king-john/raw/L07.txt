--- DESCRIPTION ---
How John ran England in person: demesne, sheriffs, scutage, feudal incidents, and the 1207 tax.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Richard had been an absentee king. John was the opposite. From his accession in 1199 until his death in 1216 he was almost continuously present in his English kingdom, especially after the loss of Normandy in 1204 closed his French refuges. He travelled constantly, hearing pleas, checking accounts, demanding documents and frightening sheriffs. The pipe rolls grew thicker every year. The result was the most intensively administered kingdom in medieval Europe &mdash; and the most resented.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">A King Who Was Always There</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Personal kingship was not optional in 1200. The medieval king ruled by being seen. John&rsquo;s itinerary shows him moving from royal manor to royal manor every few days &mdash; Westminster, Windsor, Marlborough, Clipstone, Nottingham, York &mdash; with a household of clerks, knights, hounds and falcons trailing behind. His itinerant courts of justice, called the eyre, followed roughly the pattern Henry II had built and Hubert Walter had sharpened. Thanks to these tours, the king was a real and present authority everywhere in his kingdom, not a distant figure in London.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">After 1204 his presence intensified for a darker reason: he had nowhere else to go. With Normandy lost, his ability to escape his English subjects was gone. The same king who had once divided his time between two realms now spent every winter in England. Government became more responsive but also more arbitrary. Disputes that would once have waited weeks for a royal hearing were settled within days; rulings that would once have been buffered by distance were now imposed at close range.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how often John toured England, why his presence intensified after 1204, and one administrative consequence of this constant royal proximity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>John was the most personally present English king of his century. After the loss of Normandy in 1204 he toured the kingdom constantly, hearing pleas, checking accounts and supervising sheriffs in person; the pipe rolls thickened year by year. The cost was that royal pressure was now felt everywhere, every day.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Royal Revenues: the Demesne and the Sheriffs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The starting point of royal income was the <dfn class="term" data-def="Lands held directly by the king and farmed for revenue, rather than granted out to tenants for service; one of the steadiest sources of medieval royal income.">royal demesne</dfn>: the lands the Crown held and farmed for itself rather than granting out to tenants. Rents from demesne manors, profits from royal mills, fishponds and forests, and the customs paid in royal markets all flowed back to the Exchequer. Demesne revenue had the great virtue of not requiring baronial consent, since the lands were the king&rsquo;s own.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The king&rsquo;s chief officer in each shire, responsible for collecting royal revenue, presiding over the shire court, leading the county levy, and arresting those accused of crimes against the Crown.">sheriff</dfn> was the practical engine of all this. In every shire he collected the rents and dues of the demesne, the customary &lsquo;county farm&rsquo; (a fixed sum owed to the Crown each year), the proceeds of justice (fines and confiscations), and various local taxes. He paid in twice a year at the Westminster Exchequer, where his accounts were audited and recorded on the pipe rolls. Sheriffs typically bought their offices &mdash; literally paid the Crown a sum to be appointed &mdash; and recouped their investment, plus profit, over their term. John exploited this. He sold and unsold sheriffdoms with greater frequency than any predecessor, raising cash and rotating men he distrusted.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Feudal Incidents</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The miscellaneous payments and rights a feudal lord could demand from his tenants on certain occasions: relief, wardship, marriage, escheat and aids.">Feudal incidents</dfn> were the windfall payments owed to the king by his tenants-in-chief on specific occasions. They mattered because they were old, accepted, and could be applied at the king&rsquo;s discretion. The five main incidents were: <strong>relief</strong> (an inheritance fee paid by the heir of a deceased baron); <strong>wardship</strong> (the right of the king to administer the lands of a baron who died leaving an underage heir, and to keep the income until the heir came of age); <strong>marriage</strong> (the right to choose a husband for the heiress of a tenant-in-chief, often sold to the highest bidder); <strong>escheat</strong> (the return of a fief to the Crown if the tenant died without heirs or was convicted of treason); and <strong>aids</strong> (special payments due in three customary cases &mdash; ransom of the king, knighting of his eldest son, marriage of his eldest daughter).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">John applied these incidents with unprecedented severity. The traditional going rate for a baronial relief had been around &pound;100. John demanded reliefs of &pound;1,000, &pound;5,000, even (in the case of William fitz Alan) &pound;7,000. Marriages of heiresses were sold for huge cash sums &mdash; Geoffrey de Mandeville had to pay a 20,000-mark fine in 1214 to marry Isabel of Gloucester. Wardships were extended to the limit. The legal language of feudalism was the same as it had been under Henry II; the price of obeying that language had multiplied.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">The traditional &lsquo;bad King John&rsquo; image is misleading. Modern historians, especially W.L. Warren, stress his administrative competence: detailed pipe-roll record-keeping, energetic itinerant justice, careful taxation experiments. He inherited a financially exhausted kingdom from Richard&rsquo;s crusade and ransom and squeezed every shilling out of it. The problem was not that he was lazy or stupid &mdash; he was neither &mdash; but that his methods of squeezing revenue were too efficient and too arbitrary, and that his application of feudal incidents was so harsh that it broke the political consent on which medieval kingship rested.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Scutage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A money payment due from a tenant-in-chief in lieu of personal military service; literally &lsquo;shield-money&rsquo;, traditionally paid only when the king summoned an army.">Scutage</dfn> &mdash; literally &lsquo;shield-money&rsquo; &mdash; was the cash a baron could pay instead of leading his quota of knights to the king&rsquo;s campaigns. It had been levied occasionally under Henry II and Richard. John levied it eleven times in seventeen years, sometimes when he had no campaign in progress. The going rate doubled, then tripled. By 1214 the rate was &pound;3 per knight&rsquo;s fee &mdash; high enough that the &lsquo;Northerners&rsquo;, the great barons of the north of England, refused to pay it for the disastrous Bouvines campaign. Scutage thus combined two deeply unpopular features. It taxed the barons to fund foreign war, and it bypassed their consent: the king did not need to summon them for advice if all he wanted was their money.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The 1207 Tax on Income and Moveables</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In 1207 John tried something genuinely new. With his eye on a campaign to recover Normandy, he summoned a great council and won approval for a tax of one-thirteenth on every free man&rsquo;s income and the value of his movable goods. This was the first English tax assessed on personal wealth as a whole, rather than on land or feudal status, and it brought in the staggering sum of &pound;57,000 &mdash; more than two ordinary years of royal revenue at a single stroke.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Tallages on the Anglo-Jewish community (special arbitrary taxes) added a further stream &mdash; the 1210 Bristol Tallage alone was set at 66,000 marks. Combined with the demesne, the sheriffs, the feudal incidents, the eleven scutages, and the 1207 thirteenth, John&rsquo;s revenue rose dramatically. By the early 1210s the Crown was bringing in around &pound;50,000 a year, twice what it had managed at Richard&rsquo;s death.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the year of the new income and moveables tax, the proportion taxed, and the sum it raised.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1207 John levied an unprecedented tax of one-thirteenth on every free man&rsquo;s income and movable goods, raising about &pound;57,000 &mdash; more than two ordinary years of royal revenue. Combined with eleven scutages, harsher feudal incidents and tallages on the Jewish community, his methods doubled royal income but generated the resentment that fed the baronial revolt.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">John&rsquo;s revenue methods were technically legal &mdash; reliefs, wardships, marriages, scutage and tallages were all old. The political problem was the rate and frequency. When a relief that used to cost &pound;100 cost &pound;7,000, when scutage was levied eleven times in seventeen years, when widows could be forced to remarry the king&rsquo;s candidate at any price he set, the great barons concluded that the king was using law as a weapon. Magna Carta&rsquo;s most precise clauses &mdash; on relief sums, on widows&rsquo; remarriage, on scutage requiring counsel, on the wardship of estates &mdash; all map onto these specific grievances.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n20">The financial machine John built was the most efficient in Europe. It also produced the resentment that, between 1212 and 1215, would push his great barons into open revolt. Government and finance were inseparable from politics under John &mdash; and the next lesson examines the dispute with the Papacy that came on top of all the financial pressure.</p>

<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">After 1204 John was almost continuously present in England, ruling through constant personal tours, the eyre, and the network of sheriffs paying in twice a year at the Exchequer.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">Royal income was built on the demesne, sheriffs, feudal incidents (relief, wardship, marriage, escheat, aids) and scutage; John applied each at unprecedented rates &mdash; reliefs of &pound;1,000&ndash;7,000, eleven scutages in seventeen years.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">The 1207 tax of one-thirteenth on income and moveables raised about &pound;57,000 in a single year, doubling royal revenue and creating the precise grievances later listed in Magna Carta.</li></ul>
