John’s death, the boy king Henry III, and how William Marshal saved the dynasty as Protector.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">When King John died at Newark Castle on the night of 18&ndash;19 October 1216, the cause of England looked lost. Half the kingdom &mdash; including London, the Midlands and the south-east &mdash; was in the hands of rebel barons and the army of Prince Louis of France. The legitimate heir was a nine-year-old boy nobody had ever heard of, hidden at Devizes. The royal treasury was empty, the great seal had reportedly been lost in the Wash, and the only major royal asset left in firm loyalist hands was the great castle of Dover. That the dynasty survived the next twelve months &mdash; and that Magna Carta survived with it &mdash; is the achievement of one man: <dfn class="term" data-def="Earl of Pembroke (c.1146&ndash;1219); the most respected knight in Anglo-Norman Europe, loyal to John throughout the war; appointed Protector and Regent for the boy king Henry III in October 1216.">William Marshal</dfn>, the seventy-year-old Earl of Pembroke who agreed to act as Protector for the boy king Henry III.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Condition of England in October 1216</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The kingdom John left was in worse shape than England had been since 1066. The First Barons&rsquo; War had been running for fifteen months. Prince Louis of France controlled London and the south-east; the rebel barons held the great northern castles of Lincoln, York and Carlisle and most of East Anglia; loyalist resistance was a patchwork of garrisoned castles &mdash; Dover, Windsor, Corfe, Nottingham &mdash; rather than a contiguous territory. The rebel coalition had received reinforcements from Capetian France through the Cinque Ports; mercenary captains in royal pay had been imperfectly paid for months.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Royal finances were close to collapse. The pipe rolls for 1215&ndash;16 show revenue from the rebel-held shires effectively at zero. John&rsquo;s baggage train, lost in the tidal estuary of the Wash on 12 October, had carried not just the legendary regalia but a substantial part of his portable treasury &mdash; cash, jewels, plate &mdash; whose loss remains a matter of debate but was certainly damaging at the worst possible moment. The Anglo-Jewish community, taxed to ruin in 1210 and 1213, was no longer a credit reservoir. Without continental allies and without a tax base, the loyalist cause depended on the prestige of the dynasty alone.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the date of John&rsquo;s death, the fraction of the kingdom under rebel control, and three of the loyalist castles still in royal hands.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>King John died at Newark Castle on 18&ndash;19 October 1216 of dysentery contracted on the Lincolnshire campaign. Roughly half the kingdom &mdash; London, much of the Midlands, the south-east, and the great northern castles &mdash; was held by rebel barons supporting Prince Louis of France. Loyalist territory was a network of garrisoned castles including Dover, Windsor, Corfe and Nottingham.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Problem of the Succession</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">John&rsquo;s heir was his eldest son, Henry, born at Winchester on 1 October 1207 and so just turned nine when his father died. The boy had no military experience, no political following of his own, and almost no public profile. He had been raised at Devizes by his guardian Peter des Roches. The contrast with Louis &mdash; an adult prince of the strongest royal house in Christendom, in physical command of the rebel cause, anointed in Westminster Abbey in spirit if not yet in fact &mdash; could hardly have been sharper.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The succession faced three immediate problems. First, legal status: a child king under medieval English custom required a recognised <dfn class="term" data-def="A person appointed to govern a kingdom on behalf of a monarch who is too young, absent or otherwise unable to rule; in 1216 the title used was &lsquo;rector regis et regni&rsquo;, Protector of the king and kingdom.">regent</dfn>, and the ordinary candidates &mdash; the king&rsquo;s mother, eldest brother, or senior baron &mdash; were either unsuitable or absent. Second, political legitimacy: the rebels had repudiated the Plantagenet line and sworn fealty to Louis; Henry needed a counter-symbol that could draw waverers back. Third, finance: a regency could not pay mercenaries on a hope of future loyalty.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">The loyalist solution had three parts, executed in days. On 28 October Henry was crowned in <strong>Gloucester Cathedral</strong> &mdash; not Westminster, which was in rebel hands &mdash; by the papal legate Guala Bicchieri; a circle of gold belonging to his mother, Isabella of Angoul&ecirc;me, was used in place of the lost regalia. The boy did homage for England to Pope Innocent III&rsquo;s successor, Honorius III, formally placing the kingdom under papal protection. And on 11 November 1216 a council at Bristol named William Marshal as <strong>Protector of the king and the kingdom</strong>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">It is tempting to ask why the loyalists did not pick an adult Plantagenet rather than a nine-year-old. There was no plausible candidate. John&rsquo;s elder brother Geoffrey had died in 1186 and his son Arthur had been murdered, almost certainly by John, in 1203. Richard had died childless in 1199. The Plantagenet line had narrowed to John&rsquo;s direct descendants. A boy with weak personal authority but indisputable legitimacy was actually an asset against a strong but foreign claimant: every ounce of legal continuity with the older England was on Henry&rsquo;s side, while Louis depended on a thin claim through his wife. The papal legate&rsquo;s coronation at Gloucester turned that legal advantage into a religious one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">William Marshal Accepts the Regency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">William Marshal was around seventy in October 1216, then the oldest age a fighting man of his rank could expect to reach. He had served Henry II, Richard, and John as a household knight and as a senior commander; he had remained loyal to John even at the worst moments of 1215&ndash;16. His prestige across the Anglo-Norman aristocracy was unrivalled: contemporaries called him &lsquo;the best knight in the world&rsquo;. His acceptance of the regency, by his own account in his biography <em>L&rsquo;Histoire de Guillaume le Mar&eacute;chal</em>, came reluctantly and with a vow that he would &lsquo;carry the king on his shoulders&rsquo; from island to island if needed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The Marshal&rsquo;s first move was extraordinary and decisive. Within days of taking the regency he persuaded the council to <strong>reissue Magna Carta</strong> in the boy king&rsquo;s name (12 November 1216). The clauses targeting John personally &mdash; including the Twenty-five enforcement committee &mdash; were quietly dropped, but the protections on inheritance, taxation by counsel, justice, and the freedom of the Church were retained. By doing this Marshal stripped the rebels of their political programme: the rebel barons had taken up arms over Magna Carta, and now Magna Carta was the law of the new king. Within months wavering moderates began returning to the loyalist camp.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: the date Henry III was crowned, the place, who crowned him, and the date Marshal first reissued Magna Carta.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Henry III was crowned at Gloucester Cathedral on 28 October 1216 by the papal legate Guala Bicchieri, using a circle of gold borrowed from his mother in place of the lost regalia. William Marshal was named Protector at Bristol on 11 November and immediately reissued Magna Carta in the boy king&rsquo;s name on 12 November 1216, stripping the rebellion of its programme.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The War Won: Lincoln, Sandwich and the Treaty of Lambeth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The military turning point came in May 1217. While Louis besieged Dover, the loyalist army under Marshal and the papal legate moved north against a French and rebel force investing Lincoln Castle. On 20 May 1217 Marshal &mdash; aged seventy &mdash; personally led the loyalist charge through the city&rsquo;s streets at the <strong>Battle of Lincoln</strong>, contemporaries calling the chaotic action &lsquo;Lincoln Fair&rsquo; for the plunder taken afterwards. The rebel commander, the Count of Perche, was killed; large numbers of barons were captured; the strategic balance turned overnight.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">A French reinforcement fleet was destroyed off Sandwich on 24 August 1217 by an English squadron under Hubert de Burgh; Eustace the Monk, Louis&rsquo;s celebrated naval commander, was beheaded on his own deck. With supplies cut off and the rebel cause hollowing out, Louis sued for peace. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace settlement of 11&ndash;20 September 1217 ending the First Barons&rsquo; War; Louis renounced his claim to the English throne, Marshal&rsquo;s government promised amnesty to the rebel barons, and the rebellious bishops and clergy were absolved by the papal legate.">Treaty of Lambeth</dfn> of September 1217 ended the war: Louis renounced his claim to the English throne, the rebel barons received an amnesty, the rebellious clergy were absolved, and Louis was paid 10,000 marks to leave. England had survived.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Modern historians, from Sidney Painter onwards, have judged the Marshal&rsquo;s twenty-six months as Protector among the most successful regencies in English medieval history. He inherited a kingdom half-occupied and a treasury drained; he died in May 1219 leaving a peaceful, papal-protected, charter-bound kingdom for the boy king to grow into. His political masterstroke &mdash; reissuing Magna Carta on the loyalist side &mdash; transformed the document from a baronial peace treaty for John&rsquo;s lifetime into a continuing constitutional document of the realm. His military leadership at Lincoln in 1217 demonstrated that personal valour still mattered in twelfth-century kingship. Without him the dynasty would probably not have survived 1217. The Marshal is the central reason that Magna Carta did survive &mdash; reissued again in 1225 by his successors and confirmed by Edward I in 1297. His achievement was to ensure that the document of 1215, annulled within ten weeks, became part of English law for the next eight centuries.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">William Marshal died at Caversham on 14 May 1219 and was buried in the Temple Church in London. He had served four kings and carried the dynasty across the gap between John and the adult Henry III. The reign of Richard and John, which had begun at Westminster in 1189, ended in 1219 &mdash; with England safely in possession of a charter, a peace, and a king who would rule for fifty-six years.</p>
