--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Big Three’s competing aims, Wilson’s Fourteen Points, and why the peace conference was doomed to compromise.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Big Three</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace treaty signed in June 1919 that officially ended World War One between Germany and the Allied Powers.">Treaty of Versailles</dfn> was shaped by three leaders with very different goals. Understanding their clashing aims is key to explaining why the treaty ended up the way it did.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down all three leaders, their countries, and one thing each wanted from the peace settlement.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The “<dfn class="term" data-def="The nickname for the three leaders who dominated the Paris Peace Conference: David Lloyd George of Britain, Georges Clemenceau of France, and Woodrow Wilson of the USA.">Big Three</dfn>” were <strong>David Lloyd George</strong> of Britain, <strong>Georges Clemenceau</strong> of France, and <strong>Woodrow Wilson</strong> of the USA. Germany was not invited. Although 32 nations attended the <dfn class="term" data-def="The meeting of victorious Allied nations held in Paris from January 1919 to negotiate the peace treaties ending World War One.">Paris Peace Conference</dfn> from January 1919, and Italy made it a “Big Four,” Italy was virtually ignored — so in reality it was the Big Three who decided the treaty over six months of talks.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">David Lloyd George (Britain)</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>David Lloyd George</strong> won re-election in 1918 using slogans like “Hang the Kaiser,” “Make Germany Pay,” and “squeeze the German lemon till the pips squeak.” Almost a million British soldiers had died, so the public demanded harsh punishment. Privately, though, Lloyd George knew that crippling Germany would hurt British trade — before the war, Germany had been one of Britain’s biggest trading partners. He was a practical deal-maker, caught between Clemenceau’s desire for revenge and Wilson’s idealism.</p>

</figure>

<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">Lloyd George wanted to reduce the German Navy, which had threatened the British Empire. He also hoped to take over some German colonies. But he feared that pushing Germany too hard could drive it toward communism, as had happened in Russia in 1917.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">He was suspicious of the League of Nations — he did not want it to have its own army, in case it gained power over Britain. He supported peace because it was good for business, but always looked for practical deals rather than grand ideals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">His middle-ground approach aimed to punish Germany enough to satisfy voters while keeping it strong enough to trade with — and to act as a buffer against the spread of <dfn class="term" data-def="The communist political movement that took power in Russia after the 1917 Revolution.">Bolshevism</dfn> from the east.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Georges Clemenceau (France)</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Georges Clemenceau</strong> — nicknamed “The Tiger” — was 77 when the conference began. He had been just 29 when Germany invaded France in 1871, so in his lifetime Germany had invaded his homeland <em>twice</em>. His son was wounded in the war. He wanted revenge: high <dfn class="term" data-def="Financial compensation demanded from Germany by the Allied Powers for the damage caused during World War One.">reparations</dfn>, the return of Alsace-Lorraine, the Rhineland demilitarised, and ideally Germany broken up into smaller states.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in three sentences why Clemenceau wanted to punish Germany so harshly. Include at least one statistic.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>France had suffered huge damage — much of the fighting on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The main theatre of war in Western Europe during World War One, stretching from Belgium through north-eastern France.">Western Front</dfn> took place on French soil. Over <strong>1.4 million French soldiers</strong> were killed, and large areas of northern France were destroyed. Clemenceau wanted Germany to pay for all of it. He also wanted the League of Nations to have its own army to protect France from any future attack.</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Diagram showing Georges Clemenceau's six key aims at the Paris Peace Conference — revenge, punishment, Alsace-Lorraine, reparations, military restrictions, and the Rhineland buffer zone" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_clemenceau.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n12">Woodrow Wilson (USA)</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Woodrow Wilson</strong> was an idealist. The USA had only joined the war in 1917 and suffered around 100,000 casualties — far fewer than Britain or France — with no damage to American soil. Wilson had a poor opinion of empires and wanted a fair, “just” peace through his <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of 14 principles published by President Wilson in January 1918 as a basis for ending the war fairly.">Fourteen Points</dfn>. He worried that punishing Germany too harshly would only create a desire for revenge. However, American <dfn class="term" data-def="A policy of avoiding involvement in the political affairs of other countries.">isolationism</dfn> meant many at home wanted nothing to do with Europe’s problems.</p>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Diagram showing Woodrow Wilson's six key aims at the Paris Peace Conference — idealism, Fourteen Points, League of Nations, no harsh punishment, fear of future war, and late US entry" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_wilson.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Where the Big Three Disagreed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Big Three clashed on almost every major issue:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. For each disagreement (land, reparations, military, colonies), write down what each leader wanted and why.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Key Disagreements</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n30">
<li><strong>Land:</strong> Clemenceau wanted to break Germany into smaller states. Wilson and Lloyd George blocked this, fearing it would cause future wars.</li>
<li><strong>Reparations & trade:</strong> Clemenceau wanted to cripple Germany financially. Lloyd George wanted to keep Germany strong enough to buy British goods — Germany had been Britain’s second biggest trade partner before the war.</li>
<li><strong>The League of Nations:</strong> Clemenceau wanted the League to have its own army to protect France. Lloyd George did not — he feared it would interfere with the British Empire. Wilson wanted an army to help keep the peace.</li>
<li><strong>Disarmament:</strong> Wilson wanted <em>all</em> countries to disarm. Clemenceau only wanted Germany to disarm.</li>
<li><strong>Self-determination:</strong> Wilson wanted every ethnic group to rule itself. Lloyd George feared this would threaten the British Empire. Both Britain and France used the idea to grab German colonies instead.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Wilson’s Fourteen Points</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Published in <strong>January 1918</strong>, the Fourteen Points set out Wilson’s plan for lasting peace. The key ideas included:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list as many of Wilson’s Fourteen Points as you can from memory. Then check — which ones did you forget?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Fourteen Points (Summary)</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n31">
<li><strong>1.</strong> No secret treaties — all diplomacy to be open</li>
<li><strong>2.</strong> Free access to the seas in peace and war</li>
<li><strong>3.</strong> Free trade between countries</li>
<li><strong>4.</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="The reduction or withdrawal of military forces and weapons.">Disarmament</dfn> by all countries</li>
<li><strong>5.</strong> Colonies to have a say in their own future</li>
<li><strong>6–13.</strong> Specific territorial changes — Belgium independent, Alsace-Lorraine to France, <dfn class="term" data-def="The right of people to decide their own form of government and which country they belong to.">self-determination</dfn> in Eastern Europe, an independent Poland with sea access</li>
<li><strong>14.</strong> A <dfn class="term" data-def="An international organisation proposed by Wilson to settle disputes between countries peacefully and prevent future wars."><strong>League of Nations</strong></dfn> to settle disputes between countries</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Problems with Wilson and self-determination</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">Wilson was widely seen as arrogant and too idealistic. Clemenceau mocked him: <strong>“Mr. Wilson bores me with his Fourteen Points; why, God Almighty has only Ten!”</strong> Wilson thought the USA had “won” the war and took the moral high ground, but he would not listen to others.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">His biggest blind spot was <strong>self-determination</strong>. Eastern Europe’s ethnic groups were hopelessly mixed together — for example, Austria-Hungary alone had dozens of nationalities living side by side. Drawing neat borders along ethnic lines was impossible. Many Germans ended up living outside Germany, storing up problems for the future. Britain and France also had huge empires with many colonies, so self-determination threatened their own power too.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Reception of the Fourteen Points</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">When Wilson published his Fourteen Points in <strong>January 1918</strong>, Germany ignored them and kept fighting. But after defeat in <strong>November 1918</strong>, Germany accepted the <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement to stop fighting.">Armistice</dfn> expecting a fair peace based on those same points. The other leaders, however, had serious objections.</p>

</div>
<div class="timeline-event">

<p>Germany surrenders expecting fair treatment based on the Fourteen Points.</p>
</div>

<div class="timeline-date">January 1919</div>

<p>32 nations meet at Versailles. Clemenceau and Lloyd George challenge many of Wilson’s points. Germany is excluded.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">June 1919</div>
<h4>Treaty of Versailles signed</h4>
<p>The final treaty is far harsher than Germany expected. Many of Wilson’s ideals are watered down or dropped.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Lloyd George rejected freedom of the seas because it threatened British naval power. Both he and Clemenceau knew self-determination threatened their own empires. Clemenceau saw the points as far too soft given the damage France had suffered.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26">Wilson arrived in Paris as a hero, but his position quickly weakened. Clemenceau was set on punishing Germany. Lloyd George needed to balance public demands for punishment with keeping trade alive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Wilson gave up many of his ideals to secure the one thing he cared about most: the League of Nations. Ironically, the US Senate later refused to <dfn class="term" data-def="To formally approve or confirm a treaty or agreement.">ratify</dfn> the treaty, so America never joined the League.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The Big Three each had different aims shaped by their own country’s experience of the war. Wilson’s Fourteen Points raised hopes of a fair peace, but Clemenceau and Lloyd George had other priorities. The result was a compromise that left nobody satisfied.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n32">
<li><strong>Every clause was a compromise:</strong> Clemenceau wanted revenge, Wilson wanted fairness, and Lloyd George wanted trade — each had to give ground, leaving all three unhappy.</li>
<li><strong>Germany expected a fair peace but got a harsh one:</strong> Germany accepted the Armistice based on the Fourteen Points. The final treaty was far tougher, creating a lasting sense of betrayal.</li>
<li><strong>Self-determination caused as many problems as it solved:</strong> Eastern Europe’s mixed ethnic groups made neat borders impossible, leaving millions of Germans outside Germany and storing up future conflict.</li>
</ul>
