--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Growing Optimism in the Late 1920s</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The mid-to-late 1920s saw growing optimism about peace. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1924 plan that restructured Germany’s reparations payments and provided US loans to stabilise the German economy.">Dawes Plan</dfn> (1924) had stabilised Germany’s economy by providing US loans, ending the hyperinflation crisis. But it made Germany entirely dependent on American money — a weakness that would prove disastrous when the Depression hit. The biggest agreements of this period were made <strong>outside</strong> the League of Nations. The great powers trusted treaties between themselves more than they trusted the League’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that peace is maintained by countries acting together to prevent aggression.">collective security</dfn>.</p>
<!-- LOCARNO TREATIES -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Locarno Treaties (1925)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of agreements signed in October 1925 at Locarno, Switzerland.">Locarno Treaties</dfn> were signed in October 1925 in Switzerland. For the first time since the war, Germany was <strong>voluntarily</strong> entering agreements rather than having terms forced on it.</p>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Comparison of three 1920s peace agreements — Locarno Treaties (1925), Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928), and the Disarmament Conference (1932) — showing how all major diplomacy happened outside the League of Nations and lacked enforcement." src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_1920s_agreements.jpg"/>
</figure>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The treaties created the “Spirit of Locarno” — a period of real warmth between former enemies. As a direct result, <strong>Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">But these agreements were made <strong>outside the League</strong>. This showed that the great powers trusted direct deals more than collective security. Germany’s refusal to guarantee its eastern borders hinted at future ambitions that would eventually shatter the peace.</p>
<!-- KELLOGG-BRIAND PACT -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An international agreement signed in August 1928 in which 62 nations, including Germany, France, and the USA, promised not to use war as a means of settling international disputes.">Kellogg-Briand Pact</dfn> was signed in August 1928 — the peak of 1920s optimism. It was co-authored by US Secretary of State Frank Kellogg and French Foreign Minister Aristide Briand.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 nations that signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Kellogg-Briand Pact</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li>Signed by <strong>62 nations</strong>, including Germany, France, and — importantly — the <strong>USA</strong>.</li>
<li>All signatories promised <strong>not to use war</strong> to settle disputes.</li>
<li><strong>Fatal flaw:</strong> there was <strong>no enforcement mechanism</strong>. If a country broke the pact, nothing would happen.</li>
<li>It did not cover wars of <strong>self-defence</strong> — a loophole so big that any aggressor could claim they were defending themselves.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <strong>USA</strong> — not even a League member — signed this pact. The most important diplomacy was happening <strong>outside Geneva</strong>, not within the League. If even peace agreements bypassed the League, what purpose did it serve?</p>
<!-- DISARMAMENT CONFERENCE -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Disarmament Conference (1932–1934)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">One of the League’s main aims was <dfn class="term" data-def="The reduction of a country’s military forces and weapons.">disarmament</dfn>. Germany had been forced to disarm by the Treaty of Versailles. Other countries were supposed to follow — but none did. Britain wanted to cut spending but still had a huge empire to protect. France refused to reduce its weapons because it feared Germany.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In 1932, the League organised a <strong>World Disarmament Conference</strong> in Geneva. It was meant to be the next big step towards peace. Instead, it became a disaster.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what year did Germany leave the League of Nations after the conference failed?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Disarmament Conference</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n22">
<li>Hitler pointed out that Germany was the <strong>only country</strong> that had actually disarmed — everyone else had broken their promise.</li>
<li>He demanded France <strong>reduce its army to Germany’s size</strong>. France refused.</li>
<li>Germany <strong>walked out</strong> of the conference — and then <strong>left the League of Nations</strong> (October 1933).</li>
<li>Behind the scenes, <strong>Hitler was secretly rearming Germany</strong> the whole time.</li>
<li>The conference collapsed with <strong>no agreement</strong>. France began to rearm and build border defences.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The failure of the Disarmament Conference was a turning point. The League had failed in one of its core missions. Countries now began rearming instead of disarming, and the optimism of the 1920s was well and truly over.</p>

<h4>Kellogg-Briand Pact signed</h4>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The 1920s agreements looked hopeful, but they all bypassed the League and lacked enforcement. When the Disarmament Conference collapsed and Germany walked out, the era of optimism was over.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n23">
<li><strong>Diplomacy bypassed the League:</strong> Locarno and Kellogg-Briand were negotiated outside the League, proving powerful nations did not trust collective security.</li>
<li><strong>Germany’s eastern borders were left unguaranteed:</strong> Locarno secured western borders, but Germany refused to guarantee its borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia — a warning sign for the late 1930s.</li>
<li><strong>The Disarmament Conference failed:</strong> The League could not deliver on one of its core aims. Hitler used the conference to claim victimhood, then left both the conference and the League while secretly rearming.</li>
</ul>
