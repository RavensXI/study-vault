--- DESCRIPTION ---
War guilt, reparations, territorial losses, military restrictions — the key terms imposed on Germany in 1919.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Treaty of Versailles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In <strong>June 1919</strong>, Germany was invited to the Palace of Versailles outside Paris. Its representatives were told to sign the <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace treaty signed on 28 June 1919 at the Palace of Versailles that officially ended World War One between Germany and the Allied Powers.">Treaty of Versailles</dfn> or face invasion. They had no choice. Germany had been excluded from all negotiations — the terms were decided by the Big Three and handed over as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A German term meaning 'dictated peace.' Germans used this word to describe the Treaty of Versailles because they had no say in the negotiations and were forced to accept the terms under threat of invasion.">Diktat</dfn>, a dictated peace with no discussion allowed.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the four areas of punishment in the Versailles Treaty: Military, Territorial, Economic, and which Article?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Treaty Categories</div>
<p>The Treaty punished Germany across four main areas:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n31">
<li><strong>Military Terms</strong> — severe restrictions on armed forces</li>
<li><strong>Territorial Terms</strong> — loss of land in Europe and overseas</li>
<li><strong>Economic Terms</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="Financial compensation demanded from Germany by the Allied Powers for the damage caused during World War One.">reparations</dfn> and resource extraction</li>
<li><strong>Article 231</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to accept sole responsibility for causing World War One.">War Guilt Clause</dfn></li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Military Terms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Treaty stripped Germany’s military power. For a country where the army was a source of huge national pride and respect, these terms were deeply humiliating.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Military restrictions in detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">The army was cut to just <strong>100,000 men</strong> with no <dfn class="term" data-def="Compulsory enlistment of citizens into military service.">conscription</dfn>. Germany was banned from having an <strong>air force</strong>, <strong>tanks</strong>, or <strong>submarines</strong>. The navy was limited to <strong>6 battleships</strong> and <strong>15,000 sailors</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A zone where no military forces or fortifications are permitted.">Rhineland</dfn> — the area on the French border — was demilitarised and occupied by French troops. No German soldiers were allowed there, removing any chance of a surprise attack on France.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An international organisation proposed by Wilson to settle disputes between countries peacefully and prevent future wars.">League of Nations</dfn> was set up to prevent future wars, but it had no army of its own.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Territorial Terms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Germany lost <strong>10% of its land</strong> and <strong>12% of its population</strong>, along with all overseas colonies. These losses stripped the country of key industrial regions, farmland, and millions of German-speaking citizens.</p>
<figure class="diagram" data-narration-id="n11">
<img alt="Diagram showing Germany's territorial losses under the Treaty of Versailles, including Alsace-Lorraine to France, Posen and West Prussia to Poland, and the Polish Corridor separating East Prussia from the rest of Germany" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_territorial_losses.jpg"/>
</figure>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Territorial losses in detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Alsace-Lorraine</strong> was returned to France, reversing the German takeover from 1871. The <strong>Saar coalfields</strong> were handed to France under League control for 15 years, stripping Germany of vital resources.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>West Prussia, Upper Silesia, and Posen</strong> were given to Poland. The <strong>Polish Corridor</strong> — a strip of land giving Poland access to the sea at <strong>Danzig</strong> (a German city) — split Germany in two, cutting off East Prussia from the rest of the country.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Germany lost <strong>26% of its coal</strong> and <strong>half of its iron and steel</strong> resources. Eight new countries were created across Eastern Europe through the wider peace settlement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">All overseas <strong>colonies</strong> were taken and given to Allied powers as <dfn class="term" data-def="Territories taken from defeated nations and administered by Allied powers on behalf of the League of Nations.">mandates</dfn> under the League of Nations — in practice, run by Britain and France.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The political union of Austria and Germany.">Anschluss</dfn> — union with German-speaking Austria — was banned. Germans saw this as a clear denial of self-determination.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Economic Terms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The economic penalties aimed to weaken Germany and compensate the Allies for war damage. Combined with the loss of land, they crippled Germany’s ability to recover.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Economic terms in detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">A commission set the final <dfn class="term" data-def="Financial compensation demanded from Germany by the Allied Powers for the damage caused during World War One.">reparations</dfn> bill at <strong>£6.6 billion</strong> in 1921. France would receive <strong>50%</strong> (they had wanted 70%), Britain <strong>30%</strong>, and the rest shared among smaller allies. The bill was so huge it was estimated to take <strong>until 1988</strong> to pay off.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Reparations equalled about <strong>8% of Germany’s national income</strong>. Crucially, the exact amount was not revealed until two years after signing — Germany had agreed to pay without knowing how much.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Germany also had to make forced <strong>coal deliveries</strong> to France, Belgium, and Italy, further draining resources it needed for its own recovery.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Article 231: The War Guilt Clause</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The most hated part of the Treaty was <strong>Article 231</strong> — the <dfn class="term" data-def="Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to accept sole responsibility for causing World War One.">War Guilt Clause</dfn>. It forced Germany to accept <strong>total blame</strong> for starting the war.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="From memory, what key legal justification did Article 231, the War Guilt Clause, provide for the Allies' punishments?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Article 231</div>
<p>The War Guilt Clause had a key legal purpose: by saying Germany alone started the war, the Allies could justify every other punishment — reparations, disarmament, and land losses. Without Article 231, they had no formal basis for such severe terms.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Germans were furious. Many did not believe they were solely to blame — Austria-Hungary had declared war on Serbia, and Russia had been the first to mobilise troops. German propaganda had told the public they were fighting a defensive war. The clause felt like a lie imposed by the victors, and it fuelled lasting resentment that nationalist politicians would exploit for years.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n26">How the Terms Connected</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">The Treaty’s punishments were not separate — they reinforced each other, making Germany’s situation far worse than any single term alone.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the industrial regions Germany lost, the % of coal lost, and the total reparations amount.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Interconnected Terms</div>
<p>Losing industrial regions like the <strong>Saar</strong> and <strong>Upper Silesia</strong> (26% of coal, half of iron and steel) directly made it harder to pay £6.6 billion in reparations. Being forced to deliver coal to France, Belgium, and Italy on top of this created an impossible situation. Military restrictions meant Germany could not resist by force. The Treaty was a compromise — none of the Big Three got everything they wanted — but for Germany, it was crushing.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The Treaty dismantled German power through military, territorial, and economic penalties, all justified by Article 231. Germany was excluded from negotiations and forced to sign under threat of invasion, creating deep resentment that shaped European politics for decades.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n32">
<li><strong>Germany was attacked on every front:</strong> 100,000-man army, 10% of land lost, £6.6 billion in reparations, and forced to accept sole war guilt — the penalties left Germany weakened, humiliated, and angry.</li>
<li><strong>Article 231 was the foundation:</strong> The War Guilt Clause legally justified every other punishment. Without it, the Allies had no formal basis for reparations or disarmament.</li>
<li><strong>The terms reinforced each other:</strong> Losing industrial land made reparations harder to pay, coal deliveries drained what was left, and military cuts prevented resistance — each term made the others worse.</li>
</ul>
