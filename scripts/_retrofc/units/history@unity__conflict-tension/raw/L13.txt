--- DESCRIPTION ---
The occupation of Czechoslovakia, Britain’s guarantee to Poland, and the shock Hitler-Stalin agreement of August 1939.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The End of Appeasement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chamberlain’s promise of “peace for our time” lasted less than six months. In March 1939, Hitler invaded the rest of Czechoslovakia, tearing up the <dfn class="term" data-def="The agreement signed in September 1938 between Britain, France, Germany, and Italy that allowed Hitler to annex the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia in exchange for a promise of no further territorial expansion.">Munich Agreement</dfn>. <dfn class="term" data-def="The policy pursued by Britain and France in the 1930s of making concessions to aggressive powers (especially Nazi Germany) in the hope of avoiding war.">Appeasement</dfn> was dead.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what region's German speakers gave Hitler an excuse to invade Czechoslovakia.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Sudetenland had a German-speaking majority, so Hitler could claim self-determination. The rest of Czechoslovakia was <strong>not</strong> German. This invasion proved Hitler wanted <strong>empire</strong>, not just unity for German speakers.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Britain and France responded with a <strong>guarantee to Poland</strong> — if Hitler attacked, they would declare war. The era of giving in was over. The question was no longer <em>whether</em> war would come, but <em>when</em>.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Stalin’s Dilemma</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Soviet leader <strong>Joseph Stalin</strong> was suspicious of both sides. He had alliance offers from the Western democracies and from Nazi Germany, but strong reasons to distrust each.</p>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Stalin's dilemma in 1939 — two options compared side by side: ally with Britain and France (pros: collective defence; cons: excluded from Munich, weak Drax mission, unreliable guarantee) versus sign pact with Germany (pros: time to rebuild army, gain territory; cons: Germany's stated goal was to destroy communism, deal was temporary)" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_stalin_dilemma.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Nazi-Soviet Pact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">On <strong>23 August 1939</strong>, the world was stunned. Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union — sworn ideological enemies — signed a <dfn class="term" data-def="A non-aggression agreement signed on 23 August 1939 between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, also known as the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact.">Nazi-Soviet Pact</dfn>. Also called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The alternative name for the Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939, named after the Soviet Foreign Minister Vyacheslav Molotov and German Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop who signed the agreement.">Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact</dfn>, it was a non-aggression deal with devastating secret clauses. Nazi Foreign Minister <strong>Joachim von Ribbentrop</strong> flew to Moscow to meet Stalin and Soviet Foreign Minister <strong>Molotov</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the countries Stalin gained influence over in the secret Pact with Hitler.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Secret Protocol</div>
<p>The Pact included a <strong>secret clause</strong> agreeing to <strong>divide Poland</strong> between Germany and the USSR. It also gave Stalin a <strong>sphere of influence</strong> over the Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania) and parts of Finland — a buffer zone against future German attack.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Why Hitler Signed</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The Pact was a tactical masterstroke for Hitler. It gave him a <strong>green light to invade Poland</strong> without Soviet attack from the east. Germany could avoid a <dfn class="term" data-def="A military situation in which a country must fight enemies on two separate geographical fronts simultaneously.">two-front war</dfn> — the nightmare that had destroyed Germany in World War One. It also stopped Britain and France from forming an alliance with the USSR that could surround Germany. On top of this, the USSR promised <strong>one million tons of food, oil and raw materials</strong> plus electric equipment and trains — vital resources for the coming war.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Why Stalin Signed</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Stalin’s decision was logical, if cynical. The Red Army was badly weakened — his <dfn class="term" data-def="Stalin's campaign of political repression in the 1930s, in which hundreds of thousands were executed or imprisoned.">purges</dfn> had executed <strong>81 out of 103 generals</strong>, leaving the military poorly led. The Pact gave him <strong>time to rearm</strong>. He used this time to move factories deeper into Russia, behind the <strong>Ural Mountains</strong>, preparing for when Hitler eventually betrayed him. He also gained <strong>territory</strong> — eastern Poland and influence over the Baltic states, creating a buffer zone. In return, Stalin sent Germany <strong>30 warplanes</strong> and samples of other weapons.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why Stalin distrusted promises to Poland, leading him to sign the Nazi-Soviet Pact.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Stalin’s Calculation</div>
<p>Stalin had been alienated at Munich. He did not trust the guarantee to Poland. Signing with Hitler was not an endorsement of Nazism — it was a <strong>survival strategy</strong>: gain time, gain territory, and let the capitalist nations fight each other first.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Timeline: The Road to War</h2>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n21">

<p>German troops march into Prague. The Munich Agreement is torn up. Hitler’s aims clearly go beyond uniting German speakers.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">March 1939</div>

<p>They pledge to defend Poland if Hitler attacks. Appeasement is officially over.</p>
</div>

<h4>Diplomatic negotiations intensify</h4>
<p>Both sides approach Stalin. Britain sends Drax by slow boat with no power to agree anything. Stalin sees this as proof they are not serious.</p>
</div>

<h4>Nazi-Soviet Pact signed</h4>
<p>Molotov and Ribbentrop sign in Moscow. The secret protocol divides Poland and Eastern Europe. The last barrier to invasion is removed.</p>
</div>

<h4>Germany invades Poland from the west</h4>
<p>With his eastern flank secured, Hitler launches the invasion. On <strong>17 September</strong>, the USSR attacks from the east. War begins.</p>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The invasion of Czechoslovakia killed appeasement and led to the guarantee to Poland. The Nazi-Soviet Pact removed Soviet opposition and gave Hitler the confidence to invade, making war almost certain.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li><strong>Appeasement died in Prague:</strong> Hitler’s March 1939 invasion proved his aims went beyond self-determination. Britain and France finally committed to resisting him with the guarantee to Poland.</li>
<li><strong>The Pact was a cynical bargain:</strong> Hitler avoided a two-front war and gained resources. Stalin gained time to rearm, territory, and a buffer zone. Both knew it was temporary.</li>
<li><strong>Western failures pushed Stalin toward Hitler:</strong> Exclusion from Munich, the weak Drax mission, and distrust of the Poland guarantee convinced Stalin that signing with Germany was his best option — sealing Poland’s fate.</li>
</ul>

Do not just call it “evil” — strong answers explain the <em>reasoning</em> behind decisions.</p>
