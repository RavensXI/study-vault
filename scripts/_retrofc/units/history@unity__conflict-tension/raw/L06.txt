<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mixed Fortunes for the League</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The 1920s were a mixed decade for the League. It settled some disputes and did great humanitarian work. But it also exposed serious weaknesses — especially when powerful nations were involved.</p>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="League of Nations scorecard — successes with small nations (Aaland Islands, Upper Silesia, Bulgaria) versus failures with powerful nations (Vilna, Corfu). Pattern: the League only worked against small nations." src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/conflict-tension/diagram_league_scorecard.jpg"/>
</figure>
<!-- SUCCESSES -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The League’s Successes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The League resolved several territorial disputes in the early 1920s. This showed <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that peace is maintained by countries acting together to prevent aggression.">collective security</dfn> could work — at least when the nations involved were small enough to accept the League’s rulings.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Åland Islands (1921) — Sweden vs. Finland</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Åland Islands sit in the Baltic Sea between Sweden and Finland. Both claimed the islands, and the dispute threatened to turn violent.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The League ruled in favour of <strong>Finland</strong>. Sweden was disappointed but <strong>accepted the decision</strong>. A genuine success — the League resolved a dispute without a shot being fired.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Upper Silesia (1921) — Germany vs. Poland</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">Upper Silesia was a valuable industrial region on the Germany–Poland border. Both nations claimed it, and the Treaty of Versailles had not settled the issue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The League surveyed the area and organised a <dfn class="term" data-def="A direct vote by the people of a region to decide an important political question, such as which country they wish to belong to.">plebiscite</dfn> (public vote). The result was close, so the League <strong>split the region</strong> based on how each area voted — making sure both sides kept good rail links and water supplies. Germany was unhappy but accepted the outcome. Another success.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Bulgaria (1925) — Greek Invasion</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">In 1925, Greek soldiers were killed in a border clash with Bulgaria. Greece responded by <strong>invading Bulgarian territory</strong>. Bulgaria asked the League for help.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The League acted fast: it <strong>condemned Greece</strong> and ordered a withdrawal. Greece obeyed. On the surface, another success.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">But Greece bitterly complained of <strong>double standards</strong>. Just two years earlier, Italy had invaded the Greek island of Corfu in very similar circumstances — and the League did nothing to stop Italy. The League was only tough on weaker nations. This complaint would prove prophetic.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<!-- HUMANITARIAN WORK -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Humanitarian Work</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Beyond settling disputes, the League did hugely valuable humanitarian work through its specialist commissions. This is often overlooked but represents some of the League’s greatest achievements.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover, what were the ILO's actions, the number of prisoners returned, and the diseases the League fought?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Humanitarian Achievements</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n27">
<li><strong>Refugee Commission:</strong> Returned about <strong>400,000 prisoners of war</strong> to their home countries after World War One.</li>
<li><strong>Health Organisation:</strong> Led global campaigns against <strong>malaria</strong> and <strong>leprosy</strong>, saving countless lives.</li>
<li><strong>International Labour Organisation (ILO):</strong> Improved working conditions worldwide, including banning <strong>lead paint</strong> and limiting <strong>child labour</strong>. In 1930 it helped Greece set up social insurance for the unemployed. However, its 1919 attempt to ban children under 14 from working was <strong>ignored by most members</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Slavery Commission:</strong> Freed about <strong>200,000 slaves</strong> in Sierra Leone and fought forced labour across Africa and the Middle East.</li>
<li><strong>Opium Board:</strong> Worked to reduce the drug trade worldwide.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<!-- FAILURES -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The League’s Failures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The League’s failures in the 1920s were fewer than its successes, but far more serious. Each involved a powerful nation defying the League — exposing the fatal weakness at the heart of collective security.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Vilna (1920) — Poland Seizes Lithuania’s Capital</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">Vilna (modern-day Vilnius) was Lithuania’s historic capital. It had a large Polish-speaking population, and in 1920 a Polish army seized it by force.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Lithuania appealed to the League. The League <strong>condemned Poland</strong> and ordered a withdrawal — but Poland refused. <strong>France saw Poland as a key ally against Germany</strong> and would not take real action. Without French support, the League was powerless.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A major failure. The League made a ruling, a member ignored it, and nothing happened. It showed the League’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Penalties imposed on a country to force it to obey international law.">sanctions</dfn> and moral authority meant nothing if powerful members would not enforce them.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Corfu (1923) — Mussolini Defies the League</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21">In August 1923, an Italian general and four of his team were <strong>murdered</strong> while surveying the Greek–Albanian border for the League. Italy’s leader, <strong>Mussolini</strong>, blamed Greece and demanded heavy compensation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">When Greece refused, Mussolini <strong>bombarded and occupied the Greek island of Corfu</strong>. Greece appealed to the League. The League condemned Italy, but Mussolini <strong>refused to accept the League’s authority</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The dispute was handed to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A body of senior diplomats from the major Allied powers (Britain, France, Italy, Japan) that existed alongside the League of Nations.">Conference of Ambassadors</dfn> — a powerful group of countries (Britain, France, Italy, Japan) that met separately from the League. It <strong>sided with Italy</strong>, forcing Greece to apologise and pay up. Mussolini in particular used this body to bully the League into doing what he wanted.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">A disastrous failure. It proved <strong>powerful nations could ignore the League</strong> and get their way through intimidation. The Conference of Ambassadors undermined the League’s authority — it showed the League was not the all-powerful body it was meant to be.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The 1920s showed the League could do good work with humanitarian projects and smaller disputes. But Vilna and Corfu exposed cracks that would widen badly in the 1930s.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n28">
<li><strong>Success with small nations, failure with powerful ones:</strong> The League settled disputes between weaker countries (Aaland Islands, Upper Silesia, Bulgaria). But when major powers like Italy or France were involved, no one enforced the rules against them.</li>
<li><strong>Valuable humanitarian work:</strong> Commissions returned 400,000 POWs, fought diseases, freed 200,000 slaves in Sierra Leone, and improved working conditions — though some reforms (like the ILO’s child labour ban) were ignored by members.</li>
<li><strong>Corfu set a dangerous precedent:</strong> Mussolini defied the League and the Conference of Ambassadors sided with him. This sent a clear signal: aggressive nations could bully their way to victory. Japan and Italy would exploit this lesson in the 1930s.</li>
</ul>
