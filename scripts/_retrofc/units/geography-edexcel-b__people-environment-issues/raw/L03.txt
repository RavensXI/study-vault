--- DESCRIPTION ---
How rainforests function, why they are threatened by deforestation and indirect climate change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Are Tropical Rainforests Special?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tropical rainforests cover only about 6% of the Earth’s surface but contain more than half of all known land species. They sit in a belt around the equator — the Amazon, the Congo and parts of Southeast Asia — where temperatures stay between 25°C and 30°C and rainfall exceeds 2,000 mm a year. Constant warmth and moisture create the most productive ecosystem on Earth: lush, layered, and humming with life. The enquiry for this lesson is what allows the rainforest to function so spectacularly — and why those same systems are so easily disrupted.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Layered Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A rainforest works in vertical stories. The <strong>emergent layer</strong> rises 40–55 metres above the ground — tall hardwoods like the kapok tree poke through the canopy, exposed to the most sun and wind. The <strong>canopy</strong> at 30–45 metres forms a dense roof that captures most of the light and is home to perhaps 70% of rainforest species. Below that is the cooler, darker <strong>understorey</strong> of saplings and shrubs, and a thin <strong>forest floor</strong> where leaf litter is broken down within weeks by termites, fungi and bacteria.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch the four rainforest layers, labelling the height range and one species or feature for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rainforests have four vertical layers — emergent (40–55 m), canopy (30–45 m), understorey, and forest floor. Most species and most photosynthesis happen in the canopy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Plant and Animal Adaptations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Plants and animals are finely adapted to the climate. <strong>Drip-tip leaves</strong> shed heavy rain quickly to stop fungus growing on the leaf surface. <strong>Buttress roots</strong> — huge above-ground supports that flare out from the trunk — stabilise tall trees in shallow soil. <strong>Lianas</strong> climb other trees to reach the sunlight. Many animals are arboreal — sloths, spider monkeys, toucans — with adaptations like prehensile tails, strong limbs and bright colours that help them communicate or camouflage among the leaves. Frogs lay their eggs in pools of rainwater trapped in bromeliads — tiny ecosystems within the canopy.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The rainforest’s nutrient store is held mostly in the living trees, not the soil. Leaves, fruits and dead animals fall to the floor and are decomposed within weeks by warm, moist air full of fungi and insects. Nutrients are taken up almost immediately by shallow roots and recycled into new growth. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous movement of nutrients between living plants, animals, leaf litter and soil within an ecosystem.">nutrient cycle</dfn> here is fast and tight; remove the trees and the system collapses, because the soil itself holds little. This is why deforested land becomes infertile within a few years.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">A Complex Food Web</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Energy flows through a rainforest in a dense <dfn class="term" data-def="A network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem showing how energy moves from producers to consumers.">food web</dfn>. Producers (trees, lianas, ferns) capture solar energy through photosynthesis. Primary consumers eat them — insects, sloths, monkeys. Secondary consumers like jaguars, anacondas and harpy eagles eat the herbivores. Decomposers break everything down. The web is dense because there are so many species: lose one and others can usually take its place. But lose a keystone species — like a top predator or a fig tree that feeds dozens of birds — and a chain reaction can ripple through the whole system.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain in two sentences why rainforests have rapid nutrient cycling and what happens when trees are removed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rainforests have <strong>high biodiversity, fast nutrient cycling and complex food webs</strong>. Most nutrients are stored in the living trees, not the soil — making cleared land surprisingly poor for long-term farming.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Direct Threats: Deforestation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The clearing of forest by humans for agriculture, logging, mining or settlement, usually permanent.">Deforestation</dfn> is the biggest direct threat. The Amazon has lost around 20% of its area since 1970. Six causes drive most of the clearance. <strong>Commercial agriculture</strong> — cattle ranching in Brazil, palm oil plantations in Indonesia, soybean fields for animal feed — takes the largest single share. <strong>Subsistence farming</strong> using slash-and-burn methods clears smaller patches that often degrade after a few harvests. <strong>Hardwood logging</strong> for mahogany and teak removes the largest trees. <strong>Fuel wood</strong> collection by local people thins the forest. <strong>Mining</strong> for iron ore, bauxite and gold (in places like Carajás and Yanomami territory) strips and pollutes large areas. <strong>HEP dams</strong>, like the Belo Monte project in Brazil, flood huge tracts to generate electricity, displacing communities and clearing more access roads.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Indirect Threats: Climate Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Climate change is an indirect but rising threat. Hotter, drier conditions cause <strong>ecosystem stress</strong>: trees shed leaves, growth slows, and pests and diseases spread further. The Amazon experienced unusual droughts in 2005, 2010 and 2015–16 which caused tree die-back and triggered enormous wildfires — ironic, because rainforests are normally too damp to burn. There is a tipping point: scientists warn that if 25% of the Amazon is lost, the whole forest could shift from absorbing carbon dioxide to releasing it, accelerating climate change globally. Around 17–20% has already been lost.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Tropical rainforests are sometimes called the ‘lungs of the planet’. They store an estimated 200 billion tonnes of carbon, regulate the global hydrological cycle by releasing water vapour, and house species that supply 25% of modern medicines (including treatments derived from the rosy periwinkle for childhood leukaemia). Their loss damages indigenous people directly — tribes such as the Yanomami face threats from miners and disease — but also affects everyone through climate, weather and food prices.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Rainforests have a four-layer structure (emergent, canopy, understorey, floor) and adaptations like buttress roots and drip-tip leaves help plants and animals thrive in hot, wet conditions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Nutrients are stored mainly in the living trees, not the soil. Fast nutrient cycling and dense food webs explain rainforests’ high biodiversity — and why cleared land becomes infertile.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Direct threats (commercial farming, logging, mining, HEP) and indirect threats (drought and wildfires from climate change) have removed nearly a fifth of the Amazon, edging closer to a tipping point.</li>
</ul>
