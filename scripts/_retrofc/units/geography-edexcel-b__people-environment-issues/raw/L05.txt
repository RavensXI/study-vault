<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Can Forests Be Protected?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tropical rainforests and the taiga both face heavy pressure, but they are not lost causes. Across the world, governments, NGOs and indigenous communities are experimenting with ways to save what is left while still allowing people to make a living. The question this lesson explores is whether global agreements and sustainable management actually work — or whether they only slow the loss.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Global Action: CITES</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Signed in 1973 and now ratified by 184 countries, <dfn class="term" data-def="The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, an international agreement controlling trade in threatened plants and animals.">CITES</dfn> regulates the international trade in endangered plant and animal species. Mahogany, rosewood and ramin — valuable rainforest hardwoods — are listed, meaning exports require special permits. Animal trade controls cover orangutans, jaguars and parrots. CITES has reduced trade in some species (the global ivory ban cut elephant poaching sharply in some countries) but enforcement is patchy — illegal logging and wildlife smuggling remain billion-pound criminal industries. CITES works best when wealthy importing countries also commit to inspections at customs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Global Action: REDD</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The UN’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation, a UN scheme that pays developing countries to keep forests standing.">REDD</dfn> programme — Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation — pays developing countries to keep forests standing rather than cutting them down. Norway has pledged hundreds of millions of dollars to Brazil and Indonesia under REDD-style agreements. The logic is straightforward: forests store carbon, so paying for that storage is cheaper than building expensive carbon-capture technology. Critics argue REDD can be undermined by weak monitoring (some payments have gone to areas that were never threatened) and by political change — Brazil’s deforestation rates jumped under President Bolsonaro from 2019, despite earlier REDD progress.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences how CITES and REDD differ in what they protect and how they work.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>CITES</strong> regulates trade in endangered species. <strong>REDD</strong> pays developing countries to keep forests standing for carbon storage. CITES uses laws and bans; REDD uses cash incentives.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Why Some Areas Are Improving and Others Are Not</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Deforestation rates are not the same everywhere. <strong>Costa Rica</strong> has reversed forest loss: payments to landowners for environmental services and the growth of ecotourism mean tree cover has risen from 26% in the 1980s to over 50% today. <strong>Indonesia</strong> has slowed but not reversed loss after a 2019 moratorium on new palm-oil licences. <strong>Brazil</strong> cut Amazon deforestation by roughly 80% between 2004 and 2012 through enforcement, satellite monitoring and the Soy Moratorium — an agreement among soy traders not to buy from newly cleared land. But progress can be reversed by politics: 2019–2022 saw a sharp rise again before tougher rules returned in 2023.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">In 2006, after pressure from Greenpeace and supermarket chains, the largest grain traders agreed not to buy soya beans from any land in the Brazilian Amazon cleared after a fixed cut-off date. Satellite monitoring and farm registration made it work. By 2014, soy expansion accounted for less than 1% of new Amazon deforestation, down from over 30%. The lesson: when consumer demand, traders and government enforcement align, a market-led ban can work fast.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sustainable Forest Management</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Even forests that are used commercially can be managed sustainably. <dfn class="term" data-def="Cutting only specific mature trees while leaving the surrounding forest standing, allowing regeneration.">Selective logging</dfn> takes only mature trees of certain species, leaving the rest of the canopy intact. <dfn class="term" data-def="Replanting trees after harvesting to maintain or restore forest cover.">Replanting</dfn> with native species (rather than monoculture plantations) restores ecological function. The <strong>Forest Stewardship Council (FSC)</strong> certifies wood products from forests managed to strict environmental and social standards — FSC labels appear on garden furniture, paper and books in UK shops, telling consumers their purchase has not driven deforestation. In the taiga, FSC has helped persuade Swedish and Canadian companies to spare old-growth forest and leave wildlife corridors between cuts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Alternative Livelihoods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Long-term protection only works if local people can earn a living from a standing forest. <dfn class="term" data-def="Tourism that focuses on undisturbed natural areas, designed to be low-impact and to fund conservation and local livelihoods.">Ecotourism</dfn> takes visitors into rainforest reserves with trained local guides; Costa Rica’s Monteverde Cloud Forest reserve generates millions of dollars a year and employs hundreds of people. <strong>Sustainable farming</strong> on the forest edge — agroforestry, shade-grown coffee, smallholder cacao — gives families an income without further clearance. Indigenous land rights matter too: studies show forests under indigenous management have lower deforestation rates than even most national parks. The Yanomami in Brazil and the Sami in northern Sweden are pushing for stronger legal recognition for this reason.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two examples of sustainable forest management and two alternative livelihoods that protect forests.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sustainable management means selective logging, replanting and FSC certification. Alternative livelihoods — ecotourism, agroforestry, shade-grown coffee — let people earn from forests without destroying them.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What About the Taiga?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The taiga is harder to protect because it is so vast and so remote. National parks like Wood Buffalo (Canada) and the protected wilderness of northern Sweden conserve large blocks of old-growth boreal forest. Sustainable forestry — longer rotation cycles of 80–120 years, leaving wildlife corridors and old standing trees — is increasingly required in Sweden and Finland. But conflicting views remain: indigenous communities want full protection, governments often back logging and oil revenue, and conservationists push for wilderness reserves. Compromises usually allow some commercial use while protecting the most ecologically valuable areas.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n17">No single tool will save the world’s forests. The combination that works — international agreements like CITES and REDD, market pressure from supermarkets, FSC labelling, satellite enforcement and local livelihoods — is messy but increasingly effective where it is taken seriously.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">CITES regulates trade in endangered species through bans and permits; REDD pays developing countries to keep forests standing for carbon storage. Both have helped but enforcement is uneven.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Sustainable management combines selective logging, replanting and FSC certification — allowing forests to be used without being destroyed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Alternative livelihoods like ecotourism (Costa Rica’s Monteverde) and agroforestry give people an income from standing forest. Indigenous-managed forests show lower clearance rates than many parks.</li>
</ul>
