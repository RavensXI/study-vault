--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to use sources, weigh costs and benefits and justify a decision on a global issue.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Different Kind of Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Edexcel B the people-and-environment unit paper ends with a 12-mark <dfn class="term" data-def="A long-answer exam question that asks the student to choose between proposed options for solving a real-world geographical issue, justifying the choice with evidence.">decision-making</dfn> essay. You are given a resources booklet — maps, graphs, statistics, photographs, quotes — about a geographical issue drawn from Topics 7–9. You must choose between three proposed solutions and justify your choice. There is no ‘correct’ answer; what counts is the quality of your reasoning. Top-band answers do four things: weigh costs and benefits, use evidence from the booklet, balance opposing views, and reach a clearly justified conclusion.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Reading the Resources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Spend 5–7 minutes reading the booklet before writing. Annotate it. For each resource, ask: <em>what does this tell me?</em> and <em>which option does it support?</em>. Look for <strong>quantitative data</strong> (graphs, percentages, costs, numbers of people affected) — concrete numbers strengthen arguments. Look for <strong>qualitative data</strong> (quotes, photographs) — these capture human and environmental impacts. Look for <strong>spatial information</strong> on maps — which areas are at risk, which gain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall four sources of evidence in a typical resources booklet: name them and one thing each tells you.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 12-mark decision essay rewards specific evidence from the booklet. Citing ‘Figure 3 shows that the protected area covers 1,200 km²’ scores; vague references to ‘the resources’ do not.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Weighing Options</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Each option in a decision essay has a mix of advantages and disadvantages, often analysed using <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of weighing the costs of an option against its benefits to help decide whether it is worthwhile.">cost-benefit analysis</dfn>. The best answers think across three dimensions: <strong>economic</strong> (cost, jobs, GDP impact, who pays), <strong>environmental</strong> (CO₂, biodiversity, pollution, land-use change) and <strong>social</strong> (people affected, equity, displacement, cultural value). A solution that wins on cost may lose on environment; a solution that protects the environment may displace people. Knowing this in advance helps you write a balanced answer instead of just picking one option and dismissing the others.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Stakeholders and Perspectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Different groups have different interests. <dfn class="term" data-def="People, communities or organisations affected by a decision and who therefore have an interest in its outcome.">Stakeholders</dfn> are people, organisations or communities affected by a decision. In a rainforest decision, stakeholders might include: Indigenous peoples (traditional rights and livelihood), small-scale farmers (need land), logging companies (profit), national governments (development goals), Indigenous-rights NGOs (advocacy), climate scientists (carbon stock), tourists (ecotourism revenue). Listing the stakeholders and what each wants prevents you from defaulting to a one-sided answer.</p>

Option 1 brings short-term jobs but long-term forest loss and CO₂ release. Option 2 protects biodiversity and carbon stocks but blocks economic development; risks illegal incursion if not enforced. Option 3 balances economic and environmental aims, gives Indigenous communities a stake, but is slower and harder to scale. A justified conclusion might pick option 3 with reasoning: ecotourism aligns Indigenous, environmental and economic interests — but only if backed by enforcement and infrastructure.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Structuring the Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A strong 12-mark decision essay has six paragraphs. Paragraph 1: state your decision and preview your reasons. Paragraph 2: option A — advantages with evidence. Paragraph 3: option A — disadvantages. Paragraphs 4–5: same treatment for option B and option C. Paragraph 6: justified conclusion that explicitly contrasts the chosen option with the alternatives. Aim for around 350–450 words. Reference at least three booklet sources by figure number or title (‘Figure 4 shows…’).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the three dimensions you should weigh in a decision essay (economic, environmental, social) and give one example consideration in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Top-band 12-mark answers (10–12 marks) explicitly compare the chosen option with the rejected ones. Saying ‘option B is best’ without showing why options A and C are weaker drops you to mid-band.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Common Pitfalls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Five common ways students lose marks: (1) <strong>summarising rather than evaluating</strong> — just describing the resources scores low. (2) <strong>One-sided answers</strong> that ignore costs of the chosen option. (3) <strong>No specific evidence</strong> — vague references to ‘the figures’ instead of named numbers. (4) <strong>No clear decision</strong> at the end — the question demands one. (5) <strong>Generic answers</strong> not rooted in the specific issue. Practising past papers across rainforest, energy and biosphere topics builds the muscle for choosing fast.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The 12-mark decision essay tests whether you can choose between options using booklet evidence, balanced reasoning and clear justification.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Strong answers weigh economic, environmental and social impacts; identify stakeholders and their views; cite specific evidence from the booklet by figure number.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">A six-paragraph structure (decision, three options weighed, conclusion) and explicit comparison between chosen and rejected options secures top-band marks.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For the 12-mark decision essay, plan in pencil for 3–5 minutes before writing. Bullet your reasoning across all three options before committing to your choice. The structure that scores highest: introduction with decision, weighed costs and benefits of all three options, justified conclusion explicitly comparing the chosen option to the others. Cite at least three booklet sources by figure or title. Avoid listing — develop chains of reasoning.</p>
