Why pressure on the biosphere is rising and how Malthus and Boserup explain population-resource links.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Is Demand on the Biosphere Rising?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every meal you eat, every glass of water you drink and every device you charge depends on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The thin layer of life on Earth where living organisms exist, including the soil, water and lower atmosphere.">biosphere</dfn>. As the world’s population grows and lifestyles change, the pressure on these natural systems is climbing fast. The enquiry running through this lesson is simple but urgent — can the biosphere keep providing food, water and energy for everyone, or will demand outstrip what the planet can renew?</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Four forces are driving demand upwards. First, population growth: from 2.5 billion in 1950, the world reached 8 billion in 2022 and is projected to peak near 10 billion around 2080. Second, rising affluence — as countries become wealthier, diets shift towards meat and dairy, which require far more land and water per calorie than grains. Third, urbanisation — cities now hold over 56% of people and use vast amounts of energy and water. Fourth, industrialisation — emerging economies like India and Indonesia are building factories that depend on fossil fuels and minerals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four drivers of rising demand on the biosphere with a one-line explanation for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Demand on the biosphere is rising due to population growth, rising affluence, urbanisation and industrialisation. Together they push food, water and energy use to record highs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Demand for Food</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates global food demand will be 60% higher in 2050 than it was in 2010. Two changes drive this. More mouths, of course — an extra two billion people. But also <dfn class="term" data-def="A shift towards diets richer in meat, dairy and processed foods, typical of countries growing wealthier.">dietary transition</dfn>: as China’s middle class expanded, average meat consumption rose from 13 kg per person in 1980 to over 60 kg today. Producing one kilogram of beef takes around 15,000 litres of water and far more land than a kilogram of wheat. So as wealth rises, so does the land and water bill of every meal.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Demand for Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Around 70% of all freshwater used by humans goes to agriculture — especially for crops like rice and cotton, and for the grain fed to livestock. Industry takes another 20%, and households the rest. Water demand is rising twice as fast as population because of meat-rich diets, more factories and longer showers in wealthier homes. The result is <dfn class="term" data-def="When demand for water in a region exceeds the amount available, even when rainfall is adequate.">water stress</dfn>: parts of northern China, the western United States, North Africa and South Asia already pump groundwater faster than rain can refill it.</p>

Soviet planners diverted its two feeder rivers to irrigate cotton fields. By 2014 the eastern lobe had dried up completely. Fishing communities collapsed, dust storms now carry pesticide-laden sand across the region, and infant mortality in nearby villages rose sharply. The Aral Sea is the textbook example of human demand outrunning what the biosphere can supply.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Demand for Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Global energy use has more than doubled since 1980 and the International Energy Agency expects another 25% rise by 2040. Most of that extra demand comes from emerging economies: China overtook the United States as the world’s largest energy consumer in 2009 and India is on track to do the same by 2030. Households want lighting, fridges, air conditioning and electric vehicles. Factories want electricity for steel, cement and chemicals. Currently about 80% of energy comes from fossil fuels, so rising demand also means rising carbon emissions and pressure on forests and oceans.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write the rough percentage shares: how much freshwater goes to farming, industry and homes?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Around 70% of freshwater is used by farming, 20% by industry and 10% by households. Meat-rich diets and industrialisation push agricultural and industrial water demand up the fastest.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Malthus — The Pessimist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Two opposing theories try to explain what happens when population meets resources. In 1798, the English clergyman Thomas <dfn class="term" data-def="An English economist who argued that population grows faster than food supply, leading to famine and crisis.">Malthus</dfn> argued that population grows geometrically (1, 2, 4, 8…) while food supply only grows arithmetically (1, 2, 3, 4…). Sooner or later population must outrun food, causing famine, disease or war — what he called ‘positive checks’. Modern Malthusians (or <strong>neo-Malthusians</strong>) point to droughts, fisheries collapse and rising food prices as evidence that the theory still applies. Critics argue Malthus was wrong in 1798 — food supply has grown far faster than he predicted — but right that any finite planet must have limits.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Boserup — The Optimist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In 1965 the Danish economist Ester <dfn class="term" data-def="A Danish economist who argued that rising population pressure forces innovation, allowing food supply to keep pace.">Boserup</dfn> turned Malthus on his head. She argued that necessity is the mother of invention: when population pressure rises, people invent new technologies and farming methods to feed themselves. Examples include the medieval shift from two-field to three-field crop rotation; the Green Revolution from the 1960s, when high-yield wheat and rice varieties tripled output in India and Mexico; and modern precision agriculture using GPS-guided tractors and drip irrigation. For Boserup, demand drives progress — not collapse.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The honest answer is ‘both, and it depends’. Boserup looks correct globally — humans have fed nearly five times as many people as in 1900. But Malthus looks correct regionally: Yemen, Somalia and parts of the Sahel face recurring food crises where population growth, climate stress and conflict have outrun local food supply. Innovation also has costs — the Green Revolution doubled yields but contaminated groundwater with fertilisers and made farmers dependent on imported seeds. The bigger question is whether innovation can keep pace with climate change, which is reducing yields in many tropical regions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Population projections from the UN now suggest world population will plateau, not explode, peaking somewhere between 9.5 and 10.5 billion by 2080. That gives a window for technology to keep up — but only if energy, food and water systems are reformed. The next lessons explore one of the biosphere’s most stressed regions: tropical rainforests and the taiga, where rising demand is colliding with fragile ecosystems.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Population growth, rising affluence, urbanisation and industrialisation are pushing demand for food, water and energy to record levels — freshwater use is dominated by agriculture (70%), and energy demand will rise another 25% by 2040.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Malthus argued population must outrun food, leading to crisis (positive checks). Examples like the Aral Sea and Sahel famines show the theory still has force.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Boserup argued rising demand drives innovation — the Green Revolution, drip irrigation and precision agriculture have repeatedly raised food output, supporting her view.</li>
</ul>
