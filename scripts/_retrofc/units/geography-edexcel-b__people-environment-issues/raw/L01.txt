--- DESCRIPTION ---
How climate shapes the distribution of biomes and how the biosphere supports life.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Patchwork of Living Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Look at any global map of vegetation and you can see Earth’s major <dfn class="term" data-def="Large-scale ecosystems found around the world that share similar climate, plants and animals, such as tropical rainforest or tundra.">biomes</dfn> arranged in roughly horizontal bands. Hot and wet equatorial belts hold tropical rainforests. Hot and dry zones at 30° latitude are deserts. Mid-latitudes hold temperate forests and grasslands. Cold high-latitude bands carry boreal forest (taiga) and tundra. The pattern is not coincidence: it is the product of global atmospheric circulation, ocean currents and altitude.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Major Biomes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Edexcel asks you to know the global distribution and characteristics of seven major biomes. <strong>Tropical rainforest</strong> straddles the equator (Amazon, Congo, Indonesia) — year-round warmth, 2,000+ mm of rainfall, very high biodiversity. <strong>Temperate deciduous forest</strong> covers western Europe, eastern North America and East Asia — four seasons, 600–1,500 mm rainfall, oak/beech/maple trees. <strong>Boreal (taiga) forest</strong> circles the northern hemisphere (Siberia, Canada, Scandinavia) — cold, low rainfall, conifers like spruce and pine. <strong>Tropical grasslands (savanna)</strong> sit between rainforest and desert — wet/dry seasons, scattered trees. <strong>Temperate grasslands</strong> include the prairies and steppes — cold winters, hot summers, treeless. <strong>Hot deserts</strong> at 30° latitude (Sahara, Atacama) get under 250 mm rainfall a year. <strong>Tundra</strong> ringing the Arctic has frozen ground (permafrost) and very short summers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the seven biomes with the key climate fact and one named region for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Biomes form latitudinal bands because temperature and rainfall depend on global atmospheric circulation. Hadley-cell rising air at the equator gives rainforest; sinking air at 30° gives desert; rising Ferrel-cell air at 60° gives temperate forest; sinking Polar air gives tundra.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why Climate Drives Distribution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Three climate factors shape biome distribution: <strong>temperature</strong>, <strong>precipitation</strong> and <strong>sunshine hours</strong>. Tropical regions are hot because the Sun’s rays strike near vertically; polar regions are cold because rays are slanted. Rainfall depends on the rising arms of circulation cells (equator, 60°) where air cools, condenses and dumps moisture. Sunshine hours influence growing seasons — Arctic summers have 24-hour daylight that lets tundra plants flower briefly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Local Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Climate isn’t the only thing that matters. <strong>Altitude</strong> can recreate latitudinal patterns vertically — Mount Kilimanjaro near the equator has tropical forest at the base, alpine grassland higher up, and an icecap at the summit. <strong>Soil type</strong>: rainforest grows on poor soils because nutrients cycle quickly through living matter; deserts often have alkaline crust soils that limit plant choice. <strong>Drainage</strong>: poor drainage creates wetlands and bogs even in dry regions. <strong>Rock type</strong>: granite uplands hold acidic moorland; chalk supports calcium-loving species.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Biotic</strong> components are the living parts of a biome — plants, animals, fungi, bacteria. <strong>Abiotic</strong> components are the non-living physical parts — soil, rock, water, air, temperature, sunlight. The two are interdependent: plants need abiotic water and sunlight to grow; their decay enriches abiotic soils. Disrupt one and the other suffers — cutting tropical rainforest reduces transpiration, which reduces local rainfall, which slows regrowth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Biosphere as a Life-Support System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The thin layer of life on Earth where living organisms exist, including the soils, oceans and lower atmosphere.">biosphere</dfn> provides goods and services to people. <strong>Goods</strong> are direct outputs: food (crops, fish, livestock), timber and fibre, medicines (a quarter of modern drugs come from rainforest plants), and fuels (firewood, biofuels). <strong>Services</strong> are less visible but vital: regulating the atmosphere’s composition (forests absorb CO₂ and release O₂), filtering water as it moves through soils and wetlands, recycling nutrients, pollinating crops, providing fresh-water storage. Without these services, modern civilisation could not function.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list two goods and two services the biosphere provides, with examples of who depends on each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The biosphere provides goods (food, timber, medicines, fuel) and services (atmospheric regulation, water purification, pollination, nutrient cycling). The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment estimated these services are worth over $33 trillion a year — roughly the size of the entire global economy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Indigenous and Local People</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">For <dfn class="term" data-def="The peoples descended from the original inhabitants of an area, often holding traditional knowledge of and rights over their lands.">Indigenous</dfn> peoples and rural communities, the biosphere is daily life. The Yanomami of the Amazon harvest hundreds of plant species for food, medicine, building material and ritual use. The Sami of northern Scandinavia herd reindeer through the taiga. Maasai pastoralists graze cattle on East African savanna. These livelihoods depend on intact biomes and on traditional knowledge built up over centuries. Pressure on biomes — from logging, mining, climate change — threatens these communities first and most directly.</p>

Identifying biomes from climate graphs is a regular exam task — practise pattern recognition.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Earth has seven major biomes (rainforest, temperate forest, taiga, savanna, temperate grassland, desert, tundra), arranged in latitudinal bands shaped by global atmospheric circulation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Local factors — altitude, soil, drainage and rock type — modify the broad climate pattern, creating finer-scale ecological variation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The biosphere provides goods (food, medicine, fuel) and services (atmospheric regulation, water purification, pollination) that are essential to people, especially Indigenous and rural communities.</li>
</ul>
