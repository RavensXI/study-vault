--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why settlers headed west: Manifest Destiny, the Oregon Trail from 1836 and the 1849 Gold Rush.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Belief, Not a Description</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the summer of 1845 a New York editor named John O&rsquo;Sullivan coined the phrase <dfn class="term" data-def="The 19th-century belief that the United States was destined by God to expand across North America from the Atlantic to the Pacific.">Manifest Destiny</dfn>. Writing in defence of the annexation of Texas, he claimed it was &ldquo;the fulfilment of our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence for the free development of our yearly multiplying millions.&rdquo; The 2026 Edexcel specification deliberately calls this a <em>belief</em> rather than a concept &mdash; because it was the belief that pushed people onto the wagon trails, not the academic phrase.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Manifest Destiny pulled together three strands of nineteenth-century American thinking: a Protestant idea that the United States had a divine mission, an early racial doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="A 19th-century racial belief that English-speaking Protestants of northern European descent were superior and destined to lead.">Anglo-Saxonism</dfn> that imagined English-speaking Protestants as natural leaders of the continent, and a republican confidence that representative government should spread. It was, importantly, an idea about the right to expand &mdash; not a description of an empty land. The Plains were already home to hundreds of thousands of people in dozens of Indigenous nations, and Manifest Destiny&rsquo;s real political work was to legitimise pushing them aside.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: who coined Manifest Destiny, in what year, and the three strands of thinking it combined.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The phrase &lsquo;Manifest Destiny&rsquo; was coined by John O&rsquo;Sullivan in 1845. It combined Protestant religious mission, Anglo-Saxon racial confidence and republican expansionism, and was used to justify westward settlement onto land that was already inhabited.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Push and Pull: Why Migrants Went</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Manifest Destiny was a story migrants told themselves; the reasons they actually packed up and went were more practical. The 1837 financial Panic ruined banks and farms across the East. Generations of cotton and tobacco growing had exhausted soils in the older states. Eastern cities were crowded, factory work was punishing, and religious minorities such as the Mormons faced violent persecution. These were the <dfn class="term" data-def="Negative conditions in a place of origin that drive people to leave (e.g. economic depression, soil exhaustion, persecution).">push factors</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Positive conditions in a destination that draw migrants towards it (e.g. cheap land, freedom of worship, gold).">pull factors</dfn> were just as strong. Cheap or free farmland in Oregon and California; reports of fertile valleys; access to the Pacific trade with Asia; freedom of worship in places where no establishment church could chase you; and, by 1848, gold. Newspapers, guidebooks and government promotion all amplified the message. Together, push and pull turned a slow trickle of trappers and missionaries in the 1830s into a great westward migration in the 1840s.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Oregon Trail from 1836</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The route west that opened first ran 2,000 miles from Independence, Missouri across the prairies, through the Rocky Mountains at South Pass, down the Snake River and on to the Willamette Valley in Oregon. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 2,000-mile wagon route from Missouri to Oregon, used by emigrants from 1836 onwards.">Oregon Trail</dfn> was first travelled by wagon in 1836, when missionaries Marcus and Narcissa Whitman drove a wagon through to what is now Washington State, proving the route was possible for families. Through the 1840s, &lsquo;wagon trains&rsquo; of forty to a hundred families gathered in Missouri each spring, elected captains, and set off behind ox-drawn wagons in early May.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Speed was everything. Trains had to cross the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada before October, when winter snows could trap them in the high passes. The journey took four to six months. River crossings, dust, drought, cholera and a steady drip of accidents wore down even prepared families &mdash; thousands of graves dotted the route. Yet by 1846 the trail was carrying perhaps 4,000 emigrants a year, and by the early 1850s tens of thousands.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A common student error is to write that Manifest Destiny was about &lsquo;filling empty land&rsquo;. The Great Plains, the Rockies and the Far West were already home to dozens of Indigenous nations &mdash; the Lakota, Comanche, Nez Perce, Cheyenne and many more &mdash; with their own settled or nomadic ways of life. The doctrine&rsquo;s political work was to <em>legitimise</em> dispossession, not to describe a vacuum. The 2026 specification reframes the wording from &lsquo;concept&rsquo; to &lsquo;belief in&rsquo; Manifest Destiny precisely to make this point. Writing about it as if the land were genuinely empty caps an answer at the lower bands.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The 1849 California Gold Rush</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In January 1848 a foreman called <dfn class="term" data-def="The carpenter and foreman who spotted gold in the millrace at Sutter&rsquo;s Mill in California in January 1848.">James Marshall</dfn> spotted gold flakes in the millrace of John Sutter&rsquo;s sawmill in the Sierra Nevada foothills. By 1849, after President Polk confirmed the find in his message to Congress, the news had set off a worldwide rush. Around 300,000 people poured into California between 1848 and 1855 &mdash; the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="A migrant who joined the California Gold Rush, named for the peak year of arrivals in 1849.">forty-niners</dfn>&rsquo; of 1849 alone numbered nearly 90,000. They came not only from the eastern United States but from China, Mexico, Chile, Britain and Australia. San Francisco grew from a village of around 800 people in 1848 to a port city of 25,000 by 1850.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">For Edexcel, the importance of the Gold Rush is what came AFTER it, not the moment of discovery itself. California became a state in 1850 &mdash; faster than any territory before it. The federal need to link the new state to the rest of the Union helped drive the Pacific Railroad debate that would lead to the Transcontinental Railroad. Chinese migration to California began on a scale that would later spark legislation. Mining camps modelled the boom-and-bust pattern repeated in every later gold strike, including the Black Hills in 1874. And the Plains nations between the Missouri and the gold fields suddenly mattered to a federal government that needed safe overland passage to its newest state.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three CONSEQUENCES of the Gold Rush (not the moment of discovery itself).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1849 Gold Rush brought around 300,000 people to California from across the world, helped California become a state in 1850, accelerated railroad debate and made overland routes across Indigenous Plains territory a federal priority.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A Mythical and a Real West</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Manifest Destiny, the Oregon Trail and the Gold Rush together created the public imagination of the West that would shape the next sixty years &mdash; a story of brave families, providential mission and free land.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The reality behind the myth</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The reality on the ground was more complicated: an already-inhabited continent, mortality of around 4&ndash;6% on the wagon trails (mainly from cholera, drowning and accidents), and gold that overwhelmingly enriched the merchants and bankers of San Francisco rather than the diggers themselves. Many forty-niners returned home poorer than they had left. Top-band answers hold both the myth (Manifest Destiny, providence, free land) and the reality (dispossession, hardship, uneven gain) together rather than choosing only one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Manifest Destiny, coined by John O&rsquo;Sullivan in 1845, was a belief in providential American expansion that combined religion, race and republicanism &mdash; and which legitimised settlement onto already-inhabited Indigenous lands.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">From 1836 the 2,000-mile Oregon Trail carried wagon trains from Missouri to the Pacific Northwest; push factors (the 1837 Panic, soil exhaustion, persecution) and pull factors (cheap land, religious freedom, gold) drove migration.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The 1849 Gold Rush brought ~300,000 people to California; its IMPORTANCE was the consequences &mdash; California statehood in 1850, international migration, the rise of San Francisco and federal pressure to link the state to the East.</li>
</ul>
