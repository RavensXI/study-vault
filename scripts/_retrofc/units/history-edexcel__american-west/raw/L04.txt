--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Permanent Indian Frontier, the 1851 Indian Appropriations Act and the first Fort Laramie Treaty.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Removal to a &lsquo;Permanent&rsquo; Line</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the 1830s the United States had a settled answer to what it called &lsquo;the Indian question&rsquo;: removal. The 1830 Indian Removal Act, signed by President Andrew Jackson, gave the federal government the power to relocate Indigenous nations west of the Mississippi River. The most notorious case was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced relocation of the Cherokee and other south-eastern nations west of the Mississippi in 1838&ndash;39, in which thousands died.">Trail of Tears</dfn> in 1838&ndash;39, when the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek and Seminole were marched west under military escort; thousands died of exposure, hunger and disease on the journey.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Out of removal grew the idea of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A line west of the Mississippi designated by the US in the 1830s as &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; in perpetuity, to keep settlers and Indigenous nations apart.">Permanent Indian Frontier</dfn>. This was a line drawn on maps from the 1830s, dividing the United States proper from a vast &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; west of the Mississippi where, according to federal policy, Indigenous peoples could live free of settler intrusion. A chain of military forts &mdash; Fort Leavenworth, Fort Gibson, Fort Smith &mdash; was meant to police the line. The 2026 Edexcel specification names this policy explicitly: it is the spec&rsquo;s starting point for understanding US-Indigenous tension.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: what was the Permanent Indian Frontier, when was it set out, and which Act underpinned it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Permanent Indian Frontier was a line west of the Mississippi, set out in the 1830s under the Indian Removal Act of 1830. Federal policy promised it would separate settlers from Indigenous peoples in perpetuity &mdash; a promise that lasted barely two decades.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why the &lsquo;Permanent&rsquo; Frontier Failed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">By 1851 the line was dead in everything but name. Three pressures had broken it. The first was migration: from 1836 the Oregon Trail carried thousands of emigrants directly across it each summer. The second was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1848 treaty ending the Mexican-American War, in which Mexico ceded California and the South-West to the United States.">Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo</dfn> of 1848, which ended the Mexican-American War and handed the United States California, New Mexico and the South-West &mdash; vast new territories on the far side of the &lsquo;permanent&rsquo; line. The third was gold: the 1849 Rush sent 300,000 people pouring across the Plains in five years.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The map made no sense any longer. &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; sat between two halves of the United States, and the federal government wanted safe overland passage between them. Settlers also clashed with Plains nations along the trails &mdash; over wood, water, grazing for animals and the disease that wagon trains brought with them. Indigenous nations, for their part, watched the buffalo herds being disturbed by the trail traffic. Both sides increasingly demanded that Washington do something. The era of separation was finished; what would replace it was Concentration.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The 1851 Indian Appropriations Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In February 1851 Congress passed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1851 US law that funded the creation of reservations on the Plains, marking the legal beginning of the reservation system.">Indian Appropriations Act</dfn>. The Act funded the creation of <dfn class="term" data-def="An area of land set aside by the US government for Indigenous nations, on which they were expected to live and not leave without permission.">reservations</dfn> on the Plains &mdash; specific areas of land set aside for individual Indigenous nations, on which they were expected to live and not freely leave. This was the legal beginning of the reservation system. The Act also funded annual <dfn class="term" data-def="An annual payment of money or goods made by the US Government to an Indigenous nation in return for territorial concessions.">annuities</dfn> &mdash; payments of money and goods promised in return for territorial concessions.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Behind the law was a new doctrine that historians now call <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1850s US policy of moving Indigenous nations onto specific reservations rather than keeping them apart from settlers.">concentration policy</dfn>. Where the Permanent Indian Frontier had tried to keep settlers and Indigenous peoples apart on either side of a line, concentration would put each Indigenous nation on its own patch of land while opening the spaces in between to settlers and railroads. It was, in effect, the end of the idea that Indigenous nations could keep the entire western interior to themselves &mdash; and the beginning of the framework that would dominate US-Indigenous relations until the Dawes Act of 1887.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The First Fort Laramie Treaty, 1851</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In September 1851, around 10,000 representatives of the Lakota Sioux, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow, Shoshone, Assiniboine, Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara gathered at Fort Laramie in modern-day Wyoming. It was one of the largest inter-tribal councils ever held with the United States, organised by the US Indian agent <dfn class="term" data-def="US Indian agent who organised the multi-nation council that produced the first Fort Laramie Treaty in 1851.">Thomas Fitzpatrick</dfn>. On the Indigenous side, signatories included <dfn class="term" data-def="Brul&eacute; Lakota leader and signatory to the first Fort Laramie Treaty in 1851.">Conquering Bear</dfn>, the Brul&eacute; Lakota leader.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The terms were clear on paper: Indigenous nations recognised the right of safe passage on the Oregon Trail and accepted defined territories on the Plains; in return, the US promised annual annuities of $50,000 for fifty years and recognised those territories. The Treaty did not transfer land to settlers &mdash; in 1851 it was still about regulating co-existence. Yet within months Congress reduced the annuity term to ten years, and even those payments arrived late or short, often spoiled when they did arrive. The Treaty was signed but never ratified by Congress in the agreed form. Trust between the Plains nations and the US Government, already thin, was thinner by 1854.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">No. The June 2022 examiner report on this option flags the confusion as a recurring weakness that caps answers at the lower bands. From this lesson on, every reference to Fort Laramie should carry its date. <strong>1851</strong> is the multi-nation council, annuities and recognition of safe passage on the Oregon Trail. <strong>1868</strong> &mdash; covered later in this unit &mdash; is the second treaty, which ended Red Cloud&rsquo;s War, closed the Bozeman Trail and granted the Black Hills to the Lakota. Same fort; very different treaties; very different outcomes. Lock the date to the terms whenever you write about either.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">A Shift in Policy and a Loss of Trust</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Looking back, the events of 1851 mark a turning point. Federal policy moved from <em>separation</em> (the Permanent Indian Frontier) to <em>concentration</em> (specific reservations and annuity diplomacy). Indigenous nations gained, on paper, formal US recognition of defined territories. But because Congress reneged on the annuity terms, the Treaty was experienced by Plains leaders as another broken promise. Every later confrontation in this unit &mdash; from Little Crow&rsquo;s War in 1862 to Red Cloud&rsquo;s War in 1866&ndash;68 &mdash; sat on top of this initial loss of trust.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the date and ONE distinctive term of each Fort Laramie Treaty &mdash; 1851 and 1868 &mdash; without looking back.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1851 Fort Laramie Treaty promised safe passage on the Oregon Trail in return for $50,000 in annuities for fifty years. Congress quickly cut the term to ten years, and payments were late or short &mdash; undermining trust in every later negotiation.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Permanent Indian Frontier (set out under the 1830 Indian Removal Act) tried to separate settlers from Indigenous peoples along a line west of the Mississippi, but Oregon migration, the 1848 Mexican Cession and the 1849 Gold Rush had broken it by 1851.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The 1851 Indian Appropriations Act funded reservations and annuities, marking the legal start of the reservation system and a shift from separation to concentration policy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The first Fort Laramie Treaty (1851) gathered around 10,000 representatives of the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow and others; it promised safe passage on the Oregon Trail in return for annuities, but Congress cut the term and payments were unreliable, planting deep distrust.</li>
</ul>
