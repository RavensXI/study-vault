<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Patchwork of Nations, Not a Single People</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the early nineteenth century the Great Plains stretched from the Mississippi to the Rocky Mountains, a vast grassland the size of western Europe. It was already home to hundreds of thousands of people in dozens of distinct nations &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The largest Plains nation, made up of seven council fires including the Oglala, Hunkpapa and Brul&eacute;.">Lakota Sioux</dfn>, the Cheyenne, the Arapaho, the Comanche, the Crow and the Pawnee, alongside many others. Each had its own language, leadership and territory. The Crow, for example, were long-standing rivals of the Lakota and would later scout for the US Army against them. To lump them together as one undifferentiated people is to miss the politics of the Plains entirely.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The 2026 Edexcel specification reflects this by using the term <dfn class="term" data-def="The 2026 specification term for the diverse nations of the Great Plains, used to underline that these were sovereign nations.">Indigenous peoples</dfn>. The change matters: it signals that these were sovereign nations, not a passive backdrop to settlement. Naming a specific nation &mdash; Lakota, Cheyenne, Comanche &mdash; whenever the spec lets you, will sharpen any answer you write for the rest of this unit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four Indigenous Plains nations from memory, and write one sentence on what made each distinct.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Lakota Sioux, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, Crow and Pawnee were distinct nations with their own languages, leaderships and rivalries. Treating them as one people is the single most common mistake in this unit.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Bands, Tribes and Chiefs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Within each nation, people lived in <dfn class="term" data-def="A small extended-family group of around 50&ndash;500 people, the everyday unit of Plains life.">bands</dfn> &mdash; small groups of perhaps 50 to 500 related families who travelled and hunted together. Several bands made up a tribe, and several tribes a nation. Leadership did not work as it did in Europe. There was no all-powerful king. Instead, <dfn class="term" data-def="A respected leader chosen for wisdom, bravery or generosity, whose authority depended on persuading his people rather than commanding them.">chiefs</dfn> were chosen for their wisdom, bravery or generosity, and their authority rested on persuasion. A chief who lost the trust of his people simply lost his following.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Big decisions &mdash; whether to move camp, to make peace, to go to war &mdash; were debated by a council of elders and warrior-society leaders. <dfn class="term" data-def="Plains military brotherhoods that policed the camp, organised hunts and led war parties (e.g. the Cheyenne Dog Soldiers).">Warrior societies</dfn>, such as the Cheyenne Dog Soldiers, kept order on the hunt and led war parties. This decentralised system was a real strength on the Plains, but it would later confuse US negotiators, who often signed treaties with one chief and assumed the whole nation was bound.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Horse and the Buffalo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Plains life rested on two animals. The horse, brought to the Americas by Spanish colonists and spread to the Plains in the 1700s, transformed everything. A nation that had once walked could now ride hundreds of miles a year, follow vast buffalo herds and outrun rivals. By 1835 horse wealth was the measure of a Plains family&rsquo;s standing &mdash; the Comanche, in particular, ran herds in the tens of thousands.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The other animal was the buffalo, or bison &mdash; an estimated 30 million on the Plains in 1835. The Lakota, Cheyenne and others were nomadic hunters who followed the herds with the seasons. They lived in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A portable Plains dwelling made of buffalo hides stretched over wooden poles, designed to move with the herds.">tipi</dfn>, a portable home of hides over poles that could be struck and re-pitched in under an hour. Almost every part of the buffalo was used: meat for food, hide for tipi covers and clothing, sinew for bowstrings, bone for tools, hooves boiled for glue, the bladder turned into a water-skin. Lose the buffalo, and the whole way of life unravels &mdash; a fact the US Army would exploit ruthlessly later in the period.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Not all of them. Nations such as the Pawnee and Mandan combined seasonal buffalo hunting with farming corn, beans and squash from settled earth-lodge villages along the Missouri River. The fully nomadic horse-and-buffalo culture of the Lakota and Cheyenne was relatively recent &mdash; built on the spread of the horse in the 1700s. Treating &lsquo;Plains life&rsquo; as a timeless image of tipis and buffalo-chasing flattens centuries of change. The horse-and-buffalo Plains the textbooks describe was, by 1835, only a few generations old.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Beliefs about Land, Spirit and Nature</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Plains spiritual life centred on a belief that the natural world was alive with sacred power. The Lakota called the creator <dfn class="term" data-def="The Lakota name for the Great Spirit, the sacred power present in all of creation.">Wakan Tanka</dfn>, the Great Spirit. Mother Earth was honoured as a giver of life. Sacred sites &mdash; above all the <dfn class="term" data-def="A range of sacred hills in modern South Dakota, central to Lakota spiritual life.">Black Hills</dfn> of modern-day South Dakota &mdash; were places where the spirit world was felt most strongly. Young men marked the passage to adulthood with a <dfn class="term" data-def="A spiritual ritual in which a young Lakota or Cheyenne man fasted alone for several days to receive a guiding vision.">vision quest</dfn>: alone, fasting, awaiting a vision that would guide their adult life. Medicine men acted as healers and as bridges between the living and the spirit world.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Crucially, the land itself was not seen as something to be owned. It was used in common &mdash; a hunting territory belonged to the nation as a whole, not to a private family. There were no fences, no deeds, no surveyors. This belief sat directly across from the American settler idea that land was a 160-acre rectangle to be claimed, fenced and inherited. The clash was structural, not just political: two systems of property could not occupy the same ground.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">War, Counting Coup and the Misreading of Plains Conflict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Inter-tribal war on the Plains had a logic of its own. Raids for horses and revenge between, for example, the Lakota and the Crow were frequent and dangerous, but they were not aimed at total destruction. Honour was won by daring, not by body counts. The highest deed for a Lakota or Cheyenne warrior was <dfn class="term" data-def="A Plains practice of touching an enemy in battle without killing him &mdash; the highest mark of bravery.">counting coup</dfn> &mdash; touching an enemy in battle and getting away alive, without needing to kill. White observers, expecting European-style warfare, often misread these raids as savage anarchy.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">From the 1850s onwards, US officials repeatedly told Eastern audiences that the Plains nations were &lsquo;naturally warlike&rsquo; and could only be controlled by force. The reality was an honour-based system of limited warfare, with its own conventions, treaties and councils &mdash; layered on top of deep, ongoing rivalries such as Lakota against Crow. Dropping the &lsquo;savage&rsquo; framing and treating Plains warfare as a different system, not the absence of one, is the move that unlocks higher marks on this topic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write a short paragraph: explain why the Indigenous Plains way of life would inevitably clash with American ideas of property.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For the nomadic Plains nations, the buffalo was food, shelter, tools and clothing in one animal, and land was a shared territory rather than something owned. Both beliefs would put them on a collision course with American settlers from c1840 onwards.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Plains in c1835 were home to many distinct nations &mdash; Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, Crow, Pawnee &mdash; with their own languages, leaders and rivalries; treating them as a single people is the unit&rsquo;s biggest exam trap.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Daily survival rested on the horse and the buffalo: nomadic hunting, the tipi, and the use of every part of the bison from meat to bone tools.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Spiritual beliefs centred on Wakan Tanka, sacred land such as the Black Hills, vision quests and counting coup &mdash; a worldview that defined land as shared rather than owned, setting up a structural clash with US settlers.</li>
</ul>
