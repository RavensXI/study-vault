Trail-blazers, the Donner disaster and the Mormon migration of 1846–47 to Utah.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mountain Men: The First Pathfinders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Long before wagon trains, the trails west were scouted by a small, hard fraternity of fur trappers known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Fur trappers and traders of the 1820s&ndash;1840s who scouted the western trails and mapped the mountain passes.">mountain men</dfn>. Operating from the early 1820s, they hunted beaver in the Rocky Mountains for the eastern hat trade and lived for months among Indigenous nations such as the Crow and the Shoshone. Names you should know are <dfn class="term" data-def="Mountain man and trail-blazer who founded Fort Bridger in Wyoming in 1842; later guided wagon trains.">Jim Bridger</dfn>, who founded Fort Bridger in 1842 as a supply post on the Oregon Trail, <dfn class="term" data-def="Mountain man who in 1824 mapped South Pass through the Rockies, the gentle crossing later used by every wagon train.">Jedediah Smith</dfn>, who in 1824 located <dfn class="term" data-def="A broad, gentle crossing of the Rocky Mountains in modern Wyoming, used by every later wagon train west.">South Pass</dfn> through the Rockies, and Kit Carson, who would later guide the US Army.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Their economic moment was short. By 1840 silk had replaced beaver fur in fashionable hats and the great rendezvous gatherings of trappers were over. But the maps they had drawn, the passes they had charted and the diplomatic relationships they had built with Indigenous peoples were inherited by the wagon trains of the 1840s. Without South Pass and Fort Bridger there would have been no realistic Oregon or California Trail.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name three mountain men with one specific contribution each to later western migration.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mountain men of the 1820s and 1830s mapped the mountain passes, founded forts and built relationships with Indigenous nations. Their work made the Oregon and California Trails possible after the fur trade collapsed around 1840.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Donner Party: A Lesson in What Could Go Wrong</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In May 1846 a group of 87 emigrants led by the brothers George and Jacob Donner left Missouri for California. They were a typical wagon train at first &mdash; mainly families, well supplied, on schedule. The decision that would destroy them was taken at Fort Bridger in late July, when they chose to follow a guidebook by <dfn class="term" data-def="Author of an 1845 guidebook whose untested &lsquo;shortcut&rsquo; misled the Donner Party into the salt deserts and a fatal delay.">Lansford Hastings</dfn>, who claimed a new route &mdash; the &lsquo;Hastings Cutoff&rsquo; &mdash; would save them weeks across the Wasatch Mountains and the Great Salt Lake Desert.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The shortcut was a disaster. Hastings had never taken loaded wagons through it. The Wasatch range had to be hacked through tree by tree, costing precious days. The salt desert dried out the oxen and split the train. By the time the Donners reached the Sierra Nevada in late October, they were a month behind schedule and already short of food. The first heavy snow of the year fell on 28 October 1846 &mdash; a freak early storm. They were trapped at what is now Donner Lake.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Roughly half of the party died over the winter of 1846&ndash;47 &mdash; from cold, starvation and exhaustion. A small group attempted a snowshoe rescue and got through. Some of the survivors, after weeks without food, resorted to cannibalism to stay alive. When relief parties from California reached the camps in February and March 1847, they found a tragedy that newspapers across the country reported in horrified detail.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Why the Mormons Went West</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Mormon story is the opposite of the Donner story &mdash; an organised migration that succeeded because of disciplined planning. <dfn class="term" data-def="Founder of the Latter-day Saint movement in 1830; murdered by a mob at Carthage Jail, Illinois, in 1844.">Joseph Smith</dfn> founded the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in upstate New York in 1830, claiming to have been led by an angel to a new scripture, the Book of Mormon. From the start the Mormons faced violent persecution &mdash; in New York, Ohio, Missouri (where Governor Boggs issued an &lsquo;extermination order&rsquo; in 1838) and finally Illinois.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Their distinct beliefs and the practice of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Mormon practice in this period of plural marriage, in which a man could have more than one wife.">polygamy</dfn> &mdash; plural marriage &mdash; outraged neighbours, and Mormon political and economic power frightened them. In June 1844 a mob stormed Carthage Jail in Illinois and murdered Joseph Smith and his brother Hyrum. The young Church now needed both a leader and a refuge. <dfn class="term" data-def="Mormon leader after Joseph Smith&rsquo;s death; led the migration to the Salt Lake Valley in 1846&ndash;47.">Brigham Young</dfn>, head of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, took charge.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Mormon Trek of 1846&ndash;47</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Young deliberately chose somewhere no one else wanted: the basin of the Great Salt Lake, in Mexican territory beyond the Rockies. The migration of 1846&ndash;47 covered roughly 1,300 miles from Nauvoo in Illinois to the Salt Lake Valley. It was unlike any other wagon train. Young sent a pioneer company ahead in 1847 to build way stations, mark the trail and plant crops; the main body followed in disciplined groups; an emigration fund organised by the Church paid for poorer families.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Once in the valley, the Mormons named their territory <dfn class="term" data-def="The Mormon name for their settlement around the Great Salt Lake; later organised as the Utah Territory.">Deseret</dfn>. They began irrigation farming the moment they arrived &mdash; channelling water from the canyons onto land that no neighbouring tribe was farming &mdash; and built Salt Lake City to a planned grid. The community was tightly organised under Young&rsquo;s direction, with the ward (local congregation) as the unit of social welfare.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Three differences explain the gap. First, leadership: Brigham Young exercised firm authority over a religious community that obeyed him; the Donner Party was a coalition of independent families. Second, planning: pioneer companies, way stations and a perpetual emigration fund replaced individual decisions about routes and supplies. Third, geography: the Salt Lake basin was somewhere no other power wanted, so the Mormons did not have to fight for it &mdash; they only had to make it productive, which their irrigation methods and the ward system did. Many of these themes of organisation, leadership and timing are exactly what an analytical narrative on the period rewards.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Mormon Trek of 1846&ndash;47 covered 1,300 miles from Nauvoo to the Salt Lake Valley under Brigham Young&rsquo;s leadership. Pioneer companies, way stations, an emigration fund and irrigation farming turned a religious refuge into the planned settlement of Deseret.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Mountain men such as Jim Bridger and Jedediah Smith mapped South Pass and the western forts in the 1820s and 1830s, making the later wagon trails possible.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Donner Party (1846&ndash;47) shows the cost of trusting an untested guidebook: the Hastings Cutoff lost them weeks, leaving them trapped by an early Sierra Nevada storm; about half died.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Mormon migration of 1846&ndash;47 succeeded under Brigham Young&rsquo;s leadership through pioneer companies, way stations, an emigration fund and irrigation farming, founding the planned settlement of Deseret in the Salt Lake Valley.</li>
</ul>
