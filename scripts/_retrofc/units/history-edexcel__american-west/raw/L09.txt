<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Texas Surplus Meets an Eastern Hunger</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1865 Texas had a problem that turned out to be a fortune. Across the southern plains roamed an estimated five million wild <dfn class="term" data-def="A hardy long-horned breed of cattle descended from Spanish stock, well-adapted to the dry Texas plains.">Texas longhorns</dfn> &mdash; descended from Spanish cattle and untouched during four years of Civil War. In Texas they were worth $3&ndash;$4 a head. In the beef-hungry cities of the post-war North, the same animal could fetch $30&ndash;$40. Linking those two markets, across a thousand miles of open Plains, would create the &lsquo;beef bonanza&rsquo; that turned ranching into one of the great industries of the West.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The link was the railroad. As the Kansas Pacific pushed west across Kansas after 1865, it created the chance to ship cattle east in days rather than walking them across half the continent. What the cattle industry needed was someone to bring the longhorns to the railhead in bulk &mdash; and a profitable place to put them down.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: how many wild longhorns roamed Texas by 1865, the price gap between Texas and the East, and why the Civil War mattered for the industry.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1865 around five million wild Texas longhorns roamed the southern plains, untouched by the Civil War. A longhorn worth $3&ndash;$4 in Texas could fetch $30&ndash;$40 in the beef-hungry post-war North &mdash; the price gap that drove the &lsquo;beef bonanza&rsquo;.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Joseph McCoy and the Birth of the Cow Town</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1867 a 29-year-old Illinois cattle dealer named <dfn class="term" data-def="The Illinois cattle dealer who in 1867 built Abilene as the first cow town, where Texas longhorns were shipped east by rail.">Joseph McCoy</dfn> bought a small Kansas town called Abilene, beside the Kansas Pacific line. He built a hotel, a stockyard for 3,000 cattle, an office and a set of loading pens. Then he sent riders south into Texas with a promise: bring your longhorns to Abilene and we will buy them, weigh them and load them onto trains for Chicago. By December 1867 around 35,000 head had walked the Chisholm Trail north to McCoy&rsquo;s pens. By 1871 the number was 700,000 in a single season. McCoy had invented the <dfn class="term" data-def="A frontier town built beside a railhead where cattle from the long drive were sold and loaded onto trains for eastern markets.">cow town</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">As the railroads spread, the cow towns moved. Abilene led the way (1867&ndash;71), then Newton, Wichita, Ellsworth and Caldwell took turns; from 1875 Dodge City became the &lsquo;Queen of the Cow Towns&rsquo; on the Western Trail. Each was a creation of the railroad, briefly riotous and then quiet again as the cattle moved on.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Goodnight, Loving and Iliff: New Trails, New Markets</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Abilene was not the only model. In 1866 the Texas rancher <dfn class="term" data-def="Texas rancher who pioneered the Goodnight–Loving Trail (1866) and helped shape Plains ranching as a national industry.">Charles Goodnight</dfn> and his partner Oliver Loving drove 2,000 longhorns west and north into New Mexico and Colorado, supplying army posts at Fort Sumner and the mining camps of Denver. Their route &mdash; the Goodnight&ndash;Loving Trail &mdash; opened a market that the railroads alone could not reach: the army garrisons feeding the Indian Wars, and the reservations whose annuities included beef rations.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">North of the Texas trail-heads a different model took root. <dfn class="term" data-def="Pioneer of open-range Plains ranching; supplied army posts and reservations with beef from the 1860s.">John Iliff</dfn>, a Colorado rancher, realised that the empty grass north of the Arkansas River would fatten cattle for free. By the late 1860s Iliff was running tens of thousands of cattle on the unfenced range, supplying the army, the reservations and the Union Pacific construction crews. His model &mdash; the open range &mdash; was copied across Wyoming, Montana, the Dakotas and the Texas Panhandle through the 1870s and early 1880s. Iliff did not just sell cattle to the Plains; he ranched ON them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Long Drive</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The seasonal trail-driving of cattle, often 1,000–1,500 miles, from Texas to a railhead or northern range.">long drive</dfn> was the engine of the early industry. A single drive of around 2,500&ndash;3,000 cattle covered 1,000&ndash;1,500 miles in two to three months, supervised by 10&ndash;12 cowboys. The Chisholm Trail (Texas to Abilene) ran from 1867 to about 1884; the Western Trail (Texas to Dodge City) ran from the mid-1870s. The Goodnight&ndash;Loving Trail ran west and north into New Mexico and Colorado.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">A drive followed a strict pattern. Cattle were branded in spring, gathered at the round-up, and walked north at around 12&ndash;15 miles a day. Each crew had a trail boss, a cook with a chuck wagon, two point riders at the front, swing and flank riders along the herd, and the youngest hands &mdash; the &lsquo;drag&rsquo; riders &mdash; eating dust at the back. Stampedes, river crossings, drought and storms could ruin a season&rsquo;s work in a single afternoon.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Cowboy: Identity and Reality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The Hollywood cowboy of the 20th century was overwhelmingly white. The historical cowboy was not. Around one-third of Plains cowboys were Black &mdash; freedmen, formerly enslaved, who came west looking for work the South would not give them &mdash; and around one-third were Hispanic, descended from the Mexican <dfn class="term" data-def="A Mexican cattle herder; the source of much of the cowboy's vocabulary, dress and ranching skills.">vaquero</dfn> tradition that gave the cowboy his chaps, his lariat, his branding methods and most of his vocabulary (corral, lasso, rodeo, ranch).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The work was hard, seasonal and badly paid &mdash; around $25&ndash;$40 a month, with food and a horse provided. A cowboy might spend three months on the trail, three more on the round-up, and the winter laid off entirely. The romantic image of the &lsquo;Wild West&rsquo; cowboy hero developed mostly AFTER the open-range era, in dime novels, Buffalo Bill&rsquo;s Wild West Show (from 1883) and later film.</p>

The historical cowboy was a low-paid seasonal labourer, on horseback for ten to fourteen hours a day, eating beans and bacon out of a chuck wagon. The romantic image was largely manufactured AFTER the open range collapsed in the &lsquo;Big Die-Up&rsquo; winter of 1886&ndash;87. Buffalo Bill Cody&rsquo;s Wild West Show (touring from 1883) sold a staged version of the cowboy across the United States and Europe; dime novels and, later, Hollywood film cemented it. <h2 data-narration-id="n19">Ranchers vs Homesteaders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">By the late 1870s the Plains were squeezed between two settler economies. Ranchers wanted unfenced range; homesteaders wanted fenced fields. Drovers&rsquo; herds trampled crops; quarrels over water rights ran through every drought year; barbed wire (patented by Joseph Glidden in 1874) gave homesteaders a cheap way to enclose their land for the first time. The slow-burn quarrels of the 1870s would explode into the Wyoming range wars of the 1890s &mdash; the Johnson County War &mdash; covered in Lesson 12. The cattle industry created the West&rsquo;s most distinctive figure, but it also set up its most violent late-period conflict.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and name McCoy, Goodnight and Iliff with one specific contribution each that an examiner would credit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>McCoy built Abilene as the first cow town in 1867; Goodnight blazed the Goodnight&ndash;Loving Trail (1866) into the army-and-mining market; Iliff pioneered open-range ranching on the northern Plains. Together they turned a Texas surplus into a national industry &mdash; and set the conditions for the rancher&ndash;homesteader conflict that erupted in the 1890s.</p>
</div>
