Marshals, sheriffs, Pinkertons and vigilantes — how federal law grew on the Plains.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Layered System of Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a settler in 1860s Kansas was robbed by a stagecoach gang, who actually came after the thieves? The answer was rarely &lsquo;the police&rsquo; in any modern sense. Across the West three different layers of authority worked &mdash; and often clashed. At the federal level, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A federal law officer appointed by the President to enforce US law across a whole territory.">US marshal</dfn> covered an entire territory and answered to Washington. At the county level, an elected <dfn class="term" data-def="A county-level law officer elected by local voters, with jurisdiction over a single county.">sheriff</dfn> covered the rural districts. Inside towns, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A town-level law officer appointed by the mayor or town council to police a single settlement.">town marshal</dfn> appointed by the mayor kept the peace on the streets.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">These jurisdictions overlapped untidily. A train robber crossing two counties and a territorial line could be chased by a town marshal at the start, a county sheriff in the middle, and a US marshal once the loot crossed into a federal mail car. Each layer had its own pay, its own paperwork and often its own grudges. The story of law and order on the Plains between c1860 and c1895 is the slow process by which these three layers learned to work together &mdash; and the rise of federal power above them all.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three layers of frontier law and who appointed each one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Frontier law worked through three layers: US marshals (federal, appointed by the President for a whole territory), county sheriffs (elected locally) and town marshals (appointed by mayors). Their jurisdictions overlapped, and federal authority grew only slowly across the period.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Federal Influence Grew</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Civil War (1861&ndash;65) left behind a much stronger federal state. After the war, Washington could raise more revenue, command a larger army and reach further into the West than before. Three pressures pulled federal authority westwards. First, the new transcontinental and branch railroads &mdash; built with federal land grants &mdash; were a national investment that needed national protection. Train robbery and rustling were not just local crimes; they hit the US mail, federal currency and inter-state shipments. Second, territorial governments answered directly to Washington until they earned statehood, so judges, marshals and Indian agents were federal appointees. Third, the army was redeployed across the Plains after 1865 and remained the most visible federal presence in many districts.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">As a result the everyday business of crime &mdash; stagecoach robbery, train robbery and cattle rustling &mdash; became a federal-scale problem. The express company <dfn class="term" data-def="A private express and banking company that ran stagecoaches and shipped gold, mail and cash; it employed its own armed messengers and detectives.">Wells Fargo</dfn> hired its own armed messengers and a small detective force, working alongside US marshals to recover stolen shipments. By the 1870s the federal courts in territories such as Wyoming and Dakota had a steady caseload of train and mail-coach prosecutions, paid for from Washington.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Pinkerton&rsquo;s National Detective Agency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Public law enforcement was thin. To fill the gap, private agencies stepped in. In 1850 the Scottish-born former Chicago detective Allan Pinkerton founded <dfn class="term" data-def="Allan Pinkerton's private detective agency, founded in 1850 and hired by railroads and banks to track outlaws and protect shipments.">Pinkerton&rsquo;s National Detective Agency</dfn>. After the Civil War the Pinkertons became the most important private police force in the country: hired by railroad companies to ride the carriages, by banks to recover stolen gold, and by federal authorities to track gangs across state lines. Their best-known target was the James-Younger gang, who robbed banks and trains in the 1870s. Pinkerton&rsquo;s agents tracked them for years, often paid by railroad bonds rather than government salary.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The Pinkertons were not perfect &mdash; their methods were sometimes brutal, including a 1875 raid on the James family farm that killed Jesse James&rsquo;s nine-year-old half-brother. But they showed that the federal&ndash;territorial&ndash;local system needed reinforcement, and they normalised the idea of policing across jurisdictions.</p>

A territory the size of Wyoming or Montana might have a single US marshal, a handful of deputies and a county sheriff who was also the local farrier. The June 2022 examiner report warned against vague answers about &lsquo;the federal government bringing law&rsquo; &mdash; the highest band wants you to name the institutions (US marshals, Pinkertons, county sheriffs) and the events (Montana Vigilantes 1863&ndash;65, Henry Plummer&rsquo;s hanging) that made the system work in practice.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Vigilantes: Law Without Officers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Where federal officers were thinly spread, communities took the law into their own hands. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An informal committee of local men who took the law into their own hands, often hanging suspected outlaws without trial.">vigilance committee</dfn> was an informal committee of local men &mdash; often merchants, miners or ranchers &mdash; who hunted, tried and hanged suspected outlaws without waiting for a court. The most famous example was the Montana Vigilantes of 1863&ndash;65 in the gold-mining settlements of Bannack and Virginia City. They hanged at least 21 men, including the Bannack sheriff Henry Plummer, whom they accused of secretly leading a road-agent ring that had robbed gold shipments and killed travellers.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Plummer&rsquo;s hanging in January 1864 split contemporary opinion. Some Western newspapers celebrated the vigilantes as the only force willing to act when the territorial government could not. Others worried that lynch law would replace court law permanently. The pattern would repeat: vigilance committees reappeared wherever official law was thin, from California mining camps to the Wyoming range wars of the 1890s. Vigilantism was not the absence of law &mdash; it was a parallel system that competed with the official one.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Plummer was the elected sheriff of Bannack &mdash; the only law officer for hundreds of miles. The vigilantes accused him of secretly leading the &lsquo;Innocents&rsquo;, a road-agent ring blamed for around 100 robberies and several murders along the gold-shipment routes. Whether the charges were true is still debated by historians, since the Vigilantes left no court record. The case captured the central tension of the period: the man wearing the badge could be the same man leading the gang, and there was no second institution close enough to check him. Out of cases like Plummer&rsquo;s came the slow demand for federal marshals, paid Pinkerton agents, and eventually statehood.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Statehood: The Milestone of Settled Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A territory was a federal possession run from Washington. A state was a self-governing member of the Union with its own legislature, its own courts and its own police powers. The progression from territory to state was the milestone that finally settled law and order. Nebraska became a state in 1867, Colorado in 1876, and the Dakotas, Montana and Washington in 1889 &mdash; six states admitted in a single year. Wyoming and Idaho followed in 1890, and Utah in 1896. Each admission shifted policing from federally appointed marshals to elected state officials, with a more developed court system and tax base.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Statehood did not end frontier crime overnight. The Johnson County War of 1892 in Wyoming &mdash; only two years a state &mdash; showed that even with full state government, large landowners could still hire private gunmen and outrun local sheriffs. But by the early 1890s the pattern set in motion in this lesson was clear: a federal&ndash;territorial system, reinforced by private agencies, supplanted by elected state authority. The story would pay off in Lessons 11 and 12, when sheriffs Wyatt Earp and Pat Garrett, and the Johnson County cattle barons, tested the new system to its limits.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and list two reasons federal influence grew on the Plains after 1865, and one event that shows the limits of official law.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Federal influence grew because the post-Civil War state was stronger, the railroads needed national protection, and territorial governments answered to Washington until statehood. The 1863&ndash;65 Montana Vigilantes &mdash; who hanged Sheriff Henry Plummer in 1864 &mdash; show how quickly informal lynch law filled gaps where official law was thin.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Frontier law had three overlapping layers: US marshals (federal, territory-wide), county sheriffs (elected) and town marshals (appointed by mayors), with the army as a fourth presence in many districts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Federal influence grew after the Civil War because of a stronger central state, the railroads needing national protection, and territorial governments answering to Washington &mdash; with Pinkerton&rsquo;s National Detective Agency (founded 1850) reinforcing the official system on behalf of railroads and banks.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Statehood &mdash; Nebraska 1867, Colorado 1876, six states in 1889 &mdash; was the milestone that shifted authority from federal marshals to elected state officials, though vigilance committees such as the 1863&ndash;65 Montana Vigilantes showed that lynch law kept reappearing wherever official law was thin.</li>
</ul>
