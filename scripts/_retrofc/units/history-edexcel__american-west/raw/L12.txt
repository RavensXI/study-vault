<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Boom to Bust on the Open Range</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Through the 1870s and early 1880s the Plains had become a cattle gold mine. The long drives from Texas to Abilene, the new refrigerated railcars and a steady London market for chilled beef pulled British and eastern American investment onto the open range. By 1885 a single Wyoming county might run 100,000 head of cattle on grass that, ten years earlier, had carried buffalo. Speculators talked of doubling money in a season. The land&rsquo;s carrying capacity was being ignored.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Two warnings were already on the books. Joseph Glidden&rsquo;s 1874 patent for <dfn class="term" data-def="Twisted wire with sharp barbs, patented by Joseph Glidden in 1874, used to fence the open range cheaply.">barbed wire</dfn> was being strung across homestead claims faster than the cattle barons could buy them out. And the dry summer of 1886 had left grass thin going into the winter. What broke the system was the weather.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two reasons the open range collapsed and one piece of evidence for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The open-range cattle industry collapsed for two linked reasons: ranchers had overstocked the grass during the 1880s boom, and the &lsquo;Big Die-Up&rsquo; winter of 1886&ndash;87 killed 50&ndash;90% of cattle in some Wyoming and Montana herds. After 1887 ranchers shifted to smaller, fenced ranches with hay-fed winters &mdash; the era of long drives and unbranded mavericks was over.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Big Die-Up, 1886&ndash;87</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The winter that began in November 1886 was the worst in living memory on the northern Plains. Blizzards drove temperatures to &minus;40&deg;C across Wyoming, Montana and the Dakotas. Cattle drifted south, piled into draws and froze in their thousands. Some ranches lost 90% of their stock; even careful operators lost half. When the spring thaw came in 1887 the carcasses were so thick along fence lines that contemporaries described whole valleys filled with rotting cattle. British investors pulled their money out within months.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The shape of ranching changed permanently. Ranchers who survived enclosed their land with barbed wire, cut and stored hay through the summer, and bred smaller numbers of higher-quality cattle that could be sheltered in the winter. The era of the long drive ended. So did the era of the unbranded <dfn class="term" data-def="An unbranded calf or stray, traditionally claimed on the open range by whoever first roped it.">maverick</dfn> &mdash; on a fenced ranch every animal had an owner.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">New Farming on the Plains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Alongside the cattle bust, settled farming was finally taking hold. The mechanical reaper and the combine harvester, John Deere&rsquo;s steel plough that broke the dense Plains sod, refrigerated railcars that shipped grain and beef east, and the huge <dfn class="term" data-def="Very large mechanised wheat farms in the Dakotas, owned by syndicates and run by paid managers and seasonal labour.">bonanza farms</dfn> of the Dakotas turned the High Plains into a wheat-growing region within a decade. The same railroads that had carried cattle north now carried wheat, beef and milk east to Chicago and on to Britain. Farming and ranching no longer competed for the same open grass; barbed wire had separated them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Hollywood paints the late frontier as ungoverned territory until heroic sheriffs arrived. The reality is more complicated: by 1880 most cow towns and mining camps had paid town marshals, county sheriffs and access to federal courts. The OK Corral was not a fight over an absence of law &mdash; it was a fight inside the law, between Earp&rsquo;s town marshals and the Clantons&rsquo; rural cattle interests. Likewise the Johnson County War of 1892 was triggered precisely because the cattle barons could no longer get their way through elected sheriffs and juries. The story of this lesson is the limits of frontier law where money and politics outran it &mdash; not its absence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Showdown Lawmen: Earp, Holliday and the OK Corral</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By 1881 Tombstone, Arizona, was a silver-boom town with around 7,000 people and three of the Earp brothers wearing badges. Wyatt Earp was assistant town marshal and a part-owner of saloon and gambling interests. Doc Holliday, the dentist-turned-gambler, was a personal friend. The Clantons and McLaurys were rural cattlemen who had clashed with the Earps over rustling allegations and a stagecoach robbery investigation. On 26 October 1881 the two sides met behind the OK Corral. Around 30 seconds of gunfire left three of the Clanton-McLaury party dead and Virgil and Morgan Earp wounded.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The OK Corral was small &mdash; six men, three deaths &mdash; but its significance was symbolic. It showed that the difference between &lsquo;lawman&rsquo; and &lsquo;outlaw&rsquo; on the late frontier was often a question of who held the badge that morning. Earp moved across his career between marshal, gambler, saloon-owner and unofficial enforcer. The legal system in Tombstone struggled to convict either side: a coroner&rsquo;s inquest cleared the Earps; a follow-up hearing found in their favour again. The Earps left Arizona within a year.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Billy the Kid and the Lincoln County War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Three years earlier, in New Mexico Territory, the same blurred frontier law had produced Henry McCarty &mdash; better known as Billy the Kid. The Lincoln County War (1878) was a feud between two rival merchant-rancher factions that had captured the county&rsquo;s sheriff, courthouse and federal contracts. Billy fought on the losing side. By 1880 he was wanted for the murder of a sheriff and a string of cattle thefts. Sheriff Pat Garrett tracked him to Fort Sumner and shot him there in July 1881. Billy was 21.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Billy&rsquo;s actual importance was modest &mdash; he was one outlaw among many. What made him a national figure was the speed at which the Lincoln County War became a story about how local government could be captured by competing rancher factions, and how the elected sheriff might be the most dangerous man in the room. The OK Corral and the Lincoln County War were the same problem in two states: the official law was now in place, but it was still negotiable for those who had money or guns.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Johnson County War, April 1892</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">By the early 1890s the cattle industry had been hit twice over: the Big Die-Up had wiped out winter herds, and homesteaders were claiming and fencing land the cattle barons used to graze freely. In Wyoming&rsquo;s Johnson County, members of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A cattle barons&rsquo; trade association in Wyoming that drew up &lsquo;death lists&rsquo; of suspected rustlers and hired Texas gunmen during the 1892 Johnson County War.">Wyoming Stock Growers Association</dfn> believed elected sheriffs and juries were now packed with small ranchers and homesteaders sympathetic to rustlers. They drew up a list of around 70 men they accused of cattle theft and hired around 50 Texas gunmen to ride in and execute them.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">The expedition reached Wyoming in early April 1892 and killed two suspected rustlers at the KC Ranch before being surrounded by a hastily organised local posse of 200&ndash;300 men. The cattle barons&rsquo; force took refuge at the TA Ranch and was only rescued when President Benjamin Harrison ordered the US Cavalry to intervene. Back in court the trial was packed with friendly jurors paid for by the Stock Growers; every defendant was acquitted. The state legislature, dominated by ranchers, blocked any further investigation. The Johnson County War became a national scandal that exposed exactly how far frontier law could be bent when wealth and politics were on the same side.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the Johnson County War shows the limits of frontier law and order in the 1890s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Johnson County War (April 1892) saw the Wyoming Stock Growers Association hire 50 Texas gunmen to kill suspected rustlers; only US Cavalry intervention prevented a massacre, and a packed jury acquitted every defendant. The OK Corral (26 October 1881) and the Lincoln County War (1878) had already shown that being on the losing side of a feud could matter more than being inside the law.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Overstocking and the &lsquo;Big Die-Up&rsquo; winter of 1886&ndash;87 ended the open range; ranchers shifted to smaller, fenced ranches with hay-fed winters, and barbed wire (Glidden 1874) replaced the long drive and the unbranded maverick.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The OK Corral (26 October 1881) and the Lincoln County War (1878) showed the limits of frontier law: the difference between marshal and outlaw could be a badge or a feud rather than a clear principle.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The Johnson County War of April 1892 saw the Wyoming Stock Growers Association hire 50 Texas gunmen against suspected rustlers; only US Cavalry intervention and a packed jury kept it from being a massacre, exposing how far elected law could still be bent for cattle-baron money.</li>
</ul>
