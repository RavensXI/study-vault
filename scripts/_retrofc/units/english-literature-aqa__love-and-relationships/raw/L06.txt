--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sonnets, dramatic monologues, free verse — how form, structure, and language shape meaning.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Form Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the AQA exam, AO2 rewards analysis of the poet’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The conscious choices a writer makes about language, form, and structure to create specific effects on the reader.">methods</dfn>. Understanding why a poet chooses a sonnet over free verse, or a dramatic monologue over a lyric, is essential for top-band answers. The form of a poem is not decoration — it is part of the meaning.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Sonnet Tradition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 14-line poem, traditionally associated with love, with a specific rhyme scheme and metre.">sonnet</dfn> is the traditional form for love poetry (14 lines, iambic pentameter). Three poems in the anthology use or subvert the sonnet form:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Sonnet 29 (Barrett Browning)</strong> follows the Petrarchan sonnet structure (octave + sestet) with a volta, reflecting the tradition of devoted love poetry. The form matches the content: sincere, structured devotion. <strong>Love’s Philosophy (Shelley)</strong> is not strictly a sonnet but uses 16 lines with a song-like ABAB scheme — its regular form supports the logical, persuasive argument. <strong>Mother, any distance (Armitage)</strong> loosely follows sonnet structure but breaks it: the poem is 15 lines, not 14, and the final line runs over — mirroring the son exceeding the mother’s reach, breaking free of the “form” she represents.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does Armitage subvert the sonnet form in 'Mother, any distance'?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Armitage’s 15-line poem breaks the 14-line sonnet convention — the extra line mirrors the son going beyond the mother’s reach. Using a love-poem form for a parent-child relationship subverts expectations. Form becomes a metaphor for breaking free.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Dramatic Monologues</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem in which a single character speaks, revealing their personality and motivations — often unintentionally exposing their flaws.">dramatic monologue</dfn> gives voice to a single speaker, revealing character through their own words. The reader must read between the lines to see what the speaker cannot (or will not) acknowledge.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Porphyria’s Lover (Browning)</strong> uses the dramatic monologue to expose the speaker’s delusion: he describes murder in a calm, rational tone, revealing psychopathy. The form creates chilling irony — the speaker thinks he is justified, but the reader sees madness. <strong>The Farmer’s Bride (Mew)</strong> uses the same form to show frustrated desire: the farmer’s perspective is the only one we hear, and his wife is silenced. The monologue form itself becomes an act of control.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Key Structural Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Enjambment</strong> (lines running on without punctuation) creates momentum and mirrors flowing emotion: Barrett Browning’s thoughts “twine and bud” across lines. <strong>Caesura</strong> (mid-line pauses) creates sudden stops: Hardy’s “We stood by a pond that winter day,” pauses before revealing the bleak setting. <strong>Stanza breaks</strong> can mark shifts in time, mood, or focus: Heaney’s final stanza in “Follower” reverses the entire poem.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three structural techniques and give one example from the anthology for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key structural techniques to discuss: enjambment (momentum, flowing thought), caesura (pause, emphasis), volta (turning point in sonnets), cyclical structure (entrapment), stanza shifts (changed perspective). Always link the technique to its <strong>effect on meaning</strong>, not just identify it.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Form is meaning: sonnets for love, dramatic monologues for character revelation, free verse for spontaneity and flow.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Subverting form is as significant as following it: Armitage’s 15-line “sonnet” breaks free, just as the son breaks from the mother.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Always explain the <strong>effect</strong> of a technique, not just identify it — this is what earns AO2 marks in the comparison essay.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Never just <em>identify</em> a technique — always explain its <strong>effect</strong>. Instead of “Sheers uses a simile,” write: “Sheers’s simile ‘like swans, / settled after flight’ connects the couple’s reconciliation to the natural devotion of swans that mate for life, suggesting their bond is instinctive and enduring.” This analysis earns AO2 marks because it explains <em>how</em> the technique creates meaning.</p>
