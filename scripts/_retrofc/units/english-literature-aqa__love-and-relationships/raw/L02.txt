--- DESCRIPTION ---
Four poems about parents and children — love, distance, memory, and letting go.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">“Before You Were Mine” — Carol Ann Duffy (1993)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Duffy imagines her mother’s life before she was born — young, glamorous, carefree. The speaker pictures her mother “in your polka-dot dress … on the corner” laughing with friends. The title claims <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of belonging or entitlement, as if one person has ownership of another's time, attention, or identity.">possessiveness</dfn>: the mother existed “before you were mine” implies the speaker now owns her. Duffy explores how parenthood transforms identity — the mother became “mine” and lost her independent self.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The poem uses vivid sensory details: “Your polka-dot dress blows round your legs. Marilyn.” The full stop before “Marilyn” gives the word the force of revelation — the mother is compared to Marilyn Monroe, suggesting glamour and desirability. The speaker’s tone is admiring but tinged with guilt: “I knew you would dance like that.” She knows her birth changed her mother forever. Key technique: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The position from which a story or poem is told; the speaker's relationship to the events affects the reader's understanding.">perspective shift</dfn> — Duffy writes as a child imagining a past she never witnessed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does the title 'Before You Were Mine' suggest about the mother-daughter relationship?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The title reveals possessiveness — the speaker claims ownership of the mother. The poem explores how parenthood transforms identity: the mother’s glamorous, independent past was surrendered when she became a mother. The tone blends admiration with guilt.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">“Mother, any distance” — Simon Armitage (1993)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Armitage uses the extended metaphor of a measuring tape to explore a son leaving home. The mother holds one end while the son walks away: “You at the zero-end, me with the spool of tape.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison developed across an entire poem, where one thing is consistently described in terms of another.">extended metaphor</dfn> is brilliantly physical — the tape connects them but must eventually break as the son gains independence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">“I reach towards a hatch that opens on an endless sky / to fall or fly” — the son faces the open world. “Fall or fly” captures the uncertainty of independence: it could go either way. The mother’s “fingertips still pinch / the last one-hundredth of an inch” — she holds on as long as possible, reluctant to let go. The poem captures the universal tension between a parent’s desire to protect and a child’s need for freedom.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Day Lewis watches his son walk away to school for the first time. The memory is eighteen years old but “has not worn away” — the pain of separation is permanent. The son is described “like a satellite / Wrenched from its orbit” — the <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison using 'like' or 'as' to highlight a similarity between two different things.">simile</dfn> suggests the child is being torn from the parent’s gravitational pull. The final lines resolve with acceptance: “How selfhood begins with a walking away, / And love is proved in the letting go.” This <dfn class="term" data-def="A statement that appears contradictory but reveals a deeper truth.">paradox</dfn> — love is proved by releasing, not holding — is the poem’s central wisdom.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">“Follower” — Seamus Heaney (1966)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Heaney describes his childhood admiration for his father, a skilled ploughman. The father is presented as physically powerful: “His shoulders globed like a full sail strung / Between the shafts and the furrow.” The simile “globed like a full sail” makes the father seem heroic, almost mythical. The child “stumbled in his hobnailed wake” — clumsy, small, literally following in his father’s footsteps.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The final stanza reverses the poem: “But today / It is my father who keeps stumbling / Behind me, and will not go away.” The roles have switched — the father is now old and dependent, following the son. The verb “stumbling” transfers from child to father. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A reversal of situation, expectation, or roles — where the end-state is the opposite of the starting position.">reversal</dfn> is both tender and uncomfortable: the son is frustrated (“will not go away”) even as he remembers his own clumsy devotion.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how Heaney reverses the roles of father and son in 'Follower.'">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Heaney’s “Follower” reverses roles: the child who once stumbled behind his heroic father now finds the ageing father stumbling behind him. The poem explores how family relationships change over time — and the guilt that comes with finding a parent burdensome.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Armitage vs Day Lewis:</strong> Both explore separation — a child leaving a parent. Armitage writes from the child’s perspective (“fall or fly”); Day Lewis writes from the parent’s (“love is proved in the letting go”). Both capture the tension between protection and freedom.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Heaney vs Day Lewis:</strong> Both present fathers, but from opposite directions. Heaney moves from childhood admiration to adult guilt; Day Lewis moves from parental pain to acceptance. Both use physical imagery (ploughing, walking) to embody emotional experience.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Duffy explores possessive love and the guilt of knowing motherhood changed her mother’s identity forever.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Armitage and Day Lewis both explore separation but from opposite perspectives — child leaving vs parent watching.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Heaney’s role reversal captures how family relationships shift over time, blending admiration with guilt.</li></ul>
