--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to structure a 30-mark comparison — finding links, weaving analysis, and using connectives.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Comparison Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Paper 2, Section B gives you one named poem (printed on the page) and asks you to compare it with one poem of your choice from the anthology. The question is worth <strong>30 marks</strong>: AO1 (12 marks — ideas and comparisons), AO2 (12 marks — language, form, structure analysis), AO3 (6 marks — context). You have approximately <strong>45 minutes</strong>. The named poem is <strong>open book</strong> (printed), but your chosen poem is <strong>closed book</strong> — you must recall it from memory.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How are the 30 marks split across AO1, AO2, and AO3?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO1 = 12 marks (ideas, interpretations, comparison). AO2 = 12 marks (methods — language, form, structure). AO3 = 6 marks (context). The majority of marks reward analysis and comparison, not plot summary. AO2 is equally weighted with AO1 — you <em>must</em> discuss methods to access the top band.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Choosing Your Comparison Poem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">You must choose wisely. The best comparison poem is one that shares the <strong>theme</strong> of the question but takes a <strong>different approach</strong>. Similarities give you connection points; differences give you analytical depth. Prepare 3–4 “go-to” poems that you know well enough to quote from memory.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Pre-planned pairings of poems that share themes but differ in approach, tone, or methods — prepared before the exam for quick selection.">Prepared pairings</dfn> save time in the exam. For example: if the named poem is about loss, you might choose “Neutral Tones” (bitter disillusionment) or “Eden Rock” (serene acceptance). If it is about desire, choose “Singh Song!” (joyful) or “Porphyria’s Lover” (possessive). The greater the contrast, the richer the comparison.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Essay Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Introduction (2–3 sentences):</strong> State the theme and your argument. Name both poems and their poets. Example: “Both Sheers in ‘Winter Swans’ and Hardy in ‘Neutral Tones’ use pathetic fallacy to present strained relationships, but while Sheers’s couple find reconciliation through nature, Hardy’s speaker remains trapped in bitter disillusionment.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Body paragraphs (3–4):</strong> Each paragraph should discuss <em>both</em> poems, organised around an aspect of the theme (not poem-by-poem). Use <dfn class="term" data-def="Words or phrases that signal comparison or contrast between ideas, such as 'similarly,' 'in contrast,' 'while... however,' 'both poets suggest.'">comparative connectives</dfn>: “Similarly,” “In contrast,” “While [Poet A] presents…, [Poet B] suggests…” Each paragraph needs: a point comparing ideas, quotations from both poems, analysis of methods (AO2), and embedded context (AO3).</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write an introduction that names two poems, states the theme, and outlines your argument in 2-3 sentences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Structure: Introduction (thesis + both poems named) → 3–4 paragraphs (both poems in every paragraph, organised by aspect of theme) → Conclusion (link to poets’ wider messages). Never write about one poem then the other — <strong>weave</strong> them together throughout.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Model Paragraph Structure (PECLC)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>P</strong>oint: State the comparison. “Both poets use natural imagery to present emotional states, but with contrasting outcomes.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>E</strong>vidence (Poem 1): Quote from the named poem. “Sheers describes the couple’s hands as ‘like swans, settled after flight.’”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>C</strong>omment (AO2): Analyse the method. “The simile connects the couple to swans that ‘mate for life,’ suggesting their bond is instinctive and enduring.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>L</strong>ink to Poem 2: Compare with your chosen poem. “In contrast, Hardy uses pathetic fallacy to present love as drained and lifeless: ‘the sun was white, as though chidden of God.’”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>C</strong>ontext: Embed AO3. “Hardy’s Victorian pessimism, shaped by personal disillusionment, contrasts with Sheers’s modern, more optimistic view that relationships can recover.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Contrast:</strong> In contrast, However, While [Poet A]…, [Poet B]…, Conversely, On the other hand</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Development:</strong> [Poet B] takes this further by…, [Poet A]’s approach differs because…, This contrasts sharply with…</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">The comparison question is 30 marks (AO1: 12, AO2: 12, AO3: 6). The named poem is printed; your chosen poem is from memory.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Weave both poems together in every paragraph — never write about them separately. Use comparative connectives throughout.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Prepare 3–4 “go-to” poems with memorised quotations. Choose poems that share the theme but take different approaches.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>The single biggest mistake is writing about one poem, then the other. One well-developed comparison is worth more than five shallow ones.</p>
