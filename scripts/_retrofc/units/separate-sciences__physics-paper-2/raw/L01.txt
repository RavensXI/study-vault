--- DESCRIPTION ---
Types of forces, gravity and weight calculations, and how to find the resultant force.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Forces?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A push or pull that acts on an object, measured in newtons (N).">force</dfn> is a push or a pull that acts on an object. Forces are measured in newtons (N) and are vector quantities — they have both a magnitude (size) and a direction.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Forces can be <dfn class="term" data-def="Forces that act between objects that are physically touching.">contact forces</dfn> or <dfn class="term" data-def="Forces that act between objects that are not touching, such as gravity and magnetism.">non-contact forces</dfn>. Contact forces include friction, air resistance, tension, and normal contact force. Non-contact forces include gravitational, electrostatic, and magnetic forces.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Newton's third law: what happens when two objects interact and what can you say about the forces?"><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A force is an interaction between two objects. When two objects interact, each exerts a force on the other — these are equal in size and opposite in direction (Newton’s third law).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Gravity and Weight</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The force of attraction between any two masses; on Earth it pulls objects toward the centre of the planet.">Gravity</dfn> is a non-contact force that attracts any two objects with mass. The strength of gravity depends on the masses of the objects and the distance between them.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The force acting on an object due to gravity, measured in newtons.">Weight</dfn> is the force of gravity acting on an object. It depends on the mass and the gravitational field strength:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$W = mg$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where \(W\) is weight in newtons, \(m\) is mass in kilograms, and \(g\) is gravitational field strength in N/kg. On Earth, \(g \approx 10\) N/kg. On the Moon, \(g \approx 1.6\) N/kg.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. It does not change with location.">Mass</dfn> is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. It is the same everywhere. Weight is a force and depends on the gravitational field strength at that location. An astronaut with a mass of 80 kg weighs 800 N on Earth (\(80 \times 10\)) but only 128 N on the Moon (\(80 \times 1.6\)).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Weight is measured using a <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that measures force by the extension of a calibrated spring, also called a spring balance.">Newton meter</dfn> (spring balance). Mass is measured using a balance.</p>
</div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Resultant Forces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">When multiple forces act on an object, they can be replaced by a single force that has the same effect. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A single force that has the same effect as all the individual forces acting on an object combined.">resultant force</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain how to calculate resultant force when forces act in same direction versus opposite directions."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>If forces act in the same direction, add them. If forces act in opposite directions, subtract the smaller from the larger. The resultant force acts in the direction of the larger force.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n15">If the resultant force on an object is zero, the object is in <dfn class="term" data-def="The state of an object when all forces acting on it are balanced, so there is no change in motion.">equilibrium</dfn>. This means it is either stationary or moving at a constant velocity. If the resultant force is not zero, the object will accelerate in the direction of the resultant force.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing all the forces acting on a single object, represented as arrows.">free body diagram</dfn> shows all the forces acting on a single object. Each force is drawn as an arrow starting from the object, with the length of the arrow representing the force’s magnitude and the direction showing which way it acts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">For example, a book on a table has two forces: weight acting downwards and the normal contact force acting upwards. If these are equal, the resultant force is zero and the book stays still.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Forces are vector quantities; they can be contact (friction, tension) or non-contact (gravity, magnetic)</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Weight = mass \(\times\) gravitational field strength (\(W = mg\)); mass stays the same, weight changes with location</li><li data-narration-id="n23">The resultant force determines motion: zero resultant means equilibrium; non-zero resultant causes acceleration</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When a question asks about forces, always draw a free body diagram first. Label all forces clearly with their names and sizes. Remember: weight and mass are different — weight is a force (in newtons) and mass is the amount of matter (in kilograms). If the question mentions “resultant force is zero,” state that the object is in equilibrium — either stationary or at constant velocity.</p>
