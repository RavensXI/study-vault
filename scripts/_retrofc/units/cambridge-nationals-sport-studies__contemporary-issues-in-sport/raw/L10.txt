What NGBs do for their sport, plus technology in sport — the benefits and the drawbacks.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are National Governing Bodies?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An organisation that governs and regulates a specific sport within a country, responsible for rules, competitions, development, coaching standards and anti-doping.">National Governing Bodies (NGBs)</dfn> are the organisations that run individual sports within the UK. Each sport has its own NGB, and they are responsible for everything from setting the rules of the game to ensuring players are protected and the sport continues to grow. Named examples you need to know include: <strong>The FA</strong> (Football Association, governing football in England), <strong>RFU</strong> (Rugby Football Union, governing rugby union in England), <strong>ECB</strong> (England and Wales Cricket Board, governing cricket), and <strong>LTA</strong> (Lawn Tennis Association, governing tennis in Great Britain). Others include England Athletics and British Cycling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name four named NGBs with the sport each governs. Can you also name one function each NGB carries out?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four NGBs you must be able to name are: The FA (football), RFU (rugby union), ECB (cricket) and LTA (tennis). Each governs its sport by promoting participation, developing coaching and officiating, organising competitions, amending rules, and supporting anti-doping.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Functions of an NGB</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">NGBs carry out several key functions. <strong>Promoting participation</strong>: NGBs run campaigns and initiatives to attract new players and retain existing ones &mdash; the FA&rsquo;s &ldquo;Biggest Kick&rdquo; grassroots football programme is one example of community-level recruitment. <strong>Developing coaching and officiating</strong>: NGBs set standards and deliver qualifications for coaches and referees, ensuring the sport is well led at every level. The RFU, for example, manages the England Coaching Framework and referee development pathways at county level.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Organising competitions</strong>: NGBs run the competition structures from national leagues and cups down to regional and club competitions. The ECB manages domestic cricket from the Hundred through to county cricket and recreational leagues. <strong>Amending rules</strong>: NGBs can update playing regulations to improve safety, spectator experience or fairness &mdash; law changes in rugby union (restrictions on high tackles) have been driven by the RFU and its international counterpart. <strong>Anti-doping</strong>: NGBs work alongside UKAD to enforce anti-doping policies in their sport and educate athletes about clean sport.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Technology in Sport &mdash; An Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Technology has transformed modern sport from multiple directions: it helps athletes <strong>perform better</strong>, officials <strong>make fairer decisions</strong>, medical teams <strong>keep players safer</strong>, and spectators <strong>enjoy a richer experience</strong>. Examiners want students to analyse technology through at least two of these lenses, not just describe one device.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and name two pieces of sports technology, then for each one identify one positive AND one negative effect.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Technology in sport affects four groups: athletes (performance and safety), officials (accuracy of decisions), spectators (engagement and experience), and the sport itself (reputation and flow of the game). When evaluating technology, always consider the impact on more than one of these groups.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Technology Helping Officials</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Video Assistant Referee &mdash; a technology used in football that allows officials to review key decisions (goals, penalties, red cards and mistaken identity) using video footage.">VAR (Video Assistant Referee)</dfn> was introduced into the Premier League in the 2019&ndash;20 season. VAR allows a team of officials in a video operations room to review four categories of decision: goals, penalty incidents, red card offences, and cases of mistaken identity. Supporters argue that VAR eliminates clear and obvious errors that previously changed match outcomes unjustly. Critics argue that it disrupts the flow of the game, introduces inconsistency in how &ldquo;clear and obvious&rdquo; is interpreted, and undermines the spontaneity of goal celebrations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A ball-tracking technology using multiple cameras to accurately determine whether a ball has crossed the goal line in football, or to call line decisions in tennis and cricket (DRS).">Hawk-Eye</dfn> operates differently in different sports. In tennis, it tracks ball trajectory to determine whether a shot landed in or out. In cricket, it is part of the Decision Review System (DRS), showing whether a delivery would have hit the stumps (LBW decisions). In football, GoalLine technology (similar in principle) determines definitively whether the ball has crossed the goal line. These systems remove human error from objective factual decisions &mdash; &ldquo;did it cross the line?&rdquo; &mdash; and are widely accepted as positive improvements.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>GPS performance vests</strong> are worn by football and rugby players in training and in some competitions, tracking distance covered, sprint speed, heart rate and work rate zones. Coaches use this data to monitor training loads and reduce injury risk. <strong>Prosthetic limbs</strong> &mdash; particularly carbon-fibre running blades used by Paralympic sprinters &mdash; are an example of technology dramatically transforming what was previously thought possible in athletic performance. <strong>Video analysis software</strong> allows coaches and athletes to break down technique, study opponents and identify weaknesses with a precision impossible by eye alone. <strong>Wearable sensors</strong> in rugby helmets measure impact force, helping medical staff identify potential concussion incidents in real time.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Technology and the Spectator Experience</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Technology also shapes how fans experience sport. Large stadium screens (known as <strong>jumbotrons</strong>) show replays, statistics and highlight close-ups, keeping spectators engaged even in the largest venues. Television broadcasting uses slow-motion cameras, ball-tracking graphics and data overlays (e.g. passing maps in football, wagon-wheel charts in cricket) to deepen understanding for viewers at home. <strong>Fan apps</strong> and in-stadium Wi-Fi allow spectators to access statistics, order food or watch replays in real time on their phones. These innovations have made watching sport more informative and immersive than at any previous time.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Negatives of Technology in Sport</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Despite its benefits, technology in sport creates problems. <strong>Cost and unequal access</strong>: VAR systems, Hawk-Eye installations and GPS tracking kits are expensive. Premier League clubs can afford them; grassroots clubs cannot. This creates a technological divide between elite and amateur sport that can feel unfair. <strong>Flow of the game</strong>: VAR in football has been widely criticised for long delays during which crowd atmosphere deflates and the natural momentum of the match is lost. <strong>Undermining officials</strong>: when technology frequently overturns referee decisions, confidence in human officiating can erode &mdash; both among spectators and among officials themselves. <strong>Data privacy</strong>: GPS vests and biometric trackers generate detailed personal data about athletes that raises questions about storage, ownership and consent.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">NGBs govern individual sports in the UK. Named examples: The FA (football), RFU (rugby union), ECB (cricket), LTA (tennis). Key functions: promoting participation, developing coaching, organising competitions, amending rules, supporting anti-doping.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Technology helps athletes (GPS vests, prosthetics), officials (VAR, Hawk-Eye, GoalLine technology) and spectators (stadium screens, broadcast graphics). These improvements increase accuracy, safety and engagement.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Technology also has negatives: high cost limits access to elite clubs only, VAR disrupts game flow, repeated overturns undermine officials&rsquo; authority, and biometric data raises privacy concerns. Evaluation must cover both sides.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">The 8-mark Evaluate question on technology might ask: &ldquo;Has the introduction of VAR improved football?&rdquo; To reach the top band, you need to argue <strong>both sides</strong> (VAR reduces clear errors vs. disrupts flow and consistency), include a <strong>specific applied example</strong> (e.g. a Premier League decision reviewed or overturned, or the precise categories VAR covers), and reach a <strong>justified conclusion</strong> that is more nuanced than simply &ldquo;yes&rdquo; or &ldquo;no&rdquo; (e.g. VAR improves factual accuracy but has not improved the overall experience for many fans). For NGB questions, always name the governing body and pair it with a real function &mdash; &ldquo;The FA promotes participation&rdquo; earns far more than &ldquo;the NGB promotes the sport.&rdquo;</p>
