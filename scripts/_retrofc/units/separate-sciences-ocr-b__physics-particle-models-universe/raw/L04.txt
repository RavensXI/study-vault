How models help us understand a universe far older than us. Key concepts and applications explained.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Our Solar System and Beyond</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sun and all the objects bound to it by gravity, including eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets.">solar system</dfn> consists of the Sun and all the objects gravitationally bound to it — the eight planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune), their moons, and countless smaller bodies such as asteroids and comets. The planets orbit the Sun in nearly circular paths, all in the same direction. Gravity provides the centripetal force that keeps them in orbit — the gravitational pull continuously changes the direction of each planet's velocity, keeping it in a circular path without changing its speed.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Our solar system sits within the <dfn class="term" data-def="A gravitationally bound system of stars, gas, dust and dark matter. Galaxies range enormously in size — the Milky Way contains roughly 100–400 billion stars.">galaxy</dfn> called the Milky Way. The universe contains billions of galaxies, each containing hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of stars. On this scale, distances are so vast that light — travelling at roughly 300 000 km per second — takes years, millennia, or even billions of years to cross them.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the eight planets of the solar system in order from the Sun, then state what keeps them in orbit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Planets orbit the Sun in (almost) circular paths because gravity provides a centripetal force. An object in a circular orbit moves at constant speed but changing velocity — its direction changes continuously, so velocity changes even though speed does not. For a stable orbit, if the orbital speed changes, the radius of the orbit must also change. The further a planet is from the Sun, the slower its orbital speed and the longer its year.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Life Cycle of a Star</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n6">Stars form from vast clouds of gas (mainly hydrogen) and dust. Gravity pulls the cloud inward: as the gas is compressed, work is done on it, and the particle model predicts the temperature rises (compressed gas gets hotter because the particles collide more forcefully as they are pushed closer together). When the core temperature rises sufficiently, hydrogen nuclei gain enough kinetic energy to overcome their electrostatic repulsion and undergo <dfn class="term" data-def="The joining of two light atomic nuclei (typically hydrogen isotopes) to form a heavier nucleus, releasing large amounts of energy.">nuclear fusion</dfn> — fusing into helium nuclei and releasing enormous energy.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This energy release creates an outward radiation pressure that balances the inward gravitational collapse. A star in this stable phase — like our Sun — is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A star in the stable phase of its life cycle, during which it fuses hydrogen into helium in its core, producing energy that balances gravitational collapse.">main sequence star</dfn>. Our Sun has been on the main sequence for roughly 4.6 billion years and is expected to remain there for a similar period.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What Happens When a Star Leaves the Main Sequence</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">When a star&rsquo;s hydrogen fuel runs low, fusion can no longer balance gravity. The core contracts and heats further, causing the outer layers to expand dramatically. The star becomes a <dfn class="term" data-def="A stage in stellar evolution in which a star has exhausted its core hydrogen and expanded to many times its original size, with a cooler, red outer surface.">red giant</dfn> (for a star like the Sun) or a red supergiant (for a much more massive star).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A star like the Sun eventually sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving behind a dense, hot remnant called a white dwarf, which slowly cools over billions of years.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">A much more massive star ends its life more violently. Fusion in a massive star&rsquo;s core builds up progressively heavier elements until iron is reached — iron cannot release energy by fusion. The core then collapses catastrophically and the outer layers are expelled in a <dfn class="term" data-def="An explosive stellar event in which a massive star's core collapses and its outer layers are violently expelled, briefly outshining entire galaxies and scattering heavy elements through space.">supernova</dfn> explosion. The remnant may become a neutron star or a black hole.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Red-Shift and the Expanding Universe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When astronomers examine light from distant galaxies, they find that the dark absorption lines in the spectrum are shifted towards longer, redder wavelengths compared to the same lines measured in a laboratory. This phenomenon is called <dfn class="term" data-def="An increase in the observed wavelength of light from a source that is moving away from the observer; the further a galaxy is, the greater its red-shift.">red-shift</dfn>. It arises because the galaxy is moving away from us: as light travels through expanding space, its wavelength is stretched.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A key observation is that more distant galaxies show a greater red-shift — they are moving away from us faster. This pattern holds in every direction, which means the universe as a whole is expanding: space itself is growing, carrying galaxies apart with it. Scientists use this relationship between distance and recession speed as evidence for the expanding universe model.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what red-shift tells us about the motion of distant galaxies, and what conclusion scientists draw from the fact that more distant galaxies show greater red-shift.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Red-shift: light from receding galaxies is stretched to longer (redder) wavelengths. More distant galaxies have greater red-shift, meaning they are moving away faster. This is evidence that the universe is expanding.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Big Bang Model (IaS3 &mdash; Scientific Models)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">If the universe is currently expanding, then tracing back in time suggests it was once much smaller, hotter and denser. The scientific explanation for the origin of the universe is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific model proposing that the universe began from an extremely hot, dense state approximately 14 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.">Big Bang model</dfn>. According to this model, the universe began roughly 14 billion years ago in a state of extreme temperature and density, and has been expanding and cooling ever since.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The acceptance of the Big Bang model is a good example of how scientific models develop as evidence accumulates (IaS3). When Edwin Hubble published his observations of galactic red-shifts in 1929, showing that galaxies were receding at speeds proportional to their distance, it provided the first strong observational support for an expanding universe. The model became far more secure in 1965 when Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson detected a faint microwave glow coming uniformly from all directions in the sky — the <dfn class="term" data-def="Faint microwave radiation detected uniformly in all directions, interpreted as the cooled remnant of radiation from the early, extremely hot universe shortly after the Big Bang.">cosmic microwave background (CMB)</dfn>. This radiation is interpreted as the cooled remnant of the intense heat of the early universe.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Improved Telescopes Strengthened the Big Bang Model</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Over the last century, telescope technology has improved dramatically. Ground-based observatories can detect visible light from billions of light-years away. Space-based telescopes can observe in wavelength ranges — infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, microwave — blocked by Earth&rsquo;s atmosphere. Removing the atmosphere also eliminates atmospheric blurring, allowing much finer detail to be resolved.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Each new generation of observational data has refined our picture of the early universe, the large-scale structure of galaxies, and the expansion rate. These improved data have consistently supported and strengthened the Big Bang model, rather than overturning it — illustrating how accumulating evidence increases scientific confidence in a model (IaS3).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the two main pieces of evidence for the Big Bang model, identifying the scientists associated with each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two key pieces of evidence for the Big Bang: (1) Hubble&rsquo;s 1929 observations showing that galactic recession speed increases with distance — red-shift evidence for an expanding universe. (2) Penzias and Wilson&rsquo;s 1965 detection of the cosmic microwave background — the cooled remnant of radiation from the early universe.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Stars form from collapsing gas clouds; nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium in the core provides energy that balances gravitational collapse during the main sequence. Massive stars end as supernovae; Sun-like stars become red giants then white dwarfs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Red-shift — the stretching of wavelengths from receding galaxies — shows that more distant galaxies move away faster, evidence that the universe is expanding.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The Big Bang model explains the expanding universe as originating from an extremely hot, dense state roughly 14 billion years ago. The cosmic microwave background (detected 1965) and galactic red-shifts (Hubble, 1929) are its two main observational pillars.</li>
</ul>
