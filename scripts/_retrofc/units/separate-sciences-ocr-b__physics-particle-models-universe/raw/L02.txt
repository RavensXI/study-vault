--- DESCRIPTION ---
How particles explain elasticity and stress in materials, including Hooke's law.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Forces on Solid Materials</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">To stretch, bend or compress a solid object, you need to apply <em>more than one</em> force. A single force acting on an object simply accelerates it — it takes at least two forces, acting in opposite directions, to change its shape. Think of pulling both ends of a rubber band: one hand pulls left, the other pulls right, and the band stretches between them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A model of matter in the solid state in which particles are held in fixed positions by forces between them; applying external forces changes particle separation and deforms the material.">particle model</dfn> explains why this happens. In a solid, particles are held in place by forces of attraction and repulsion between neighbouring particles. Stretching the material pulls particles further apart, increasing the attractive forces. Compressing it pushes particles closer together, increasing the repulsive forces. When the external force is removed, these inter-particle forces can restore the original shape — if the material has not been overstretched.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain, in particle terms, why stretching a spring gives it the ability to exert a restoring force.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stretching, bending or compressing a solid requires two forces acting in opposite directions. The inter-particle forces in the solid resist the change in particle separation, which is the microscopic origin of the restoring force.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Elastic and Plastic Deformation</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Deformation in which the material returns to its original shape and size when the applied force is removed.">Elastic deformation</dfn> occurs when a material springs back to its original shape once the force is removed. The inter-particle forces are strong enough to restore the original arrangement. A stretched spring or a stretched rubber band can both show elastic deformation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Deformation in which the material does not return to its original shape after the applied force is removed; the change is permanent.">Plastic deformation</dfn> occurs when the force is large enough to permanently rearrange the particles. Particles slip past each other into new positions, and when the force is removed the material stays in its new deformed shape. Bending a metal paperclip until it stays bent is an example of plastic deformation. The particle model distinguishes these two types: elastic deformation keeps inter-particle spacings within the restoring-force range, while plastic deformation moves particles to entirely new equilibrium positions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Hooke&rsquo;s Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Robert Hooke, working in the seventeenth century, discovered that for many springs and elastic materials, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The increase in the length of a spring or elastic material when a force is applied, equal to the stretched length minus the natural length.">extension</dfn> is directly proportional to the applied force — provided the material is not stretched too far. This is now called <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that extension is proportional to force for a spring or other elastic object, as long as the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.">Hooke&rsquo;s law</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The mathematical form of Hooke&rsquo;s law is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$F = k \times e$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Where \(F\) is the force applied in newtons (N), \(k\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the stiffness of a spring or elastic object, equal to force divided by extension, measured in N/m.">spring constant</dfn> in N/m, and \(e\) is the extension in metres (m). A stiffer spring has a higher spring constant: it requires a larger force to produce the same extension.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the Hooke's law equation including the symbol for spring constant and its unit. State what happens to the spring if the limit of proportionality is exceeded.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hooke&rsquo;s law: \(F = ke\). On a force&ndash;extension graph the relationship is a straight line through the origin up to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum force up to which a spring or material obeys Hooke's law; beyond this, the force–extension graph curves and the material may be permanently deformed.">limit of proportionality</dfn>. Beyond this point the graph curves, and if the spring is permanently deformed it has exceeded its elastic limit.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Hang a spring from a clamp stand and measure its natural (unstretched) length with a ruler. Add known masses one at a time and record the new length each time. Calculate the extension by subtracting the natural length from the stretched length.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Plot force (y-axis) against extension (x-axis). The straight-line section through the origin shows Hooke&rsquo;s law is obeyed; the gradient of this section equals the spring constant \(k\). When the line begins to curve upward, the limit of proportionality has been exceeded.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Worked example:</strong> A spring has a natural length of 15.0 cm. With a 200 g mass (weight ≈ 2.0 N) attached it stretches to 21.0 cm. Calculate the spring constant.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Extension: \(e = 21.0 - 15.0 = 6.0\) cm = 0.060 m. \(k = F \div e = 2.0 \div 0.060 \approx 33\) N/m.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Reduce parallax error when reading lengths by using a set square or a pointer at eye level. Always remove the loads gently after the experiment to check whether the spring has been permanently deformed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Linear and Non-Linear Behaviour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Not all elastic materials show a linear (straight-line) force&ndash;extension relationship. A rubber band, for example, can stretch a great deal before the force rises sharply &mdash; its force&ndash;extension graph is a curve throughout, not a straight line at the start. Both the spring (linear up to its limit) and the rubber band (non-linear throughout) are elastic, because both return to their original shape, but only the spring obeys Hooke&rsquo;s law in its initial range.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Elastic Potential Energy (HT)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
      <p data-narration-id="n21">When work is done to stretch or compress a spring within its limit of proportionality, energy is stored as <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy stored in a stretched or compressed elastic object that has not been deformed beyond its elastic limit.">elastic potential energy</dfn>. This can be recovered when the force is removed.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n22">For a spring obeying Hooke&rsquo;s law, the energy stored is:</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n23">$$E_e = \frac{1}{2} k e^2$$</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Worked example:</strong> A spring with \(k = 50\) N/m is stretched by 0.10 m. Energy stored = \(\frac{1}{2} \times 50 \times 0.10^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 50 \times 0.01 = 0.25\) J.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n25">On a force&ndash;extension graph, the elastic potential energy equals the area under the straight-line section — a triangle with area \(\frac{1}{2} \times F \times e\), which is the same as \(\frac{1}{2} ke^2\) since \(F = ke\).</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and distinguish between elastic and plastic deformation using a particle-level explanation in two or three sentences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elastic deformation: material returns to original shape; inter-particle forces restore the arrangement. Plastic deformation: particles slip permanently into new positions; the material does not recover. The boundary between them is the elastic limit of the material.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Stretching, compressing or bending a solid requires two forces in opposite directions; the inter-particle forces provide a restoring force when the deforming forces are removed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Hooke&rsquo;s law states \(F = ke\): extension is proportional to force up to the limit of proportionality, giving a straight-line graph through the origin with gradient \(k\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Elastic deformation is reversible; plastic deformation is permanent. Elastic potential energy \(E_e = \frac{1}{2}ke^2\) is stored in a stretched spring and can be fully recovered if the elastic limit is not exceeded.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">When using Hooke&rsquo;s law, always check that the question says the spring is &lsquo;within its limit of proportionality&rsquo; before applying \(F = ke\) &mdash; if the graph shows a curve, the equation does not apply. For the elastic potential energy equation \(E_e = \frac{1}{2}ke^2\), ensure the extension is in metres and the spring constant is in N/m. A common error is to halve the force rather than the whole expression — write out every step of the substitution.</p>
