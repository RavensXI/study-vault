--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why gas pressure changes when you compress or heat it, plus pressure in liquids.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Gas Pressure and the Particle Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A gas exerts <dfn class="term" data-def="A force per unit area exerted by gas particles colliding with the walls of their container, measured in pascals (Pa).">gas pressure</dfn> on any surface it is in contact with. The particle model explains why: gas particles are in constant, rapid, random motion. When they collide with the walls of a container, each collision exerts a tiny force on the wall. The total pressure is the combined effect of billions of these collisions per second.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">If the volume of the container is reduced at constant temperature, the particles are confined to a smaller space. They travel shorter distances between collisions with the walls, so they hit the walls more frequently. More collisions per second means a greater pressure. Conversely, increasing the volume at constant temperature reduces the collision frequency and lowers the pressure.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain in particle terms why squeezing a gas into half its volume doubles its pressure.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gas pressure is caused by particle collisions with container walls. Decreasing the volume (at constant temperature) increases the frequency of collisions and therefore increases the pressure. Increasing the volume decreases pressure.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Boyle&rsquo;s Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Robert Boyle, working in the 1660s, measured the relationship between pressure and volume for a trapped gas at constant temperature. He found that pressure and volume are inversely proportional: as one doubles, the other halves. This is now known as <dfn class="term" data-def="For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, pressure × volume = constant; as pressure increases, volume decreases proportionally.">Boyle&rsquo;s law</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The mathematical form is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where \(p_1\) and \(V_1\) are the initial pressure and volume, and \(p_2\) and \(V_2\) are the final pressure and volume. Pressure is measured in pascals (Pa) and volume in cubic metres (m³). Boyle&rsquo;s law applies only when temperature and the amount of gas are kept constant.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Applying Boyle&rsquo;s Law — Worked Example</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">A gas occupies 0.60 m³ at a pressure of 200 kPa. The gas is compressed at constant temperature until its volume is 0.20 m³. Calculate the new pressure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Using \(p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\):</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">\(p_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{200 \times 0.60}{0.20} = \frac{120}{0.20} = 600\) kPa</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Always check units are consistent before substituting. Pressure can be in Pa or kPa as long as both \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) use the same unit.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Absolute Zero</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Temperature affects gas pressure too. Raising the temperature of a gas at constant volume gives the particles more kinetic energy — they move faster and collide with the walls more forcefully and more frequently, so pressure increases. Cooling the gas has the opposite effect. Scientists found that extrapolating the pressure&ndash;temperature relationship for any gas reaches zero pressure at the same temperature: &minus;273°C. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The lowest possible temperature, −273°C or 0 K, at which particles have minimum possible kinetic energy and gas pressure would reach zero.">absolute zero</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A temperature scale starting at absolute zero (0 K = −273°C), where a change of 1 K equals a change of 1°C.">Kelvin scale</dfn> uses absolute zero as its starting point (0 K). To convert: \(T(K) = T(°C) + 273\). The Kelvin scale is important for gas law calculations because pressure and volume are proportional to temperature in kelvin, not in Celsius.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the conversion from Celsius to Kelvin and state what absolute zero means in particle terms.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Absolute zero = &minus;273°C = 0 K. At absolute zero, particles would have minimum possible kinetic energy. To convert to kelvin: add 273. Gas pressure and volume are proportional to temperature in kelvin.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Pressure in Fluids</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Any substance that can flow — both liquids and gases are fluids.">fluid</dfn> (liquid or gas) exerts pressure at right angles (normal) to any surface it is in contact with. This pressure acts equally in all directions at a given depth. The equation for pressure on a solid surface is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">$$P = \frac{F}{A}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Where \(P\) is pressure in pascals (Pa), \(F\) is the force in newtons (N), and \(A\) is the area in square metres (m²). One pascal equals one newton per square metre.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Pressure at Depth in a Liquid — Upthrust and Floating</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Pressure in a liquid increases with depth because there is a greater weight of liquid above. The pressure also depends on the density of the liquid. The equation for pressure due to a column of liquid is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">$$P = \rho g h$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Where \(\rho\) (rho) is the density of the liquid in kg/m³, \(g\) is gravitational field strength (approximately 10 N/kg near Earth&rsquo;s surface), and \(h\) is the depth in metres.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Because pressure increases with depth, the upward force on the bottom surface of a submerged object is greater than the downward force on its top surface. This net upward force is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The net upward force exerted by a fluid on a submerged or partially submerged object, caused by the greater pressure at greater depth.">upthrust</dfn>. An object floats when the upthrust equals its weight. An object sinks when its weight exceeds the upthrust. Whether an object floats or sinks depends on the relationship between its density and the density of the fluid: an object denser than the fluid sinks; one less dense than the fluid floats.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Worked example:</strong> Calculate the pressure at 5.0 m depth in sea water (density 1025 kg/m³). \(P = 1025 \times 10 \times 5.0 = 51\,250\) Pa &asymp; 51 kPa (plus atmospheric pressure above the water surface).</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Atmospheric Pressure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Earth&rsquo;s atmosphere is a layer of gas held around the planet by gravity. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The pressure exerted by the weight of the atmosphere above a surface, approximately 100 000 Pa at sea level.">atmospheric pressure</dfn> at sea level is approximately 100 000 Pa (100 kPa). Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude because there is less air above — fewer particle collisions occur from the thinner air overhead.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the equation for pressure at depth in a fluid and explain why atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <div class="higher-only">
  <p>Pressure at depth \(h\) in a fluid: \(P = \rho g h\). Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude because the column of air above is shorter and less dense, so fewer particle collisions occur per unit area.</p>
  </div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Gas pressure is caused by particle collisions with container walls. Boyle&rsquo;s law: \(p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\) at constant temperature — decreasing volume increases pressure proportionally.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Absolute zero (&minus;273°C = 0 K) is the temperature at which particles have minimum kinetic energy. The Kelvin scale is required for proportional gas-law calculations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Pressure in a liquid increases with depth: \(P = \rho g h\). Upthrust arises because the deeper surface of a submerged object experiences greater pressure than the upper surface. Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">When applying Boyle&rsquo;s law \(p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\), check that temperature is stated as constant — the law does not apply if temperature changes. For pressure-at-depth calculations using \(P = \rho g h\), remember that \(h\) is the depth below the surface, not the total height of the container. Exam questions sometimes give data in cm³ or litres — always convert to m³ before substituting. Also, do not confuse gas pressure (from \(P = F/A\)) with liquid pressure (from \(P = \rho g h\)) &mdash; identify which formula the context demands before writing.</p>
