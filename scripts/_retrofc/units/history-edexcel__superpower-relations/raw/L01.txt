--- DESCRIPTION ---
How wartime allies became Cold War enemies through conferences, ideology and the atomic bomb.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Grand Alliance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">During the Second World War, the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union formed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The wartime alliance between the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union against Nazi Germany.">Grand Alliance</dfn> to defeat Nazi Germany. Despite their shared enemy, the three powers had very different political systems and deep suspicions of each other. Understanding these tensions is essential for explaining why the Cold War began almost as soon as the fighting stopped.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain why the Grand Alliance is called a marriage of convenience.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Grand Alliance was held together by a common enemy — Nazi Germany. Once that enemy was defeated, the ideological differences between capitalism and communism resurfaced and drove the superpowers apart.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ideological Differences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The USA was a <dfn class="term" data-def="An economic system based on private ownership, free markets and individual profit.">capitalist</dfn> democracy. It believed in free elections, freedom of speech, and private enterprise. The Soviet Union was a <dfn class="term" data-def="A political system in which the state owns the means of production and a single party controls government.">communist</dfn> state. It believed the state should own all industry and land, and a single party — the Communist Party — should rule. These ideologies were fundamentally opposed: each side saw the other as a threat to its way of life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Stalin believed capitalist countries would eventually try to destroy communism. Truman believed communism destroyed individual freedom and had to be contained. Churchill, though initially allied with Stalin, warned in his 1946 “Iron Curtain” speech that Soviet expansion threatened European liberty.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Stalin</strong> was deeply suspicious of the West. The USSR had suffered around 27 million deaths in the war, and Stalin wanted a buffer zone of friendly states in Eastern Europe to prevent future invasion. <strong>Roosevelt</strong> (and later <strong>Truman</strong>) wanted to spread democracy and free trade globally; Truman was far more hostile to communism than Roosevelt had been. <strong>Churchill</strong> feared Soviet expansion but needed American support to rebuild Britain’s war-shattered economy.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Wartime Conferences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Grand Alliance met at three major conferences to plan the post-war world. Each conference revealed growing tensions between the allies.</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n10">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">November 1943</div>
<h4>Tehran Conference</h4><p>The first meeting of Stalin, Roosevelt and Churchill. They agreed to open a second front in France (D-Day, June 1944) and that the USSR would declare war on Japan after Germany’s defeat. The atmosphere was broadly cooperative, though Stalin resented the delayed second front.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">February 1945</div>
<h4>Yalta Conference</h4><p>With Germany close to defeat, the “Big Three” agreed to divide Germany into four zones of occupation, hold free elections in liberated countries, set up the United Nations, and allow the USSR to enter the war against Japan. Stalin agreed to free elections in Poland, though the meaning of “free” would soon be disputed.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">July–August 1945</div>
<h4>Potsdam Conference</h4><p>Roosevelt had died and was replaced by Truman, who was far more suspicious of Soviet intentions. Churchill lost the 1945 election halfway through and was replaced by Attlee. Disagreements emerged over Poland’s western border, German reparations, and the future of Eastern Europe. Truman revealed the successful test of the atomic bomb during the conference, increasing Soviet suspicion.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three wartime conferences in order and state the mood at each one.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The three conferences — Tehran (1943), Yalta (February 1945) and Potsdam (July 1945) — show a clear pattern: cooperation gave way to suspicion, then open disagreement as the common enemy disappeared.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Impact of the Atomic Bomb</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In August 1945, the USA dropped atomic bombs on <dfn class="term" data-def="The two Japanese cities destroyed by American atomic bombs in August 1945, killing over 200,000 people.">Hiroshima and Nagasaki</dfn>, forcing Japan’s surrender. The bomb transformed superpower relations in several ways.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">First, it gave the USA a monopoly on nuclear weapons, making it the most powerful country in the world. Second, Truman used the bomb partly to intimidate the Soviet Union — a tactic historians call “atomic diplomacy.” Third, Stalin felt threatened and accelerated the Soviet nuclear programme. The USSR tested its own atomic bomb in 1949, launching the <dfn class="term" data-def="The competition between the USA and USSR to develop more powerful and numerous nuclear weapons.">arms race</dfn>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Did the Atomic Bomb Increase Tensions?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">Truman told Stalin about the bomb at Potsdam but gave no details. Stalin already knew about it through Soviet spies. The bomb made Stalin feel the USA could bully the USSR, so he was determined to develop his own. This mutual suspicion fuelled the arms race and deepened the divide between East and West. Some historians argue the bomb was dropped on Japan partly to demonstrate American power to the Soviets, not just to end the Pacific War.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">From Alliance to Rivalry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By late 1945, the Grand Alliance was effectively over. The wartime need for cooperation had gone, and the ideological gulf between capitalism and communism dominated. Truman saw Soviet expansion in Eastern Europe as aggression; Stalin saw it as self-defence. Neither side trusted the other, and both began preparing for a long struggle — the <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of political hostility and military tension between the USA and USSR from 1945 to 1991, stopping short of direct military conflict.">Cold War</dfn>.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The Grand Alliance was a temporary partnership held together by the need to defeat Nazi Germany — once that enemy was gone, ideological differences resurfaced.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The three conferences (Tehran, Yalta, Potsdam) show a clear shift from cooperation to suspicion and disagreement, especially after Roosevelt’s death and Truman’s harder line against the USSR.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The atomic bomb gave the USA a decisive advantage, but it also accelerated Soviet efforts to build their own bomb, launching the arms race that defined the Cold War.</li></ul>
