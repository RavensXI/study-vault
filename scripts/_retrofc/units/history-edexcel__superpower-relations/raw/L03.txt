--- DESCRIPTION ---
How nuclear weapons, the Warsaw Pact and Hungary's failed revolt intensified the Cold War.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Arms Race</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The competition between the USA and USSR to develop more powerful and numerous nuclear weapons, beginning with the Soviet atomic bomb test in 1949.">arms race</dfn> was one of the most dangerous features of the Cold War. After the USA dropped atomic bombs on Japan in 1945, it held a nuclear monopoly. However, in 1949 the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb — far earlier than the Americans expected. This shocked the USA and triggered a cycle of escalation that lasted decades.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write the arms race chain: list each weapon test with its country and year.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The USA tested the first hydrogen bomb in 1952 — a weapon 1,000 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb. The USSR tested its own hydrogen bomb just a year later in 1953. By the late 1950s, both sides had developed <dfn class="term" data-def="Rockets capable of carrying nuclear warheads across continents, making it possible to strike enemy cities without using aircraft.">intercontinental ballistic missiles</dfn> (ICBMs), meaning each superpower could destroy the other in minutes.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The arms race created a balance of terror known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that neither superpower would launch a nuclear attack because the other side would retaliate and both would be destroyed.">Mutually Assured Destruction</dfn> (MAD). Neither side could attack without being destroyed in return, so nuclear war became too dangerous to risk. While MAD prevented direct conflict, it also meant the world lived under the constant threat of annihilation.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Formation of the Warsaw Pact (1955)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1955, the Soviet Union formed the <dfn class="term" data-def="A military alliance of communist countries formed in 1955 as the Soviet response to NATO, including the USSR, Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Albania.">Warsaw Pact</dfn> — a military alliance of communist countries in Eastern Europe. This was a direct response to West Germany joining NATO in the same year. The Warsaw Pact bound the satellite states to the USSR militarily, giving the Soviet Union the right to station troops across Eastern Europe.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>NATO</strong> (1949) was a voluntary alliance of Western nations committed to collective security. Members included the USA, Britain, France, Canada and others. <strong>The Warsaw Pact</strong> (1955) was the communist equivalent, but membership was not voluntary — it was imposed by Moscow. Together, these two alliances divided Europe into armed camps. If war broke out, every member of each alliance was bound to fight, turning any local conflict into a potential world war.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Events Leading to the Hungarian Uprising (1956)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Hungary had been a Soviet satellite state since 1948, governed by the brutal Stalinist leader Mátyás Rákosi. Hungarians resented Soviet control: the secret police terrorised the population, living standards were poor, and Hungarian resources were exported to the USSR. When Stalin died in 1953, his successor <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet leader from 1953 to 1964, who denounced Stalin's brutality in his 1956 'Secret Speech' and appeared to offer a more relaxed approach to governing the satellite states.">Khrushchev</dfn> gave a “Secret Speech” in February 1956 denouncing Stalin’s crimes. This encouraged hopes of reform across Eastern Europe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In Poland, workers’ protests in June 1956 led to the appointment of the reformer Gomulka, who was accepted by Khrushchev. Inspired by Poland’s example, Hungarian students and workers began demanding change: they wanted free elections, the withdrawal of Soviet troops, and the return of the reformist leader Imre Nagy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Hungarian Uprising</h2>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n12">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">23 October 1956</div>
<h4>The Uprising Begins</h4><p>Students and workers took to the streets of Budapest. They tore down a giant statue of Stalin and attacked the secret police headquarters. Soviet troops initially withdrew from the city.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">24 October – 1 November 1956</div>
<h4>Nagy Takes Power</h4><p>Imre <dfn class="term" data-def="The Hungarian Prime Minister who led the 1956 uprising, announcing Hungary's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact before being arrested and executed by the Soviets.">Nagy</dfn> became Prime Minister. He announced reforms: free elections, the end of one-party rule, and — crucially — Hungary’s withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact. This was a step too far for Khrushchev.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">4 November 1956</div>
<h4>Soviet Invasion</h4><p>Khrushchev sent 1,000 tanks and 200,000 troops into Budapest. Fierce street fighting lasted two weeks. Around 2,500 Hungarians were killed, 200,000 fled as refugees, and Nagy was arrested. He was later executed in 1958.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the specific announcement that triggered the Soviet invasion of Hungary.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The USSR crushed the Hungarian Uprising because Nagy announced Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact. Khrushchev could accept limited reform, but losing a satellite state from the military alliance would weaken Soviet control of Eastern Europe and encourage other countries to break away.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">International Reaction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Western response was limited. The USA condemned the Soviet invasion publicly and accepted Hungarian refugees, but did not intervene militarily. President Eisenhower was unwilling to risk nuclear war over Hungary. Britain and France were distracted by the Suez Crisis in Egypt. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An international organisation founded in 1945 to maintain peace and security between nations.">United Nations</dfn> debated the crisis but could take no action because the USSR used its veto in the Security Council.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Didn’t the West Intervene?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">The USA’s policy of containment meant preventing the <em>spread</em> of communism, not removing it where it already existed. Hungary was already inside the Soviet sphere of influence. Direct military intervention would have risked nuclear war. The Suez Crisis also diverted British and French attention. The lack of Western intervention sent a clear message to Eastern Europe: the West would not risk war to liberate Soviet satellite states.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The arms race escalated from atomic to hydrogen bombs and ICBMs, creating Mutually Assured Destruction — neither side could attack without being destroyed in return.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Warsaw Pact (1955) formalised the military division of Europe and gave the USSR the right to station troops in Eastern European satellite states.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The Hungarian Uprising (1956) showed that the USSR would use force to maintain control of its satellite states, and that the West would not intervene to liberate them.</li></ul>
