--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the superpowers tried to reduce tensions in the 1970s through treaties and agreements.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Was Détente?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A period of reduced tension between the USA and USSR during the 1970s, marked by arms control treaties, trade agreements and diplomatic cooperation.">Détente</dfn> is a French word meaning the relaxation of tension. In the context of the Cold War, it refers to the period during the 1970s when the USA and USSR actively tried to improve relations and reduce the risk of nuclear war. Both superpowers had reasons to pursue détente.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Did Both Sides Want Détente?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>The USA</strong> wanted détente because the Vietnam War (1955–75) had been enormously costly in both lives and money, reducing public appetite for confrontation. The arms race was ruinously expensive, and the Cuban Missile Crisis had shown how close the world could come to nuclear destruction. <strong>The USSR</strong> also wanted to reduce military spending. Its economy was struggling under the burden of the arms race, and the Sino-Soviet split (the breakdown in relations between the USSR and China) meant the USSR now faced potential enemies on two fronts. Both sides recognised that managing their rivalry was safer and cheaper than escalating it.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">SALT 1 (1972)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The first Strategic Arms Limitation Talks resulted in <dfn class="term" data-def="The first Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty, signed by Nixon and Brezhnev in 1972, which froze the number of ICBMs and SLBMs each side could hold.">SALT 1</dfn>, signed by President Nixon and Soviet leader Brezhnev in May 1972. The treaty froze the number of <dfn class="term" data-def="Intercontinental Ballistic Missiles — long-range nuclear missiles launched from land-based silos.">ICBMs</dfn> and submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) each side could hold at their current levels for five years. It also included the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty, which limited each side to two missile defence sites.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain two limitations of SALT 1 that allowed the arms race to continue.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>SALT 1 was significant because it was the first agreement between the superpowers to limit nuclear weapons. However, it only froze existing numbers — it did not reduce them. It also did not cover new technologies like <dfn class="term" data-def="Multiple Independently-targetable Re-entry Vehicles — missiles carrying several nuclear warheads, each aimed at a different target.">MIRVs</dfn> (missiles carrying multiple warheads), so the arms race continued in new forms.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Helsinki Accords (1975)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In August 1975, 35 countries including the USA and USSR signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1975 agreement between 35 nations covering security, economic cooperation and human rights, seen as the high point of détente.">Helsinki Accords</dfn>. These were not a legally binding treaty but a set of agreements covering three areas, known as “baskets.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Basket 1 — Security:</strong> All signatories accepted the post-war borders in Europe, meaning the West formally recognised Soviet control of Eastern Europe. <strong>Basket 2 — Economic cooperation:</strong> East and West agreed to increase trade, exchange technology and improve transport links. <strong>Basket 3 — Human rights:</strong> All signatories agreed to respect basic human rights, including freedom of thought, speech and movement.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what each side gained from the Helsinki Accords, from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Helsinki Accords were the high point of détente. The USSR gained Western recognition of its European borders (Basket 1), but the human rights commitments (Basket 3) later became a powerful tool for dissidents in Eastern Europe to challenge communist governments — something the USSR had not anticipated.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">SALT 2 (1979)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Negotiations for a second SALT treaty took seven years. <dfn class="term" data-def="The second Strategic Arms Limitation Treaty, signed by Carter and Brezhnev in 1979 but never ratified by the US Senate due to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.">SALT 2</dfn> was signed by President Carter and Brezhnev in June 1979. It set equal limits on the number of strategic nuclear delivery vehicles (ICBMs, SLBMs and bombers) each side could hold — a maximum of 2,250 each. It also limited the number of MIRVed missiles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">However, SALT 2 was never ratified by the US Senate. In December 1979, the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan, and Carter withdrew the treaty from Senate consideration as a protest. Détente was effectively over.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Limits of Détente</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14">Détente reduced tensions but never resolved the fundamental differences between the superpowers. The arms race continued in new forms (MIRVs, cruise missiles). The USSR continued to support communist movements worldwide. The USA continued to support anti-communist governments, even dictatorships. Human rights commitments in the Helsinki Accords were largely ignored by the Soviet Union. When strategic interests clashed — as in Afghanistan — détente collapsed quickly. Critics argued détente was merely a pause, not a genuine peace.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Other Arms Control Treaties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Détente also built on earlier agreements reached after the Cuban Missile Crisis. The <strong>Outer Space Treaty</strong> (1967) prevented the placement of nuclear weapons in space. The <strong>Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty</strong> (1968) aimed to stop the spread of nuclear weapons to non-nuclear states. Together with SALT 1 and the Helsinki Accords, these agreements created a framework of arms control that, while imperfect, helped manage the superpower rivalry throughout the 1970s.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Détente was driven by mutual exhaustion — the cost of the arms race, the Vietnam War, and the Cuban Missile Crisis convinced both sides that managing their rivalry was better than escalating it.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">SALT 1 (1972) froze missile numbers, and the Helsinki Accords (1975) recognised European borders and included human rights commitments — but SALT 2 was never ratified due to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Détente reduced tensions but never solved the fundamental ideological divide — when the USSR invaded Afghanistan in 1979, détente collapsed and the “Second Cold War” began.</li></ul>
