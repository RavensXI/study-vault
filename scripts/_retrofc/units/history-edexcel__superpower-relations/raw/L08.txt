--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Gorbachev's reforms led to the fall of the Berlin Wall and the end of the Cold War.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Gorbachev’s “New Thinking”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In March 1985, <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet leader from 1985 to 1991 who introduced radical reforms (glasnost and perestroika) and sought to reduce Cold War tensions, ultimately overseeing the dissolution of the Soviet Union.">Mikhail Gorbachev</dfn> became the leader of the Soviet Union. At 54, he was much younger than his predecessors and brought a fundamentally different approach to governing. Gorbachev recognised that the Soviet Union was in deep trouble: the economy was stagnating, the war in Afghanistan was draining resources and morale, the arms race was cripplingly expensive, and ordinary Soviet citizens faced shortages of basic goods.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Gorbachev introduced what he called “new thinking” — a willingness to reform the Soviet system and to negotiate with the West rather than confront it. His two signature policies were <dfn class="term" data-def="Meaning 'openness' — Gorbachev's policy of allowing greater freedom of speech, press freedom and political debate within the Soviet Union.">glasnost</dfn> (openness) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Meaning 'restructuring' — Gorbachev's policy of reforming the Soviet economy by introducing some market-based elements and reducing state control.">perestroika</dfn> (restructuring).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define glasnost and perestroika, and explain what Gorbachev intended versus what actually happened.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Glasnost</strong> meant greater freedom of speech, press freedom and political debate. <strong>Perestroika</strong> meant reforming the economy by reducing central planning and introducing some market-based elements. Gorbachev wanted to modernise the Soviet system, not destroy it — but his reforms ultimately unleashed forces he could not control.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Reducing Cold War Tensions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Gorbachev and Reagan developed a surprisingly productive relationship. Despite their ideological differences, both leaders recognised the need to reduce the nuclear threat. They held a series of summit meetings that transformed the Cold War.</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n7">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">November 1985</div>
<h4>Geneva Summit</h4><p>Gorbachev and Reagan met for the first time. No major agreements were reached, but the two leaders established a personal rapport and agreed to hold further meetings.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">October 1986</div>
<h4>Reykjavik Summit</h4><p>Gorbachev proposed eliminating all nuclear weapons within ten years. The talks collapsed because Reagan refused to abandon <dfn class="term" data-def="Reagan's proposed space-based missile defence system, which Gorbachev demanded be abandoned as a condition for further arms reduction.">SDI</dfn>. However, the summit showed both sides were serious about major arms reductions.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">December 1987</div>
<h4>INF Treaty</h4><p>Gorbachev and Reagan signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (1987), which eliminated all land-based nuclear missiles with ranges of 500-5,500 km — the first treaty to actually reduce the number of nuclear weapons.">Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty</dfn>, eliminating all land-based nuclear missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres. This was the first treaty to actually <em>reduce</em> (not just limit) nuclear weapons. Nearly 2,700 missiles were destroyed.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">Previous agreements (SALT 1, SALT 2) had only <em>limited</em> the growth of nuclear arsenals. The INF Treaty was the first to require the actual <em>destruction</em> of nuclear weapons — nearly 2,700 missiles were eliminated. It included on-site inspections to verify compliance, demonstrating a level of trust that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier. The treaty removed an entire class of weapons from Europe and showed that genuine disarmament was possible.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Impact on Eastern Europe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Gorbachev’s reforms had a transformative effect on Eastern Europe. He made clear that the Soviet Union would no longer use force to keep communist governments in power — effectively abandoning the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Soviet policy that the USSR would intervene in any socialist country where communism was under threat, abandoned by Gorbachev in the late 1980s.">Brezhnev Doctrine</dfn>. This was sometimes called the “Sinatra Doctrine” (after Frank Sinatra’s song “My Way”) — satellite states could now go their own way.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Without the threat of Soviet military intervention, opposition movements across Eastern Europe gained momentum. In Poland, the Solidarity trade union movement won free elections in June 1989. In Hungary, the government opened its border with Austria, allowing East Germans to flee west. In Czechoslovakia, the “Velvet Revolution” peacefully removed the communist government. In Romania, the dictator Ceauşescu was overthrown and executed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Fall of the Berlin Wall</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">On 9 November 1989, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical barrier that had divided East and West Berlin since 1961, whose opening on 9 November 1989 became the defining moment of the end of the Cold War.">Berlin Wall</dfn> was opened. The East German government, overwhelmed by mass protests and the flood of citizens leaving through Hungary, announced that citizens could cross the border freely. Thousands of jubilant Berliners climbed onto the Wall and began tearing it down with hammers and picks. The event was broadcast live around the world and became the defining image of the end of the Cold War.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="From memory, state the exact dates of the fall of the Berlin Wall and the dissolution of the Soviet Union.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 symbolised the end of communist control in Eastern Europe. Germany was formally reunified on 3 October 1990. The Wall that had divided East and West for 28 years was gone.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Collapse of the Soviet Union</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Gorbachev’s reforms, intended to save the Soviet system, ultimately destroyed it. Glasnost allowed people to criticise the government openly for the first time, and many demanded independence for their republics. Perestroika failed to deliver economic improvement, increasing public frustration. Nationalist movements surged in the Baltic states (Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia), Ukraine, and other Soviet republics.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In August 1991, hardline communists attempted a <dfn class="term" data-def="The failed attempt by hardline communists to overthrow Gorbachev in August 1991, which backfired and accelerated the dissolution of the Soviet Union.">coup</dfn> against Gorbachev, fearing his reforms had gone too far. The coup failed, largely because Boris Yeltsin (President of the Russian Republic) rallied opposition. However, the failed coup fatally weakened Gorbachev’s authority. One by one, Soviet republics declared independence.</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<span>The End of the Warsaw Pact & the Soviet Union</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">The <strong>Warsaw Pact</strong> was formally dissolved on 1 July 1991, as its member states had already abandoned communism. On <strong>25 December 1991</strong>, Gorbachev resigned as President of the Soviet Union. The following day, 26 December 1991, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The formal end of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics on 26 December 1991, when the country dissolved into 15 independent states.">Soviet Union was officially dissolved</dfn>, splitting into 15 independent states. Russia, under Boris Yeltsin, emerged as the largest successor state. The Cold War was over after 46 years.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="List five factors that caused the end of the Cold War from memory, with one sentence on each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Cold War ended not with a military victory but with the internal collapse of the Soviet system. Gorbachev’s reforms, Reagan’s economic pressure, the arms race burden, the Afghan war, nationalist movements, and the fundamental weaknesses of the communist command economy all contributed to the dissolution of the USSR on 26 December 1991.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Gorbachev’s glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) were intended to reform the Soviet system, but they unleashed forces — free speech, nationalist movements, economic expectations — that he could not control.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The INF Treaty (1987) was the first agreement to actually reduce nuclear weapons, and the abandonment of the Brezhnev Doctrine allowed Eastern European countries to break free from Soviet control, leading to the fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Soviet Union dissolved on 26 December 1991, ending the Cold War after 46 years — caused by a combination of Gorbachev’s reforms, Reagan’s pressure, the Afghan war, economic weakness, and nationalist movements.</li></ul>
