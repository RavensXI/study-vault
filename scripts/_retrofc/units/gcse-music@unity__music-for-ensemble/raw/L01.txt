<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is an Ensemble?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of musicians who perform together, typically a small group rather than a full orchestra.">ensemble</dfn> is a group of musicians who perform together. In Eduqas GCSE Music, AoS2 focuses on music written for small groups — not full orchestras. You need to study three main styles of ensemble music: <dfn class="term" data-def="Music written for a small group of instruments, usually with one player per part, intended for intimate performance spaces.">chamber music</dfn>, jazz and blues, and musical theatre. Understanding how these ensembles are put together, what instruments they use, and how the musical <dfn class="term" data-def="The way in which musical parts or layers are combined in a piece of music.">texture</dfn> is built up is essential for the listening exam.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This area of study will be assessed in the listening exam (Component 3). There will be two questions based on Music for Ensemble, so you must be confident at identifying ensemble types, naming instruments, and describing textures when you hear them.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 2 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AoS2 is assessed via the listening exam only. There will be two questions on Music for Ensemble. One may focus on musical theatre or jazz, and the other on chamber music. You need to identify instruments, ensemble types, and textures by ear.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Naming Ensembles by Size</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ensembles are named according to the number of players performing. You must learn these names because the exam may ask you to identify or name the type of ensemble you hear.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Chamber Music Ensembles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Chamber music was originally written for performance in small rooms or chambers, not large concert halls. Each player has their own individual part — there is no conductor. The players must listen carefully to each other and balance their sound together.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The most common chamber ensembles you need to know are:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Chamber Ensembles in Detail</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>String Quartet</strong> — 2 violins, viola, and cello. This is the most important chamber ensemble. The first violin usually plays the melody, while the other instruments provide accompaniment and harmonic support. Composers like Mozart and Beethoven wrote extensively for string quartet.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Wind Quintet</strong> — flute, oboe, clarinet, French horn, and bassoon. The melody is often passed between different instruments. The variety of <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality or colour of a musical sound, determined by the instrument or voice producing it.">timbre</dfn> (tone colour) across the five instruments creates rich and varied sonority.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Piano Trio</strong> — piano, violin, and cello. The piano can provide both melody and harmonic support through chords and arpeggios. The violin often carries the main melody, and the cello provides a bass line and harmonic foundation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In the Autumn 2020 Eduqas exam, a chamber music extract was played and students were asked to identify the melody instrument (cello), the performance technique (arco), the tonality (major), and the texture (homophonic). Being able to quickly recognise these features by ear is essential.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Jazz, Blues & Musical Theatre Ensembles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">AoS2 is not just about classical chamber music. You also need to understand the ensembles used in jazz and blues and in musical theatre.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Jazz and blues ensembles</strong> vary in size. A small jazz combo might include a saxophone, trumpet, piano, double bass, and drum kit. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A large jazz ensemble typically featuring sections of saxophones, trumpets, trombones, and a rhythm section.">big band</dfn> is much larger, with sections of saxophones, trumpets, trombones, and a rhythm section (piano, bass, drums, guitar). Gershwin’s “I Got Rhythm” is an example studied in class that uses a jazz ensemble.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Musical theatre ensembles</strong> combine singers with an instrumental group called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of musicians who accompany the singers in a musical theatre production, also called the band.">pit band</dfn> or pit orchestra. The size can range from a small band to a full orchestra. Vocals are central — the lead character often sings a solo with instrumental backing. Schoenberg’s “I Dreamed a Dream” from <em>Les Misérables</em> and Schwartz’s “Defying Gravity” from <em>Wicked</em> are examples from this style.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about jazz, the rhythm section (piano, bass.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In jazz, the rhythm section (piano, bass, drums) provides the harmonic and rhythmic foundation, while the front-line instruments (trumpet, saxophone, trombone) carry the melody and improvise solos. In musical theatre, the voice is the lead “instrument” and the pit band accompanies.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n39">Basso Continuo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n40">In Baroque ensemble music (c.1600–1750), nearly every piece was underpinned by a <dfn class="term" data-def="A Baroque-era harmonic accompaniment provided by a keyboard instrument (harpsichord or organ) and a bass instrument (cello or bass viol), playing from figured bass notation.">basso continuo</dfn>. This was a harmonic accompaniment typically played by two instruments working together: a <strong>keyboard instrument</strong> (harpsichord or organ) providing chords, and a <strong>bass instrument</strong> (cello, bass viol, or bassoon) reinforcing the bass line.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n41">The continuo players read from <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of musical notation where numbers written below a bass line indicate which chords the keyboard player should fill in.">figured bass</dfn> notation — a bass line with numbers written beneath it. The numbers told the keyboard player which intervals to add above the bass note, so they could fill in the appropriate chords. This was a form of controlled improvisation: the basic harmonies were specified, but the exact voicing and rhythm were left to the performer’s judgement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n42">In Bach’s <em>Badinerie</em> (a set work for this course), the basso continuo provides the harmonic foundation beneath the solo flute melody. The harpsichord fills in chords while the cello doubles the bass line, creating a rich harmonic bed for the fast-moving flute part to dance over.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n43" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the two instruments in a typical basso continuo and explain what figured bass notation tells the keyboard player.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Basso continuo = a Baroque harmonic accompaniment played by a keyboard instrument (harpsichord/organ) + a bass instrument (cello/bass viol). The keyboard reads from figured bass — numbers below the bass notes indicating which chords to play. The continuo is the harmonic backbone of Baroque ensemble music.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n44">Vocal Ensembles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n45">Ensembles are not limited to instruments. <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of singers performing together, named by size: solo, duet, trio, quartet, chorus/choir.">Vocal ensembles</dfn> are groups of singers performing together, and they are named by size just like instrumental ensembles: a <strong>solo</strong> (one singer), <strong>duet</strong> (two singers), <strong>trio</strong> (three singers), <strong>quartet</strong> (four singers), and <strong>chorus</strong> or <strong>choir</strong> (a large group).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n46">Singing <dfn class="term" data-def="Vocal music performed without any instrumental accompaniment."><em>a cappella</em></dfn> means performing with voices only — no instrumental accompaniment at all. The voices must supply melody, harmony, rhythm, and bass all by themselves. A cappella groups are common in barbershop quartets, gospel choirs, and some modern pop arrangements.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n47">Vocal ensembles blend differently from instrumental ones because human voices share a very similar <em>timbre</em>. To create contrast and variety, composers give different voice parts contrasting melodic lines, use imitation between parts, or alternate between solo passages and full choral sections. In popular music, <strong>backing vocals</strong> provide harmonic support beneath a lead singer, often singing sustained “oohs” and “aahs” or echoing key phrases.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n48" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the vocal ensemble sizes (solo through chorus) and define a cappella.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vocal ensembles: solo (1), duet (2), trio (3), quartet (4), chorus/choir (large group). <em>A cappella</em> = voices only, no instruments. In the exam, if you hear singing with no instrumental accompaniment, describe it as <em>a cappella</em>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Texture in Ensemble Music</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Texture describes how the different musical parts or layers are combined. The Eduqas spec requires you to identify and describe texture in ensemble music. There are four main textures you must know:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A musical texture with a single melodic line and no accompaniment.">Monophonic</dfn></strong> — a single melodic line with no accompaniment. For example, a solo violin playing a melody on its own, or a singer performing unaccompanied. This is the thinnest possible texture.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A musical texture where one part has the melody and the other parts provide accompaniment, such as chords.">Homophonic</dfn></strong> — one part has the melody while other parts accompany it with chords or a rhythmic pattern. This is the most common texture in ensemble music. A string quartet playing with the first violin on melody and the other instruments providing chordal support is homophonic.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A musical texture with two or more independent melodic lines played at the same time.">Polyphonic</dfn></strong> — two or more independent melodic lines happening at the same time. Each part has its own melody. This is also called contrapuntal texture. You might hear this in a fugue or when instruments imitate each other’s melodies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Unison</strong> — all parts play the same melody at the same time, at the same pitch (or in octaves). This creates a powerful, focused sound.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Texture Changes During a Piece</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Composers rarely keep the same texture throughout an entire piece. They manipulate texture to create contrast and interest. A chamber piece might begin monophonically with a solo cello, then become homophonic when the piano enters with chords, and build to a thick polyphonic texture where all instruments play independent melodies. Describing how texture changes is a common exam question.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">In the Autumn 2020 exam, students were asked to describe the melody and accompaniment in a chamber music extract. Answers that earned credit included: the melody was legato, diatonic, conjunct, and used a wide pitch range, while the accompaniment featured an ostinato, broken chords (arpeggios), and a slow harmonic rhythm. Being specific about what each layer does is the key to high marks.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Texture in the Three AoS2 Styles</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Chamber music:</strong> Texture is often homophonic (melody and accompaniment) but can include polyphonic passages where instruments imitate each other. The texture tends to be transparent because there are only a few players.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Jazz and blues:</strong> Texture is typically homophonic during the “head” (main melody), with the rhythm section accompanying. During solos, the texture thins as one instrument improvises over a chord pattern. In a big band, the texture is thicker with layered <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated musical phrase, often played by a section of instruments to accompany a soloist.">riffs</dfn> and call-and-response passages.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Musical theatre:</strong> Texture varies widely. A solo ballad might be monophonic (voice alone) before the pit band joins to create a homophonic texture. Ensemble numbers with multiple characters singing different lines at the same time produce polyphonic texture.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Applying This to the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">In the listening exam, you will hear extracts from ensemble pieces and be expected to name the ensemble, identify instruments, and describe the texture. The Autumn 2020 paper included a question on a musical theatre extract (“Tell Me It’s Not True” from <em>Blood Brothers</em> by Willy Russell) and a separate question on a chamber music extract. Both tested your ability to recognise instruments, textures, and other musical features by ear.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Always use the correct musical vocabulary. Don’t just say “the music is thick” — say “the texture is homophonic with the first violin playing the melody and the other strings providing chordal accompaniment.” Specific language earns marks.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n36">Ensembles are named by size: duet (2), trio (3), quartet (4), quintet (5), sextet (6), septet (7), octet (8). Know the specific instruments in a string quartet, wind quintet, piano trio, and big band.</li><li data-narration-id="n37">The four main textures are monophonic (single line), homophonic (melody plus accompaniment), polyphonic (multiple independent melodies), and unison (all parts playing the same melody).</li><li data-narration-id="n38">In the exam, always use precise musical vocabulary and describe what each layer of the texture is doing — not just the texture name.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n34">When describing texture in a listening question, always name the specific texture type (monophonic, homophonic, polyphonic, or unison) and then explain what each part is doing. For example: “The texture is homophonic — the cello plays a legato melody while the piano provides broken chord accompaniment.” The command word “describe” means you need to give detail about what you hear, not just label it. If the question asks you to “explain,” you must also say why the composer made that choice or what effect it creates.</p>
