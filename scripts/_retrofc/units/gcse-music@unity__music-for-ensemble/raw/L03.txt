<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Jazz and Blues?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jazz and blues are two closely related styles of music that developed in the United States. Blues originated in the late 19th century from African American communities in the Deep South, while jazz grew out of blues, ragtime and other styles in the early 20th century. Both genres place a strong emphasis on <dfn class="term" data-def="Music that is created spontaneously during a performance, rather than being written down in advance.">improvisation</dfn>, expressive playing, and a distinctive use of rhythm and harmony.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In the Eduqas GCSE Music exam, jazz and blues falls under AoS2: Music for Ensemble. You need to understand how instruments work together in jazz and blues ensembles, the theory behind the <dfn class="term" data-def="A six-note scale used in blues and jazz, formed by adding flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th notes to a major scale.">blues scale</dfn>, and how improvisation shapes the music.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about jazz and blues music commonly uses swung.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jazz and blues music commonly uses swung rhythms, extended chords, blues notes, and improvisation. The bass instrument plays a very important role in both styles, providing the harmonic and rhythmic foundation.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Jazz and Blues Ensembles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of musicians who play jazz music together, ranging from a small combo (trio or quartet) to a large big band.">jazz ensemble</dfn> can vary in size. A small jazz combo might be a trio or quartet, while a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large jazz ensemble typically featuring sections of saxophones, trumpets, trombones and a rhythm section.">big band</dfn> features full sections of brass, woodwind and rhythm instruments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Typical instruments in a jazz or blues ensemble include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Melody instruments:</strong> Trumpet, saxophone, clarinet, trombone, voice</li>
<li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Rhythm section:</strong> Drum kit, bass guitar or double bass, piano or keyboard, guitar</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Horn instruments — such as trumpets, trombones and saxophones — are central to jazz. Violin is not commonly used in traditional jazz and blues ensembles. The bass is extremely important because it outlines the chord progression and drives the rhythm forward.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">In a big band, the saxophone section, trumpet section and trombone section each have distinct roles. Saxophones often carry the main melody or play harmonised <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated musical phrase, typically used as an introduction or refrain in popular music and jazz.">riff</dfn> patterns. Trumpets frequently take the lead melody and play powerful, bright lines. Trombones provide harmonic support and add depth. The rhythm section — piano, bass, drums and guitar — keeps time, sets the groove and outlines the chord changes. During solo sections, one instrument improvises while the rest of the ensemble accompanies or plays written <dfn class="term" data-def="Background figures played or sung behind a soloist, often consisting of repeated chords or riffs.">backing figures</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Extended Chords</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Chords that contain more than three notes, such as 7th, 9th or 13th chords.">Extended chords</dfn> are any chord with more than three notes. In jazz and blues, simple triads are rarely used on their own. Instead, musicians add extra notes on top of the basic triad to create a richer, more colourful sound. The most common extended chords are:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Dominant 7th (e.g. C7):</strong> A major triad plus a flattened 7th note. This is the most important chord type in blues.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Major 7th (e.g. Cmaj7):</strong> A major triad plus the natural 7th note. Gives a smooth, warm sound.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Minor 7th (e.g. Cm7):</strong> A minor triad plus a flattened 7th note. Creates a mellow, darker sound.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>9th chords and beyond:</strong> Add the 9th (and sometimes 11th or 13th) on top of a 7th chord for even richer harmony.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In jazz, chord symbols are written differently from classical music. For example, “C” means C major, “Cm” or “C-” means C minor, “C7” means C dominant 7th, and “C+” means C augmented.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/gcse-music/music-for-ensemble/diagram_jazz_and_blues.jpg" alt="Diagram: Jazz and Blues" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define extended chords in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Extended chords are a defining feature of jazz and blues harmony. Dominant 7th chords appear on almost every chord in a standard 12-bar blues progression. If you hear rich, colourful chords with added notes, this is a strong sign the music is jazz or blues.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Blues Notes and the Blues Scale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="Notes that are flattened (lowered) to create the characteristic sad or gritty sound of blues music; specifically the flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th degrees of the major scale.">Blues notes</dfn> are where musicians flatten certain notes of the major scale to create the distinctive sad, gritty or emotional sound of blues and jazz. The three blues notes are the flattened 3rd, flattened 5th and flattened 7th degrees of the scale.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The blues scale is built by taking the notes of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A five-note scale commonly using notes 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of the major scale.">pentatonic scale</dfn> and adding the flattened 5th. This gives a six-note scale. For example, in C major the blues scale is: C – E♭ – F – G♭ – G – B♭.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Musicians often bend, slide or scoop into these blues notes rather than playing them cleanly. This technique adds expression and is one of the main reasons blues and jazz sound so different from classical or pop music.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Blues Notes in Different Keys</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">To find the blues notes in any key, simply flatten the 3rd, 5th and 7th degrees of the major scale:</p>
    <ul>
    <li><strong>C major:</strong> Blues notes are E♭, G♭ and B♭</li>
    <li><strong>G major:</strong> Blues notes are B♭, D♭ and F</li>
    <li><strong>E♭ major:</strong> Blues notes are G♭, B𝄫 (A) and D♭</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Being able to identify and construct blues scales in different keys is a skill that could come up in the listening exam. Practise writing them out so you can do it quickly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Swung Rhythms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Jazz and blues music commonly uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A rhythmic feel where pairs of quavers are played unevenly, with the first quaver held longer than the second, creating a bouncy, lilting groove.">swung rhythms</dfn>. In straight rhythm, pairs of quavers are even and equal. In swing, the first quaver of each pair is held longer and the second is shortened, creating a “long-short” feel. This gives the music a bouncy, lilting groove that is instantly recognisable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Swing feel is so important to jazz that a piece can sound completely different when played straight versus swung. Blues does not always have a slow tempo — it can be fast, medium or slow — but it almost always uses some degree of swing.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Improvisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Improvisation is one of the most important features of jazz and blues. It means creating music spontaneously during a live performance, rather than reading from a written score. In a typical jazz performance, the ensemble plays the melody (known as the “head”) together, and then individual musicians take turns to improvise solos over the chord progression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">During a solo, the other members of the ensemble continue to accompany. The rhythm section keeps the groove going, and other instruments may play quiet background riffs or chords. The soloist draws on the blues scale, extended chords and their own musical ideas to create something unique each time.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><dfn class="term" data-def="A musical technique where one part (the call) is answered by another part (the response), often between a vocalist and an instrument.">Call and response</dfn> is a key feature of blues music. In Bessie Smith’s “Lost Your Head Blues,” the vocal line sings a phrase (the call) and the trumpet answers with a contrasting phrase (the response). This interaction between voice and instrument is a defining texture in blues and creates a conversational effect between the performers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Key Listening Examples</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Your teacher resources reference three important pieces that illustrate different aspects of jazz and blues:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Ray Charles – “Hallelujah, I Love Her So”:</strong> Listen for the use of extended chords (dominant 7ths) and accidentals (blues notes). The harmony is rich and colourful, typical of blues-influenced jazz.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Bessie Smith – “Lost Your Head Blues”:</strong> A classic example of call and response between the vocal and trumpet. The trumpet melody features blues notes, bends and improvised decoration — all typical features of blues.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Miles Davis – “So What”:</strong> A famous example of a jazz combo (trumpet, saxophone, piano, double bass, drums). Listen for the prominent bass line in the solo section, the use of improvisation, and the interaction between instruments.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n38" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the listening exam, you may be asked to identify features of jazz or blues in an unheard extract. Listen for: swung rhythms, extended chords, blues notes, improvisation, call and response, and the prominent role of bass and horn instruments.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n41">Jazz and blues ensembles use horn instruments (trumpet, saxophone, trombone) for melody and a rhythm section (bass, drums, piano, guitar) to keep time and outline chords. Extended chords such as dominant 7ths, major 7ths and 9ths are essential to the harmony.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">The blues scale is formed by adding the flattened 3rd, 5th and 7th to a major scale. Blues notes give the music its characteristic sad or gritty sound, and musicians often bend or slide into them for expression.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n43">Improvisation and swung rhythms are defining features of both jazz and blues. Call and response — especially between voice and instrument — is a key texture in blues music.</li>
</ul>

If you hear improvisation, mention which instrument is soloing and what the rest of the ensemble is doing. For extended listening responses, structure your answer by working through the musical elements: melody, harmony, rhythm, texture and instrumentation. This ensures you cover enough points to access higher marks.</p>
