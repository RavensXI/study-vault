<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Four Roles, Every Ensemble</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In AoS2, you need to understand not just <em>which</em> instruments are in an ensemble, but <em>what each instrument is doing</em>. Every ensemble — whether it is a string quartet or a big band — divides the musical work into four key roles:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Melody</strong> — plays the lead part or the main tune.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Accompaniment</strong> — may play <dfn class="term" data-def="A secondary melody played against the main melody.">countermelodies</dfn>, imitation lines, or rhythmic ideas that support the melody.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Harmonic support</strong> — fills in inner parts of <dfn class="term" data-def="Two or more notes sounded together to create harmony.">chords</dfn>, giving the music its harmonic colour.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Rhythm section</strong> — keeps time and sets the <dfn class="term" data-def="The feel or pattern of the beat that drives the music forward.">groove</dfn>.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The same instrument can take on more than one role within a piece. For example, the piano in a piano trio can play the melody with the right hand while providing harmonic support and rhythmic drive with the left hand.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Let’s look at how these four roles are shared across the four ensembles covered in this unit: the string quartet, wind quintet, piano trio and big band.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">String Quartet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A chamber ensemble of two violins, a viola and a cello.">string quartet</dfn> comprises four instruments: 1st violin, 2nd violin, viola and cello. Because all four instruments belong to the same family, they blend together closely, but each has a distinct role.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>String Quartet — Role Breakdown</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Melody:</strong> The 1st violin often plays the main tune. It sits in the highest register, so it naturally cuts through the texture.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Accompaniment:</strong> The 2nd violin and viola may provide countermelodies, rhythmic figures, or <dfn class="term" data-def="When a melodic idea is repeated by a different instrument shortly after the original.">imitation</dfn> of the melody.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Harmonic support:</strong> The viola and cello fill in the inner parts of chords, giving the music richness and depth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Rhythm section:</strong> There is no dedicated rhythm section. Instead, the pulse is maintained through <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a note is played, e.g. staccato (short and detached) or legato (smooth and connected).">articulation</dfn> and bowing techniques such as pizzicato or tremolo.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Wind Quintet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A chamber ensemble of flute, oboe, clarinet, French horn and bassoon.">wind quintet</dfn> comprises five instruments: flute, oboe, clarinet, French horn and bassoon. Unlike the string quartet, these instruments come from different families (woodwind and brass), so they produce a wider range of <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality or colour of a sound that distinguishes one instrument from another.">timbres</dfn>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Wind Quintet — Role Breakdown</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Melody:</strong> The flute, oboe and clarinet often share melodic ideas, passing the tune between them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Accompaniment:</strong> The bassoon and French horn provide countermelodies and harmonic lines underneath the melody.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Harmonic support:</strong> The French horn, bassoon and clarinet fill chords and inner parts, adding warmth to the harmony.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Rhythm section:</strong> There is no dedicated rhythm section. The pulse is maintained through articulation and phrasing — for example, staccato quavers in the bassoon can create a sense of rhythmic drive.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Piano Trio</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A chamber ensemble of piano, violin and cello.">piano trio</dfn> comprises three instruments: piano, violin and cello. The piano is unique because it can play melody, chords and bass all at the same time, making this a very flexible ensemble.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Piano Trio — Role Breakdown</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Melody:</strong> The violin is the main melodic instrument. The cello sometimes takes the tune, and the piano’s right hand may also share melodic ideas.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Accompaniment:</strong> The piano’s left hand and the cello provide broken chords and rhythmic figures beneath the melody.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Harmonic support:</strong> The piano’s left hand and the cello underpin the harmonies, filling in the bass register.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Rhythm section:</strong> There is no dedicated rhythm section. Piano articulation — such as repeated chords or arpeggiated patterns — maintains the pulse.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Big Band</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A large jazz ensemble typically made up of saxophones, trumpets, trombones and a rhythm section.">big band</dfn> is much larger than the other ensembles studied in this unit. It consists of a large horn section and a rhythm section.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The horn section typically includes:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Saxophones:</strong> 2 alto, 2 tenor, 1 baritone</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Trombones:</strong> 3 tenor, 1 bass</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Trumpets:</strong> 4 (sometimes including a flugelhorn)</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n33">The rhythm section typically includes drum kit, electric guitar, bass guitar (or double bass), and piano.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define in a big band, the melody in your own words.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In a big band, the melody is usually played by the lead trumpet or lead alto saxophone. Other horns often double or harmonise the melody, or play <dfn class="term" data-def="Short, repeated rhythmic or melodic patterns played by the horn section in jazz.">riffs</dfn> and stabs in a call-and-response style.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Big Band — Role Breakdown</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Melody:</strong> The lead trumpet and lead alto sax often carry the main tune. Other horns may double the melody or provide harmonised versions of it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Accompaniment:</strong> Other horns play stabs, riffs, or <dfn class="term" data-def="A musical conversation where one part plays a phrase and another part answers it.">call-and-response</dfn> figures behind the lead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Harmonic support:</strong> The piano, guitar and trombones reinforce the harmonic structure by playing chords and filling inner parts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Rhythm section:</strong> The drums, bass, guitar and piano maintain tempo and groove. This is the first ensemble we have studied with a <em>dedicated</em> rhythm section.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n56">Sonatas</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n57">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A composition for one or two instruments, typically a solo instrument with keyboard accompaniment, or keyboard solo.">sonata</dfn> is a composition written for one or two instruments. In its most common form, a sonata is for a <strong>solo instrument plus keyboard</strong> (such as a violin sonata with piano accompaniment) or for <strong>piano solo</strong>. The term has been used across the Baroque, Classical, and Romantic eras, though the structure and style evolved over time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n58">In the <strong>Baroque era</strong>, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Baroque ensemble piece for two melody instruments and basso continuo, typically involving three or four players despite the name.">trio sonata</dfn> was one of the most important chamber music forms. Despite its name, a trio sonata actually involves <strong>three or four players</strong>: two melody instruments (such as two violins, or a flute and an oboe) plus the basso continuo (harpsichord and cello). The “trio” refers to the three written parts (two melody lines and one bass line), not the number of performers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n59">In the <strong>Classical and Romantic eras</strong>, the sonata became a work for a solo instrument with piano, or for piano alone. The roles were clearly defined: the <strong>soloist</strong> carries the melody and provides expressive, virtuosic playing, while the <strong>keyboard</strong> provides harmonic support, accompaniment patterns, and sometimes shares melodic ideas. Mozart, Beethoven, and Chopin all wrote famous piano sonatas and sonatas for violin and piano.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n60" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define sonata and trio sonata. How many players actually perform in a Baroque trio sonata, and why is the name misleading?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sonata = a composition for 1–2 instruments (solo instrument + keyboard, or piano alone). Baroque trio sonata = 2 melody instruments + basso continuo (3–4 players, despite “trio” in the name). In the exam, remember that “trio sonata” does not mean three players — it means three written parts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n39">Expression and Communication in Ensembles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n40">Knowing the roles is only half the picture. For the exam, you also need to explain how ensembles use the <dfn class="term" data-def="The building blocks of music: melody, harmony, rhythm, dynamics, tempo, texture, timbre, articulation, and structure.">elements of music</dfn> to communicate emotions and moods. Here is how some key elements are used expressively:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Dynamics:</strong> A sudden shift from <em>piano</em> (quiet) to <em>fortissimo</em> (very loud) can create shock or drama. A gradual crescendo builds tension and anticipation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42"><strong>Tempo:</strong> A fast tempo can suggest excitement or anger, while a slow tempo often creates sadness or tenderness.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n43"><strong>Articulation:</strong> Staccato notes can feel playful or agitated; legato playing sounds smooth and lyrical, often evoking love or calm.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n44"><strong>Melody:</strong> A rising, conjunct (stepwise) melody may feel hopeful. A falling, disjunct (leaping) melody can feel unsettled or dramatic.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n45"><strong>Harmony:</strong> Major keys and consonant chords suggest happiness. Minor keys and <dfn class="term" data-def="Clashing, tense-sounding combinations of notes.">dissonant</dfn> chords suggest sadness, fear or tension.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n46"><strong>Texture:</strong> A thin texture (e.g. a solo instrument) can feel vulnerable. A thick, layered texture creates power and grandeur.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n47" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In the exam, always describe <em>how</em> the parts interact, not just what each instrument is doing individually. AoS2 is about Music for Ensemble, so you need to understand how musicians work together to create an effect.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n48">For example, in <em>Defying Gravity</em> by Stephen Schwartz (from the musical <em>Wicked</em>), the orchestra builds drama through a crescendo, thickening texture and rising vocal melody. In <em>Prologue</em> by Stephen Sondheim (from <em>Into the Woods</em>), anticipation is created through repeated rhythmic patterns, a gradual build in dynamics, and the layering of different vocal parts.</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n49">In chamber ensembles (string quartet, wind quintet, piano trio), there is no dedicated rhythm section. The pulse is maintained through articulation, bowing techniques, or the piano’s rhythmic patterns. Roles are flexible — instruments often swap between melody and accompaniment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n50">In a big band, the rhythm section (drums, bass, guitar, piano) is a permanent, dedicated part of the ensemble. The horn section is also divided into clearly defined groups (saxophones, trumpets, trombones) that often play together in <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of different musical layers sounding at the same time.">homophonic</dfn> blocks of harmony.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n52">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n53">Every ensemble divides its musical work into four roles: melody, accompaniment, harmonic support and rhythm section — but chamber ensembles have no dedicated rhythm section.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n54">The big band is the only ensemble studied here with a permanent rhythm section (drums, bass, guitar, piano); in chamber groups, the pulse is maintained through articulation and playing technique.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n55">In the exam, always explain how parts <em>interact</em> and use precise musical vocabulary (dynamics, articulation, texture, harmony) to describe how an ensemble creates expression and mood.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n51">When an exam question asks you to “explain how the music creates” a mood or effect, structure your answer around the elements of music: mention melody, dynamics, tempo, texture, harmony and articulation. For each point, name the <em>specific instrument or section</em> and describe <em>what it does</em> and <em>why that creates the effect</em>. For example: “The lead trumpet plays a rising melody at forte, which creates a sense of excitement because…” Avoid vague statements like “it sounds good” — always use precise musical vocabulary.</p>
