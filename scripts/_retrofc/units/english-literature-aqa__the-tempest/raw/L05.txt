<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Prospero: The Magician-Duke</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Prospero is the play’s protagonist, antagonist, director, and moral centre all at once. He is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character with contradictory qualities — Prospero is both sympathetic (wronged exile) and troubling (authoritarian controller).">contradictory</dfn> figure: a wronged duke who deserves justice, but also an authoritarian who enslaves Caliban, binds Ariel, controls Miranda’s marriage, and manipulates everyone on the island. His magic gives him god-like power, yet he is driven by very human emotions — bitterness, anger, and eventually compassion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Shakespeare presents Prospero as a figure who must learn to let go. In Milan, he was too absorbed in books and magic, allowing Antonio to seize power. On the island, he becomes obsessed with control — staging every encounter, punishing disobedience, and choreographing the reunion. His growth comes when Ariel’s compassion shames him into choosing “virtue” over “vengeance.” By breaking his staff and drowning his book, he surrenders the tools that define him. In the epilogue, stripped of magic, he is vulnerable: “Now my charms are all o’erthrown, / And what strength I have’s mine own, / Which is most faint.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: What are three ways Prospero uses magic to control other characters, and what does each reveal about his character?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Prospero’s character arc moves from control to surrender. He creates the storm, puts Miranda to sleep, binds Ariel with threats, enslaves Caliban, orchestrates the Ferdinand–Miranda meeting, stages the masque, and traps his enemies. His decision to forgive and renounce magic is not easy — the word “rarer” acknowledges this. For AO1, track how his relationship with power changes across the play.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Caliban: Monster, Victim, Poet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Caliban is the most debated character in the play. Prospero and Miranda call him a “thing of darkness,” a “monster,” and “a born devil.” These labels define how the colonial master sees the colonised subject. But Shakespeare gives Caliban some of the play’s most beautiful and eloquent language: “Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not.” This poetry reveals an interior life that contradicts the “monster” label entirely.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Caliban’s claim to the island is presented as legitimate: “This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak’st from me.” He has been <dfn class="term" data-def="Stripped of land, rights, or status by a more powerful group — Caliban’s experience mirrors the dispossession of indigenous peoples under European colonialism.">dispossessed</dfn> by Prospero’s arrival. His line “You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse” encapsulates the colonial dynamic: the coloniser’s “gift” becomes the weapon the colonised turns against them. Yet Caliban’s attempted assault on Miranda complicates any simple reading of him as a noble victim. Shakespeare refuses to simplify him.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Post-colonial critics have recast Caliban as the play’s true hero — the indigenous inhabitant whose land and culture are stolen by a European coloniser. Aimé Césaire’s <em>A Tempest</em> (1969) rewrites the play from Caliban’s perspective, making him a symbol of anti-colonial resistance. In this reading, Caliban’s “curses” are acts of <dfn class="term" data-def="Active opposition to authority or control, especially by a colonised or oppressed group.">resistance</dfn>, his knowledge of the island represents indigenous wisdom that the coloniser exploits, and Prospero’s “civilising” mission is a cover for domination. For AO3, this contextual lens can transform your analysis of every Caliban scene.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ariel: Spirit, Servant, Conscience</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Ariel is a spirit of the air who longs for freedom. Unlike Caliban, Ariel serves Prospero willingly (if not happily), performing every task with skill and even artistry. Ariel creates the storm, leads Ferdinand to Miranda with a song, appears as a harpy to terrify the nobles, and stages the wedding masque. Ariel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to change shape or form at will — Ariel takes on multiple guises throughout the play, reflecting the spirit’s association with transformation and illusion.">shapeshifting</dfn> reflects the protean nature of theatrical performance itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Ariel’s most significant moment comes in Act 5, when the spirit describes the nobles’ suffering and adds: “Mine [affections] would [become tender], sir, were I human.” This is the play’s emotional pivot. A non-human creature expresses more compassion than Prospero himself, shaming the magician into mercy. Ariel functions as Prospero’s conscience — the voice that reminds him what humanity should mean, even though Ariel is not human.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Miranda: Innocence and Agency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Miranda has grown up in isolation, knowing only Prospero and Caliban. Her response to the world is defined by wonder and empathy: “O, I have suffered / With those that I saw suffer!” and later, “O brave new world, / That has such people in’t!” (The latter is bitterly ironic, since the “brave new world” includes Antonio and Sebastian.) Miranda is often read as a passive figure controlled by her father, but she shows real <dfn class="term" data-def="The capacity to act independently and make one’s own choices, as when Miranda defies Prospero’s instructions by revealing her name to Ferdinand.">agency</dfn>: she defies Prospero by telling Ferdinand her name, offers to carry his logs, and proposes marriage herself.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Antonio, Ferdinand, and Gonzalo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Antonio</strong> is the play’s unreformed villain. He usurped Prospero, plots to murder Alonso, and never apologises or shows remorse. His silence at the end is one of the play’s most troubling moments — forgiveness is offered but never accepted. Antonio represents the limit of Prospero’s power: magic can compel obedience but cannot force genuine repentance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Ferdinand</strong> serves as a foil to Caliban. Both serve Prospero, but Ferdinand serves willingly out of love, while Caliban serves under compulsion. Ferdinand’s willingness to carry logs proves his worth and allows the dynastic marriage that resolves the political conflict. He is honourable but relatively straightforward — his function is structural rather than psychological.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Gonzalo</strong> is the play’s moral compass. He saved Prospero and Miranda with food, water, and books. His utopian vision in Act 2 reflects genuine idealism, even if Antonio and Sebastian mock it. Prospero’s first acknowledgement in Act 5 is to Gonzalo: “Holy Gonzalo, honourable man.” Gonzalo represents the possibility that goodness can survive in a corrupt world.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Shakespeare structures the characters as <dfn class="term" data-def="Characters who contrast with each other to highlight particular qualities — e.g. Ferdinand (willing service) vs Caliban (forced labour), or Gonzalo (honesty) vs Antonio (treachery).">foils</dfn>. Ferdinand’s willing service contrasts Caliban’s enslavement. Gonzalo’s honesty contrasts Antonio’s treachery. Ariel’s obedient service contrasts Caliban’s rebellion. Miranda’s innocence contrasts Antonio’s corruption. These contrasts are deliberate: they force the audience to ask whether goodness is innate (nature) or learned (nurture) — a question Shakespeare never definitively answers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Draw a quick diagram from memory showing how Ferdinand, Caliban, and Ariel each serve Prospero differently, and what each type of service represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The play’s characters are structured as foils: Ferdinand (love) vs Caliban (force), Gonzalo (loyalty) vs Antonio (betrayal), Ariel (spirit-obedience) vs Caliban (physical-rebellion). These contrasts test whether goodness is innate or shaped by experience — the nature vs nurture debate that runs through the entire play.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Prospero is contradictory: a wronged duke who deserves justice but also an authoritarian controller. His arc moves from absolute power to voluntary surrender.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Caliban is monster, victim, and poet simultaneously — Shakespeare refuses to simplify him. Post-colonial readings recast him as the play’s true protagonist.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The characters function as foils: Ferdinand vs Caliban (willing vs forced service), Gonzalo vs Antonio (honesty vs treachery), Ariel vs Caliban (obedience vs rebellion).</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">When analysing characters for AQA, always discuss how Shakespeare <em>presents</em> them — through language, imagery, structural position, and what other characters say about them. For Caliban, contrast what Prospero calls him (“thing of darkness,” “monster”) with what he says himself (“Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises”). This gap between reputation and reality is where the best AO2 analysis lives. Always use “Shakespeare presents…” not “Caliban is…”</p>
