<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Act 1, Scene 1: The Storm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The play opens with a violent tempest that threatens to sink a ship carrying Alonso (King of Naples), his son Ferdinand, Antonio (Prospero’s treacherous brother), Sebastian, Gonzalo, and others. The storm is significant because it immediately disrupts the normal social hierarchy: the Boatswain shouts at the nobles, telling them to stay out of the way: “What cares these roarers for the name of king?” The sea does not respect rank or title. This line establishes a key theme — the fragility of human <dfn class="term" data-def="The right or ability to give orders, make decisions, and enforce obedience — a central theme in The Tempest, where different characters claim authority through different means.">authority</dfn> when confronted by nature’s power.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Gonzalo, the honest old counsellor, tries to calm everyone: “The wills above be done! But I would fain die a dry death.” His characteristic mix of faith and dry humour marks him out from the start as the play’s moral compass. Meanwhile, the mariners struggle and Antonio curses. The scene is chaotic, noisy, and terrifying — and then we discover it was all staged by Prospero using magic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Who says ‘Hell is empty and all the devils are here’ and what does it reveal about how the storm is perceived?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The storm is artificial — created by Prospero through Ariel’s magic. No one is harmed, as Prospero later confirms: “Not a hair perished.” The key quotation “Hell is empty and all the devils are here” (spoken by Ferdinand in Ariel’s report) captures how terrifying the illusion was. It also foreshadows the play’s concern with the boundary between appearance and reality.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Act 1, Scene 2: Prospero’s Exposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Scene 2 is the longest in the play and serves as a massive <dfn class="term" data-def="Background information provided to the audience through dialogue or narration, filling in events that occurred before the play begins.">exposition</dfn> dump. Prospero tells Miranda their history: he was the Duke of Milan, a scholar so absorbed in his books and magic that he neglected his duties and allowed his brother Antonio to seize power. Antonio conspired with Alonso, King of Naples, and Prospero and the infant Miranda were set adrift in “a rotten carcass of a butt” — a leaky boat. Only Gonzalo’s secret kindness, providing food, water, and Prospero’s beloved books, kept them alive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Prospero’s telling is emotionally charged and controlling. He repeatedly interrupts himself to check Miranda is listening: “Dost thou attend me?” and “Thou attend’st not.” This reveals his need for control — even over the narrative of his own suffering. Miranda’s response is pure compassion: “O, I have suffered / With those that I saw suffer!” She feels others’ pain instinctively, establishing her as the play’s emotional heart.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Miranda has grown up on the island with no memory of Milan and no contact with other humans besides her father and Caliban. Her name means “admirable” or “wonderful” in Latin, and she lives up to it: she responds to the shipwreck with immediate empathy, not fear. Her famous line later in the play — “O brave new world, / That has such people in’t!” — is deeply ironic because the people she admires include the very men who betrayed her father. Her innocence is both her virtue and her vulnerability. Shakespeare uses her to ask whether goodness comes from nature or experience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ariel: The Bound Spirit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A spirit of the air, bound to serve Prospero in exchange for being freed from imprisonment in a pine tree by the witch Sycorax.">Ariel</dfn> is an airy spirit who serves Prospero in exchange for a promise of freedom. Before Prospero arrived, Ariel had been imprisoned inside a “cloven pine” by the witch Sycorax for refusing to carry out her “earthy and abhorred commands.” Prospero freed Ariel but bound the spirit to his own service instead. When Ariel asks for liberty, Prospero responds with anger: “Dost thou forget / From what a torment I did free thee?”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This exchange is troubling. Prospero saved Ariel from one form of imprisonment only to impose another. He holds his act of rescue over Ariel as a permanent debt: “I must / Once in a month recount what thou hast been, / Which thou forget’st.” (actual line: Prospero must remind Ariel of her own history because Ariel tends to forget it — not that Ariel acknowledges a monthly debt to Prospero) The relationship is one of service, gratitude, and control. Ariel obeys, but always with one eye on the promised reward: freedom. Ariel’s yearning for liberty — “Do you love me, master?” — gives the character an emotional depth that goes beyond mere obedience.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Caliban: “This Island’s Mine”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The son of the witch Sycorax and an unnamed devil (Setebos was the god Sycorax worshipped, not Caliban’s father). Born on the island before Prospero’s arrival, he claims it as his birthright.">Caliban</dfn> is the most complex and debated character in the play. He is the son of Sycorax and the unnamed devil (described by Prospero as “the devil himself”). Setebos is the god that Sycorax worshipped — not Caliban’s father. When Prospero first arrived, Caliban and Prospero had a warm relationship: Caliban showed Prospero the island’s resources, and Prospero taught Caliban language. But after Caliban attempted to assault Miranda, Prospero enslaved him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Caliban’s great speech of resentment makes his claim clear: “This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak’st from me.” He articulates the experience of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a group of people is stripped of their land, culture, or rights by a colonising power.">dispossessed</dfn>: “You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse.” This is one of the play’s most powerful lines. Language — the supposed gift of civilisation — has only given Caliban the tools to express his rage at being subjugated. The coloniser’s “gift” becomes the colonised’s weapon.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Caliban’s quotation about language and explain what it reveals about the coloniser-colonised relationship.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse.” This quotation encapsulates the colonial dynamic: Prospero sees teaching language as a civilising gift, while Caliban sees it as a tool of oppression that only lets him articulate his suffering. The word “profit” is bitterly ironic — the only profit Caliban has gained from his colonisation is the ability to voice his dispossession.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">In Act 1, Scene 2, Prospero uses magic to control every character: he creates the storm, puts Miranda to sleep, summons and threatens Ariel, and punishes Caliban with cramps. His magic is both a narrative device and a symbol of authority. It allows him to stage-manage every encounter on the island, from Ferdinand’s meeting with Miranda to the nobles’ wandering. But this control comes at a cost — it isolates him and prevents genuine human connection. The play’s resolution requires Prospero to renounce his magic and trust in human relationships instead.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The opening storm is artificial — Prospero’s magic controls nature and disrupts social hierarchy, establishing the play’s themes of power and illusion from the very first scene.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Caliban’s “You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse” is the play’s most powerful statement about colonialism — the “gift” of civilisation becomes a weapon of resistance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Prospero controls Ariel through obligation and Caliban through force; both relationships raise questions about the legitimacy and morality of power.</li></ul>
