<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Colonialism: Prospero as Coloniser</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The most influential modern reading of <em>The Tempest</em> interprets the play through the lens of <dfn class="term" data-def="A critical approach that examines literature from the perspective of colonised peoples, questioning the power dynamics between coloniser and colonised.">post-colonialism</dfn>. In this reading, Prospero is the European coloniser: he arrives on an island already inhabited by Caliban, claims ownership, imposes his language and culture, and forces the native into servitude. Caliban’s resentment — “This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak’st from me” — is the cry of every dispossessed indigenous people under European empire.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The parallels with Jacobean colonialism are striking. England was actively colonising the New World when Shakespeare wrote the play. The Virginia Company had established Jamestown in 1607, and the <em>Sea Venture</em> shipwreck of 1609 provided direct source material. Prospero’s treatment of Caliban mirrors the colonial pattern: initial cooperation (“When thou cam’st first, / Thou strok’dst me and made much of me”), followed by exploitation, forced labour, and cultural erasure.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: How does Caliban’s line about language (‘I know how to curse’) work as a metaphor for colonialism? Name the specific irony Shakespeare creates.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse.” This line is central to post-colonial readings. The coloniser’s “gift” of language was intended to “civilise” Caliban, but he uses it to resist. The bitter irony of “profit” — a word drawn from the commercial vocabulary of colonialism — exposes the transaction: Prospero expected gratitude and obedience in return for education, but Caliban refuses to play the grateful native.</p>
</div>

While you should not treat Césaire’s version as identical to Shakespeare’s, knowing that this reading exists demonstrates sophisticated contextual understanding (AO3). <h2 data-narration-id="n6">Magic as Art and Theatre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Prospero’s magic can be read as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure, object, or action in a literary work that represents something beyond its literal meaning — Prospero’s magic allegorically represents Shakespeare’s creative power.">allegory</dfn> for Shakespeare’s art. Both Prospero and Shakespeare create illusions that move, terrify, and delight audiences. Prospero stages the storm, the masque, the harpy banquet, and the spirits — all of which are theatrical spectacles. His famous line, “Our revels now are ended,” is widely read as Shakespeare’s own farewell to the stage. When Prospero breaks his staff and drowns his book, it mirrors the playwright laying down his pen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">This <dfn class="term" data-def="A device in drama or literature that draws attention to its own status as a work of art, reminding the audience that they are watching a constructed fiction.">metatheatrical</dfn> reading transforms every use of magic in the play into a commentary on the power and limitations of art. Prospero can create stunning illusions, but he cannot force genuine repentance (Antonio never apologises). Art can move people emotionally, but it cannot change their fundamental nature. In the epilogue, Prospero acknowledges this: stripped of his theatrical magic, he depends on the audience’s “indulgence” — their willingness to be moved.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Unities: Time, Place, and Action</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><em>The Tempest</em> is Shakespeare’s most structurally disciplined play. It observes the three classical <dfn class="term" data-def="Dramatic principles derived from Aristotle: unity of time (action within a single day), unity of place (a single location), and unity of action (one main plot with interconnected subplots).">unities</dfn>: the action takes place on one island (place), within approximately three hours of real time (time), and follows a single overarching plan orchestrated by Prospero (action). Prospero himself draws attention to this: “What is the time o’ th’ day? … The time ’twixt six and now / Must by us both be spent most preciously.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The tight structure creates a sense of controlled intensity that mirrors Prospero’s own orchestration. Everything on the island happens according to his design — the characters are moved around like actors hitting their marks. This structural choice reinforces the metatheatrical reading: the island is a stage, Prospero is the director, and the other characters are performers in a play they do not know they are in. For AO2, the unities are a structural feature you can analyse to show how form reflects meaning.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">The epilogue is spoken directly to the audience by Prospero alone. He has renounced his magic and freed Ariel; now he asks to be freed himself by the audience’s applause. “Now my charms are all o’erthrown, / And what strength I have’s mine own, / Which is most faint.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A concluding speech in a play, typically delivered directly to the audience by a character who steps outside the fiction of the drama.">epilogue</dfn> operates simultaneously as a character’s plea, an actor’s curtain call, and a playwright’s farewell. The final couplet — “As you from crimes would pardoned be, / Let your indulgence set me free” — invokes the Lord’s Prayer (“Forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us”). The audience becomes the final authority: they hold the power to release or condemn.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Name three structural features of The Tempest and explain how each reinforces the theme of control and illusion.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Tempest’s structure reinforces its themes. The unities of time, place, and action mirror Prospero’s absolute control. The masque-within-a-play is a performance within a performance within a performance. The epilogue breaks the fourth wall, collapsing the boundary between Prospero’s magic and Shakespeare’s theatre. Form and content are inseparable — the play’s structure <em>is</em> its meaning.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Nature Versus Nurture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Prospero calls Caliban “A devil, a born devil, on whose nature / Nurture can never stick.” This is the clearest statement of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The debate about whether a person’s character and behaviour are determined by their inborn qualities (nature) or by their upbringing and environment (nurture).">nature versus nurture</dfn> debate in the play. Prospero believes Caliban is inherently evil and that education (“nurture”) has failed. But the play complicates this position: Caliban’s beautiful poetry suggests an inner sensitivity that contradicts the “born devil” label, and the fact that Antonio — a European noble raised with every civilising advantage — is equally treacherous undermines Prospero’s claim that only Caliban is irredeemable by nature.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Post-colonial readings recast Prospero as a coloniser and Caliban as the dispossessed native. Caliban’s “I know how to curse” turns the coloniser’s “gift” into a weapon of resistance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Prospero’s magic allegorically represents Shakespeare’s art: both create powerful illusions but cannot force genuine change. The epilogue completes this parallel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The classical unities of time, place, and action mirror Prospero’s controlled orchestration of events, reinforcing the metatheatrical dimension where form reflects meaning.</li></ul>
