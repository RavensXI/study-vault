<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Three Unities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>An Inspector Calls</em> is built on the classical <dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic principle originating in ancient Greek theatre requiring that a play takes place in one location (unity of place), within a single continuous time period (unity of time), and follows one main storyline (unity of action).">three unities</dfn> of time, place, and action. The entire play takes place in the Birling dining room, in real time, on a single evening, following one investigation. This is a deliberate structural choice. By confining the action to a single room and a single evening, Priestley creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of being enclosed and unable to escape — created in the play by the single-room setting that traps the characters with the Inspector.">claustrophobic</dfn> intensity. There is nowhere for the Birlings to hide. They cannot leave the room, change the subject, or wait for the Inspector to go away. The dining room — which opened the play as a place of celebration and warmth — becomes a moral courtroom.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The real-time structure also means the audience experiences the same pressure as the characters. There are no time jumps, no scene changes, no relief. Each revelation follows the last without a break. For a 1945 theatre audience sitting in the auditorium, this created an immersive experience: they were trapped in the Birling dining room just as the family was trapped by the Inspector. Priestley uses the three unities not as an academic exercise but as a weapon — the structure itself forces accountability.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three unities and why does Priestley use them in An Inspector Calls?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three unities (time, place, action) transform the Birling dining room from a celebration venue into a courtroom. The claustrophobic, real-time structure means neither the characters nor the audience can escape the Inspector's moral investigation. The setting itself embodies Priestley's argument: moral accountability is inescapable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Lighting: Pink to Hard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Priestley's most famous <dfn class="term" data-def="Instructions written by the playwright indicating how actors should move, speak, or how the set should look — in An Inspector Calls, the stage directions carry significant symbolic meaning.">stage direction</dfn> is the lighting instruction at the start of Act 1: "The lighting should be pink and intimate until the Inspector arrives, and then it should be brighter and harder." This is the play's central visual metaphor. "Pink" lighting is soft, warm, and flattering — it represents how the Birlings see themselves. They exist in a rosy glow of self-satisfaction, wealth, and confidence. "Brighter and harder" light is harsh, exposing, forensic — it represents the truth the Inspector brings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The lighting change happens at the exact moment the Inspector arrives, linking his presence directly to the revelation of truth. In performance, this shift is dramatic: the audience physically sees the room change. The warm glow vanishes and the characters are suddenly exposed — every expression, every reaction, visible in harsh light. No other playwright achieves such a powerful effect with a single stage direction. The lighting is not a background detail but a structural device that shapes the entire play's meaning.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

    <span>The Doorbell: Interrupting Capitalism</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Inspector's arrival is announced by a doorbell that interrupts Birling mid-speech. Birling is in the middle of his capitalist philosophy — "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself" — when the "sharp ring of a front door bell" cuts him off. This is symbolically loaded: the Inspector (representing collective responsibility) literally interrupts capitalism. Birling's selfish worldview is silenced by the arrival of moral accountability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The stage direction specifies "sharp" — not a polite knock but an insistent, unavoidable ring. The doorbell pierces the cosy, self-congratulatory atmosphere like a warning. Priestley ensures that the audience connects the Inspector's arrival with the interruption of Birling's philosophy. The moral challenge begins at the exact moment the capitalist monologue is cut short. From this point on, Birling never regains control of the evening.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Dramatic Irony: The Audience Knows More</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic device where the audience knows something the characters on stage do not — in An Inspector Calls, this operates on multiple levels simultaneously.">Dramatic irony</dfn> is Priestley's most sustained technique. It operates on at least three distinct levels throughout the play. First, there is historical irony: Birling's predictions about the Titanic ("unsinkable"), war ("the Germans don't want war"), and the economy ("steadily increasing prosperity") are all catastrophically wrong, and the 1945 audience knows it. This undermines his authority from the opening scene.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Second, there is investigative irony: as the Inspector's questioning progresses, the audience often grasps the implications before the characters do. The most devastating example is Mrs Birling's insistence in Act 2 that "the father of the child" should be held "entirely responsible" — not knowing the father is Eric. The audience sees the trap; Mrs Birling does not. Sheila tries desperately to stop her mother — "Mother — stop — stop!" — but Mrs Birling ploughs on, condemning her own son with every sentence.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Photograph Technique</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The Inspector shows his photograph of Eva Smith to only one character at a time, refusing to let others see it. Gerald challenges this: "We didn't see the photograph, did we?" This technique serves multiple purposes. It focuses each character's individual guilt — they cannot hide behind someone else's confession. It creates mystery: the audience cannot be certain that every character is describing the same woman. And it raises a structural question the play never answers: if each character knew a different woman, the Birlings have exploited not one Eva Smith but many — making the systemic nature of their exploitation even clearer.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The photograph is also significant because it is the only evidence the Inspector brings. He has no files, no documents, no backup. His power comes entirely from moral authority and the characters' own guilty consciences. Priestley suggests that truth does not need institutional power — it only needs someone willing to ask the right questions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: three levels of dramatic irony in the play, with an example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dramatic irony operates on three levels: (1) historical — the audience knows Birling's predictions are wrong; (2) investigative — the audience sees implications before characters do (Mrs Birling condemning Eric); (3) structural — the cyclical ending means the audience knows the investigation will repeat while the characters believe they have escaped.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Cyclical Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The play's ending is its most powerful structural device. After the Inspector leaves and the family discovers he was not a real inspector, Birling celebrates: the "famous younger generation who know it all. And they can't even take a joke." Then the telephone rings. A girl has died. A real inspector is on his way. The play ends with the family "staring, dumbfounded." This <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where the ending echoes or returns to the beginning, implying that events will repeat — in An Inspector Calls, the final phone call means the investigation will start again.">cyclical structure</dfn> means the entire evening is about to happen again.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The cyclical ending achieves several things. It punishes the older generation: they were given a chance to learn and wasted it, so they must face the consequences for real. It validates the younger generation: Sheila and Eric were right that the Inspector's identity did not matter. And it extends the warning to the audience: Priestley is saying that Britain has been given a second chance after the wars. The 1912 Birlings failed to learn. Will the 1945 audience make the same mistake?</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The three unities create claustrophobic intensity — the single room becomes a moral courtroom where accountability is inescapable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The lighting change from "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder" is the play's central visual metaphor: self-deception replaced by truth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Dramatic irony operates on three levels (historical, investigative, structural), and the cyclical ending extends Priestley's warning to the audience: learn the lesson or history repeats.</li></ul>
