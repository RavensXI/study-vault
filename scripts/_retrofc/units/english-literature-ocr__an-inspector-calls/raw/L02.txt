Act 1 — the engagement dinner, Birling's speeches, the Inspector's arrival and Eva's sacking.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Engagement Dinner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 1 opens with the Birling family celebrating Sheila's engagement to Gerald Croft in their dining room. Priestley's <dfn class="term" data-def="Instructions in a script describing how the set, lighting, sound and characters should appear or behave.">stage directions</dfn> describe the room as having "good solid furniture" with "dessert plates and champagne glasses" — every detail signals wealth, comfort, and self-satisfaction. The Birlings are at the peak of their confidence. Arthur has just been talking about his potential knighthood, Sheila is admiring her engagement ring, and Gerald has been accepted into the family. Priestley builds this mood of celebration deliberately so that the Inspector's arrival will shatter it more effectively.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The most important stage direction in the entire play appears here. The lighting should be "pink and intimate until the Inspector arrives, and then it should be brighter and harder." This is not a casual note about atmosphere — it is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An object, colour, or image that represents something beyond its literal meaning.">symbol</dfn> at the heart of Priestley's design. The "pink" light represents the Birlings' rose-tinted view of themselves: warm, comfortable, flattering. When the Inspector arrives, the "brighter and harder" light represents truth, scrutiny, and moral accountability. The family can no longer hide in their comfortable glow.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does the lighting change from 'pink and intimate' to 'brighter and harder' symbolise?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The lighting change — "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder" — is the play's central visual metaphor. It represents the shift from comfortable self-deception to the harsh light of moral truth. Every production must make this transition visible to the audience.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Birling's Speeches: Capitalism on Display</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Before the Inspector arrives, Arthur Birling delivers a series of after-dinner speeches to Gerald and Eric. These speeches serve multiple dramatic functions. First, Birling outlines his philosophy: "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own — and I'm talking as a hard-headed, practical man of business." This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A political and economic system based on private ownership, free markets, and the pursuit of profit — represented in the play by Birling's philosophy of looking after yourself.">capitalist</dfn> worldview that Priestley wrote the entire play to challenge. Birling believes that selfishness is not just acceptable but admirable — the sign of a practical, successful man.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Second, Birling makes predictions that the 1945 audience would know to be catastrophically wrong. He calls the Titanic "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable." He insists that "the Germans don't want war. Nobody wants war." He predicts "steadily increasing prosperity." Every single statement is wrong. The Titanic sank on 15 April 1912 — the same year the play is set. The First World War broke out in 1914. The Great Depression followed in 1929. The Second World War began in 1939. Priestley uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary device where the audience knows the true outcome of events that the characters do not, creating tension, irony, or humour.">dramatic irony</dfn> to destroy Birling's authority before the Inspector even rings the doorbell.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Birling's Speech Matters Structurally</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Priestley places Birling's speech immediately before the Inspector's arrival for a reason. The audience has just heard Birling dismiss community as "nonsense" and predict a rosy future that they know ended in two world wars. When the Inspector arrives seconds later, the audience is already primed to side with him against Birling. This structural choice means the Inspector does not need to win the argument — Birling has already lost it. The doorbell that interrupts Birling's speech is itself symbolic: it cuts off his capitalist monologue mid-flow, as if the moral world itself is refusing to let him continue.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Birling also reveals his obsession with social status during this scene. He tells Gerald that he is "in the running" for a knighthood and that the engagement will bring the Birlings and Crofts — "one of the oldest county families" — together. This social climbing exposes Birling's values: people are valued for their status, not their character. It foreshadows his treatment of Eva Smith, who has no status and is therefore disposable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Inspector Arrives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Inspector Goole arrives and immediately transforms the atmosphere. Priestley describes him as creating "an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness." He is not physically large — the "massiveness" is moral authority. He speaks "carefully, weightily" and "has a disconcerting habit of looking hard at the person he addresses before actually speaking." This technique — the pause before speaking — gives the Inspector power over every conversation. He makes the Birlings wait, forcing them into uncomfortable silence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Inspector announces that a young woman called Eva Smith has died in the infirmary after swallowing disinfectant. He then begins his interrogation with Arthur Birling, revealing that Birling sacked Eva from his factory in September 1910. Eva had been one of the leaders of a strike demanding a pay rise from twenty-two shillings and sixpence to twenty-five shillings a week. Birling refused: "If I'd agreed to this demand for a new rate, we'd have added about twelve per cent to our labour costs." When Sheila says "these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people," Birling dismisses her entirely. This exchange encapsulates the play's central conflict: Birling sees workers as a cost to be minimised; the Inspector sees them as human beings with rights.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what does the Inspector mean by 'these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people'?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila's line "these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people" directly challenges Birling's capitalist worldview. It is the first statement of the play's central argument: that human beings have inherent value and cannot be treated as disposable resources. Birling's inability to understand this point is precisely what Priestley wants the audience to reject.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Sheila's Confession at Milwards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Inspector then turns to Sheila. He reveals that after being sacked by Birling, Eva found work at Milwards, an expensive department store. Sheila had Eva sacked because Eva held up a dress against herself and "it just suited her. She was the right type for it, just as I was the wrong type" — and when Sheila caught Eva smiling, she "went to the manager at Milwards and I told him that if they didn't get rid of that girl, I'd never go near the place again." Sheila used her family's economic power — the threat of withdrawing custom — to destroy a young woman's livelihood over a moment of jealousy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">What distinguishes Sheila from her father is her immediate, genuine <dfn class="term" data-def="Deep regret and guilt for a wrongdoing, accompanied by a desire to make amends.">remorse</dfn>. She says "If I could help her now, I would" and is visibly distressed. Priestley uses Sheila's reaction to model the correct moral response for the audience. While Birling tries to justify his actions, Sheila accepts responsibility without reservation. This contrast between father and daughter establishes the <dfn class="term" data-def="A difference in attitudes and values between older and younger people within the same family or society.">generational divide</dfn> that runs through the entire play: the older generation defends its selfishness; the younger generation recognises it as wrong.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Eva Smith: The Absent Everywoman</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Eva Smith never appears on stage. She exists only through the Inspector's account and the other characters' confessions. Her name is symbolic: "Eva" echoes "Eve," suggesting she represents all women, while "Smith" is the most common English surname — she represents the ordinary, the overlooked, the expendable. By keeping her absent, Priestley forces the audience to focus not on Eva's suffering but on the Birlings' guilt. The audience must judge the privileged, not pity the victim. Eva is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who represents all members of a group — standing for ordinary people rather than being a unique individual.">everywoman</dfn> figure: she could be any working-class woman destroyed by the indifference of those with power.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The Inspector later tells the family that Eva also used the name "Daisy Renton" — a detail that will become significant in Act 2 when Gerald recognises it. The multiple names reinforce her representative quality: she is not one woman but many, each identity representing a different phase of exploitation by a different member of the privileged class.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The lighting change from "pink and intimate" to "brighter and harder" is the play's central visual metaphor — comfortable illusion replaced by moral truth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Birling's dramatically ironic predictions ("unsinkable," no war, prosperity) destroy his credibility before the Inspector arrives, priming the audience to reject his capitalist philosophy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Sheila's immediate remorse contrasts with Birling's self-justification, establishing the generational divide between those who accept responsibility and those who refuse it.</li></ul>
