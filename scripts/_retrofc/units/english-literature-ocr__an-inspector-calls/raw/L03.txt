--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Gerald and the Palace Bar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 2 opens with the Inspector turning his attention to Gerald Croft. Gerald recognises the name "Daisy Renton" — the alias Eva Smith used after being sacked from Milwards — and his reaction is immediate: he gives "a start" and Sheila notices. Gerald confesses that he met Eva the previous spring at the Palace Bar, a place he describes as a haunt of "women of the town" — in other words, a venue associated with <dfn class="term" data-def="The buying and selling of sexual services, a major social issue in Edwardian England that affected vulnerable working-class women with few other economic options.">prostitution</dfn>. Eva was being harassed by Alderman Meggarty, a local councillor, and Gerald intervened.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Gerald installed Eva as his mistress, giving her money and a set of rooms. He describes their relationship with genuine feeling: "She was young and pretty and warm-hearted — and intensely grateful." However, Priestley ensures the audience sees the power imbalance beneath Gerald's apparent kindness. Gerald had wealth, status, and freedom; Eva had nothing. She was "intensely grateful" because she was desperate. When Gerald ended the affair because "I had to go away for several weeks" on business, Eva "didn't blame me at all. I must say she didn't." The fact that Gerald emphasises Eva's lack of blame is revealing — he needs her forgiveness to excuse his own <dfn class="term" data-def="Using someone's vulnerability for one's own benefit, even when appearing to help them.">exploitation</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what makes Gerald's treatment of Eva exploitative despite his genuine feelings?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gerald's relationship with Eva appears to be the kindest treatment she receives, but Priestley reveals it as exploitation dressed in gentlemanly concern. Gerald had total power: he chose when the relationship began, controlled its terms, and ended it when convenient. Eva's "gratitude" was the gratitude of someone with no other options — not genuine freedom of choice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sheila's Growing Moral Authority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">During Gerald's confession, Sheila's role in the play shifts significantly. She moves from being a suspect to becoming almost a co-interrogator alongside the Inspector. When Gerald tries to send her out of the room to spare her feelings, Sheila refuses: "Why should I? It's my turn to ask questions." She has absorbed the Inspector's moral clarity and now applies it to Gerald. She returns the engagement ring — not in anger, but in honest recognition that the relationship was built on lies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Sheila also begins to function as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic device where a character voices insights that the audience is thinking, expressing the moral position the playwright wants the audience to adopt.">chorus figure</dfn>, commenting on the action and warning other characters about what is coming. She tells her mother: "You mustn't try to build up a kind of wall between us and that girl. If you do, the Inspector will just break it down." This line is significant because it shows Sheila has understood the Inspector's power — he will reach the truth regardless of the family's resistance. It also creates dramatic irony, since Mrs Birling ignores the warning completely.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Gerald's Exit and Return</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">After his confession, Gerald asks to leave to "be alone for a while" and goes for a walk. His exit is structurally important: it removes him from Mrs Birling's interrogation, meaning he does not witness the devastating revelation about Eric. When Gerald returns in Act 3, he has not been changed by the experience in the way Sheila has — he immediately begins trying to prove the Inspector was a fake. Priestley uses Gerald's absence to show that physical distance from the moral reckoning allows him to avoid genuine transformation. He represents the comfortable middle ground: not as callous as the Birlings, but not willing to truly change either.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Gerald's position is particularly interesting because he belongs to a higher social class than the Birlings — his family are "an old country family — landed people." Where Birling's wealth comes from industry (new money), Gerald's comes from land (old money). Priestley suggests that both forms of privilege are equally capable of exploitation, even when they wear a more charming face.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Mrs Birling and the Charity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Inspector's interrogation of Sybil Birling is the play's most devastating sequence. Mrs Birling is chair of the Brumley Women's Charity Organisation — the last safety net available to a desperate, pregnant woman. Eva came to the committee using the name "Mrs Birling," which Sybil found "a piece of gross impertinence." She used her influence to have the committee refuse Eva's appeal. The charity — the one institution that existed specifically to help women like Eva — rejected her because of <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong and unreasonable feeling that people of a particular social class are inferior and undeserving.">class prejudice</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Mrs Birling's language reveals the depth of her snobbery. She calls Eva's manner "a lot of silly nonsense" and dismisses her claim to be married as a lie because "she was claiming elaborate fine feelings and scruples that were simply absurd in a girl in her position." This is the most chilling line Mrs Birling speaks — she is saying that a working-class woman is incapable of having genuine feelings or moral standards. It dehumanises Eva entirely and exposes the class system's ultimate cruelty: the denial that the poor are fully human.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: why is Mrs Birling's rejection of Eva the most significant act of cruelty in the play?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Birling's cruelty is the most significant because she represents organised charity — society's formal mechanism for helping the vulnerable. When she rejects Eva, it is not just personal cruelty but institutional failure. Priestley argues that class prejudice corrupts even institutions designed to do good, making the Welfare State necessary.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Dramatic Irony Trap</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The climax of Act 2 is one of the most brilliantly constructed passages of dramatic irony in English drama. The Inspector asks Mrs Birling who she thinks should bear the primary responsibility for Eva's situation. Mrs Birling, still unaware that her own son is the father of Eva's child, declares that "the father of the child" should be held "entirely responsible — he should be dealt with very severely." She insists he should "be made an example of" and that "he ought to be dealt with very severely."</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Sheila and the audience see the trap closing. Sheila tries desperately to stop her mother: "Mother — stop — stop!" But Mrs Birling continues, digging herself deeper with every sentence. She is, without knowing it, condemning her own son to the harshest possible judgement — and she has done so publicly, making it impossible to retract. The act ends with Eric's entrance, described by Priestley with devastating simplicity: "Eric enters, looking extremely pale and distressed. They stare at him. He comes in, the door slowly closes behind him. Curtain." The slow-closing door seals the family's fate. The 1945 audience would have felt the full weight of Mrs Birling's <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive pride or self-confidence that leads to a character's downfall — Mrs Birling's class snobbery blinds her to the truth about her own family.">hubris</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Inspector's Control of Information</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Throughout Act 2, the Inspector controls information with surgical precision. He knows the full story before he begins — his questions are not investigative but revelatory, designed to force each character to confess publicly. He insists on "one line of enquiry at a time" and refuses to let characters see the photograph simultaneously. This method ensures that each person must face their individual guilt without hiding behind someone else's confession.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The Inspector also allows Mrs Birling to condemn herself. He could have revealed that Eric is the father at any point, but he deliberately lets Mrs Birling call for the harshest punishment before the truth emerges. This is not cruelty but dramatic justice: Priestley ensures that Mrs Birling's class prejudice traps her in her own moral judgement. She has publicly demanded punishment for her own son — and she cannot take it back.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Gerald's treatment of Eva reveals how exploitation can wear a kind face — his power over her was absolute, regardless of his genuine feelings.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Mrs Birling's rejection of Eva at the charity represents institutional failure: class prejudice corrupts even organisations designed to help the vulnerable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The dramatic irony of Mrs Birling condemning 'the father' without knowing it is Eric is one of the most devastating structural devices in English drama — she traps herself in her own moral judgement.</li></ul>
