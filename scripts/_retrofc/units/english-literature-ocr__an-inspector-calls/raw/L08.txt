<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Responsibility — Individual and Collective</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The duty to care for others and accept the consequences of one's actions — the play explores both personal responsibility (each character's individual guilt) and collective responsibility (society's shared duty to its members).">Responsibility</dfn> is the play's central theme and the word that connects every character, every scene, and every structural choice Priestley makes. The Inspector's thesis statement — "We don't live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other" — defines the entire play. Individual responsibility is explored through the chain of events: Birling sacked Eva, Sheila had her dismissed from Milwards, Gerald used and discarded her, Mrs Birling denied her charity, Eric assaulted her. Each character bears personal guilt. But Priestley's deeper argument is about collective responsibility: no single person killed Eva — society killed her through systemic indifference.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Birling explicitly rejects collective responsibility: "a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own." He calls community "nonsense." The entire play exists to demolish this position. By the final phone call, the audience knows that Birling's philosophy leads not to prosperity but to "fire and blood and anguish." Priestley argues that the Welfare State — where society collectively provides healthcare, housing, and education for all — is the only moral alternative to the Birlings' world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: explain the difference between individual and collective responsibility in the play, with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Individual responsibility: each Birling contributed separately to Eva's death. Collective responsibility: their actions formed a chain, and the society they represent — one that allows the powerful to exploit the vulnerable without consequence — is ultimately to blame. Priestley's argument is that both forms of responsibility must be acknowledged for society to change.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme 2: Class and Privilege</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of social hierarchy based on wealth, birth, and occupation — in Edwardian England, class determined almost every aspect of a person's life, from employment to legal rights.">class system</dfn> is the mechanism through which the Birlings exploit Eva. Every character uses their social position as a weapon: Birling sacks Eva because striking workers are a cost to his profits; Sheila uses her family's spending power to have Eva dismissed from Milwards; Gerald accesses Eva sexually because his wealth gives him power over a woman with no options; Mrs Birling rejects Eva's charity appeal because a working-class woman using the name "Birling" is "a piece of gross impertinence"; Eric forces himself on Eva while drunk, knowing there will be no consequences for a man of his class.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Priestley presents three distinct class layers: the landed aristocracy (Gerald's family, "an old country family — landed people"), the industrial middle class (the Birlings), and the working class (Eva). All three upper layers exploit the lower one. Priestley's point is that class privilege is not a personal failing — it is a system. Even when individuals within it show genuine feeling (Gerald, Sheila), the system ensures that the vulnerable are destroyed. Only collective structural change — not individual charity — can break the cycle.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Eva's vulnerability is compounded by her <dfn class="term" data-def="The social and cultural expectations placed on people based on sex — in 1912, women had fewer legal rights, limited employment options, no vote, and were economically dependent on men.">gender</dfn>. In 1912, women could not vote (full suffrage came only in 1928), had limited employment rights, and were economically dependent on men or institutions. Eva is exploited sexually by Gerald and Eric — two men whose class privilege protects them from any consequences. She is dismissed by the one woman who should have helped her (Mrs Birling), whose class prejudice overrides any gender solidarity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Priestley shows that class and gender oppression reinforce each other. A working-class woman in 1912 had almost no power: she could be sacked, exploited, and abandoned with no legal recourse. The only institution that might have helped — Mrs Birling's charity — was controlled by the very class that created her suffering. Priestley argues that structural inequality, not individual cruelty, is the real villain. This is why the play advocates systemic change (the Welfare State), not just personal kindness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Theme 4: The Generational Divide</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Priestley structures the play's moral argument around the <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference in attitudes, values, and moral responses between older and younger characters — the older Birlings refuse to accept responsibility while Sheila and Eric embrace it.">generational divide</dfn>. The older generation — Mr and Mrs Birling — refuse to change. After the Inspector leaves, they seize on the possibility that he was fake as proof that nothing matters. Birling is relieved: "the whole thing's different now." Mrs Birling insists: "she only had herself to blame." Neither acknowledges any moral failing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The younger generation responds differently. Sheila declares: "It doesn't make any difference — you still did the things you did." Eric reinforces her: "The fact remains that I did what I did. And Mother did what she did." They understand that moral responsibility is not contingent on the Inspector's identity. Priestley's message to the 1945 audience is clear: the older generation (represented by the Birlings) created two World Wars through their selfishness. The younger generation (represented by Sheila and Eric) must reject their parents' values and build a fairer society. The hope for Britain lies with the young — if they choose to learn.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All themes interconnect: class privilege enables exploitation; gender compounds vulnerability; individual irresponsibility creates collective failure; the generational divide offers hope; and morality demands systemic change, not just personal kindness. Top-band answers show these connections — themes do not exist in isolation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Theme 5: Morality and Social Justice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Inspector represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The fair distribution of wealth, opportunities, and privileges within a society — the Inspector argues that social justice requires active collective effort, not passive individual goodwill.">social justice</dfn> — the idea that a fair society requires active structural change, not just personal charity. His warning — "fire and blood and anguish" — is not a prediction but a consequence. The wars happened because the Birlings' world refused to change. If the 1945 audience returns to those values, the same destruction will follow. Priestley's play is a moral argument with political teeth: the Welfare State is not optional generosity but existential necessity.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">If your paper asks you to compare an extract from <em>An Inspector Calls</em> with an unseen modern drama or prose extract, the key skill is <strong>genuine comparison at the level of method</strong>. Do not write about one text then the other — alternate between both in every paragraph. Use <dfn class="term" data-def="Words and phrases that signal comparison between texts: similarly, in contrast, whereas, both writers, while Priestley uses X, the unseen writer achieves Y.">comparative connectives</dfn> throughout: “While Priestley uses dramatic irony to undermine Birling's confidence, the unseen writer achieves a similar effect through...”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Structure each paragraph: <strong>Point</strong> (a comparative claim about both texts) → <strong>Evidence from Text A</strong> (quotation + AO2 language/structure analysis) → <strong>Comparison with Text B</strong> (connective + quotation + analysis) → <strong>Context</strong> (AO3 embedded within analysis, not bolted on). This integrated approach earns marks across AO1 (response), AO2 (methods analysis), and AO3 (context) simultaneously. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact question wording and mark weightings.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Whole-Text Essays</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A whole-text essay will ask you to discuss <em>An Inspector Calls</em> beyond a single extract — you need examples from across all three acts. Structure 3–4 paragraphs, each making a distinct analytical point. Always remember that this is a <strong>play</strong>, not a novel: discuss stage directions, lighting, audience response, and dramatic techniques alongside language analysis. Embed context (AO3) within your analysis — never write a standalone 'context paragraph' that just lists historical facts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Prepare 8–10 versatile quotations that can be adapted to any question. The most useful are: "unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable" (dramatic irony / capitalism), "a man has to mind his own business" (Birling's philosophy), "these girls aren't cheap labour — they're people" (the play's thesis), "pink and intimate" / "brighter and harder" (lighting metaphor), "We are members of one body" (collective responsibility), "fire and blood and anguish" (warning / consequence), "It doesn't make any difference" (Sheila's moral clarity), "You're not the kind of father a chap could go to" (family dysfunction / Eric).</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Responsibility (individual and collective) is the central theme — the chain-of-events structure makes it a dramatic argument, not just a moral statement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Class, gender, and generation interact: class privilege enables exploitation, gender compounds vulnerability, and only the younger generation's willingness to change offers hope.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">For OCR Part (a), compare at the level of method, alternate between texts, and embed AO3. For Part (b), range across all three acts, analyse as a play (not a novel), and connect everything to Priestley's political purpose.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">The single most important skill for Part (a) is genuine comparison. Every paragraph must reference both texts. Use the formula: 'While Priestley presents [theme] through [dramatic technique], the unseen writer achieves a [similar/contrasting] effect through [technique].' For Part (b), always connect your analysis to Priestley's purpose — why he makes each dramatic choice. Top-band answers don't just describe what happens; they analyse how Priestley uses characters, structure, and language as political arguments for collective responsibility.</p>
