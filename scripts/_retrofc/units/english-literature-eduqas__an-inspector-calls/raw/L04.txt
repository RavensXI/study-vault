<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Eric’s Confession: Drinking, Theft, and Shame</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 3 opens with Eric returning to face the Inspector. His involvement with Eva Smith is the most disturbing of all. He met her at the Palace Bar — the same venue where Gerald found her — and forced himself on her while drunk. He admits: “I was in that state when a chap easily turns nasty.” Eva became pregnant, and Eric stole money from his father’s business to support her. He gave her about fifty pounds in total — a substantial sum in 1912 — but Eva refused to accept more because she suspected it was stolen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eric’s language throughout his confession reveals both guilt and helplessness. He describes himself not as a villain but as someone who was never given the tools to behave differently. His most revealing line comes when he rounds on his father: “You’re not the kind of father a chap could go to when he’s in trouble.” This accusation is devastating because it redirects blame from Eric’s individual failings to the Birling family’s emotional dysfunction. Birling’s obsession with business, reputation, and status has left his son unable to seek help from his own father. Priestley suggests that <dfn class="term" data-def="The emotional detachment and lack of genuine care within a family focused on appearances and social status rather than honesty and support.">dysfunctional family relationships</dfn> are a product of the capitalist, class-obsessed world the Birlings inhabit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does Eric’s line ‘You’re not the kind of father a chap could go to’ reveal about the Birling family?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eric’s accusation — “You’re not the kind of father a chap could go to when he’s in trouble” — is one of the play’s most important lines. It reveals that Birling’s prioritisation of business and reputation over family has created a household where a son cannot even ask his father for help. Priestley uses this to show that capitalist values corrupt not just society, but personal relationships within the family.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Inspector’s Final Speech</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">With all five characters exposed, the Inspector delivers his final speech. It is the play’s climax — a <dfn class="term" data-def="A speech in drama that delivers the playwright’s central message directly to the audience, often functioning as a moral or political statement.">dramatic monologue</dfn> that functions as Priestley’s direct address to his 1945 audience. The Inspector says:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">“But just remember this. One Eva Smith has gone — but there are millions and millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths still left with us, with their lives, their hopes and fears, their suffering, and chance of happiness, all intertwined with our lives, with what we think and say and do. We don’t live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other. And I tell you that the time will soon come when, if men will not learn that lesson, then they will be taught it in fire and blood and anguish.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The speech operates on multiple levels. The repetition of “millions and millions and millions” hammers home the scale of social injustice — Eva is not unique; she represents an entire class of exploited people. “We are members of one body” echoes the Christian principle of the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:27), giving the speech a quasi-religious authority. And the warning of “fire and blood and anguish” is both a biblical echo and a direct reference to the two world wars that the 1945 audience had just survived.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of three words, phrases, or clauses used for emphasis and rhythmic effect, common in speeches and persuasive writing.">tricolon</dfn> “fire and blood and anguish” uses the conjunction “and” between each noun (polysyndeton), which slows the rhythm and gives each word equal weight. “Fire” evokes the Blitz; “blood” evokes the trenches and battlefields; “anguish” captures the emotional devastation that lingers after physical destruction. The warning is conditional: “if men will not learn that lesson” — Priestley leaves the door open. The catastrophe is not inevitable; it is a consequence of choosing selfishness over responsibility. The 1945 audience is being told: you have a second chance. Do not waste it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The biblical register of the speech — “members of one body,” “taught it in fire” — gives the Inspector an almost prophetic authority. He speaks not as a police inspector but as a moral or spiritual force. This links to the mystery of his identity: he may be a ghost, an angel, a time traveller, or simply a dramatic device. What matters is that his message carries the weight of absolute truth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Aftermath: Was It a Hoax?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">After the Inspector leaves, the Birlings and Gerald react in sharply divided ways. Birling and Mrs Birling immediately try to minimise the evening’s events. Gerald rings the Infirmary and discovers that no girl has died from swallowing disinfectant. Birling is triumphant: “the whole story’s just a lot of moonshine.” He and Gerald piece together the possibility that the Inspector was not a real police officer, and that the photographs shown to each person may have been of different girls. The older generation’s relief is palpable — and, for the audience, deeply unsettling.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Sheila and Eric refuse to join this celebration. Sheila’s response is the play’s moral compass: “You’re pretending everything’s just as it was before.” Eric agrees: “I don’t see much nonsense about it when a girl goes and kills herself. You lot may be letting yourselves out nicely, but I can’t.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="The moral and ideological split between older characters who resist change and younger characters who accept responsibility in the play.">generational divide</dfn> is absolute. The older characters care about whether they will face consequences; the younger characters care about whether they did wrong.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Sheila’s and Eric’s responses when their parents try to dismiss the Inspector, and explain why Priestley gives them these lines.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The split between the generations is Priestley’s clearest structural message. Birling and Mrs Birling are relieved because the Inspector may have been a hoax — their concern is escaping scandal, not moral accountability. Sheila and Eric insist that whether the Inspector was real is irrelevant: they still behaved terribly. Priestley tells his audience that moral responsibility does not depend on whether you get caught.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Cyclical Ending</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The play’s final moments are its most theatrical. Just as the Birlings are congratulating themselves, the telephone rings. Birling answers and reports, ashen-faced: “That was the police. A girl has just died — on her way to the Infirmary — after swallowing some disinfectant. And a police inspector is on his way here — to ask some — questions.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">This <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where the ending mirrors or returns to the beginning, creating a sense of repetition, inevitability, or unresolved conflict.">cyclical ending</dfn> is devastating. The entire evening is about to happen again. For the audience, the message is inescapable: if you refuse to learn from the Inspector’s visit, history will repeat itself. The Birlings had a chance to change and rejected it; now they face the consequences. Priestley warns his 1945 audience: you too have been given a chance by the end of the war. Do not be like the Birlings.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Ending’s Multiple Interpretations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The cyclical ending has been interpreted in several ways. One reading is that the Inspector was a supernatural warning — a ghost or time traveller who showed the Birlings what was about to happen, giving them a chance to change. They failed, so the real events now begin. Another reading is that the evening was a moral rehearsal: the Inspector tested their consciences, and the phone call is the real test. A third interpretation is political: Priestley is saying that the 1912 warnings about class, exploitation, and war were ignored, leading to catastrophe. Britain now stands at a similar crossroads in 1945.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">What all interpretations share is the idea that ignoring moral responsibility has consequences. The phone call is not just a plot twist — it is Priestley’s final, urgent warning. The audience leaves the theatre knowing that the cycle will continue unless people change.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Eric’s confession exposes the Birling family’s dysfunction: his line “You’re not the kind of father a chap could go to” reveals that capitalist values corrupt even family relationships.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Inspector’s final speech — “We are members of one body… fire and blood and anguish” — is Priestley’s direct appeal to his 1945 audience to choose collective responsibility over selfish individualism.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The cyclical ending (the phone call announcing a real death and a real inspector) warns that ignoring moral responsibility leads to history repeating itself.</li></ul>
