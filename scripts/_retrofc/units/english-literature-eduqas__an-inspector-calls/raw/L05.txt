Detailed analysis of all six characters: Arthur, Sybil, Sheila, Eric, Gerald, and the Inspector.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Arthur Birling: Capitalism Personified</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Arthur Birling is a wealthy factory owner, a magistrate, and a former Lord Mayor of Brumley. He is described in the stage directions as “a heavy-looking, rather portentous man” — the adjective “portentous” suggests someone who takes himself very seriously, but the word also means “ominous,” hinting that Birling’s arrogance will bring consequences. He is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who has risen to a higher social class through wealth rather than birth, often anxious about status and acceptance.">social climber</dfn>: his wife is “his social superior” and he is desperate for a knighthood. Every decision he makes is driven by profit, reputation, or both.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Birling’s defining quotation is “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” This is not just a personal philosophy — it is the capitalist creed that Priestley wants to dismantle. Birling sacks Eva for striking, cares more about his knighthood than her death, and celebrates when the Inspector appears to be a hoax. He is the play’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character in fiction who embodies an idea, social group, or ideology rather than existing as a fully rounded individual.">representative figure</dfn> for selfish capitalism, and Priestley makes him unchanging to show that this ideology is beyond redemption.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three things drive every decision Arthur Birling makes in the play?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling is driven by profit, reputation, and status. He sacks Eva to protect profits, fears the Inspector because of his knighthood, and is relieved when the evening seems to be a hoax because it means no scandal. Priestley uses him as a warning: this is what happens when a society prioritises wealth over people.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sybil Birling: The Ice of the Upper Class</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Mrs Birling is described as “a rather cold woman” and “her husband’s social superior.” Where Arthur is loud and blustering, Sybil is controlled and icy. Her <dfn class="term" data-def="Prejudice or discrimination based on a person’s social class, typically by those of higher status looking down on those below.">class prejudice</dfn> is absolute: she refers to Eva as “girls of that class” and finds her use of the name “Mrs Birling” “impertinent.” She is the chair of the Brumley Women’s Charity Organisation, which makes her rejection of Eva even more damning — the person whose job it is to help the vulnerable is the one who turns Eva away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Mrs Birling is the play’s most static character. She never accepts responsibility. Even after the Inspector’s speech, she remains “rather agitated but quite determined.” Priestley uses her to show that the upper class is not just selfish but structurally incapable of empathy. Her inability to recognise Eva as a human being — rather than as a member of a lower class — is the coldest cruelty in the play.</p>

    <span>Sheila Birling: The Conscience That Awakens</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Sheila begins the play as a spoilt, materialistic young woman — “Oh — it’s wonderful!” she exclaims about her engagement ring, with a <dfn class="term" data-def="An exclamation that reveals the speaker’s feelings or character through its emotional tone and spontaneity.">revealing exclamation</dfn> that shows how sheltered and superficial her life has been. Her confession at Milwards reveals how casually she wielded her class power to have Eva sacked. But unlike her parents, Sheila is immediately and genuinely ashamed: “I know I’m to blame — and I’m desperately sorry.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">By Act 2, Sheila has become the Inspector’s ally. She warns Gerald: “You fool — he knows.” She returns her engagement ring, rejecting the comfortable life her parents planned for her. By Act 3, she directly confronts her parents: “You’re pretending everything’s just as it was before.” Priestley uses Sheila’s transformation to show that the younger generation can learn, can change, and can reject the values of their parents. She is the audience’s model: recognise your complicity, feel genuine remorse, and act differently.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Eric Birling: Weakness Turned to Moral Strength</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Eric is introduced as “not quite at ease, half shy, half assertive” — a young man who does not fit comfortably into his family’s world. He drinks heavily (his mother is the last to know), and his treatment of Eva is the most violent: he forces himself on her while drunk and then steals money from his father’s business to support her. His behaviour is inexcusable, but Priestley complicates our judgement by showing that Eric is also a product of his environment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Eric’s most powerful moment is his accusation: “You’re not the kind of father a chap could go to when he’s in trouble.” This line exposes the emotional emptiness of the Birling household. Eric’s weakness is partly caused by a family that values respectability over honesty. By the end of the play, Eric has grown. He refuses to celebrate when the Inspector is discredited: “You lot may be letting yourselves out nicely, but I can’t.” Like Sheila, he accepts responsibility regardless of consequences.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write one quotation each for Sheila and Eric that shows they have changed by Act 3.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila and Eric are the play’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Characters who undergo significant change or development during a narrative, reflecting the playwright’s belief that moral growth is possible.">dynamic characters</dfn>. Both begin as products of privilege, both do terrible things to Eva, and both are transformed by the Inspector’s visit. Priestley uses their change to argue that the younger generation can build a better society — if they choose to learn from the past.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Gerald Croft: The Comfortable Compromise</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Gerald is the son of Sir George Croft, which makes him socially superior to the Birlings. He is charming, confident, and emotionally affected by Daisy Renton’s death. But his sympathy is temporary. By Act 3, he is leading the investigation to prove the Inspector was a fraud: “How do we know she was really Eva Smith or Daisy Renton?” Gerald represents the person who feels genuine emotion but ultimately retreats to the safety of the status quo. Priestley suggests that feeling sorry is not enough — what matters is whether you change your behaviour.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Inspector Goole: Priestley’s Voice on Stage</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The Inspector is described as creating “an impression of massiveness, solidity, and purposefulness.” He is not physically large, but his moral authority fills the room. He controls the pace of the evening, showing each character the photograph one at a time, and he cannot be interrupted, deflected, or bullied. His name — Goole — is a homophone of “ghoul,” suggesting something supernatural or death-related.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The Inspector functions on multiple levels: as a detective figure who uncovers the truth; as a moral judge who forces each character to confront their guilt; as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who exists primarily to deliver the writer’s message, social commentary, or moral argument to the audience.">mouthpiece</dfn> for Priestley’s socialist beliefs; and as a supernatural or symbolic presence whose true nature is never explained. His final speech — “We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other” — is the play’s thesis statement. Whether he is a ghost, an angel, or a dramatic device, his message is Priestley’s: collective responsibility is not optional.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Arthur and Sybil Birling are static characters who represent the selfish, class-proud Edwardian establishment that Priestley wants his audience to reject.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Sheila and Eric are dynamic characters whose moral growth models the response Priestley wants from his audience: accept responsibility, feel genuine remorse, and change.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Inspector is Priestley’s mouthpiece, a moral force whose authority transcends the detective role. Gerald occupies a troubling middle ground — sympathetic but ultimately unwilling to change.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the Eduqas exam, character questions often ask how Priestley ‘presents’ a character. This means you must analyse the writer’s methods (AO2) — not just describe what the character does. For example, do not just say “Birling is selfish.” Say: “Priestley presents Birling as selfish through his monosyllabic, assertive declaration that ‘a man has to mind his own business,’ which is positioned immediately before the Inspector’s arrival to dramatise its failure.” Method + evidence + effect = high marks.</p>
