--- DESCRIPTION ---
How dissection and battlefield surgery began to crack Galen’s authority in the 1500s.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Climate for Knowledge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For 1,400 years Galen had been beyond serious challenge. By the early 1500s the world that protected him was changing. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 14th-17th century European cultural movement that revived classical learning and emphasised observation of the natural world.">Renaissance</dfn> revived interest in original Greek texts &mdash; including Galen&rsquo;s own &mdash; and revealed centuries of mistakes by translators. Humanist scholars argued for going back to the sources. The printing press, invented by Gutenberg around 1450, made books available across Europe. The Reformation after 1517 weakened the Church&rsquo;s monopoly on learning. Most importantly, dissection of human bodies became routine in some Italian universities. The conditions for challenging Galen finally existed &mdash; but only just.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the date and title of Vesalius&rsquo;s book, plus how many of Galen&rsquo;s anatomical errors he corrected.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1543 Andreas Vesalius published <em>De Humani Corporis Fabrica</em> (&lsquo;On the Fabric of the Human Body&rsquo;) &mdash; based on his own dissections at Padua. He corrected over 200 of Galen&rsquo;s anatomical errors, including the structure of the jaw, the breastbone and the heart.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Vesalius and the Anatomy Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Andreas <dfn class="term" data-def="Belgian anatomist (1514-1564) whose 1543 book &lsquo;De Humani Corporis Fabrica&rsquo; corrected over 200 of Galen&rsquo;s anatomical errors.">Vesalius</dfn> was a Belgian anatomist who became professor at Padua &mdash; the most famous medical school in Europe &mdash; in 1537. Unlike older professors who read Galen aloud while a barber-surgeon did the cutting, Vesalius dissected bodies himself, often using corpses he stole from a gibbet outside the city walls. What he saw repeatedly contradicted Galen. The human jaw is one bone, not two. The breastbone has three sections, not seven. The wall of the heart does not have invisible holes through which blood passes &mdash; a claim Galen had made because he had only ever dissected animals (apes, pigs and dogs), and dared not contradict the great authority.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Vesalius&rsquo;s book is famous for its illustrations. He hired artists from Titian&rsquo;s studio to produce hundreds of woodcuts showing muscles, bones and organs in poses that looked almost alive. Without the printing press, those illustrations would never have spread; with it, copies reached medical libraries across Europe within a few years. <em>Fabrica</em> was reprinted, copied and pirated across the 16th and 17th centuries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Why Was the Reaction So Hostile?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Vesalius was attacked by his own teacher Sylvius, who declared that human anatomy must have changed since Galen&rsquo;s time, since Galen could not have been wrong. Some called Vesalius a heretic. Within a few years of <em>Fabrica</em>&rsquo;s publication Vesalius was working as physician to Emperor Charles V and Philip II of Spain, no longer at Padua. He died in 1564 returning from a pilgrimage. The reaction reveals how strong the protective system around Galen had been &mdash; and how slow the actual treatment of patients was to change. Knowing the heart did not have invisible holes did not, on its own, change what physicians prescribed.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Three reasons. First, English universities continued to teach Galen as the central text. Second, English-language editions of Vesalius were rare; reading <em>Fabrica</em> required Latin and access to an expensive imported book. Third, even if a British physician accepted that the heart did not have invisible holes, this did not change what to do for a patient with a fever &mdash; the treatments still followed the four humours.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Vesalius is therefore the textbook example of the gap between discovery and application. Examiners reward students who acknowledge this gap rather than claiming Vesalius &lsquo;changed medicine&rsquo;.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Paré and the Battlefield Revolution in Surgery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">While Vesalius worked on the dissection table, Ambroise <dfn class="term" data-def="French military surgeon (1510-1590) who replaced cauterisation with ligatures and developed treatment for gunshot wounds.">Paré</dfn> worked on the battlefield. Paré was a French army surgeon who served in the wars between France, Italy, England and the Holy Roman Empire from the 1530s onwards. Standard treatment for a gunshot wound was to pour boiling oil into it, on the theory that gunpowder was poisonous and needed cauterising. In 1537, after a battle, Paré ran out of oil. He had no choice but to dress the remaining wounds with a cool salve of egg yolks, rose oil and turpentine. The next morning he found that the salve-treated soldiers were resting more comfortably and showed less inflammation than the oil-treated ones. He published his findings, and gradually the practice of pouring boiling oil into gunshot wounds was abandoned.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Paré&rsquo;s second major change was using <dfn class="term" data-def="Threads tied around a blood vessel to stop bleeding during surgery, used by Paré in place of cauterisation.">ligatures</dfn> &mdash; threads tied around blood vessels &mdash; instead of cauterising amputation stumps with red-hot irons. Cauterisation stopped bleeding fast but caused agonising pain and could kill from shock. Ligatures were slower and (without antiseptics) carried a higher infection risk &mdash; but the patient was less likely to die on the table. Paré&rsquo;s motto was carved on his statue: &lsquo;<em>Je le pansai, Dieu le gu&eacute;rit</em>&rsquo; &mdash; &lsquo;I dressed him, God healed him&rsquo;. He understood the limits of what surgery could do.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>War, chance and the role of the individual</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Paré&rsquo;s story contains three of the AQA factors at once. <strong>War</strong> created the conditions: with thousands of gunshot wounds to treat, a single battlefield surgeon could see more cases in a year than a peacetime doctor saw in a decade. <strong>Chance</strong> sparked the salve discovery: Paré ran out of oil. <strong>The role of the individual</strong> meant that a thoughtful surgeon noticed and recorded the difference, and was willing to publish a finding that contradicted his teachers. None of the three on its own was enough &mdash; remove war and there is no flow of cases; remove chance and there is no trigger; remove Paré and there is nobody to record and publish what happened.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Knowing This Matters</h2>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the year Paré ran out of oil, what he replaced it with, and what he replaced cauterisation with.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1537 Paré replaced boiling oil for gunshot wounds with a soothing salve of egg yolks, rose oil and turpentine. He also replaced cauterisation in amputations with ligatures &mdash; threads tied around blood vessels.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">A Note on Slowness in Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">It is tempting to read 1543 (Fabrica) and 1537 (Paré&rsquo;s salve) and assume British medicine transformed in the mid-16th century. It did not. Paré&rsquo;s ligatures were not standard in British surgery for over a century. Vesalius&rsquo;s anatomy was taught in some English schools by 1600 but did not displace Galen from clinical reasoning. The Renaissance shifted the climate &mdash; printing, dissection and the willingness to challenge authority &mdash; but the actual treatment of British patients was still humourist for many decades. The lesson for the factor essay is that science and technology open the door, but communication, religion and individual willingness decide how fast people walk through it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Vesalius (1543) corrected over 200 anatomical errors but his impact on routine treatment was slow &mdash; the Renaissance shifted what could be challenged, not what doctors did day-to-day.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Paré (1530s-90s) replaced boiling oil with soothing salve and cauterisation with ligatures &mdash; battlefield surgery driven by war, chance and individual observation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The printing press multiplied the impact of new ideas; without it, neither Vesalius nor Paré would have spread beyond their own students.</li>
</ul>
