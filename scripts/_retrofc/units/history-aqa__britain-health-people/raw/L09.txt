--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Sick Industrial City</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between 1801 and 1851 Britain&rsquo;s urban population doubled. Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds and Birmingham filled with back-to-back terraces sharing one privy between dozens of families. There were no sewers, no refuse collection and no clean water. Wells lay metres from cesspits. Pigs ran in courtyards. Death rates in the worst slums reached 30 per 1,000 &mdash; double the rural figure. Yet government action was blocked by a powerful idea: <dfn class="term" data-def="The 19th-century belief that government should not interfere in the lives or businesses of citizens beyond keeping order.">laissez-faire</dfn>, the Whig conviction that the state should leave individuals and businesses alone.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the four cholera epidemic dates and roughly how many died in 1831&ndash;32.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Britain suffered four major cholera epidemics in the 19th century: 1831&ndash;32 (around 52,000 deaths), 1848&ndash;49, 1853&ndash;54 and 1865&ndash;66. Each forced government to act a little more than the last.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Chadwick&rsquo;s 1842 Report</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Edwin <dfn class="term" data-def="Civil servant (1800-1890) whose 1842 Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population built the case for state-led public health reform.">Chadwick</dfn> was a lawyer and Poor Law Commissioner who collected mortality statistics from across Britain. His 1842 <em>Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population</em> argued that bad housing caused poverty, not the reverse: sick workers could not earn, and disease cost ratepayers more than sanitation would. The report sold widely, but Parliament dragged its feet for six years until cholera returned in 1848.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The 1848 Public Health Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The 1848 <dfn class="term" data-def="The first national legislation on public health in Britain, setting up a General Board of Health and allowing (but not requiring) towns to set up local boards.">Public Health Act</dfn> created a General Board of Health and allowed towns to set up local boards if death rates exceeded 23 per 1,000 or if 10% of ratepayers petitioned for one. The catch was that adoption was voluntary &mdash; <em>adoptive</em>, not compulsory. Many towns refused on grounds of cost; others adopted then dropped boards once cholera receded. Chadwick&rsquo;s authoritarian style provoked a backlash and the General Board was abolished in 1858. The Act&rsquo;s effect was real but limited.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">When cholera struck Soho in August 1854, the London doctor John <dfn class="term" data-def="London doctor (1813-1858) who proved cholera spread through contaminated water by mapping deaths around the Broad Street pump in 1854.">Snow</dfn> mapped 578 deaths and saw they clustered around one pump in Broad Street. Snow persuaded the parish to remove the pump handle on 8 September; new cases stopped. He had proved cholera was waterborne &mdash; seven years before Pasteur&rsquo;s germ theory and without a microscope. The General Board of Health rejected his findings; they preferred miasma. Snow&rsquo;s evidence won over local officials but not the medical establishment until germ theory caught up in the 1860s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Great Stink and Bazalgette&rsquo;s Sewers</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Thames sewer programme, 1858&ndash;65</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In summer 1858 hot weather turned the Thames, by then an open sewer, into the &lsquo;Great Stink&rsquo;. Curtains soaked in chloride of lime hung in Parliament; MPs could not sit. Within weeks, Parliament voted &pound;3 million for Joseph Bazalgette to build a sewer network for London. Eighty-two miles of main sewer, 1,100 miles of street sewer, four pumping stations &mdash; finished by 1865. When cholera returned in 1866 it killed thousands in unsewered districts but spared the West End and central London where Bazalgette&rsquo;s system was working. Public health had a winner.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The 1875 Public Health Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By 1875 three things had shifted. Germ theory had begun to displace miasma. The 1867 Reform Act had given working-class men in towns the vote, so MPs needed slum voters. And Disraeli&rsquo;s Conservative government had made &lsquo;sanitas sanitatum, omnia sanitas&rsquo; (sanitation is everything) a slogan. The 1875 Act made local authorities responsible for sewers, water, refuse, food inspection, slaughterhouses, building standards and notifiable diseases. It was <em>compulsory</em>, not adoptive. The same Parliament passed the Artisans&rsquo; Dwellings Act allowing slum clearance, the Sale of Food and Drugs Act, and the Rivers Pollution Prevention Act. Public health had moved from individual responsibility to state duty.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list two reasons the 1875 Act was more effective than the 1848 Act.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1875 Public Health Act made action compulsory across all local authorities, covering sewers, water, food, housing and notifiable disease. Combined with the 1875 Artisans&rsquo; Dwellings Act, it ended the laissez-faire era in British public health.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Linking to the Factor Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Public health is the cleanest case for <strong>government</strong> as the dominant factor &mdash; the 1848 and 1875 Acts could only be passed by Parliament. But government did not act alone. <strong>Communication</strong> mattered: Chadwick&rsquo;s report, Punch cartoons, Mayhew&rsquo;s <em>London Labour and the London Poor</em> and the press during the Great Stink built the political pressure. <strong>The role of the individual</strong>: Chadwick, Snow and Bazalgette each pushed reform forward. <strong>Science and technology</strong>: Snow&rsquo;s map, Bazalgette&rsquo;s engineering, eventually Koch&rsquo;s bacteriology. <strong>Chance</strong>: a hot summer in 1858 forced Parliament to act. Strong factor essays note that government became the decisive driver only once the other factors had built the case.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Industrial Britain&rsquo;s overcrowded cities and four cholera epidemics (1831&ndash;1866) forced public health onto the political agenda against laissez-faire opposition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Chadwick&rsquo;s 1842 report led to the 1848 Public Health Act, but adoption was voluntary and the Act&rsquo;s reach was limited.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Snow&rsquo;s 1854 Broad Street pump, the Great Stink of 1858, Bazalgette&rsquo;s sewers and the 1867 Reform Act all combined to produce the compulsory, comprehensive 1875 Public Health Act.</li>
</ul>
