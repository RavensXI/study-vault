--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Thousand Years on a Single Page</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The thematic study asks one large question: why has the pace and direction of medical change varied across British history? The answer lies in the interaction of seven factors &mdash; war, religion and superstition, chance, government, communication, science and technology, and the role of the individual. No single factor dominates the whole sweep. Each rises and falls with the period: religion is decisive in the Middle Ages, science in the late nineteenth century, government in the twentieth. The 16-mark <dfn class="term" data-def="The 16-mark thematic-study question that names one factor and asks students to weigh it against others using at least three examples drawn from at least two periods, reaching a substantiated overall judgement.">factors essay</dfn> rewards students who can name the named factor, weigh it against two or three others, and make a clear judgement.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list which one factor dominates each of the four spec parts &mdash; medieval, early modern, industrial, modern.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>One factor tends to dominate each period. Medieval (c1000&ndash;c1500): religion. Early modern (c1500&ndash;c1750): the role of the individual. Industrial (c1750&ndash;c1900): science and technology. Modern (c1900&ndash;today): government. Strong essays explain why the lead factor changes and how the others interact.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Mapping the Seven Factors Across the Sweep</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The AQA factor covering religious belief and supernatural ideas about disease &mdash; dominant in medieval Britain through divine-punishment explanations and Church control of dissection.">Religion and superstition</dfn> dominates medieval explanations of disease (divine punishment, saints&rsquo; cures, the Black Death of 1348 as God&rsquo;s anger), preserves classical learning through monastic libraries, and limits dissection until the Renaissance. <strong>The role of the individual</strong> shapes the early-modern disruption: Vesalius (1543), Par&eacute; (1530s&ndash;90s), Harvey (1628), Sydenham, Hunter, Jenner (1796). <strong>Science and technology</strong> takes over in the nineteenth century: Pasteur&rsquo;s germ theory (1860s), Koch (anthrax 1876, TB 1882), Lister&rsquo;s antiseptic surgery (1867), Snow&rsquo;s 1854 Broad Street pump, R&ouml;ntgen&rsquo;s X-rays (1895). <strong>Government</strong> dominates the twentieth century: 1875 Public Health Act compulsory, Liberal reforms 1906&ndash;11, NHS 1948. <strong>War</strong> repeatedly accelerates change: Crimean (Nightingale), Boer (Liberal reforms), WWI (transfusion, plastic surgery, X-rays), WWII (penicillin mass production, NHS preconditions). <strong>Chance</strong> punctuates progress: Par&eacute;&rsquo;s salve, Pasteur&rsquo;s chicken-cholera culture, Fleming&rsquo;s mould. <strong>Communication</strong> spreads everything: the printing press from c1500, the Royal Society from 1660, medical journals like <em>The Lancet</em>, mass-circulation newspapers, the Beveridge Report&rsquo;s 300,000 copies.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Structuring the 16-Mark Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A top-band essay needs a structure that returns to the named factor in every paragraph, not a tour of all seven. The four-paragraph model works:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The four-paragraph factors essay</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Introduction</strong>: Define the named factor; state your overall judgement (it was/was not the main factor) in one sentence; flag the two other factors you will weigh. <strong>Paragraph 1 &mdash; the named factor</strong>: Two examples from two periods showing the named factor in action. <strong>Paragraph 2 &mdash; counter-factor A</strong>: Strongest rival factor, with one or two examples, and a sentence explicitly comparing it to the named factor. <strong>Paragraph 3 &mdash; counter-factor B</strong>: Second rival factor, again with comparison back to the named factor. <strong>Conclusion</strong>: A substantiated overall judgement &mdash; <em>not</em> a summary. Say which factor mattered most, in which period, and why.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Worked Example: Was Government the main factor in Improving Public Health?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Government in 1348 was almost absent &mdash; the Crown ordered fish carts off the road and prayed. In 1665 it ordered shutting up of plague houses but achieved little. By 1848 the first Public Health Act was adoptive (towns chose to opt in) and largely ignored. The 1875 Public Health Act, after the 1867 working-class franchise and germ theory&rsquo;s spread, was compulsory and comprehensive. The 1948 NHS was decisive. Across this arc, government strengthens as democracy widens and science explains. <strong>Counter-factor: science and technology</strong> &mdash; without Pasteur (1860s), Koch (1882) and Snow (1854) the 1875 Act would have been a shot in the dark. <strong>Counter-factor: the role of the individual</strong> &mdash; Chadwick (1842 report), Snow, Bevan and Beveridge each translated possibilities into laws. Judgement: government became the main factor only after 1875, and only because science and individuals had given it the tools and the case to act.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write the opening sentence of a 16-mark essay on whether war was the main factor &mdash; name the factor, state your judgement, flag two rivals.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three examples, two periods, one judgement. Examiner reports flag every series that strong answers move fluently between periods (e.g. comparing 1348, 1848 and 1948) rather than writing four self-contained mini-essays.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Change, Continuity and Progress</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The spec asks not just whether change happened but whether it brought <dfn class="term" data-def="In the AQA spec, change that improves outcomes from a stated perspective &mdash; not all change is progress; loss of Roman public health after c450 was change without progress.">progress</dfn>. Some change was regression: the loss of Roman aqueducts and bath-houses after c450, replaced by less effective Christian hospitals. Some was sideways: medieval infirmaries served different purposes than classical ones, not necessarily worse. Some was contested: the 1853 Vaccination Act faced organised anti-vaccination resistance, and the 1948 NHS was attacked by the BMA. Strong essays say <em>whose perspective</em> defines progress &mdash; what improves life expectancy may not improve patient autonomy; what improves overall health may worsen care for specific groups. The rate of change accelerated unevenly: medieval slow, Renaissance start-stop, nineteenth-century rapid, twentieth-century explosive.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Avoiding the examiner&rsquo;s red flags</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">AQA principal examiner reports flag the same mistakes every series. Do not write in the first person or as a diary entry &mdash; that prevents AO2 analytical marks. Do not list factors without weighing them; listing all seven is not analysis. Do not stay in one period &mdash; the 16-mark essay requires examples from at least two periods.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Do not give vague figures &mdash; &lsquo;a lot of soldiers&rsquo; is not 40%; &lsquo;many people&rsquo; is not 30.7% of Londoners. Use the named individuals from the spec (Vesalius, Harvey, Par&eacute;, Hunter, Jenner, Pasteur, Koch, Ehrlich, Simpson, Lister, Snow, Chadwick, Nightingale, Booth, Rowntree, Beveridge, Bevan, Fleming, Florey, Chain) by name and date. Reach a substantiated overall judgement &mdash; the conclusion is what separates the top band from the rest.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">No single factor dominates the whole 1,000-year sweep &mdash; religion leads in the medieval period, individuals in the early modern, science in the industrial, government in the modern.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">A top-band 16-mark essay uses three examples from at least two periods, weighs the named factor against two rivals, and reaches a substantiated overall judgement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Not every change was progress &mdash; the loss of Roman public health, contested vaccination programmes and BMA opposition to the NHS show that progress depends on whose perspective is being judged.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">For the 16-mark factor essay, plan two minutes before you write: name the factor, write your one-sentence judgement at the top of the page, list three concrete examples drawn from at least two periods, and pick the two strongest rival factors to weigh against. Every paragraph must end by returning to the named factor &mdash; e.g. &lsquo;so government here mattered more than science because&hellip;&rsquo;. Keep one strong sentence in reserve for the conclusion that names the period in which the factor was strongest, not just whether it &lsquo;was&rsquo; the main one.</p>
