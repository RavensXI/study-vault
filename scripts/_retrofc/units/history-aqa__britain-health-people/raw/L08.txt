--- DESCRIPTION ---
How chloroform and carbolic acid moved surgery from speed to safety — and what slowed change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Surgery Before 1846: Speed Was Everything</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before <dfn class="term" data-def="A drug that produces loss of feeling or full unconsciousness so that a patient feels no pain during surgery.">anaesthetics</dfn>, surgery in Britain was a last resort. Patients screamed; surgeons worked at speed because every second of pain risked shock. The London surgeon Robert Liston could amputate a leg in 28 seconds. Operations were limited to crisis cases &mdash; trauma, tumours visible on the surface, gallstones, amputations &mdash; because anything inside the body chest or abdomen meant near-certain death from pain, blood loss or infection. Most patients refused surgery until the alternative was definitely worse.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three early anaesthetics and the year and city each was first used in surgery.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three early anaesthetics changed surgery: nitrous oxide (Davy 1799), ether (Morton, Boston 1846) and chloroform (James Simpson, Edinburgh 1847). Simpson tested chloroform on himself and friends after dinner.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Simpson and Chloroform, 1847</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">James <dfn class="term" data-def="Edinburgh obstetrician (1811-1870) who introduced chloroform as a surgical and obstetric anaesthetic in 1847.">Simpson</dfn> was an Edinburgh obstetrician searching for a better anaesthetic than ether, which irritated the lungs. In November 1847 he and two colleagues sniffed chloroform after dinner and woke up on the floor. Within days he was using it in childbirth. Chloroform was easier to administer and worked faster than ether, and it spread quickly across British surgery.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">There was sharp opposition. Some clergy quoted Genesis 3:16 &mdash; &lsquo;in sorrow shalt thou bring forth children&rsquo; &mdash; arguing that pain in childbirth was God&rsquo;s will. Surgeons feared patients dying from the anaesthetic itself; in 1848 Hannah Greener, a 15-year-old, died under chloroform during a toenail operation. The objections were overcome partly by Queen Victoria, who used chloroform when she gave birth to Prince Leopold in 1853. Royal endorsement made chloroform respectable.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The &lsquo;Black Period&rsquo; 1846&ndash;1865: anaesthetics made surgery worse</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Anaesthetics encouraged surgeons to operate longer, deeper and on patients who would once have refused. But operating theatres were filthy. Surgeons wore unwashed coats stiff with old blood, used unsterilised instruments and worked in wards crowded with infected patients. Post-operative infection &mdash; gangrene, sepsis, &lsquo;hospital fever&rsquo; &mdash; killed up to 50% of surgical patients. Surgeons called these years the &lsquo;Black Period&rsquo;: more operations, more deaths. Anaesthetics had solved the pain problem and exposed an infection problem the older system had hidden behind speed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Lister and Carbolic Acid, 1865&ndash;1867</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Joseph <dfn class="term" data-def="Glasgow surgeon (1827-1912) who introduced carbolic acid antisepsis to British surgery from 1865, publishing in The Lancet in 1867.">Lister</dfn> was a Glasgow surgeon who read Pasteur&rsquo;s germ theory in 1864. If germs in the air caused decay, Lister reasoned, they must also cause wound infection. Carbolic acid was already used to deodorise sewage in Carlisle. Lister tried it on a compound fracture in August 1865: he soaked dressings in carbolic and rinsed instruments and hands with carbolic solution. The wound healed without infection. He published his results in <em>The Lancet</em> in March 1867. <dfn class="term" data-def="Surgery that uses chemicals to kill germs on wounds, instruments and skin during the operation.">Antiseptic surgery</dfn> &mdash; killing germs during surgery &mdash; had begun.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why British surgeons resisted Lister&rsquo;s antiseptic system through the 1870s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Lister&rsquo;s carbolic spray cut amputation deaths from around 46% to 15% in his Glasgow ward by 1869. Yet many British surgeons rejected his system into the 1880s &mdash; the spray was unpleasant, results were variable in untrained hands, and surgeons could not see the germs Lister claimed to be killing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">From Antiseptic to Aseptic Surgery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Lister&rsquo;s carbolic system killed germs <em>during</em> surgery. By the 1890s a better idea had taken hold: stop germs reaching the wound in the first place. <dfn class="term" data-def="Surgery performed in a sterile environment so that no germs ever reach the wound, using sterilised instruments, gowns, masks and gloves.">Aseptic surgery</dfn> meant sterilising instruments in steam autoclaves (1880s), wearing surgical gowns and face masks, scrubbing hands before operating and using rubber gloves &mdash; introduced by William Halsted in Baltimore in 1894 after his nurse&rsquo;s hands cracked from carbolic. By 1900 most British operating theatres were aseptic rather than antiseptic, and infection rates had fallen sharply.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The First World War as a surgical accelerator</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Industrial-scale shrapnel wounds in the trenches forced surgical innovation. R&ouml;ntgen&rsquo;s X-rays (1895) became routine in field hospitals from 1914 to locate metal fragments. Blood transfusions, made possible by Karl Landsteiner&rsquo;s identification of blood groups (1901), saved lives at the front by 1916. Surgeons learned to debride wounds &mdash; cut away dead tissue before infection set in. Plastic surgery began with Harold Gillies treating facial injuries at the Queen&rsquo;s Hospital, Sidcup. War accelerated changes that peacetime medicine would have taken decades to adopt.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Linking to the Factor Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Anaesthetics and antiseptics show <strong>science and technology</strong> driving change &mdash; Pasteur&rsquo;s germ theory was the precondition for Lister&rsquo;s breakthrough, and chemistry produced the carbolic. The <strong>role of the individual</strong> matters too: Simpson and Lister both pushed personally against opposition. <strong>Religion</strong> resisted chloroform on biblical grounds. <strong>Chance</strong> appears in Simpson&rsquo;s after-dinner experiment. <strong>War</strong> accelerated the spread of antiseptic and aseptic surgery from 1914. The strongest factor essays note that no single factor explains the whole story: science enabled it, individuals pushed it, religion slowed it, war broadcast it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Anaesthetics solved surgical pain (Simpson&rsquo;s chloroform, 1847) but exposed the infection problem &mdash; the &lsquo;Black Period&rsquo; saw mortality rise as surgery grew bolder.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Lister&rsquo;s carbolic acid antisepsis (1865, published 1867) applied germ theory to wounds; resistance from British surgeons delayed acceptance into the 1880s.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Aseptic surgery (sterile theatres, gloves from 1894, autoclaves) replaced antisepsis by 1900; the First World War accelerated transfusion, X-rays and plastic surgery.</li>
</ul>
