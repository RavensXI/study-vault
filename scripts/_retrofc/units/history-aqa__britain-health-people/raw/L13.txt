--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the Beveridge Report and a Labour government built the NHS in 1948 against fierce opposition.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">War Reshapes the State</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Second World War changed what the British state was thought to be for. Evacuation moved 1.5 million urban children to rural homes from September 1939; middle-class hosts saw the lice, hunger and ragged clothes of city children for the first time. Rationing, far from causing mass hunger, improved working-class diet by guaranteeing protein and vitamins for everyone. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The temporary wartime body created in 1939 that nationalised Britain&rsquo;s hospitals to treat air-raid casualties &mdash; treated as a precedent for the postwar NHS.">Emergency Medical Service</dfn> nationalised every hospital bed in 1939 to prepare for air-raid casualties; for the first time central government ran the hospital system. By 1942 the question was no longer whether the state should provide health and welfare, but how.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the five &lsquo;giants&rsquo; in order: want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Beveridge Report (December 1942) named five &lsquo;giants on the road to reconstruction&rsquo;: want, disease, ignorance, squalor and idleness. It proposed cradle-to-grave social insurance and free healthcare for all. 300,000 copies sold &mdash; popular pressure made postwar welfare politically unavoidable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Beveridge Report, December 1942</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">William <dfn class="term" data-def="Liberal civil servant (1879-1963) whose December 1942 report Social Insurance and Allied Services proposed a universal welfare state to defeat the &lsquo;five giants&rsquo;.">Beveridge</dfn>, the civil servant who had designed the 1909 Labour Exchanges, was asked in 1941 to chair a committee on social insurance. His December 1942 report, <em>Social Insurance and Allied Services</em>, proposed a single contributory scheme covering everyone &lsquo;from cradle to grave&rsquo;. It assumed three preconditions: family allowances for children, a national health service free at point of use, and full employment. Wartime newspapers serialised it; Penguin produced a cheap paperback. 300,000 copies were sold and the report was studied by ordinary soldiers as well as policymakers. The Conservative-led wartime coalition received it cautiously, but its public profile made any postwar government&rsquo;s rejection politically impossible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The 1945 Labour Landslide</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In July 1945 the British electorate, while Churchill was still attending the Potsdam Conference, voted Labour into power with 393 seats to the Conservatives&rsquo; 197. Clement Attlee became Prime Minister. Aneurin <dfn class="term" data-def="Welsh Labour Minister of Health (1897-1960) who designed and steered the National Health Service Act 1946 and faced down BMA opposition to launch the NHS on 5 July 1948.">Bevan</dfn>, the Welsh ex-miner and MP for Ebbw Vale, became Minister of Health. The Education Act 1944 had already raised the school-leaving age and reorganised secondary schools. Family Allowances Act 1945 paid five shillings a week to mothers from the second child. National Insurance Act 1946 replaced the 1911 scheme with universal contributions. National Assistance Act 1948 supported those without insurance contributions. The NHS was the keystone of an interlocking welfare state.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The British Medical Association told members in January 1948 &mdash; six months before launch &mdash; that Bevan&rsquo;s plan was &lsquo;so grossly at variance with the essential principles of our profession that it should be rejected absolutely by all practitioners&rsquo;. Doctors feared becoming salaried civil servants and losing private practice income. Bevan made two compromises: hospital consultants could keep private patients with NHS pay-beds, and GPs would be self-employed contractors paid per registered patient, not state employees. Bevan summarised the deal privately: &lsquo;I stuffed their mouths with gold.&rsquo; The BMA voted to accept five weeks before the launch date. Without that compromise there would have been no NHS in July 1948.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">5 July 1948: The NHS Opens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">On 5 July 1948 the National Health Service Act 1946 came into force. Every British resident was registered with a GP and entitled to free hospital treatment, dental care, eye tests and prescriptions, paid from general taxation. Bevan visited Park Hospital in Manchester (now Trafford General) on the launch day. Demand exceeded every Treasury forecast: 8.5 million pairs of glasses dispensed in the first year, dental treatment for millions who had never seen a dentist, prescription numbers climbing year on year. Bevan resigned from the Cabinet in 1951 after Hugh Gaitskell introduced charges for spectacles and false teeth &mdash; a sign that NHS funding was already politically contested in its third year.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three concessions Bevan made to the BMA and explain why each one was necessary.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The NHS opened on 5 July 1948 as the first universal, tax-funded health service in the world. It was not a national consensus &mdash; Bevan won by political calculation against active medical opposition, and the deal preserved private practice inside the new service.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">From 1948 the NHS faced what every later generation has called the &lsquo;cost crisis&rsquo;. Demand grew faster than tax receipts could fund: an ageing population needing more care, expensive new drugs and technologies, rising public expectations, and the long aftershocks of COVID-19 in the 2020s.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Mental health services, historically underfunded, became a political priority from the 2010s. Antibiotic resistance, obesity-related disease and dementia all stretched the budget. The choices the NHS makes in 2026 &mdash; rationing, prescription charges, prioritisation by NICE &mdash; are direct descendants of Bevan&rsquo;s 1951 resignation. Free at point of use has remained politically untouchable; how to pay for it has not.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Linking to the Factor Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The NHS shows four factors interacting. War: WWII evacuation, EMS nationalisation and military Keynesianism shifted what was thinkable. Communication: the Beveridge Report&rsquo;s 300,000 copies turned a civil-service paper into mass politics. The role of the individual: Beveridge, Bevan and Attlee each shaped specific outcomes. Government: only a Labour landslide with parliamentary majority could legislate the 1946 Act. The strongest factor essays note that all four were necessary, and that earlier wars (1914&ndash;18) without comparable communication or government will produced no equivalent leap.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The Beveridge Report (December 1942) named the &lsquo;five giants&rsquo; (want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness) and sold 300,000 copies, making postwar welfare politically unavoidable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The 1945 Labour landslide and Bevan&rsquo;s compromise with the BMA delivered the NHS on 5 July 1948 &mdash; universal, free at point of use and tax-funded.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">From 1951 onwards the NHS faced a recurring cost crisis &mdash; ageing populations, new technology and rising demand &mdash; that continues in the 21st century.</li>
</ul>
