--- DESCRIPTION ---
Towns, monasteries and the catastrophe of 1348 — medieval public health and the Black Death.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Worlds of Public Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Medieval Britain had two very different versions of public health side by side. In the towns, narrow streets, open <dfn class="term" data-def="A pit dug in or near a town to receive human waste; the medieval equivalent of a sewer.">cesspits</dfn>, butchers throwing offal into the road and pigs roaming the lanes made cities like London famously stinking. Inside monastery walls, the same century saw piped water, latrines flushed by a stream, herb gardens and rules about hand-washing. The contrast tells you everything: medieval people knew filth was unpleasant and could be controlled &mdash; they just couldn&rsquo;t do it at scale outside religious institutions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three concrete public health measures monasteries used that towns did not.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Monasteries had piped water, flushable latrines (reredorters) and dedicated infirmaries. Medieval towns relied on cesspits, rakers (street cleaners), and royal proclamations &mdash; effective only when enforced. The gap was about money, organisation and scale, not knowledge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Public Health in Medieval Towns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most towns had local rules. London ordinances from the 1300s required butchers to slaughter outside the city walls, fined people for letting pigs run loose, and paid <dfn class="term" data-def="Medieval town workers paid to clear waste from streets and unblock drains.">rakers</dfn> to clear streets and gully-pots. Towns had piped water in some cases &mdash; the Great Conduit was built in London in 1247 to carry water from springs &mdash; but most households drew water from wells or the Thames itself. Cesspits were dug in back gardens; in dense parishes the cesspit of one building leaked into the well of another. Compare this to Christchurch Priory, where 12th-century plans show piped water through pottery and lead, latrines flushed by an underground stream, and waste taken downstream of drinking water.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Black Death Arrives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In June 1348 a ship docked at Melcombe Regis (Weymouth) in Dorset. Within weeks the disease that had already killed millions across Europe reached Bristol; by November it was in London. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The pandemic of bubonic and pneumonic plague that killed roughly one-third of England&rsquo;s population in 1348-50.">Black Death</dfn> killed roughly one-third of England&rsquo;s population &mdash; perhaps two million out of around six million people &mdash; in three years. Some villages lost three-quarters of their inhabitants. The plague struck so fast that bodies piled up in the streets of London and Bristol; emergency burial pits were dug at East Smithfield and elsewhere.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The disease appeared in two forms. <strong>Bubonic plague</strong> caused painful black swellings (buboes) in the groin, armpit and neck, with about a 60% mortality rate even with modern treatment. <strong>Pneumonic plague</strong>, the airborne form, attacked the lungs and killed almost everyone who caught it within a few days. We now know the bacterium <em>Yersinia pestis</em> was carried by fleas on rats &mdash; but in 1348 nobody had any idea.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Beliefs about the cause of the plague</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">People offered every explanation their world allowed. The most popular was that the plague was God&rsquo;s punishment for sin &mdash; preached from pulpits across the country. Others blamed miasma, pointing to the foul smells of crowded towns. The medical faculty of Paris, asked by the King of France for an explanation, blamed an unfavourable conjunction of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars in 1345. In some European towns Jewish communities were blamed and massacred &mdash; though this was less common in Britain because most Jews had been expelled in 1290. A few thinkers blamed earthquakes releasing poisonous vapours.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">All these explanations followed logically from medieval beliefs about disease. Nobody at the time had a way to test which was right.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Treatments and Prevention</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Treatments matched the explanations. Religious responses included prayer, processions, wearing crosses and the work of flagellants who whipped themselves through towns. Medical responses included bleeding, lancing the buboes, and herbal treatments such as <em>theriac</em> (a mixture of dozens of ingredients including viper flesh and opium). People held posies of flowers to ward off miasma &mdash; the rhyme &lsquo;Ring-a-ring o&rsquo; roses, a pocket full of posies&rsquo; is sometimes (though not certainly) traced to the plague.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Prevention measures &mdash; surprisingly modern in places</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Prevention was more practical than treatment. Some towns introduced <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced isolation of people, ships or goods believed to carry disease, named after the Italian quarantena (forty days).">quarantine</dfn> &mdash; though the formal forty-day quarantine was developed in Italy. In London a 1348 royal proclamation by Edward III ordered streets cleaned of filth, and the Lord Mayor enforced burials outside the city walls. The wealthy fled to the countryside; the poor stayed and died. None of these measures was based on germ theory because germ theory was 500 years away &mdash; but isolating the sick and removing rotting matter both cut transmission, even if for the wrong reasons.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three short cause-effect sentences linking the Black Death to wider social change in England.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Black Death triggered long-term change. Labour shortages led to the Statute of Labourers (1351) trying to fix wages; rising peasant power contributed to the Peasants&rsquo; Revolt of 1381; surviving workers won higher wages and gradual movement off the land. Disease drove social and economic transformation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">After the Plague</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The Black Death changed England. The shortage of workers gave survivors bargaining power. Parliament tried to freeze wages with the Statute of Labourers in 1351, but the law was widely ignored. By 1381 anger over a poll tax and the wage cap exploded into the Peasants&rsquo; Revolt. Public health measures changed too: London tightened street cleaning, butchery rules and burial regulations across the rest of the 14th century. The plague returned repeatedly &mdash; over 30 outbreaks before the Great Plague of 1665 &mdash; but never with the catastrophic force of 1348.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Linking to the Factor Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Black Death plants three factors clearly for the 16-mark essay. <strong>Chance</strong>: a single ship at Melcombe Regis triggered national catastrophe. <strong>Religion</strong>: shaped both the explanation (sin) and the response (prayer, flagellants) &mdash; and arguably worsened transmission through gathering. <strong>Government</strong>: appears for the first time as a public-health actor, with Edward III&rsquo;s 1348 proclamation and the Statute of Labourers. None succeeded in stopping the disease, but the precedent of state action mattered when government became a serious health factor in the Victorian period.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Medieval public health worked far better in monasteries (piped water, flushed latrines) than in towns (cesspits, butchery in the streets).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Black Death of 1348 killed roughly one-third of the population; explanations included God&rsquo;s punishment, miasma and astrology, none of which were correct.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Responses (quarantine, street cleaning, burial outside walls) showed government acting on public health for the first time, even without a theoretical basis.</li>
</ul>
