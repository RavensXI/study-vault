--- DESCRIPTION ---
Booth, Rowntree, the Boer War and the Liberal reforms that put the state into welfare for the first time.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Late-Victorian Britain Discovers Its Poor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1900 most middle-class Britons assumed poverty was a moral failure: drink, idleness, fecklessness. Two surveys overturned that assumption. Charles <dfn class="term" data-def="London ship-owner (1840-1916) who funded and led Life and Labour of the People in London (1889-1903), mapping poverty street by street.">Booth</dfn>, a Liverpool ship-owner, financed and led <em>Life and Labour of the People in London</em> (1889&ndash;1903). His investigators found 30.7% of Londoners lived below the poverty line. Seebohm <dfn class="term" data-def="York chocolate manufacturer (1871-1954) whose 1901 study Poverty: A Study of Town Life found 28% of York lived below the poverty line and distinguished &lsquo;primary&rsquo; from &lsquo;secondary&rsquo; poverty.">Rowntree</dfn>, a Quaker chocolate manufacturer in York, replicated the work in his own city in 1901. <em>Poverty: A Study of Town Life</em> showed 28% of York&rsquo;s population in the same condition &mdash; not just London. Rowntree distinguished &lsquo;primary&rsquo; poverty (income too low to meet basic needs) from &lsquo;secondary&rsquo; (where some money was wasted). Combined, the two reports made it impossible to keep blaming the poor for being poor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four pieces of evidence that built the case for state welfare: Booth, Rowntree, Boer War rejections and the 1906 election.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four pressures combined to push the state into welfare: Booth&rsquo;s 1889&ndash;1903 London survey (30.7% in poverty); Rowntree&rsquo;s 1901 York study (28%); the Boer War (1899&ndash;1902), which rejected 40% of working-class recruits as unfit; and the 1906 Liberal landslide.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Boer War Shock, 1899&ndash;1902</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">When Britain went to war in South Africa in 1899, recruiting officers in northern industrial cities turned away around 40% of volunteers as physically unfit. In Manchester the rejection rate exceeded 60%. The 1903 Inter-Departmental Committee on Physical Deterioration confirmed the problem and linked it to childhood undernourishment. The Boer War transformed welfare from a moral question (should we help?) into a security question (can we field an army?). National efficiency, not compassion, made reform politically irresistible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The 1906 Liberal Landslide</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Liberals won 397 seats to the Conservatives&rsquo; 156 in January 1906. Henry Campbell-Bannerman became Prime Minister, succeeded by Asquith in 1908. Three figures drove reform from inside government: David Lloyd George (Chancellor from 1908), Winston Churchill (then a Liberal, President of the Board of Trade) and William Beveridge, the young civil servant who designed Labour Exchanges. The Liberals had no welfare manifesto in 1906 &mdash; the reform programme was assembled in office, partly to head off the rising Labour Party and partly because Booth, Rowntree and the Boer War had changed the climate.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Children</strong>: Free School Meals (Education (Provision of Meals) Act, 1906) and School Medical Inspections (1907) tackled the Boer War health concerns directly. The Children Act 1908 (the &lsquo;Children&rsquo;s Charter&rsquo;) banned the sale of tobacco and alcohol to children and set up juvenile courts. <strong>Old people</strong>: the Old Age Pensions Act 1908 paid 5 shillings a week to single people over 70 with incomes below &pound;21 a year &mdash; means-tested, non-contributory, &lsquo;Lloyd George&rsquo;s pension&rsquo;. <strong>Workers</strong>: Labour Exchanges (1909) helped the unemployed find work. The Trade Boards Act (1909) set minimum wages in &lsquo;sweated&rsquo; trades. The People&rsquo;s Budget (1909) raised income and inheritance taxes to fund all this; the House of Lords blocked it, triggering the 1911 Parliament Act that ended their veto.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">National Insurance, 1911</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The flagship reform was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1911 Act that introduced compulsory contributory health insurance for workers (Part I) and unemployment insurance for select trades (Part II), funded by employee, employer and state contributions.">National Insurance Act 1911</dfn>. Part I gave 13 million workers earning under &pound;160 a year free GP care, sick pay (10 shillings a week for 26 weeks) and maternity benefits. Workers paid 4 pence a week, employers 3 pence and the state 2 pence &mdash; Lloyd George&rsquo;s slogan was &lsquo;ninepence for fourpence&rsquo;. Part II covered unemployment in seven trades vulnerable to seasonal layoffs (building, shipbuilding, engineering and others), paying 7 shillings a week for up to 15 weeks. It was the first nationwide compulsory health and unemployment insurance in Britain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list two important groups the 1911 National Insurance Act left out and explain why this mattered.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1911 Act covered the worker, not the family. Wives, children, the elderly and the chronically sick were excluded from Part I. Part II covered only seven trades. The Liberal reforms were a partial, contributory step &mdash; the universal welfare state had to wait for Beveridge and Bevan after WWII.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Opposition and Limits</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Opposition to the reforms and how they held</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The reforms faced opposition from several directions. The British Medical Association feared the loss of private patient income; doctors threatened to boycott the panel system until generous fees were agreed. Conservatives in the House of Lords blocked the People&rsquo;s Budget, forcing the constitutional crisis of 1909&ndash;11. Friendly societies that had previously sold health insurance feared losing members. The Labour Party criticised the contributory model as a regressive tax on the working class. Yet the reforms held: by 1914 free school meals reached over 150,000 children daily, pensions were paid to about 970,000 elderly people, and 13 million workers were enrolled in the new health scheme. The state had crossed a line it could not uncross.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Linking to the Factor Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Liberal reforms show four factors interacting. <strong>Communication</strong>: Booth and Rowntree turned anecdote into evidence. <strong>War</strong>: the Boer War made fitness a national-security argument. <strong>Government</strong>: only the 1906 Liberal landslide could deliver this scale of legislation. <strong>The role of the individual</strong>: Lloyd George, Churchill and Beveridge each shaped the design. The strongest factor essays note the chain &mdash; communication built evidence; war built urgency; government built law &mdash; and that in earlier periods (1348, 1665) the same diseases of poverty produced no equivalent state response.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Booth&rsquo;s London survey (1889&ndash;1903) and Rowntree&rsquo;s York study (1901) proved poverty affected almost a third of urban Britain, undermining moral explanations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The Boer War&rsquo;s recruit-rejection figures and the 1906 Liberal landslide opened the political space for reform.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Free school meals, medical inspections, old-age pensions, labour exchanges and the 1911 National Insurance Act began the welfare state, though wives, children and many trades remained outside Part I.</li>
</ul>
