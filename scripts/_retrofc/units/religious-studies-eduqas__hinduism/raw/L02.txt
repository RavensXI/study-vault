<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Puja &mdash; Hindu Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The act of worship or devotion in Hinduism, offered to a deity through ritual actions such as offering flowers, water, light and incense.">Puja</dfn> is the central act of Hindu worship. It involves offering devotion to a deity through ritual actions &mdash; presenting flowers, water, food (prasad), light and incense. Puja can take place in the home shrine or in the mandir (temple), and it connects the worshipper directly to the divine. The Bhagavad Gita (3:19) teaches about the importance of acting without attachment to results as a form of duty, and (4:38) speaks of sacred knowledge gained through sincere devotion and practice &mdash; foundational principles that underlie Hindu worship.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three key features of daily home puja. Include what is offered, what is used and what the purpose is.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Daily puja at home is typically performed at a household shrine (<em>puja ghar</em>). The worshipper may wake the deity, bathe and dress the image, offer food, flowers, incense and light a lamp, and recite prayers or mantras. This daily ritual maintains a personal relationship with the divine and structures the Hindu day around devotion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Congregational Puja in the Mandir</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Hindu temple &mdash; a sacred space housing the image (murti) of a deity, where congregational worship takes place.">mandir</dfn> is the Hindu temple &mdash; the earthly home of a deity. At its heart is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The sacred image or statue of a deity in a Hindu temple or home shrine, treated as the living presence of God.">murti</dfn>: the sacred image through which the deity is believed to be present. Hindus do not understand the murti as a mere statue; the image is treated as the living presence of God, cared for, dressed and fed as if alive. This distinction is important &mdash; criticisms that Hindus &lsquo;worship idols&rsquo; misunderstand the theological basis of murti worship.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Arti</strong> is one of the most important acts of congregational puja. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A ritual of worship in which a lamp with multiple wicks is waved before the deity in a circular motion, symbolising the offering of light and devotion.">arti</dfn> lamp with several wicks is lit and waved in a circular motion before the murti, accompanied by bells, singing and the chanting of prayers. The lamp is then offered to the congregation, who cup their hands over the flame and touch their faces &mdash; symbolically receiving the deity&rsquo;s blessing. Arti is typically performed multiple times daily in large temples.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Havan</strong> (also called homa) is a sacred fire ceremony performed as part of worship, particularly for significant occasions. Offerings &mdash; including grains, ghee (clarified butter) and herbs &mdash; are placed into a consecrated fire as the priest chants sacred texts. The fire is considered a messenger between the human and divine realms, carrying offerings upward to the gods.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Devotional love directed towards a personal deity in Hinduism &mdash; one of the main spiritual paths (marga) to liberation.">Bhakti</dfn> means devotional love &mdash; the path of personal, loving devotion to a chosen deity (<em>ishta devata</em>). The bhakti movement, which flourished across India from around the 7th century CE, stressed that liberation (moksha) could be achieved through sincere love for God, not only through ritual or knowledge. Bhakti made Hindu devotion accessible to all, regardless of caste or education.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Bhakti practice takes many forms: singing devotional hymns (<em>bhajan</em>), chanting God&rsquo;s name (<em>kirtan</em>), performing puja with heartfelt emotion, and reading or hearing the sacred stories of the gods (<em>katha</em>). The Hare Krishna movement (ISKCON) is a well-known modern expression of Vaishnavite bhakti &mdash; its members express devotion through chanting the Maha Mantra: &lsquo;Hare Krishna, Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna, Hare Hare / Hare Rama, Hare Rama, Rama Rama, Hare Hare&rsquo;.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Pilgrimage to Varanasi</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="One of the holiest cities in Hinduism, situated on the banks of the River Ganges in northern India, considered a sacred site for bathing, worship and cremation.">Varanasi</dfn> (also known as Benares or Kashi) is one of the most sacred cities in all of Hinduism. Situated on the banks of the River Ganges in northern India, it is believed to be the home of Shiva himself. Pilgrims travel to Varanasi to bathe in the sacred Ganges, which Hindus believe has the power to purify the soul and wash away sins accumulated in past lives. Bathing at the <em>ghats</em> (stone steps leading to the river) is a profoundly important spiritual act.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Dying and being cremated at Varanasi is considered especially auspicious. Hindus believe that those who die there receive liberation (<em>moksha</em>) from the cycle of rebirth directly, because Shiva himself is said to whisper the sacred mantra into the ear of the dying. Varanasi&rsquo;s famous cremation ghats &mdash; in particular the Manikarnika Ghat &mdash; burn funeral pyres continuously. For many Hindus, the hope of dying at Varanasi and being cremated there is a lifelong aspiration.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Explain in two sentences why Varanasi is important to Hindus. Include why both bathing there and dying there are significant.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Varanasi is holy because it is believed to be Shiva&rsquo;s city. Pilgrims bathe in the Ganges to purify the soul. Dying there is believed to grant moksha &mdash; liberation from the cycle of rebirth. The Ganges itself is regarded as a goddess and is considered sacred throughout its length.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Hindu Rites of Passage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hinduism marks the key transitions of life through sacred rites called <em>samskaras</em>. These rites are connected to the broader framework of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu concept that combines the duties of one's varna (social class), ashrama (life stage) and dharma (righteous duty).">varnashramadharma</dfn> &mdash; the duties associated with one&rsquo;s social position and stage of life. The Mahabharata (Book 9.60) references this framework.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Nam Samskar (Naming Ceremony)</strong>: Shortly after birth, a child is given a name in a formal ceremony. A Hindu priest may cast the child&rsquo;s horoscope and suggest names beginning with an auspicious letter. The name often reflects one of the divine qualities or names a deity, connecting the child&rsquo;s identity to the sacred from the very start of life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Mundane (Head-Shaving Ceremony)</strong>: In the early years of life, a child&rsquo;s first hair is shaved. This is seen as removing impurities carried from the previous life. It is understood as a purification rite that prepares the child for the life ahead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Upanayana (Sacred Thread Ceremony)</strong>: This initiation rite marks a boy&rsquo;s transition to student life. A sacred thread is placed across the body, symbolising the second birth into a life of religious learning and duty. Traditionally restricted to the top three varnas, the ceremony involves commitments to study, self-discipline and service to a teacher. After this ceremony, the boy is expected to begin learning the Vedas and other sacred texts.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">Hindu marriage (<em>vivah</em>) is one of the most elaborate samskaras. The central ritual involves the couple walking seven times around a sacred fire &mdash; these seven steps (<em>saptapadi</em>) represent the couple&rsquo;s seven vows to each other before God, who is symbolised by the fire. The ceremony is witnessed by the fire as the divine witness, making the marriage a sacred covenant rather than merely a legal contract.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">At death, the body is cremated rather than buried. This reflects the Hindu belief that the physical body is temporary &mdash; it is the atman (soul) that is eternal and will be reborn. The eldest son plays a crucial role: he lights the funeral pyre and, in traditional practice, may break the skull at the peak of the fire to release the atman from the body. After cremation, the ashes are scattered in a sacred river &mdash; ideally the Ganges &mdash; completing the soul&rsquo;s return to the cycle of nature.</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Puja &mdash; both in the home and in the mandir &mdash; is the central act of Hindu devotion. The murti is treated as the living presence of the deity. Arti (light offering) and havan (fire ceremony) are key practices. Bhakti (devotional love) is the spiritual path expressed through puja.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Varanasi is Hinduism&rsquo;s holiest city. Bathing in the Ganges purifies the soul; dying there is believed to grant liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Hindu rites of passage (samskaras) mark key life transitions: naming (Nam Samskar), head-shaving (Mundane), sacred thread ceremony (Upanayana), marriage (seven circles around the fire) and cremation (eldest son, ashes scattered in a river).</li>
</ul>
