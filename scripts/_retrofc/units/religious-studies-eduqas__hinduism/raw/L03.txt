<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Karma and Samsara</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu (and Buddhist) law of cause and effect: every intentional action generates consequences that shape the soul's future lives.">Karma</dfn> means &lsquo;action&rsquo; in Sanskrit. In Hinduism, it refers to the moral law of cause and effect: every intentional action &mdash; physical, verbal or mental &mdash; generates consequences that shape the soul&rsquo;s future existence. Good actions generate positive karma, leading towards better future lives and ultimately liberation. Bad actions generate negative karma, leading to suffering and lower rebirths. The Bhagavad Gita (2:13) describes how the soul passes from one body to the next just as a person moves from childhood to youth to old age &mdash; the soul continuing through a long chain of lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu concept of the cycle of birth, death and rebirth driven by karma, from which liberation (moksha) is sought.">Samsara</dfn> is the cycle of birth, death and rebirth within which all living beings are caught. The Bhagavad Gita (2:13, 2:15, 2:22) develops this teaching: just as a person casts off worn-out clothes and puts on others that are new, so the soul casts off worn-out bodies and takes on new ones. Samsara is not a punishment in itself &mdash; it is the ongoing process of the soul&rsquo;s journey &mdash; but it involves suffering because of attachment to the material world. The ultimate goal is to escape samsara entirely.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences the relationship between karma, samsara and moksha. Include the clothes analogy from the Bhagavad Gita.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Karma is the law of moral cause and effect. Samsara is the cycle of rebirth it drives. <dfn class="term" data-def="Liberation from the cycle of birth, death and rebirth (samsara) &mdash; the ultimate spiritual goal in Hinduism.">Moksha</dfn> is liberation from samsara &mdash; the soul&rsquo;s reunion with Brahman, the ultimate reality. These three concepts form the backbone of the Hindu understanding of the soul&rsquo;s journey.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Varnashramadharma &mdash; The Four Varnas</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Hindu society has traditionally been organised according to the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of four social classes or groupings in Hinduism, understood as reflecting cosmic order and associated duties.">varna</dfn> (often translated as caste). The four varnas are: <strong>Brahmin</strong> (priests and teachers), <strong>Kshatriya</strong> (warriors and rulers), <strong>Vaishya</strong> (merchants and farmers) and <strong>Shudra</strong> (labourers and servants). Beyond the four varnas are the <strong>Dalits</strong> (historically called &lsquo;untouchables&rsquo;), who were placed outside the varna system entirely and assigned the most polluting tasks.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Within each varna are many <em>jatis</em> &mdash; hereditary occupational sub-groups. The varna and jati system has had a profound impact on social life in South Asia: determining marriage partners, occupation, diet and social interaction. The status of Dalits was, for centuries, one of severe marginalisation and discrimination. Today, the Dalit community has fought for and won legal protections &mdash; the Indian constitution outlaws caste discrimination &mdash; but social prejudice can persist. In Hindu communities in the UK, varna distinctions are generally less rigidly observed than in parts of South Asia, though they still influence marriage patterns.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Several modern movements within Hinduism have challenged traditional caste hierarchies. <strong>ISKCON</strong> (the International Society for Krishna Consciousness, known as the Hare Krishna movement) teaches that all people, regardless of birth, can achieve devotion to Krishna and spiritual advancement. ISKCON accepts members of all backgrounds and nationalities, directly challenging the idea that caste is spiritually determining.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The <strong>Ramakrishna Mission</strong>, founded in the late 19th century by Swami Vivekananda, emphasised the unity of all people and the equality of all paths to God. Vivekananda taught that service to humanity was service to God (<em>daridra narayan</em> &mdash; God in the poor), directly challenging the view that Dalits were spiritually inferior. Both movements reflect an ongoing internal reform process within Hinduism.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Four Ashramas</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Alongside the varna system, Hinduism describes four stages of life (<dfn class="term" data-def="The four stages of an ideal Hindu life: student, householder, forest-dweller and renunciant, each with distinct duties.">ashramas</dfn>). The first is <strong>brahmacharya</strong> (student life) &mdash; dedicated to learning, celibacy and service to the teacher. The second is <strong>grihastha</strong> (householder) &mdash; marriage, raising children, professional work and maintaining the home. The third is <strong>vanaprastha</strong> (forest-dweller or retirement) &mdash; withdrawing from worldly duties to focus on spiritual practice. The fourth is <strong>sannyasa</strong> (renunciant) &mdash; the final stage of complete renunciation, with no home or possessions, focused solely on liberation. The ideal Hindu life moves through all four ashramas, each with its appropriate duties (<em>dharma</em>).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Hindu Festivals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Diwali</strong> (also spelled Divali) is often called the Festival of Lights. It is celebrated across South Asia and in Hindu communities worldwide, and lasts five days. For Vaishnavas (devotees of Vishnu), Diwali is associated particularly with the return of Rama and Sita to Ayodhya after their fourteen-year exile and Rama&rsquo;s defeat of the demon king Ravana &mdash; the story told in the Ramayana. Lamps and fireworks celebrate this homecoming. For Shaktas (devotees of the goddess), Diwali is associated with the goddess <strong>Lakshmi</strong> (goddess of wealth and prosperity), who is believed to visit clean, well-lit homes. Rangoli patterns, diyas (oil lamps), fireworks and gift-giving are central practices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Hindu spring festival celebrated with coloured powders, water and dancing, associated with the stories of Krishna and Radha or Holika and Prahlada.">Holi</dfn> is a joyful spring festival, celebrated with coloured powders, water and dancing. Its origins lie in two stories. The first is the story of <strong>Holika and Prahlada</strong>: Holika was a demoness who tried to kill the young devotee Prahlada (a devoted worshipper of Vishnu), but she was destroyed while Prahlada survived, demonstrating that devotion to God protects the righteous. A bonfire (<em>Holika dahan</em>) the night before the main celebration remembers her destruction. The second story celebrates the divine love of <strong>Krishna and Radha</strong> &mdash; Krishna is associated with colour and playfulness, and the throwing of coloured powder is connected to his relationship with Radha and the gopis (cowgirls) of Vrindavan.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu principle of non-violence or non-harm towards all living beings.">Ahimsa</dfn> means non-violence or non-harm. It is one of Hinduism&rsquo;s most important ethical principles, flowing from the belief that all living beings contain the atman &mdash; the divine soul. To harm any living creature is, in this sense, to harm God. Ahimsa is expressed practically through vegetarianism, which is widely practised among Hindus, particularly Brahmins and Vaishnavas. The Hare Krishna movement&rsquo;s <strong>Food for Life</strong> programme distributes free vegetarian meals to those in need &mdash; embodying both ahimsa and the duty of social service.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Environmental concern also flows from ahimsa. The <strong>Chipko movement</strong> (&lsquo;tree-huggers&rsquo;) began in the 1970s in northern India as villagers, many of them women, embraced trees to prevent loggers from cutting them down. Drawing on Hindu reverence for nature and the principle of ahimsa towards all life, the movement became an influential early example of environmental activism. The sacred forest at <strong>Vrindavan</strong> (associated with Krishna&rsquo;s childhood) has been the subject of conservation efforts by Hindu communities who regard it as sacred land.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the two stories behind the festival of Holi and state which deity each involves.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Holi has two origin stories: Holika and Prahlada (devotion to Vishnu conquers evil) and Krishna and Radha (divine love and playfulness). Diwali celebrates Rama and Sita&rsquo;s return (Vaishnavas) or honours Lakshmi (Shaktas). Both festivals are celebrated with light, colour and community.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Karma (moral cause and effect) drives samsara (the cycle of rebirth). The goal is moksha (liberation) &mdash; the soul&rsquo;s return to Brahman. The Bhagavad Gita (2:13, 2:22) uses the clothes analogy to explain how the soul moves from body to body.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The four varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) structure traditional Hindu society, with Dalits historically outside the system. Reform movements like ISKCON and the Ramakrishna Mission challenge caste discrimination. The four ashramas describe the ideal stages of a Hindu life.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Diwali (Festival of Lights) celebrates Rama&rsquo;s return and honours Lakshmi. Holi (Festival of Colours) commemorates Holika&rsquo;s destruction and Krishna&rsquo;s love for Radha. Ahimsa (non-violence) shapes vegetarianism, environmental concern and social service.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Questions about karma often invite students to explain both the concept and its implications for how Hindus live. Don&rsquo;t just define karma &mdash; explain that it motivates ethical behaviour (every action has consequences), supports the practice of ahimsa (harming others generates bad karma), and gives meaning to the varna system (one&rsquo;s birth reflects past-life karma). For festival questions, name the story, explain what it celebrates and describe two or three actual practices &mdash; examiners are looking for both knowledge of content and understanding of significance.</p>
