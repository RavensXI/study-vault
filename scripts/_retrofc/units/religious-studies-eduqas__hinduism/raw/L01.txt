<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Brahman — The Universal Soul</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Hinduism is one of the world&rsquo;s oldest living religious traditions. At its heart is the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ultimate reality or universal soul in Hinduism — the single, infinite, divine power that underlies all existence.">Brahman</dfn>: the ultimate reality, the universal soul, the single divine power that underlies everything that exists. The Chandogya Upanishad (3:14.1) describes Brahman as all of this universe and teaches that the entire cosmos arose from Brahman, lives within Brahman, and will return to Brahman. Brahman is infinite, without beginning or end, beyond complete human description.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">One important dimension of Brahman is that Hindus speak of Brahman as both one and many. Brahman is the single ultimate reality, yet Brahman is also present in all things — in every person, every creature, every aspect of the natural world. The Katha Upanishad (5:2) teaches that Brahman, the inner self, dwells within the hearts of all living beings. A classic analogy in Hindu teaching is salt dissolved in water: the salt cannot be seen, but it is everywhere in the water. In the same way, Brahman permeates all existence while remaining beyond full perception.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why Hindus say Brahman is both 'one' and 'many'. Use the salt-in-water analogy in your answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brahman is the ultimate reality and the source of all life (Chandogya Upanishad 3:14.1). Brahman pervades all things (Katha Upanishad 5:2). A helpful analogy: salt dissolved in water is everywhere yet invisible — just as Brahman permeates the universe.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Atman — The Individual Soul</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The individual soul or self in Hinduism, believed to be eternal, indestructible and ultimately identical with Brahman.">Atman</dfn> is the individual soul or true self within each living being. The Bhagavad Gita, one of Hinduism&rsquo;s most important scriptures, teaches extensively about the nature of atman. At Bhagavad Gita 2:12, the god Krishna explains to the warrior Arjuna that there was never a time when either of them did not exist, and there will never be a time when they cease to exist. This establishes the eternal nature of the individual soul.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Bhagavad Gita 2:17 states that the soul is indestructible: the body may be slain, but the soul that pervades the body cannot be destroyed. This verse is one of the most frequently cited passages in GCSE Religious Studies because it clearly distinguishes the body (mortal, changing) from the atman (immortal, unchanging). The relationship between Brahman and atman is debated within Hinduism: the Advaita Vedanta school (associated with the philosopher Shankara) teaches that atman and Brahman are ultimately one and the same — the individual soul is not separate from the universal soul. This is summarised in the Sanskrit phrase <em>Tat Tvam Asi</em> (&lsquo;Thou art that&rsquo;).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Trimurti — Three Aspects of the Divine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Hindu theology expresses the different functions of Brahman through a range of deities. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu concept of three divine aspects: Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver) and Shiva (destroyer or transformer).">Trimurti</dfn> is the three-fold form of the divine, representing the three great cosmic functions. The Kurma Purana (1:6, 1:9, 1:26) is one of the scriptural sources for this concept.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Brahma</strong> is the creator deity — responsible for bringing the universe into existence at the start of each cosmic cycle. Despite his cosmic importance, Brahma has relatively few dedicated temples in modern Hinduism, partly because creation is seen as completed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Vishnu</strong> is the preserver — responsible for maintaining and sustaining the universe. Vishnu is central to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A major tradition within Hinduism centred on devotion to Vishnu as the supreme deity.">Vaishnavism</dfn> tradition, one of the largest traditions within Hinduism. Vishnu is believed to descend to earth in physical form as an avatar whenever the balance of good and evil is threatened.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Shiva</strong> is the destroyer — but destruction in Hindu thought is not simply negative. Shiva destroys the old to make way for renewal and transformation. Shiva is central to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A major tradition within Hinduism centred on devotion to Shiva as the supreme deity.">Shaivism</dfn> tradition. The diversity between Vaishnavism and Shaivism illustrates the rich variety within Hinduism — the tradition is not monolithic but encompasses many different theological emphases.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Avatars — Krishna and Rama</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The concept of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A physical descent or incarnation of a deity on earth, particularly associated with Vishnu in Hinduism.">avatar</dfn> is central to the Vaishnavism tradition. An avatar is a descent of a deity into the physical world in a particular form. The Bhagavad Gita (4:5&ndash;8) contains a passage in which Krishna explains to Arjuna that he takes birth in the world in age after age: whenever righteousness declines and unrighteousness rises, he manifests himself. This doctrine means that Vishnu&rsquo;s avatars are not arbitrary but purposeful — appearing when the world is in moral crisis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Krishna</strong> is one of the most beloved deities in all of Hinduism. He appears in the Bhagavad Gita as Arjuna&rsquo;s charioteer, but reveals himself as a manifestation of Vishnu. Krishna is associated with divine love, playfulness and spiritual wisdom. His teachings to Arjuna on the battlefield form the core content of the Bhagavad Gita — arguably Hinduism&rsquo;s most widely read scripture worldwide.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Rama</strong> is another of Vishnu&rsquo;s principal avatars. His story is told in the epic poem the Ramayana. Rama is portrayed as the ideal king, husband and son — an embodiment of dharma (righteous duty). His battle to rescue his wife Sita from the demon king Ravana, aided by the monkey general Hanuman, is celebrated during the festival of Diwali.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Ganesh and Hanuman</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Beyond the Trimurti and Vishnu&rsquo;s avatars, two deities are especially important in popular Hindu devotion. <strong>Ganesh</strong> is the elephant-headed son of Shiva and Parvati. He is the remover of obstacles and the lord of beginnings, and is invoked at the start of new ventures, journeys and religious ceremonies. His elephant head is often explained through a story in which Shiva, unaware Ganesh was his son, severed his head, and then replaced it with the first head available — that of an elephant.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Hanuman</strong> is the monkey deity who plays a crucial role in the Ramayana epic as the devoted servant of Rama. He is associated with strength, devotion (bhakti) and selfless service. Hanuman&rsquo;s complete dedication to Rama makes him a model of devotional worship for many Hindus.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three members of the Trimurti and state the function of each. Then name two avatars of Vishnu.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Trimurti: Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver), Shiva (destroyer/transformer). Vishnu&rsquo;s avatars include Krishna (divine teacher and lover) and Rama (ideal king and embodiment of dharma). The Bhagavad Gita 2:17 teaches that the atman (individual soul) is indestructible and cannot be killed even when the body dies.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Brahman is the ultimate reality and universal soul in Hinduism — both one (the single divine power) and many (present in all things). Atman is the individual soul within each being, believed to be eternal and indestructible (Bhagavad Gita 2:17).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Trimurti represents the three cosmic functions of the divine: Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver) and Shiva (destroyer/transformer). Vaishnavism and Shaivism are the two major devotional traditions within Hinduism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Vishnu descends to earth as avatars when righteousness is threatened. Krishna and Rama are the two most important avatars. Ganesh (remover of obstacles) and Hanuman (devoted servant of Rama) are central figures in popular Hindu devotion.</li>
</ul>
