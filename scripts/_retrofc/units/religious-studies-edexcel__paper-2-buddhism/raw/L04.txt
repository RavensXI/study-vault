<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Buddhist Places of Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Buddhists worship in a variety of settings depending on their tradition. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A Buddhist place of worship for monks and laity; in Theravada countries often houses a shrine room, meditation hall and library.">vihara</dfn> is the traditional monastery-complex found in Theravada Buddhism &mdash; it typically includes a shrine room, meditation hall and library. The library&rsquo;s importance reflects the Buddhist emphasis on wisdom and study, as noted in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali sutta that asks what distinguishes good conduct in visits to monasteries, emphasising reverent and learningful behaviour.">Kimsila Sutta</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A Tibetan Buddhist monastery or meditation centre; the place of spiritual practice in Vajrayana (Tibetan) Buddhism.">gompa</dfn> is a Tibetan monastery, typically featuring elaborate shrine rooms with thangka paintings, statues of Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, and space for ritual practice. <dfn class="term" data-def="A Buddhist community centre used in the Triratna Buddhist Order (Western Buddhism), typically a meditation and teaching space open to the public.">Triratna</dfn> Buddhism (a Western movement) uses community centres rather than traditional monasteries, making practice accessible to people in secular contexts. All three types share a shrine room as their heart.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name three types of Buddhist place of worship and identify which Buddhist tradition each belongs to.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three main types of Buddhist place of worship: the <strong>vihara</strong> (Theravada monastery-complex), the <strong>gompa</strong> (Tibetan monastery) and the <strong>temple</strong> (broader term). The Kimsila Sutta describes how Buddhists should conduct themselves when visiting these places of worship &mdash; with reverence and a desire to learn.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Objects of Devotion: Buddharupas and Sacred Objects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A statue or image of the Buddha used as a focus for devotion and meditation; found in all Buddhist traditions.">buddharupa</dfn> is an image of the Buddha that serves as a focus for devotion and meditation. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali sutta in which the monk Vakkali is instructed that truly seeing the Dhamma is seeing the Buddha, not the physical body; used to justify the spiritual significance of Buddha images.">Vakkali Sutta</dfn> clarifies the theological point: Vakkali, who was ill and longing to see the Buddha, is told that seeing the Dhamma is seeing the Buddha &mdash; the physical image points beyond itself to the truth the Buddha realised. Buddhists do not worship the statue as an idol; they use it to focus their minds on the Buddha&rsquo;s qualities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Other devotional objects include the <strong>Bodhi tree</strong> (venerated at Bodh Gaya as the site of enlightenment), flowers, incense and candles offered at shrines. These offerings are not gift transactions but symbolic acts cultivating the offering of oneself to the path.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Puja</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Acts of devotion and offering in Buddhism, performed both in the vihara and at home; a form of worship that expresses reverence for the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.">Puja</dfn> refers to acts of worship and devotion. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali sutta containing a discourse on auspicious conduct; used in Theravada puja as a source of blessings.">Mangala Sutta</dfn> is recited during Theravada puja as a source of blessings. Typical puja includes offering flowers (representing impermanence), incense (representing the spread of good reputation), candles or lamps (representing wisdom), and prostrations before the buddharupa. Puja can be performed in the vihara with the Sangha or at a home shrine.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Death and Mourning Rituals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Buddhist death rituals vary significantly between traditions but all reflect the teachings on impermanence (anicca) and kamma. In Theravada communities, monks chant suttas at the bedside of the dying, and after death merits are transferred to the deceased. The body may be cremated (reflecting the teaching that the body is impermanent).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In Tibetan Buddhism, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Tibetan Buddhist text (known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead) that guides the consciousness of the deceased through the stages between death and rebirth.">Tibetan Book of the Dead</dfn> (Bardo Thodol) is read aloud to the recently deceased or dying person. It describes the stages of consciousness experienced between death and rebirth (the bardo states) and instructs the consciousness to recognise the clear light of reality and achieve liberation. This is unique to Tibetan Vajrayana Buddhism.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Festivals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The most important festival in Theravada Buddhism, celebrating the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death (parinibbana) on the full moon of the month of Vesak.">Wesak</dfn> (Vesak) is celebrated on the full moon in May/June and commemorates the Buddha&rsquo;s birth, enlightenment and parinibbana (passing into nibbana). It is the most widely observed festival across Theravada Buddhism.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four named Buddhist festivals, their tradition (Theravada, Mahayana or Tibetan) and one thing each celebrates.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key festivals: <strong>Wesak</strong> (Theravada &mdash; Buddha&rsquo;s birth/enlightenment/death); <strong>Vassa</strong>/Rain Retreat (Theravada &mdash; three-month monastic retreat with reference to Anapanasati Sutta); <strong>Kathina</strong> (Theravada &mdash; offering of robes to monks after Vassa); <strong>Uposatha</strong> days (Theravada &mdash; fortnightly days of precept-keeping and lay devotion); <strong>Hanamatsuri</strong> (Japanese/Mahayana &mdash; celebrates the Buddha&rsquo;s birth in April); <strong>Obon</strong> (Japanese &mdash; festival honouring ancestors); <strong>Parinibbana Day</strong> (Mahayana &mdash; commemorates the Buddha&rsquo;s death); <strong>Lama Tsong Khapa Day</strong> (Tibetan &mdash; commemorates the 15th century founder of the Gelug school).</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Vassa (the Rain Retreat) is a three-month period during the monsoon season when monks remain in their monasteries, intensifying practice. The Anapanasati Sutta (on mindfulness of breathing) is associated with meditation practice during Vassa. At the end of Vassa, the Kathina ceremony takes place: laity offer robes and supplies to monks, generating merit and strengthening the lay-monastic relationship. Uposatha days fall on new and full moon days: lay Buddhists take eight precepts (more than the usual five) and visit the monastery for teaching and meditation. These three practices together show how the monastic calendar structures the religious life of Theravada communities.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Buddhist places of worship include the vihara (Theravada), gompa (Tibetan) and Triratna centres; the Kimsila Sutta describes correct conduct in them. Buddharupas are focal points for devotion, not idols &mdash; the Vakkali Sutta explains their spiritual purpose.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Puja in vihara and home involves offerings of flowers, incense and prostrations; the Mangala Sutta is chanted for blessings. Death rituals vary: Theravada focuses on merit-transfer; Tibetan Buddhism uses the Tibetan Book of the Dead to guide the dying consciousness.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Key festivals: Wesak (Theravada), Hanamatsuri and Obon (Japanese), Parinibbana Day (Mahayana), Lama Tsong Khapa Day (Tibetan), Vassa and Kathina (Theravada monastic calendar). All reflect the Buddha&rsquo;s life and teachings.</li>
</ul>
