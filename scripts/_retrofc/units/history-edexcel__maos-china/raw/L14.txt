--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country to be Re-educated</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1949 around 80% of Chinese adults could not read. Most villages had no clinic; trained doctors clustered in the cities; life expectancy was c.36 years. Buddhism, Daoism, Christianity, Islam and ancestor worship shaped daily ritual. Mao&rsquo;s regime set out to rebuild all three pillars of everyday life &mdash; education, health and culture &mdash; from above. The means were the propaganda machine, the work-unit (danwei) supervision system and, after 1966, the violence of the Red Guards. The results were genuinely transformative in some areas (literacy, rural healthcare) and devastating in others (the closure of universities, the destruction of religious sites).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: roughly what was Chinese adult literacy in 1949 and in 1976? In what year was Pinyin introduced?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Adult literacy rose from around 20% in 1949 to around 70% by 1976. <dfn class="term" data-def="The official system, introduced in 1958, for writing Mandarin Chinese in the Roman alphabet; combined with simplified characters to accelerate mass literacy.">Pinyin</dfn> was introduced in 1958, alongside simplified characters &mdash; both designed to accelerate mass literacy among peasants and workers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Education before 1966</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From 1949 the CCP launched literacy campaigns aimed at adults: village classes ran in winter when farm work was light, and worker classes ran in factories. The 1958 introduction of <dfn class="term" data-def="A reduced-stroke version of Chinese characters, introduced from 1956 and standardised in 1964, designed to make reading and writing easier than the traditional script.">simplified characters</dfn> reduced the strokes needed to write thousands of common words; Pinyin gave a Roman-letter system for teaching pronunciation and indexing dictionaries. Together they cut the time needed to reach functional literacy. Soviet aid helped build new universities and technical institutes during the First Five-Year Plan; gaokao, the national university entrance examination, ran competitively until 1966.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Education was framed as service to the revolution. Schoolchildren learned arithmetic alongside passages of Mao&rsquo;s thought; agricultural and industrial work experience was built into the curriculum from primary school. By 1965, primary enrolment had reached around 80% of children of school age &mdash; a sharp gain on the pre-1949 figure of c.20%. Women&rsquo;s and girls&rsquo; literacy rose particularly rapidly because the campaigns explicitly targeted them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Collapse of Education after 1966</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Cultural Revolution wrecked formal education. From May 1966, schools and universities across China closed. The gaokao was abolished and would not return until 1977. Teachers were denounced as &lsquo;stinking ninth&rsquo; (the bottom of a hierarchy of class enemies); some were beaten to death by their own pupils. Universities reopened from 1970 with admission by class background and political reliability rather than examination, and the curriculum was reduced to revolutionary slogans plus practical work. From 1968 onwards, around 17 million urban &lsquo;sent-down youth&rsquo; were dispatched to the countryside under the <dfn class="term" data-def="Campaign launched in 1968 to send urban youth (xiaxiang, &lsquo;up to the mountains, down to the villages&rsquo;) to live and work with peasants; about 17 million were sent out by the early 1970s.">xiaxiang</dfn> campaign, in theory to learn from peasants, in practice to disperse the Red Guards Mao no longer needed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A common student misconception is that all education collapsed. Primary literacy classes in villages mostly continued, run by local cadres rather than universities; barefoot doctors&rsquo; basic training expanded; and propaganda materials reached far more peasants than Republican-era schoolbooks ever had. The collapse was concentrated at secondary and higher education, where a generation lost ten years of formal study. Strong answers separate the gains for primary literacy from the catastrophe for higher education.</p>

But healthcare was uneven: most trained doctors had been forced from cities to villages against their will after 1965, hospitals remained concentrated in urban centres, and the regime suppressed reporting of the 1959&ndash;62 famine death rates. <h2 data-narration-id="n11">Healthcare and the Barefoot Doctors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Pre-1949 China had perhaps 40,000 trained doctors for c.500 million people, mostly clustered in coastal cities. Mao attacked this as bourgeois medicine. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Roughly one million paramedical workers, formalised in 1965, given short medical training and sent to rural communes; they delivered basic care, vaccinations and family planning across the Chinese countryside.">barefoot doctors</dfn> programme, formalised in 1965, trained around one million paramedical workers in three to six months and dispatched them to communes. They carried a basic kit of medicines, taught hygiene, delivered vaccinations and ran family-planning clinics. By 1976 most communes had at least one barefoot doctor; rural infant mortality fell sharply; and life expectancy rose from c.36 in 1949 to c.66 by 1976.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Healthcare was not uniformly transformed. Trained doctors sent down to villages from 1965 often resented the move; cities lost specialist services; the worst famine years were not addressed by additional medical capacity but by the suppression of death reporting. Zhou Enlai privately pushed back against some of the most disruptive Cultural Revolution attacks on hospitals, but he could not prevent the chaos in major cities. Even so, by Western metrics the Mao era&rsquo;s rural healthcare gains are widely acknowledged, including by sympathetic historians like Mobo Gao.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Attacks on Traditional Culture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">From May 1966 the regime declared war on the &lsquo;four olds&rsquo; &mdash; old customs, old culture, old habits, old ideas. Red Guards smashed Buddhist temples, Daoist shrines and Christian churches; Confucius&rsquo;s tomb at Qufu was desecrated by Red Guards in November 1966 in a deliberate act of symbolic destruction. Mosques in the Hui Muslim regions and Tibetan Buddhist monasteries were attacked particularly violently. After the 1959 Tibetan uprising and the Dalai Lama&rsquo;s flight to India, monasteries had already been closed in large numbers; the Cultural Revolution finished the job, with most of Tibet&rsquo;s c.6,000 monasteries reportedly destroyed or damaged. Christianity, Islam and Buddhism were treated as foreign or feudal; clergy were paraded in struggle sessions, beaten and imprisoned; family ancestor altars were burned by their own owners under pressure from neighbourhood committees.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A misconception is that all religion was permanently extinguished. Folk and ancestor practices survived underground &mdash; in many villages the smashed altar was rebuilt as soon as the Red Guards left. After Mao&rsquo;s death in 1976, enforcement relaxed; Deng Xiaoping&rsquo;s reforms allowed limited reopening of temples and churches under state supervision. But the cultural damage of 1966&ndash;76 was irreversible: many ancient buildings, manuscripts, statues and artworks were destroyed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">As the wife of Mao and a former Shanghai actress, Jiang Qing took charge of culture during the Cultural Revolution. She banned almost all classical Chinese opera, foreign films and traditional novels, replacing them with eight &lsquo;model operas&rsquo; (yangbanxi) such as <em>Taking Tiger Mountain by Strategy</em> and <em>The Red Detachment of Women</em>. These operas were performed by state troupes, broadcast on radio, filmed and shown in every cinema; for nearly a decade they were almost the only live theatre or cinema available. Top-band answers name them as a deliberate cultural project, not just a destructive impulse: Jiang Qing was building a new revolutionary culture to replace what was being smashed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four cultural changes under Mao that were genuine reforms, then list three areas of major loss or destruction.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Genuine reforms: literacy from c.20% to c.70%, Pinyin (1958) and simplified characters, c.1 million barefoot doctors (1965+), life expectancy from c.36 to c.66. Major losses: schools and universities closed 1966&ndash;77 with gaokao abolished; Confucius&rsquo;s tomb desecrated 1966; most of Tibet&rsquo;s c.6,000 monasteries destroyed or damaged; almost all classical opera replaced by Jiang Qing&rsquo;s eight model operas.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Education: literacy rose from c.20% (1949) to c.70% (1976) using Pinyin and simplified characters, but the Cultural Revolution closed schools and universities and abolished the gaokao from 1966 to 1977.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Health: the c.1 million barefoot doctors (formalised 1965) raised rural healthcare and lifted life expectancy from c.36 to c.66, though urban specialist care declined and famine death rates were suppressed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Culture: the &lsquo;four olds&rsquo; campaign smashed temples, churches and monasteries (Confucius&rsquo;s tomb 1966; Tibet from 1959); Jiang Qing&rsquo;s eight model operas replaced almost all classical theatre and film for nearly a decade.</li>
</ul>
