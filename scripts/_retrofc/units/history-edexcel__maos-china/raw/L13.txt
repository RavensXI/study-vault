--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Society Built on Patriarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before 1949 Chinese family life was shaped by Confucian patriarchy. Marriage was arranged by parents, often when the bride was a child; bride prices were paid; concubinage was legal in wealthy households; and female infanticide remained common in poor villages. Women&rsquo;s feet had been bound from c.1100 onwards in elite families, breaking the bones of girls aged 4&ndash;7 to produce the prized &lsquo;three-inch lotus&rsquo; foot &mdash; an excruciating practice that left women unable to walk far or work the fields. Although <dfn class="term" data-def="The painful Chinese practice, common from c.1100 until the early twentieth century, of breaking and tightly binding the feet of young girls to produce small &lsquo;lotus&rsquo; feet considered beautiful in elite families.">foot binding</dfn> had been in long decline since the late Qing reforms of the 1900s and was banned by the Republican government in 1912, the practice persisted in remote villages into the 1940s.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in what month and year was the Marriage Law passed, and what three things did it make illegal?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Marriage Law of 1 May 1950 made arranged marriage, bride price (the &lsquo;sale&rsquo; of brides) and concubinage illegal. It established free choice in marriage, equal rights between husband and wife, and the right to divorce on either side &mdash; a revolutionary legal change that ran ahead of Chinese rural attitudes by decades.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Marriage Law of 1950</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The 1950 <dfn class="term" data-def="Communist law of 1 May 1950 establishing free-choice marriage, equal rights between spouses, the right to divorce, and bans on bride price, concubinage and arranged child marriage.">Marriage Law</dfn> was one of the first major laws of the new People&rsquo;s Republic. Drafted with input from Communist women activists such as Deng Yingchao (Zhou Enlai&rsquo;s wife), it set the minimum marriage age at 20 for men and 18 for women, banned the buying and selling of brides, and gave both spouses equal rights in property and divorce. Women lined up at courthouses across China in 1950 and 1951 to file for divorce; in 1953 alone the courts handled around 1.1 million divorce cases. For young women trapped in childhood marriages, the law was liberation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A common student misconception is that the Marriage Law was universally welcomed. In fact rural backlash was fierce. Husbands, mothers-in-law and clan elders saw the law as an attack on the family economy &mdash; a divorced wife could leave with property and remarry. Reports of beatings, suicides and murders of young women who tried to use the law&rsquo;s provisions appeared in CCP investigations through the early 1950s. Some local cadres, themselves rural patriarchs, refused to enforce it. The 1953 enforcement campaign, &lsquo;Marriage Law Implementation Month&rsquo;, was launched precisely because the law was being widely ignored.</p>

The law was on paper revolutionary; in practice its impact was uneven, depending on whether local cadres enforced it. <h2 data-narration-id="n8">Foot Binding Banned</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Foot binding had been outlawed by the Republican government in 1912 but the practice continued in pockets of rural China through the 1940s. The CCP launched fresh campaigns in the early 1950s, sending women cadres into villages to inspect girls&rsquo; feet and prosecute parents who had bound them. By the late 1950s the practice was effectively extinct. Younger women regained the ability to walk normally and work in the fields and factories &mdash; a precondition of the workforce mobilisation that followed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Communes Disrupt the Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">If the Marriage Law reshaped marriage, the communes of 1958 reshaped the family itself. Around 26,000 people&rsquo;s communes absorbed roughly 99% of rural households. Family cooking was banned in favour of communal mess halls (about 3.4 million across China by mid-1959); childcare was collective; private plots were abolished. The CCP slogan was &lsquo;production over kinship&rsquo;. Women were enlisted en masse into the workforce, while older women ran nurseries and kitchens. For working women, this meant relief from the &lsquo;double burden&rsquo; of paid labour plus domestic chores &mdash; in theory.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In practice the communes&rsquo; disruption of family life was deeply unpopular. The mess halls were poorly run, food was wasted, and during the 1959&ndash;62 famine they became sites of starvation. After the famine, Liu Shaoqi&rsquo;s recovery measures (1962&ndash;65) quietly returned cooking to the household and re-allowed small private plots. The commune as an organisation survived until 1983, but the radical attack on the household economy did not last.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Women in the Workforce</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Mao&rsquo;s most quoted line on women is &lsquo;women hold up half the sky&rsquo;. Female labour-force participation rose dramatically: by 1976 around 90% of urban women of working age had paid work, an extraordinary figure by world standards. Propaganda celebrated <dfn class="term" data-def="Cultural Revolution propaganda figures &mdash; young women working alongside men in heavy labour such as steelworks, oilfields and railway construction; symbolised gender equality through identical work.">iron girls</dfn> &mdash; teenage girls in tractor brigades, oil-field work crews and steel mills, photographed driving heavy machinery and lifting sacks of grain. Jiang Qing, Mao&rsquo;s fourth wife and the only woman in the senior leadership, became a powerful figure during the Cultural Revolution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A second misconception is that Mao gave women equality. The reality was more limited. Women&rsquo;s pay was lower (commonly counted as 7 work points to a man&rsquo;s 10); housework was unchanged, leaving women with the &lsquo;double burden&rsquo;; the Politburo remained almost entirely male. Soong Ching-ling (widow of Sun Yat-sen) and Deng Yingchao were senior figures, but Jiang Qing&rsquo;s power came from her marriage to Mao, not promotion through the party. After the Gang of Four was arrested in 1976, the iron-girl propaganda was quietly retired and gender norms in the countryside reasserted themselves.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">Western revisionist historians like Frank Dik&ouml;tter (<em>The Tragedy of Liberation</em>) emphasise the violence of the Marriage Law&rsquo;s rural enforcement and the gulf between Mao&rsquo;s rhetoric and women&rsquo;s pay packets. Sympathetic historians like Mobo Gao argue that the Mao era genuinely transformed women&rsquo;s lives &mdash; literacy, paid work, the end of foot binding, the right to divorce &mdash; in ways the post-1978 reform era did not match. Both can be tested against the evidence. Strong answers note specific gains (legal divorce, end of foot binding, mass literacy, near-universal urban employment) and specific limits (rural backlash, pay gap, double burden, almost no women in the senior leadership).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four genuine gains for women under Mao, then add three limits to those gains.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Genuine gains: end of foot binding, legal right to divorce (1950), mass literacy, near-universal urban employment by 1976. Limits: rural backlash against the Marriage Law, lower female pay (often 7 work points to a man&rsquo;s 10), unchanged housework, almost no women in the Politburo. Mao&rsquo;s slogan was &lsquo;women hold up half the sky&rsquo;; the reality was uneven.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The 1950 Marriage Law banned arranged marriage, bride price and concubinage; gave women the right to divorce; sparked rural backlash and 1.1 million divorce cases by 1953.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Foot binding, in long decline since 1912, was effectively ended by 1950s campaigns &mdash; releasing women into the workforce.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The 1958 communes disrupted family life through mess halls and collective childcare, then quietly retreated after the 1959&ndash;62 famine; women&rsquo;s gains in education and employment were real but came with the &lsquo;double burden&rsquo; and a continuing pay gap.</li>
</ul>
