--- DESCRIPTION ---
Soviet engineers, steel mills and the building of a Chinese industrial base.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Mao Wanted Industry Fast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the People&rsquo;s Republic was founded in 1949, China was overwhelmingly agricultural. Industrial output was a fraction of the United States or the Soviet Union, and most modern factories had been wrecked by the Sino-Japanese War or the Civil War. The Korean War (1950&ndash;53) made the gap impossible to ignore: Chinese soldiers fought a modern American army with First World War-era equipment. Mao concluded that the new state had to industrialise quickly &mdash; both to defend itself and to demonstrate that communism could deliver. The result was the <dfn class="term" data-def="China&rsquo;s 1953–57 economic plan, modelled on Soviet practice and built around heavy industry, Soviet aid and centrally set production targets.">First Five-Year Plan</dfn>, launched in 1953 and inspired directly by Stalin&rsquo;s Soviet model.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many Soviet 'gift' projects were promised, and roughly how many advisers came to China?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Soviet Union supplied around 156 &lsquo;gift&rsquo; projects (full factory plans and equipment) and roughly 10,000 advisers to China during the First Five-Year Plan. The aid was the backbone of Chinese industrialisation between 1953 and 1957.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Reasons for the Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Several reasons converged. First, defence: the Korean War and the threat of US air power made heavy industry urgent. Second, ideology: the Soviet model treated heavy industry &mdash; steel, coal, machine tools, electricity &mdash; as the proof and engine of socialism. Third, alliance: the 1950 Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship gave China access to Soviet engineers, blueprints and credit on favourable terms. Fourth, prestige: catching up with the imperial powers had been a Chinese political obsession since 1842, and the CCP wanted to deliver where the Qing and the GMD had failed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The plan was substantially designed by Soviet planners and run on the Chinese side by Premier Zhou Enlai, finance specialist Chen Yun and Liu Shaoqi. Mao approved the plan but did not author it &mdash; a fact that would matter when he later launched the Great Leap Forward partly as a reaction against the Soviet model.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Targets and Soviet Aid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Plan set very specific targets. By 1957, steel output was to rise from c.1.3 million tonnes to c.4.1 million tonnes; coal from c.66 million to c.113 million tonnes; electricity generation roughly to double. Around 58% of state investment went into heavy industry; consumer goods and agriculture were squeezed to pay for it. The Soviet contribution arrived in the form of <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet-funded turnkey factory packages provided to China in the 1950s, including blueprints, machinery and engineers; 156 were promised under the First Five-Year Plan.">gift projects</dfn> &mdash; full plans for steel mills (Anshan in Manchuria, Wuhan, Baotou), machine-tool factories, power stations and lorry plants. Soviet engineers and managers worked alongside Chinese counterparts; thousands of Chinese students were trained in Russia.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">It is tempting to credit (or blame) Mao for everything that happened in 1950s China, but the First Five-Year Plan was substantially designed by Soviet planners and run by Liu Shaoqi, Zhou Enlai and Chen Yun. Mao approved it and presided over it &mdash; but he chafed at depending on the Russians and at the bureaucratic, technical character of Soviet planning. Frank Dik&ouml;tter argues that the Great Leap Forward of 1958 was partly Mao&rsquo;s deliberate move away from this Soviet-style technocracy. Strong answers separate Mao&rsquo;s rhetorical leadership from the actual planners and stress that Mao was uncomfortable in the role of cautious manager.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Successes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Headline industrial output grew by around 118% over the five years &mdash; far faster than any previous Chinese government had achieved. New industrial cities took shape: Anshan became the country&rsquo;s steel capital, Wuhan a heavy-engineering hub, Changchun the centre of the new lorry industry. The urban industrial workforce expanded from c.6 million in 1952 to c.10 million by 1957. The state-run <dfn class="term" data-def="The basic Communist-era unit of urban employment in China; it issued housing, ration coupons and welfare to its workers as well as wages.">danwei</dfn> system gave urban workers housing, healthcare, ration coupons and pensions in return for political loyalty. For the workers inside the system, life genuinely improved.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Plan also delivered infrastructure that would matter for decades: rail extensions into the south-west, a doubling of installed electrical capacity, and the country&rsquo;s first wave of indigenous machine-tool production. Most headline industrial targets were met or exceeded by the end of 1957.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Failures and Strains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The successes came at real cost. Agriculture was deliberately squeezed to feed the cities and pay for imported equipment, contributing to rural discontent that fed Mao&rsquo;s acceleration of collectivisation in 1955&ndash;56. Consumer goods were scarce; clothing, household items and most food beyond the basic ration were rare. Quality control was uneven &mdash; Soviet-built plants worked, but local imitations frequently produced low-grade output. Bureaucratic planning bred its own waste: factory managers padded reports to please planners.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">There was a deeper political strain. Dependence on the USSR sat uncomfortably with Mao&rsquo;s revolutionary self-image. Stalin&rsquo;s death in March 1953 and Khrushchev&rsquo;s 1956 &lsquo;Secret Speech&rsquo; attacking Stalin&rsquo;s personality cult unsettled the Sino-Soviet relationship. Soviet aid was generous in scale but was treated by Beijing as a debt to be repaid in goods, not a free gift. Mao began to look for a Chinese alternative model &mdash; one that relied on the masses rather than on Soviet engineers.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">CCP-orthodox accounts treat the First Five-Year Plan as a clear success and the high point of co-operation with the USSR. Sympathetic historians like Mobo Gao point to the lasting infrastructure base it built &mdash; rail, electricity, steel cities &mdash; which underpinned later Chinese growth. Revisionist historians (Frank Dik&ouml;tter, Jonathan Spence) accept the headline output figures but stress the squeeze on agriculture, the inflated reporting from below, and the political tensions with Moscow. Top-band answers acknowledge both: that the Plan delivered an industrial base, and that it set up the very conflicts &mdash; over peasants, planning style and Soviet aid &mdash; that Mao would resolve by smashing the model in 1958.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three weaknesses of the First Five-Year Plan that helped push Mao towards the Great Leap.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Despite hitting most industrial targets, the First Five-Year Plan squeezed agriculture, kept consumer goods scarce, and made China uncomfortably dependent on c.10,000 Soviet advisers. By 1957 these strains had pushed Mao to abandon the model and launch the Great Leap Forward.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The First Five-Year Plan (1953&ndash;57) was modelled on Soviet practice, designed largely by Soviet planners, and run on the Chinese side by Zhou Enlai, Chen Yun and Liu Shaoqi.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Soviet aid &mdash; c.156 &lsquo;gift&rsquo; projects and c.10,000 advisers &mdash; built new industrial cities (Anshan, Wuhan, Baotou) and roughly doubled industrial output, with most headline targets met by 1957.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Plan squeezed agriculture and consumer goods, depended uncomfortably on the USSR, and pushed Mao towards the more radical, mass-mobilising Great Leap Forward of 1958.</li>
</ul>
