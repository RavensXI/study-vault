--- DESCRIPTION ---
From smashing the landlords to forcing the peasants into ever-larger collective farms.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Land Came First</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Mao Zedong proclaimed the People&rsquo;s Republic in October 1949, roughly 80% of Chinese people lived in the countryside. The CCP had won the Civil War partly by promising the peasants land. The 1950 <dfn class="term" data-def="The June 1950 law that confiscated land from landlords and redistributed it to poor peasants, ending the system of rural landlordism in China.">Agrarian Reform Law</dfn> turned that promise into policy &mdash; but the peasants who received land in 1950 only owned it for about four to six years. By 1956 nearly all rural land had been pooled into cooperatives, and by 1958 it would be absorbed into the people&rsquo;s communes. Land reform was therefore a single, staged process from private ownership to full collectivisation, not a one-off gift.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four classes the 1950 law placed every household into, and what each was supposed to get or lose.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1950 Agrarian Reform Law sorted every rural household into four classes &mdash; landlords, rich peasants, middle peasants and poor peasants. Landlords lost everything; rich peasants kept what they farmed themselves; middle peasants were left alone; poor peasants received the redistributed land.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The 1950 Agrarian Reform Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The law was drafted by Liu Shaoqi and passed on 30 June 1950. It abolished &lsquo;feudal exploitation&rsquo; and ordered the confiscation of all land owned by landlords. The land, plus farm tools and surplus grain, was redistributed to landless and poor peasants. Around 300 million peasants received roughly 47 million hectares of land in the four years that followed. Each household was assigned a class label that would follow them &mdash; and their children &mdash; for the rest of the Mao era.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The classification process was deliberately public and brutal. CCP work teams arrived in each village and called &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="Mass meetings, held in villages from 1950, in which peasants publicly denounced their former landlords for cruelty and exploitation.">speak bitterness</dfn>&rsquo; meetings, in which peasants were urged to denounce their former landlords for cruelty and exploitation. Many denunciations led to beatings and execution. Estimates of landlord deaths between 1950 and 1952 range from 1 to 2 million &mdash; some by formal trial, many by mob violence with cadre approval. Mao explicitly defended the bloodshed: &lsquo;A revolution is not a dinner party.&rsquo; The class war was meant to bind the peasants to the CCP through shared guilt as well as shared gain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Mutual Aid Teams, 1951&ndash;52</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Even before redistribution finished, the CCP began encouraging peasants to pool their work. Five to ten households would form a <dfn class="term" data-def="Small voluntary groupings of five to ten peasant households who shared labour, draught animals and tools at peak times in the farming year.">Mutual Aid Team</dfn>, sharing labour, draught animals and tools at busy times of the year. Land and animals remained privately owned. By the end of 1952 around 40% of peasant households were in some form of mutual aid arrangement. The Mutual Aid Teams were genuinely popular &mdash; small enough to feel familiar, and an obvious efficiency gain on tiny family plots.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Lower-Stage and Higher-Stage Cooperatives, 1953&ndash;56</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Lower-stage cooperatives followed in 1953&ndash;54. Around 30 to 50 households pooled land into a single farm; peasants kept formal ownership and were paid both for the labour they did and for the land they brought in. Higher-stage cooperatives, modelled on Soviet collective farms, came next. They contained 200 to 300 households, and the land itself became the collective property of the cooperative &mdash; private ownership was effectively abolished. Payment was now strictly by labour. By the end of 1956, around 96% of peasant households had been pushed into higher-stage cooperatives.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Students often assume the 1950 redistribution gave peasants permanent ownership. The reality was sharper &mdash; private ownership lasted at most four to six years before being absorbed by the higher-stage cooperatives of 1955&ndash;56, then by the people&rsquo;s communes of 1958. The point of the redistribution was political, not economic: it broke the rural elite and created peasant gratitude to the CCP. Top-band answers spell out this staged sequence rather than presenting land reform as a single event.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The &lsquo;Socialist High Tide&rsquo;, 1955</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Mao&rsquo;s personal acceleration of cooperation came in his July 1955 speech &lsquo;On the Question of Agricultural Cooperation&rsquo;, often called the &lsquo;Socialist High Tide&rsquo; speech. He attacked cautious officials &mdash; including Deng Zihui, head of the Rural Work Department &mdash; who wanted a slower, voluntary timetable. Mao argued the masses were ready and that timid leadership was the obstacle. Liu Shaoqi had until then sided with the slower approach. Once Mao spoke, the central plan was rewritten: higher-stage cooperatives that had been targeted for 1960 were to be universal by 1957.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The shift from voluntary to enforced cooperation was driven by several factors. Mao believed cooperatives would raise grain output to feed industry under the First Five-Year Plan. He also feared a return of rich-peasant power if private farming was allowed to consolidate. The earlier Gao Gang affair (1953&ndash;54), in which a senior planner was purged, had reinforced Mao&rsquo;s authority over rural policy. By December 1956 the &lsquo;High Tide&rsquo; was complete &mdash; ahead of the official schedule and well beyond what most peasants had been asked to accept.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Western historians often compare Chinese collectivisation with Stalin&rsquo;s in the USSR. Frank Dik&ouml;tter (<em>The Tragedy of Liberation</em>) and Jonathan Spence (<em>The Search for Modern China</em>) both stress that the Chinese version was at first more cautious &mdash; staged through Mutual Aid Teams and lower-stage cooperatives &mdash; before Mao&rsquo;s 1955 speech accelerated it. CCP-orthodox accounts emphasise voluntary peasant enthusiasm; the revisionist Anglophone view stresses cadre quotas and pressure. Mobo Gao&rsquo;s <em>The Battle for China&rsquo;s Past</em> notes that early cooperation did improve irrigation and small-scale infrastructure. Top-band answers acknowledge that the early stages were genuinely popular and that the 1955&ndash;56 acceleration was where the coercion arrived.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Why It Mattered for What Came Next</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By the end of 1956, China had collective land ownership in all but name. Mao now had a rural structure he believed could be pushed further &mdash; into the people&rsquo;s communes of August 1958 and the Great Leap Forward. Liu Shaoqi&rsquo;s caution about pace had been overruled. Deng Zihui had been sidelined. The intellectual class would be silenced months later in the Anti-Rightist purge. The political space for a vast experiment in agriculture had been cleared &mdash; and the famine that followed in 1959&ndash;62 was rooted in the structures put in place between 1950 and 1956.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the four-stage sequence from 1950 to 1958 in order, with one phrase about each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The sequence was: 1950 redistribution to poor peasants, 1951&ndash;52 voluntary Mutual Aid Teams, 1953&ndash;54 lower-stage cooperatives (private ownership retained), 1955&ndash;56 higher-stage cooperatives (collective ownership). The 1958 communes followed directly from this base.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The 1950 Agrarian Reform Law confiscated landlord land and redistributed it to roughly 300 million peasants, with 1&ndash;2 million landlords killed in the process.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Peasant ownership was a brief stage &mdash; Mutual Aid Teams (1951&ndash;52), lower-stage cooperatives (1953&ndash;54) and higher-stage cooperatives (1955&ndash;56) absorbed the land within six years.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Mao&rsquo;s July 1955 &lsquo;Socialist High Tide&rsquo; speech overruled cautious figures like Liu Shaoqi and Deng Zihui, accelerating collectivisation and clearing the way for the 1958 communes.</li>
</ul>
