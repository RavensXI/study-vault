--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Mao consolidated the new state, allied with Stalin, and intervened in the Korean War.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Civil War to Government</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Winning a civil war is not the same as ruling a country. By October 1949, the CCP had to govern around 540 million people across territory the size of a continent &mdash; collecting taxes, running schools and hospitals, and replacing GMD officials with party-trained cadres. Mao&rsquo;s answer was to fuse the party, the new state and the army into one machine, with himself at its head. This lesson looks at three things: what Mao&rsquo;s ideology actually said, how the new state was organised, and how the Korean War tested both at once.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three elements of Mao's blend of ideology and what each means in one phrase.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mao&rsquo;s ideology blended Marxism-Leninism, the &lsquo;mass line&rsquo; (party policy shaped by listening to peasants) and democratic centralism (debate within the party, total obedience once a decision is made). After 1949, this was filtered through the &lsquo;cult of Mao&rsquo; &mdash; a personality cult that made him the symbol of the entire revolution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Mao&rsquo;s Ideology and Authority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Marxism-Leninism &mdash; the doctrine Mao inherited from Lenin&rsquo;s Russia &mdash; argued that the urban industrial working class would lead the revolution. Mao&rsquo;s twist was to make the peasantry the engine instead, fitting Chinese conditions where over 80 per cent of the population worked the land. He summed this up as the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="Mao's principle that the party should learn from the peasantry, take their scattered ideas, and return them as policy.">mass line</dfn>&rsquo;: the party listens to the masses, takes their scattered ideas, sharpens them into policy, and returns them as orders. In practice the listening was selective; the orders were not. The other half of Mao&rsquo;s framework was <dfn class="term" data-def="Lenin's principle that the party permits internal debate but, once a decision is made, all members must obey it absolutely.">democratic centralism</dfn>: debate inside the party, total obedience once a decision was reached.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Mao&rsquo;s personal dominance was not automatic. He had defeated rivals like Wang Ming and Zhang Guotao at Yan&rsquo;an, and after 1949 he still had to manage senior comrades &mdash; Liu Shaoqi, Zhou Enlai, Lin Biao, Deng Xiaoping &mdash; who held substantial authority of their own. What set Mao apart was his record. He was credited with the survival of the Long March, the Yan&rsquo;an base, and the victory of 1949. From 1950 onwards, propaganda departments ramped up the cult of Mao through portraits, songs, posters and Liu Shaoqi&rsquo;s influential book <em>How to Be a Good Communist</em>, which urged loyalty to the party (and to its chairman) above all.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">It is easy to write about Mao as a sole ruler whose orders were never resisted. The historian Jonathan Spence and the Edexcel Paper 3 examiner reports both warn against this. Liu Shaoqi was the formal President from 1959, Zhou Enlai ran the state machinery as Premier, and Peng Dehuai commanded the army in Korea. Mao&rsquo;s authority over policy decisions was real but never total &mdash; later in this unit, you will see Liu and Deng quietly rolling back Great Leap Forward measures, and Peng Dehuai privately criticising Mao at Lushan in 1959. Strong answers weigh Mao&rsquo;s authority against the agency of these other figures.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Structure of the New State</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Power in the new People&rsquo;s Republic ran through three parallel hierarchies. The CCP under its Chairman, Mao, set policy. The State Council, headed by Premier Zhou Enlai, ran the day-to-day government. The People&rsquo;s Liberation Army, the third pillar, was loyal to the party rather than to the state. Where the three overlapped, the party always came first &mdash; senior army officers were also CCP members, and government ministers held party posts. CCP membership was the entry ticket to influence. From around 4.5 million members at liberation in 1949, the party grew rapidly: by 1957 it had over 12 million members and by 1976 around 35 million.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">At the village level, this meant a CCP <dfn class="term" data-def="A unit of organisation, often based on a workplace, village or office, that managed members' work, housing and political education in the People's Republic.">danwei</dfn> (work unit) or party committee that decided who got food rations, work assignments, schooling, and even permission to marry. Mass party membership and democratic centralism therefore did more than fill bureaucratic posts &mdash; they reached into ordinary life. The party did not simply govern the state; it became the state.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Sino-Soviet Treaty, February 1950</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In December 1949 Mao travelled to Moscow &mdash; his first journey outside China &mdash; for two months of difficult talks with Stalin. The result, signed in February 1950, was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1950 alliance between Mao's China and Stalin's USSR that brought Soviet aid, advisers and the framework for the First Five-Year Plan.">Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Alliance and Mutual Assistance</dfn>. The USSR promised $300 million of credit, sent around 10,000 advisers and engineers across the 1950s, and helped China build the heavy industry the First Five-Year Plan would require. The price was steep: most aid was in the form of loans, the Soviets controlled certain joint ventures, and Mao left Moscow privately resenting Stalin&rsquo;s coolness.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: when was the Sino-Soviet Treaty signed and what three things did it bring China?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Signed in February 1950, the Sino-Soviet Treaty brought China around $300 million in Soviet credit, c.10,000 Soviet advisers and engineers, and the political backing of Stalin&rsquo;s USSR. It anchored the new PRC in the communist bloc and underwrote the First Five-Year Plan.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why China Entered the Korean War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In June 1950, communist North Korea invaded the south. United Nations forces under American command pushed the North Korean army back to the Chinese border by October. Mao&rsquo;s decision to send around 250,000 Chinese soldiers &mdash; officially called the &lsquo;Chinese People&rsquo;s Volunteers&rsquo; under Marshal Peng Dehuai &mdash; was made at meetings of the Politburo in October 1950. Several factors weighed in:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">A US-occupied Korea on China&rsquo;s north-eastern border was a strategic threat &mdash; especially with American forces also in Taiwan and Japan.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Stalin urged intervention but, crucially, made it Mao&rsquo;s choice; this was not simply Soviet orders.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Korean War gave Mao a way to prove the PLA&rsquo;s worth on the world stage and reinforce his claim to leadership of the global communist movement.</li>
</ul>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The domestic effects of the Korean War</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Even though Korea is a foreign war, examiners reward students who can link it back to domestic politics in China. The war fed three things at home. First, propaganda &mdash; the &lsquo;Resist America, Aid Korea&rsquo; campaign rallied patriotism around the new regime and made dissent dangerous. Second, repression &mdash; the simultaneous campaign against &lsquo;counter-revolutionaries&rsquo; (1950&ndash;52) used wartime urgency to justify the execution of an estimated 700,000&ndash;2 million people accused of links to the GMD or to imperialism. Third, debt &mdash; military costs to the USSR became loans Mao would still be repaying in the late 1950s, fuelling his eventual break with Khrushchev. By the armistice in July 1953, China had lost between 180,000 and 400,000 dead but emerged with the prestige of having fought the United States to a stalemate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Mao&rsquo;s authority rested on Marxism-Leninism adapted to peasant China (the &lsquo;mass line&rsquo;), democratic centralism, and a growing personality cult &mdash; but he still had to manage senior figures like Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The new state ran through three pillars &mdash; CCP, government and PLA &mdash; with the party always on top; mass membership reached down to village danwei.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The 1950 Sino-Soviet Treaty anchored the PRC in the communist bloc; Mao&rsquo;s decision to enter the Korean War was his own and used to consolidate the regime at home.</li>
</ul>
