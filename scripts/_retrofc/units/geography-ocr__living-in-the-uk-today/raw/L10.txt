--- DESCRIPTION ---
Renewable and non-renewable energy in the UK, changing supply patterns and sustainable management strategies.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Changing UK Energy Mix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Few parts of the UK economy have changed as fast as energy. In 1990 coal generated 67% of UK electricity; in 2024 it generated effectively zero, with the last coal-fired power station (Ratcliffe-on-Soar) closing in September 2024. <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy from sources that can be replaced naturally and do not run out, such as wind, solar and hydro.">Renewables</dfn> are now the largest single source. Understanding how the UK got here, and where it must go next, is at the heart of UK environmental policy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Two categories define the UK's energy supply. <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy from sources that take millions of years to form and will eventually run out, such as coal, oil and gas.">Non-renewable</dfn> energy includes oil, gas, coal and nuclear (technically finite). Renewables include wind, solar, hydroelectric, biomass and tidal. Each has a role — the question is which mix delivers reliable, affordable and low-carbon electricity at the same time.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">UK Renewable Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Wind</strong> is the UK's renewable powerhouse: around 30% of electricity in 2024. Onshore farms (Whitelee, Black Law) cluster on Scottish and Welsh uplands; offshore farms (Hornsea, Dogger Bank) take advantage of shallow North Sea waters. <strong>Solar</strong> contributes about 5% of UK electricity, growing fast. <strong>Hydroelectric</strong> dams contribute around 2%, mostly in Scotland (Cruachan, Sloy). <strong>Biomass</strong> from converted coal stations (Drax) adds another 5% but is increasingly debated for its sustainability. <strong>Tidal</strong> remains small in scale (the Sihwa-style Swansea Bay Tidal Lagoon is paused), but research at the European Marine Energy Centre in Orkney leads the world.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The benefits are well-known: low carbon emissions, no fuel cost once built, supports UK jobs and supply chains. The challenges are intermittency (wind doesn't blow on demand), upfront cost, grid integration, and visual or ecological impact.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the UK's main renewable sources with rough percentage of electricity for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK electricity in 2024 came from renewables (~50% combined), gas (~30%), nuclear (~14%) and biomass (~5%). Coal effectively reached zero with Ratcliffe-on-Soar's closure in September 2024 — the first G7 country to phase out coal entirely.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">UK Non-Renewable Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Natural gas</strong> remains the UK's most flexible electricity source, supplying around 30% of generation and most domestic heating (about 22 million UK homes use gas central heating). The UK imports much of its gas, primarily from Norway via pipeline. <strong>Nuclear</strong> contributes about 14% of UK electricity; existing stations are ageing, and new builds at Hinkley Point C (Somerset, 3.2 GW) and Sizewell C (Suffolk, 3.2 GW planned) aim to replace them. <strong>Oil</strong> dominates UK transport (petrol, diesel, aviation), although the planned 2035 ban on new combustion-engine cars and rapid EV uptake will shift that.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sustainable Energy Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">UK strategy has three pillars. First, <strong>scaling renewables</strong>: Hornsea Two (1.32 GW) and <dfn class="term" data-def="A network of cables, transformers and substations that carries electricity from generators to homes and businesses.">grid</dfn>-connected Dogger Bank (3.6 GW under construction) will lift offshore wind capacity dramatically. Second, <strong>upgrading the grid</strong>: National Grid is investing tens of billions in HVDC interconnectors and storage to balance intermittent supply. Third, <strong>reducing demand</strong>: better building insulation, heat pumps replacing gas boilers, and the rise of electric vehicles all cut demand for fossil fuels.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Local strategies matter too. Manchester aims for 60% renewable electricity by 2030. Bristol's Zero Carbon plan funds insulation and EV charging. Orkney already produces more renewable electricity than it consumes, exporting to mainland Britain.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">Three tensions shape UK energy debates. <strong>Cost vs climate</strong> — renewable subsidies once raised bills, but new offshore wind contracts now sign at prices below gas. <strong>Local vs national interest</strong> — communities oppose new pylons even as cities demand more renewable electricity. <strong>Energy security vs imports</strong> — the 2022 gas crisis showed dependence on Russian flows even at second hand. Strategy must balance all three.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name three UK renewable sources, two non-renewable sources and one strategy for managing demand.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK net zero by 2050 is a legal target. Hinkley Point C (3.2 GW) and Sizewell C (3.2 GW) replace ageing nuclear stations. Hornsea offshore wind farms have made the UK a global leader in offshore wind. Domestic gas heating remains the largest single source of household emissions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The UK has decarbonised electricity faster than almost any country — but it has barely started on heating, transport and industry. Future success depends on integrating renewable supply, upgrading the grid and reducing demand all at once. Strong exam answers track this transition with named UK projects and statistics, and acknowledge the political and economic tensions that will decide whether net zero by 2050 is met.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">UK electricity is now around 50% renewable, 30% gas, 14% nuclear, with coal effectively at zero following Ratcliffe-on-Soar's 2024 closure.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">UK strategy combines scaling renewables (Hornsea, Dogger Bank), upgrading the grid and reducing demand through insulation, heat pumps and EVs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Net zero by 2050 is the legal target, but heating, transport and industry have barely started decarbonising; the next decade is decisive.</li>
</ul>
