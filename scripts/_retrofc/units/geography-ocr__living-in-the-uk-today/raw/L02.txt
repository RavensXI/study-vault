--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Inside a UK River Basin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.">river basin</dfn> is the entire area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Within it, the river changes character as it flows from highland source to lowland mouth. Near the source, channels are narrow and steep, dominated by erosion. In the middle course, the channel widens and meanders begin to swing across the valley. Near the mouth, the river slows, the load is dropped, and floodplains and deltas can form. Recognising these zones is the key to explaining UK river landforms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Three sets of processes shape every UK river: erosion (vertical near the source, lateral lower down), transport (traction, saltation, suspension and solution) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The dropping of the river's load when its energy decreases.">deposition</dfn> when energy falls. The balance between them shifts along the long profile, and that shift is what produces the family of river landforms.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down which type of erosion (vertical or lateral) dominates each course of a river.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vertical erosion dominates in the upper course, cutting V-shaped valleys; lateral erosion dominates in the middle and lower courses, widening valleys and forming meanders.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">River Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Each section of a river produces characteristic landforms. In the <strong>upper course</strong>, hard bands of rock create <dfn class="term" data-def="A landform where a river plunges over a band of resistant rock into a plunge pool.">waterfalls</dfn>. As the soft rock under the lip is undercut, the lip retreats upstream and a steep-sided gorge is left behind. High Force on the River Tees is a classic UK example, where the Tees plunges over the Whin Sill of resistant dolerite.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In the <strong>middle course</strong>, lateral erosion drives the formation of <dfn class="term" data-def="A bend in a river caused by erosion on the outer bank and deposition on the inner bank.">meanders</dfn>. The water flows fastest on the outside of the bend, eroding a steep cliff (river cliff). On the inside the water slows, depositing a curved beach of sediment called a slip-off slope. Over time the meanders migrate and exaggerate. When two bends grow until they touch, the river cuts a new straight path and the abandoned loop becomes an <dfn class="term" data-def="A horseshoe-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off from the main river.">ox-bow lake</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the <strong>lower course</strong>, deposition dominates. <strong>Floodplains</strong> form when overflow water spreads silt across the valley floor. <strong>Levees</strong> are natural raised banks built from coarser sediment dropped first as a river breaks its banks; finer silt is carried further across the floodplain. Together these create the broad, fertile lowlands seen along rivers like the Trent and the Severn.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">UK Case Study: The River Tees Basin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Tees rises on Cross Fell in the northern Pennines and flows about 137 km eastwards to the North Sea at Middlesbrough. It crosses several geological zones, which is why so many landforms are squeezed into a relatively short distance. In the upper course, the resistant Whin Sill produces High Force, the largest waterfall in England by volume. Freeze-thaw weathering on Cross Fell loosens material, which is then transported as bedload through the upper Tees in winter floods.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the middle course around Barnard Castle, the Tees swings across a softer landscape and shows classic meanders. Lateral erosion is well advanced; oxbows can be seen on aerial photographs near Yarm. By the lower course, the river crosses the broad Tees Lowlands. Levees border the channel and floodplain silt has built up over centuries, supporting market gardening between Stockton and Middlesbrough.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Human activity has reshaped the Tees over the last two centuries. Reservoirs at Cow Green and Selset hold back water for industrial supply, reducing flood peaks downstream but also trapping sediment. The Tees Barrage at Stockton, opened in 1995, controls tidal flow and creates a permanent freshwater stretch used for water sports and regeneration. Past straightening of meanders for shipping has accelerated flow, increasing flood risk further downstream — an outcome that managers are now reversing through new floodplain reconnections at Saltholme.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The Tees rises in one of the wettest parts of England, where annual rainfall often exceeds 2000mm. Combined with the impermeable Whin Sill rock, this produces flashy upland flow with very high peak discharges. Without that rainfall the geology alone could not have produced High Force at the rate it now retreats. Geology sets the stage; climate provides the energy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, sketch the long profile of the Tees and label one named landform from each course.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Tees shows the full UK river story in one basin: High Force in the upper course (Whin Sill geology), meanders near Yarm in the middle course, and a managed lowland floodplain at Middlesbrough. Reservoirs and the Tees Barrage are the key human controls.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">River landforms record the interaction of geology, climate and human activity at every scale. Strong exam answers always trace this chain: a rock band makes a waterfall possible, but rainfall and erosion make it actually retreat; a meander forms naturally, but flood-control schemes change how fast it migrates. Treat your case study as evidence to be quoted, not background scenery.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">River basins show a clear long profile: vertical erosion dominates the upper course, lateral erosion the middle, and deposition the lower course.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Waterfalls and gorges, meanders and ox-bow lakes, floodplains and levees are the diagnostic landforms of each section.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Tees is a strong UK case study: Whin Sill geology produces High Force, meanders near Yarm illustrate lateral erosion, and reservoirs plus the Tees Barrage show how humans modify the system.</li>
</ul>
