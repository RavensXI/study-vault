--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Unique Climate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For its latitude (50°-60°N), the UK has a remarkably mild and wet climate. The reason is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A warm ocean current that brings heat from the Gulf of Mexico to the seas around the UK.">North Atlantic Drift</dfn>, which carries warm water from the tropics across the Atlantic. Without it, winter temperatures in the UK would resemble those of similar latitudes in Canada or Russia. The UK also sits in the path of a procession of <dfn class="term" data-def="A large body of air with similar temperature and humidity throughout, named after the region it forms in.">air masses</dfn> that meet over the British Isles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Five main air masses dominate UK weather. <strong>Tropical maritime</strong> from the south-west brings warm, wet weather. <strong>Tropical continental</strong> from the south-east brings hot, dry summer heatwaves. <strong>Polar maritime</strong> from the north-west brings cool, showery weather. <strong>Polar continental</strong> from the east brings cold, dry winter conditions. <strong>Arctic maritime</strong> from the north brings sharp cold and snow. The mix of air masses is why the UK can experience four seasons in a single day — a fact familiar to every UK schoolchild.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A third influence is <dfn class="term" data-def="How distance from the sea affects the temperature of an inland location.">continentality</dfn>. The further inland, the greater the seasonal temperature range. Coastal Cornwall is mild year-round (warmest in winter, coolest in summer); inland regions like the East Midlands have hotter summers and colder winters.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the five UK air masses with one weather effect for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK weather is shaped by five air masses: tropical maritime (warm and wet), tropical continental (hot heatwaves), polar maritime (cool showers), polar continental (cold dry winters) and arctic maritime (snow and sharp cold). The North Atlantic Drift keeps the UK milder than its latitude suggests.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Extreme Weather Conditions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Air masses are also the cause of UK extreme weather. <strong>Heatwaves</strong> like July 2022 (when temperatures hit 40.3°C at Coningsby for the first time) result from blocking high pressure pulling tropical continental air over the country. <strong>Cold spells</strong> like the ‘Beast from the East’ in 2018 brought polar continental air from Siberia, dropping temperatures to -14°C in parts of England. <strong>Storms</strong> like Storm Eunice (February 2022) form when polar and tropical air clash over the Atlantic, intensifying low pressure systems that race across the British Isles. Eunice produced gusts of 122 mph at the Isle of Wight — the strongest recorded in southern England.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Climate change is making extreme weather more frequent. The UK has had its 10 hottest years on record since 2002. Heavy rainfall events have intensified by about 4% per decade since 1961, increasing flood risk. Met Office attribution studies suggest the 2022 heatwave was made roughly 10 times more likely by climate change.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">UK Case Study: The 2015-16 Cumbria Floods (Storm Desmond)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In December 2015, Storm Desmond brought a record-breaking 341.4 mm of rain to Honister Pass in 24 hours — more than the average monthly rainfall for the whole of December. The cause was a stalled atmospheric river: a long, narrow band of warm tropical maritime air loaded with moisture, dumping rain onto the Cumbrian fells. The saturated ground could absorb no more, so rivers like the Eden and Lune rose rapidly. Carlisle, Keswick and Cockermouth were among the worst-hit settlements.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Effects on people:</strong> 5,200 homes were flooded; 43,000 properties lost power; one person died. Schools, businesses and Carlisle's railway station were closed for weeks. Total economic damage exceeded £500 million. <strong>Effects on the environment:</strong> rivers carved new channels; landslides scarred the Lake District fells; topsoil and farm chemicals were washed into Lake Bassenthwaite. <strong>Long-term effects:</strong> insurance payouts hit £500 million; the ‘Flood Re’ affordable insurance scheme expanded its uptake; and rural communities lost confidence in flood defences.</p>

After the flood, the government allocated £40 million for repairs in Cumbria. The £38 million Carlisle Flood Defence Scheme was upgraded with higher walls and new pumping stations. Upstream, the ‘slowing the flow’ project at Stockdalewath used woody debris dams and tree planting to delay run-off — an example of <dfn class="term" data-def="Working with natural processes (planting trees, restoring wetlands, leaky dams) to slow river flow and reduce flood risk.">natural flood management</dfn>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Three factors combined. First, the atmospheric river dumped record rainfall on already-saturated ground. Second, the steep Cumbrian fells generated rapid run-off (the rivers rose in hours, not days). Third, deforestation and overgrazing of upland sheep pasture had stripped natural water-holding capacity from the catchments. Storm Desmond was a textbook example of climate, geology and human activity combining to amplify a single weather event.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the cause, two effects and two management responses for the 2015 Cumbria floods.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Storm Desmond delivered 341.4 mm of rain in 24 hours, flooding 5,200 homes in Carlisle, Keswick and Cockermouth and causing over £500 million in damage. Management responses included the upgraded Carlisle Flood Defence Scheme and natural flood management at Stockdalewath.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">UK weather is the daily expression of air-mass interaction; extreme weather is what happens when the system is pushed by climate change. The Cumbria floods showed how rainfall, geology, land use and human settlement combine. Strong exam answers always trace this chain — not just the weather event, but why this particular event hit this particular place this hard.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">UK weather reflects the meeting of five air masses, the warming influence of the North Atlantic Drift and continentality between coast and inland.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Extreme weather events — heatwaves, cold spells, storms — are becoming more frequent due to climate change.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Storm Desmond (2015) flooded Cumbria; management since has combined upgraded hard defences (Carlisle) with natural flood management (Stockdalewath).</li>
</ul>
