--- DESCRIPTION ---
How weathering, erosion and mass movement shape the UK's distinctive uplands, lowlands and glaciated landscapes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The UK's Distinctive Landscapes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK has a remarkably varied landscape for such a small country. Three broad types dominate: <dfn class="term" data-def="Land that is high in elevation, often mountainous, with steep slopes and thin soils.">upland</dfn> areas in the north and west (the Scottish Highlands, Lake District, Snowdonia, Pennines), <dfn class="term" data-def="Land that is low in elevation, generally flat with deep soils and gentle slopes.">lowland</dfn> areas in the south and east (the Fens, the Weald, the Thames Basin), and <dfn class="term" data-def="Areas shaped by ice during past ice ages, with U-shaped valleys, corries and tarns.">glaciated</dfn> areas in places like Snowdonia and the Lake District. Each type has been shaped by a long history of climate, geology and human activity working together over millions of years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Upland areas are usually built from harder, more resistant rocks such as granite, slate and limestone. They have a wetter, cooler climate, with thin acidic soils that limit farming to sheep grazing. Lowland areas are formed from softer rocks like clay and chalk; they have warmer, drier climates and deeper soils that support arable farming and dense settlement. Glaciated upland areas show clear evidence of past ice action, including U-shaped valleys, corries (hollows on mountainsides) and ribbon lakes such as Wastwater and Windermere. A simple rule of thumb is that the harder the rock, the slower it weathers and the higher the land tends to stand above its surroundings. The Pennines, for example, run as a granite and limestone spine through northern England because these rocks resist weathering more than the softer Carboniferous rocks of the surrounding lowlands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three landscape types of the UK with one example region for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK's three landscape types are upland (e.g. Scottish Highlands), lowland (e.g. East Anglia) and glaciated (e.g. Lake District). Their character reflects geology, climate and human use working together.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Geomorphic Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Natural processes that shape the surface of the land, including weathering, mass movement, erosion, transport and deposition.">Geomorphic processes</dfn> are the natural forces that wear down, move and rebuild the land. They operate at different speeds and scales but together they explain why landscapes look the way they do.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Weathering</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of rock in situ by physical, chemical or biological processes, without movement.">Weathering</dfn> breaks rock down where it sits. Three types matter for the UK:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Mechanical (physical) weathering</strong> — freeze-thaw is the most common in upland Britain. Water seeps into cracks, freezes overnight, expands by about 9% and prises rock apart.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Chemical weathering</strong> — rainwater is slightly acidic and slowly dissolves limestone, producing features like the limestone pavements of Malham Cove.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Biological weathering</strong> — tree roots widen cracks; lichens release weak acids that break down rock surfaces.</li>
</ul>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The downhill movement of rock and soil under gravity, including sliding, slumping and rockfall.">Mass movement</dfn> happens when gravity pulls weathered material downslope. <strong>Sliding</strong> moves a slab of rock along a flat plane, often after heavy rain. <strong>Slumping</strong> rotates a curved chunk of saturated soil on softer rocks beneath — common on UK clay coasts like Holderness. Both reshape slopes quickly compared to weathering.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Erosion, Transport and Deposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">After weathering loosens material, three further processes move and rearrange it:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Erosion</strong> wears the land away. Four main types: <em>abrasion</em> (sediment scrapes against rock), <em>hydraulic action</em> (water force compresses air in cracks), <em>attrition</em> (sediment hits sediment, becoming smaller and rounder) and <em>solution</em> (water dissolves soluble rock).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Transport</strong> carries material away. <em>Traction</em> rolls boulders along the bed; <em>saltation</em> bounces pebbles; <em>suspension</em> floats fine sediment in the water column; <em>solution</em> carries dissolved material invisibly.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Deposition</strong> drops the load when energy falls — for example, when a river slows on a floodplain or when waves lose force in a sheltered bay.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the four types of erosion with a one-line definition each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four types of erosion are abrasion, hydraulic action, attrition and solution. The four types of transport are traction, saltation, suspension and solution. The same word ‘solution’ appears in both because dissolving and carrying-dissolved are linked.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A typical UK upland slope shows weathering shattering rock at the summit, mass movement carrying material downslope, erosion sharpening valley sides, and deposition spreading sediment on the valley floor. The same processes operate on coastlines, where waves replace rivers as the main agent. Recognising the full chain — weathering, mass movement, erosion, transport, deposition — is the foundation of every river and coastal case study you will study next. The relative importance of each process changes from place to place. In the resistant granite uplands of Dartmoor, freeze-thaw weathering and slow mass movement dominate; in the soft boulder-clay cliffs of Holderness, slumping and marine erosion strip back the coastline by up to two metres a year. By comparing landscapes you can see that the same toolkit of geomorphic processes produces very different outcomes depending on geology and climate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21b">Human activity now stands alongside these natural processes as a major shaper of the UK's landscape. Reservoirs trap sediment and starve rivers downstream of new material, sea walls deflect wave energy along the coast, and centuries of farming have stripped soil from the lowlands. None of this changes the underlying processes, but it changes how those processes act on a landscape, often speeding them up. Understanding both the natural toolkit and the human overlay is what allows geographers to explain why the UK looks the way it does today.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The UK has upland, lowland and glaciated landscapes whose character is set by geology, climate and human activity working together.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Weathering breaks rock in situ; mass movement carries it downslope; erosion, transport and deposition rearrange it across the landscape.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Strong answers describe a process chain — cause to effect — rather than naming one process in isolation.</li>
</ul>
