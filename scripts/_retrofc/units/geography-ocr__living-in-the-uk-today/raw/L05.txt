--- DESCRIPTION ---
Patterns of employment, income and life expectancy across the UK, and the causes of uneven development between regions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Diverse and Unequal UK</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK is one of the world's wealthiest countries but it is also one of the most regionally unequal. A child born in the South East today can expect to live four years longer, earn 50% more and be twice as likely to attend university as one born in the North East. These differences are the geographical signature of <dfn class="term" data-def="When some places or groups of people are wealthier, healthier or have better opportunities than others.">uneven development</dfn> within a single country.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Five indicators capture this story: employment, income, life expectancy, educational attainment and access to digital infrastructure such as broadband. They are linked — people in higher-paid work tend to live longer and have better-educated children — so improving one usually improves the others.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Patterns of Diversity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The UK is also diverse. The 2021 census showed that 18% of the population identified as a non-White ethnicity, rising to 46% in London. South Asian, Black African, Black Caribbean and East Asian communities are concentrated in major cities; the rural population remains more than 95% White. Religious diversity follows a similar pattern: Christianity remains the largest faith but no longer the majority, while Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism and Judaism are well represented in cities such as Bradford, Leicester and London.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of working-age people in paid work.">Employment</dfn> patterns are similarly geographic. The South East and London have unemployment rates around 3-4%, while parts of the North East exceed 6%. Wages mirror this: the median full-time wage in London (£44,000) is roughly £10,000 above the UK average and £15,000 above some former mining towns.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the five indicators of UK uneven development with one example region for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK uneven development is measured by employment, income, life expectancy, educational attainment, ethnicity and broadband access. London leads on most measures; parts of the North East and South Wales lag behind by a generation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Causes of Uneven Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Four causes interact to produce these patterns:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Geographical location</strong> — the South East benefits from proximity to London, the financial centre, and from short links to the European mainland. Remote areas like the West Highlands or rural Wales have weaker access to large markets.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Economic change</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="The decline of traditional manufacturing and heavy industry, often replaced by services.">deindustrialisation</dfn> hit Northern coalfields, steel towns like Sheffield and shipbuilding cities like Glasgow especially hard from the 1970s onwards. Service-led recovery has been concentrated in the South East.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Infrastructure</strong> — high-speed road, rail and broadband connections cluster in the South East. The North-South divide in transport investment per head is well documented; broadband still does not reach all rural Wales and Scotland.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Government policy</strong> — long-running investment in London (Crossrail, the Underground), capital spending on the City, and clustering of headquarters in the South East has reinforced these patterns. Recent ‘levelling up’ programmes attempt to redress them, but with limited results so far.</li>
</ul>

    <span>Case Study: Economic Change in the North East</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The North East was once dominated by coal mining, shipbuilding on the Tyne and Wear, and chemical industries on Teesside. Coal employment fell from 100,000 in 1980 to under 200 today. Shipyards closed; steel works shrank. Replacement industries have been narrower — Nissan's Sunderland car plant employs around 6,000 (more if including suppliers), and call-centre work expanded but at lower wages. The result is the lowest disposable income per head of any UK region (£18,800 in 2022) and persistent gaps in life expectancy and educational attainment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Consequences of Inequality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Uneven development has real consequences. Health gaps are some of the widest in Europe: men in Blackpool die on average nine years earlier than men in Westminster. Younger people leave the North and Wales for cities, accelerating depopulation. Public services come under pressure on both ends — the North loses tax revenue, the South West and South East struggle to house essential workers due to high property prices.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why ‘Levelling Up’ Is Hard</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Reversing decades of investment patterns is slow. Skilled workers cluster where jobs already are; companies cluster where skilled workers are; this self-reinforcing ‘agglomeration effect’ pulls more growth to London. Targeted policies (HS2 northern phases, Northern Powerhouse, freeports) aim to break this cycle but face long lead times and political risk.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list four causes of uneven development in the UK and give one piece of evidence for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Uneven UK development has four interlinked causes: geographical location, economic change, infrastructure investment and government policy. Their combined effect explains why the North East has the UK's lowest disposable income while London has the highest.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">UK inequality is not random — it has clear geographical patterns rooted in history, infrastructure and policy choices. Strong exam answers always combine evidence (life expectancy gaps, income figures) with explanation (deindustrialisation, agglomeration). The next lesson on UK population looks at how migration responds to and reinforces these patterns.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">UK regional inequality is measured through employment, income, life expectancy, educational attainment, ethnicity and broadband access, with London leading and the North East lagging.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The four interlinked causes are geographical location, economic change (deindustrialisation), infrastructure investment and government policy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Health, demographic and economic consequences are stark; levelling-up policy attempts a reversal but faces long lead times and entrenched agglomeration effects.</li>
</ul>
