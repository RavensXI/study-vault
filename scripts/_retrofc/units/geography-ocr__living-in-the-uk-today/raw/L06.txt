--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the UK's population is changing, ageing population responses and 21st-century immigration impacts.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Changing UK Population</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK's population stood at around 67.6 million in 2024 and continues to grow, although more slowly than in recent decades. Birth rates have fallen, life expectancy has risen, and migration is now the main engine of population change. The result is a country that is older, more diverse and more concentrated in cities than it was in 1900.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The clearest way to track these changes is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph showing the age and sex structure of a population.">population pyramid</dfn>. In 1900 the UK had a wide base (high birth rate) and a narrow top (low life expectancy). By 2024 the pyramid had become almost rectangular, with a top-heavy bulge of people aged 60-80 — the ‘baby boomers’ born after World War II. This is the visible signature of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A model showing how a country's birth rate, death rate and population change as it develops.">Demographic Transition Model</dfn> at Stage 4 (low birth rates, low death rates) heading towards Stage 5 (slow decline).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">An Ageing Population</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Around 19% of the UK population is now over 65, up from 13% in 1980. This figure is projected to reach 25% by 2050. The causes are positive (better healthcare, nutrition and pensions) but the consequences raise difficult questions:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Pensions and welfare</strong> — the state pension cost the UK £125 billion in 2023, projected to rise sharply.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Healthcare</strong> — older people use NHS services more intensively. About 40% of NHS beds are occupied by patients over 65.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Workforce</strong> — the working-age population is shrinking relative to the retired, raising the <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of dependants (children and elderly) to working-age people in a population.">dependency ratio</dfn> and putting pressure on tax revenue.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Care</strong> — demand for residential and at-home care now outstrips supply; the ‘care crisis’ is one of the dominant political issues of the 2020s.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down four consequences of an ageing UK population, with one piece of evidence for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>About 19% of the UK population is over 65, projected to reach 25% by 2050. State pension costs hit £125 billion in 2023; 40% of NHS beds are occupied by over-65s; the ‘care crisis’ is now a national political priority.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK Government Responses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">UK governments have responded in three main ways. First, by raising the <strong>state pension age</strong> — from 60 (women) and 65 (men) historically, to 66 today and 67 by 2028. This keeps people in the workforce longer and reduces pension costs. Second, by encouraging <strong>private pension saving</strong> through automatic workplace enrolment, introduced in 2012. Third, by promoting <strong>migration of working-age people</strong> to top up the labour force; the post-2004 EU enlargement and post-Brexit health and care visas are both examples.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">21st-Century Migration to the UK</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Net migration to the UK reached a record 685,000 in 2023. The drivers and origins have changed dramatically over the century. The 2004 EU enlargement brought around 1 million Polish workers in a decade. Brexit in 2020 ended free movement and shifted recruitment towards Asia and Africa — especially under the Health and Care Worker visa, which has powered NHS staffing through the recent recruitment crisis. Refugee programmes for Hong Kong (BNO visa, 2021) and Ukraine (Homes for Ukraine, 2022) added further specific flows.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The social and economic effects are substantial. Migrant workers fill skill gaps in the NHS, agriculture, construction and hospitality; they contribute net taxes; and they reshape the cultural life of UK cities. Pressures include short-term housing strain, school place demand, and political tensions about integration and pace of change.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Looking Ahead</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">By 2050 the UK is projected to have around 75 million people, a quarter aged over 65 and even more concentrated in southern cities. Whether the workforce can support that older population without reducing economic growth depends on three policy levers: pension age, productivity, and migration. The geographical and political consequences of getting these decisions right or wrong will shape the UK for decades.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The UK population is ageing and increasingly diverse, with around 19% over 65 and 18% identifying as non-White.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Government responses to ageing include raising the pension age, encouraging private pension saving, and using migration to top up the workforce.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">21st-century migration has been driven by EU enlargement, Brexit-era Health and Care visas, and refugee programmes, all of which fill skill gaps but raise housing and integration pressures.</li>
</ul>
