--- DESCRIPTION ---
How farming, fishing, energy and water management modify UK ecosystems and environments.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Humans Reshape UK Environments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For at least 6,000 years humans have been clearing woodland, draining wetlands, planting fields and reshaping rivers in the UK. Today, only 13% of the UK's land surface is woodland and less than 3% is ‘wilderness’ in any meaningful sense. The rest is a managed environment — farmland, plantations, towns and roads — where humans use natural systems to obtain food, energy and water. Understanding how those uses work is the foundation of UK environmental policy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Modifying Ecosystems for Food</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Farming using machinery, large fields and chemical inputs to maximise yields.">Mechanised farming</dfn> dominates UK lowlands. Combine harvesters work fields hundreds of hectares across; nitrogen fertilisers, herbicides and pesticides boost yields. The UK now produces 60% of its own food — more than many countries of similar size — and exports salmon, lamb, cheese and Scotch whisky. But the cost has been ecological: hedgerows ripped out for bigger fields, soil thinned by intensive cultivation, run-off polluting rivers, and pollinator populations halved since 1980.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fishing using large vessels and modern equipment to catch fish in great quantities.">Commercial fishing</dfn> has made similar trade-offs. UK fishing fleets land around 600,000 tonnes of fish a year; species like cod, haddock and mackerel support coastal communities from Peterhead to Brixham. But North Sea cod stocks have been overfished for decades; trawling damages seabed habitats. UK quota systems and Marine Protected Areas (around 38% of UK seas in 2024) are partial responses.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down three ways humans modify UK ecosystems for food, energy and water.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Humans modify UK ecosystems in three main ways: mechanised farming and fishing for food, wind farms and fracking for energy, and reservoirs and water transfer schemes for water. Each carries trade-offs between supply and ecology.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Modifying Ecosystems for Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Wind farms</strong> are now the most visible UK energy modification. In 2024 wind generated about 30% of UK electricity. Onshore wind farms (e.g. Whitelee in East Renfrewshire, the UK's largest with 215 turbines) sit on uplands; offshore farms (Hornsea Two, off Yorkshire, capacity 1.32 GW) sit on shallow seabeds. Benefits: low-carbon electricity at scale. Trade-offs: visual impact in scenic uplands, occasional bird strike, seabed disturbance during installation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Fracking</strong> — injecting high-pressure fluid into shale rock to release gas — was trialled in the UK at Preston New Road in Lancashire from 2018-19 but suspended after small earthquakes (up to 2.9 magnitude) and intense local opposition. UK governments have since paused fracking. The episode shows how environmental and seismic risks can stop an energy modification despite economic incentives.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Modifying Ecosystems for Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A man-made lake created by damming a river, used to supply drinking water or generate electricity.">Reservoirs</dfn> capture and store water from upland catchments. Kielder Water in Northumberland (the UK's largest reservoir, 200 billion litres) supplies the North East and East Midlands and supports tourism. The Lake District's reservoirs (Haweswater, Thirlmere) feed Manchester, 100 km away, through pipelines built in the 19th century. Building these reservoirs flooded valleys, displaced communities and altered downstream river flow.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pipeline or aqueduct that moves water from a wet upland region to a drier lowland region.">Water transfer schemes</dfn> are the next layer. The Ely Ouse-Essex transfer moves water from East Anglia's wet rivers to Essex reservoirs. Plans for a UK-wide National Water Grid have circulated for decades, in response to climate-change projections of drier summers in the South East. Each transfer raises questions about whose ecosystem is sacrificed for whose drinking water.</p>

The UK's challenge is to deliver these supplies for 67 million people while protecting biodiversity, habitats and climate stability. Recent policy shifts (Environmental Land Management Schemes, Net Zero by 2050) are the start of an attempt to value both columns of the trade-off.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">UK ecosystems are not natural — they are negotiated. Every field, fishery, wind farm and reservoir represents a choice about how the UK obtains food, energy and water. The exam answer that wins highest marks always names a UK example, identifies the trade-off, and shows that environmental policy is the visible negotiation between the two sides.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">UK ecosystems are intensively modified for food (mechanised farming, commercial fishing), energy (wind farms, fracking) and water (reservoirs, transfer schemes).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Each modification carries trade-offs — biodiversity loss, seabed damage, induced earthquakes, displaced communities — that increasingly shape UK policy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Wind generates around 30% of UK electricity in 2024; fracking was paused after Preston New Road; Kielder Water supplies 200 billion litres to the North East.</li>
</ul>
