--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">UK Coastlines: A Quick Map</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK has more than 11,000 miles of coastline. Hard-rock <dfn class="term" data-def="A coast made of resistant rock that erodes slowly to form steep cliffs and rocky landforms.">erosional coasts</dfn> dominate the west and north (granites of Cornwall, sandstones of Scotland), while soft-rock <dfn class="term" data-def="A coast made of less resistant material such as clay or sand, often with low cliffs and beaches.">depositional or unstable coasts</dfn> are common in the east and south (Holderness, the Suffolk coast). Wave action, geology, climate and human management all shape these landscapes — sometimes faster than people realise.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Two wave types matter: <dfn class="term" data-def="High-energy, large waves with strong backwash that erode the coast.">destructive waves</dfn> are tall, frequent and have strong backwash, eroding the coast; <dfn class="term" data-def="Low-energy, gentle waves with strong swash that build up beaches.">constructive waves</dfn> are flatter, less frequent and have strong swash, building up beaches. Storm conditions usually bring destructive waves to UK coasts.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four main types of erosion that operate on the UK coast.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Coastal erosion is driven by hydraulic action (waves compressing air in cracks), abrasion (sediment scraping against rock), attrition (sediment hits sediment) and solution (chemical erosion of soluble rocks like chalk).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Erosional Coastal Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Differential erosion explains many of the UK's most famous landforms. Where bands of harder and softer rock meet the sea at right angles, the soft rock is eroded more quickly, forming a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wide, sheltered indentation in a coastline where soft rock has been eroded.">bay</dfn>; the hard rock is left as a <strong>headland</strong>. Old Harry Rocks in Dorset and Selwicks Bay at Flamborough Head both show the headland-bay sequence cleanly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">On a single headland, waves attack lines of weakness. Hydraulic action enlarges a fault into a <strong>cave</strong>; the cave is forced through to form an <strong>arch</strong>; the unsupported roof eventually collapses, leaving a <strong>stack</strong>; and the stack itself is eroded down to a <strong>stump</strong>. The Old Man of Hoy off Orkney is a UK example of this sequence in basalt.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Depositional Coastal Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where waves lose energy — in sheltered bays or where the coast changes direction — sediment is dropped to form <strong>beaches</strong> and <strong>spits</strong>. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A long ridge of sand or shingle extending out from the coast across an estuary.">spit</dfn> grows from longshore drift: waves approach the coast at an angle, swash carries sediment up the beach diagonally, and backwash returns it straight down under gravity. Material is moved progressively along the coast. Where the coast changes direction, the deposited material extends out into the sea. Spurn Head, on the Yorkshire side of the Humber Estuary, is an iconic UK example.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK Case Study: The Holderness Coast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Holderness coast, between Flamborough Head and Spurn Point in East Yorkshire, is the fastest-eroding coastline in Europe, with cliffs retreating by up to two metres a year. The cliffs are made of unconsolidated <strong>boulder clay</strong> — loose material left by ice sheets — which slumps when saturated. Add destructive North Sea waves and a long fetch from the north, and the result is rapid retreat. Whole villages such as Mappleton have lost streets, farms and graveyards over the past century.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Geomorphic processes here include hydraulic action and abrasion at the cliff foot, slumping of the saturated clay above, and longshore drift carrying eroded material south to feed Spurn Head. Climate matters: heavy autumn rain saturates the clay; storm winds drive bigger waves. Human activity plays a complex role. Sea defences at Hornsea and Mappleton (groynes and rip-rap) protect those settlements but starve the coast further south of sediment, accelerating erosion at unprotected stretches like Easington.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Hard engineering (sea walls, groynes, rip-rap) blocks erosion locally but can shift the problem along the coast. Soft engineering (beach nourishment, managed retreat) works with natural processes — for example, Medmerry on the Sussex coast deliberately removed a sea wall in 2013 to allow the sea to flood farmland and create a new salt marsh, protecting villages further inland. Most UK Shoreline Management Plans now combine the two.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three reasons why Holderness erodes so quickly — one geological, one climatic and one human.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Holderness retreats because boulder clay slumps easily when wet, the North Sea has a long fetch and destructive waves, and sea defences at Hornsea and Mappleton interrupt longshore drift to the south.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">UK coasts are not just shaped by waves; they are products of geology setting the resistance of the land, climate driving wave energy and saturation, and human activity rearranging both natural processes and the people exposed to risk. Holderness shows how the same physical processes that produce dramatic stacks at Flamborough Head can produce slumping cliffs and lost villages just a few miles south. A strong exam answer always traces this combination of factors.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Erosional coasts produce headlands, bays, caves, arches, stacks and stumps; depositional coasts produce beaches and spits through longshore drift.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Holderness is the fastest-eroding UK coastline because boulder clay slumps when wet, the North Sea has a long fetch, and sea defences upstream interrupt natural sediment flow.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Coastal management mixes hard and soft engineering; choices shift the problem along the coast unless they work with natural processes.</li>
</ul>
