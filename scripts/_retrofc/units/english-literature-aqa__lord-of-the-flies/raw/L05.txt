--- DESCRIPTION ---
Roger kills Piggy, the conch shatters, Ralph is hunted, and the ironic rescue arrives.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 10: The Shell & the Glasses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After Simon’s death, Ralph’s group has shrunk to just four: Ralph, Piggy, Samneric (Sam and Eric). Ralph is haunted by what they did: “That was Simon … That was murder.” Piggy insists it was “an accident” and that they were “on the outside” of the circle. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A psychological defence mechanism where a person refuses to accept reality, often to protect themselves from guilt or fear.">denial</dfn> shows how even the most rational character uses self-deception when confronted with moral horror.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jack’s tribe raids Ralph’s camp at night — not for the conch (which has lost all authority), but for Piggy’s glasses. The glasses are the only means of making fire. This shift is crucial: power no longer comes from democratic symbols (the conch) but from control of resources and technology. Jack steals not ideas but <em>tools</em> — reflecting how authoritarian regimes seize practical power while ignoring democratic principles.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does Jack's tribe steal Piggy's glasses instead of the conch?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jack’s tribe steals Piggy’s glasses because they need fire (practical power), not the conch (democratic authority). This shows that democratic symbols are worthless without the power to enforce them. Civilisation has been replaced by a system based on resource control and violence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Chapter 11: Castle Rock — Piggy’s Death</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ralph, Piggy, and Samneric go to Castle Rock to demand the glasses back. Piggy carries the conch, believing it still has power: “I got the conch! … I got a right to speak.” His faith in democratic process is both admirable and tragic — he is trying to use rules in a world that has abandoned them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Jack and Ralph fight. While they struggle, <dfn class="term" data-def="A boy who represents pure, sadistic cruelty — he kills Piggy by rolling a boulder onto him and later tortures Samneric. He embodies evil without restraint.">Roger</dfn> — perched on the cliff above — deliberately levers a huge boulder that strikes Piggy. Piggy falls forty feet onto rocks below, and the conch shatters simultaneously. Golding describes the conch exploding “into a thousand white fragments.” In one moment, both reason (Piggy) and democracy (the conch) are destroyed.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Roger’s development is one of the novel’s most disturbing threads. In Chapter 4, he throws stones at the littlun Henry but deliberately aims to miss: “Roger’s arm was conditioned by a civilisation that knew nothing of him and was in ruins.” The “taboo of the old life” still restrains him. By Chapter 11, that restraint has completely vanished — he kills Piggy without hesitation and later tortures Samneric for information.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Roger represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The deliberate infliction of pain or suffering on others for personal pleasure or satisfaction, without any practical purpose.">sadism</dfn> — cruelty for its own sake. While Jack kills for power and status, Roger kills for pleasure. Golding suggests that civilisation’s rules are the only thing preventing sadistic impulses from being acted upon. Once those rules disappear, individuals like Roger become the most dangerous.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down what Piggy's death and the conch's destruction symbolise together.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Piggy’s death and the conch’s destruction happen in the same moment. This is Golding’s most powerful symbol: the simultaneous destruction of reason and democracy. Without either, there is nothing left to oppose savagery.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chapter 12: Cry of the Hunters — The Manhunt</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Ralph is now utterly alone. Samneric have been forced to join Jack’s tribe (Roger tortured them into compliance). Jack organises a manhunt: the entire tribe searches the island for Ralph, who hides in the jungle. When they cannot find him, they set the island on fire to smoke him out — destroying the entire jungle ecosystem in the process.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Ralph runs for his life, crashing through the burning forest towards the beach. He stumbles and falls at the feet of a naval officer who has come ashore, attracted by the smoke. The officer, in his crisp white uniform, looks at the filthy, bleeding boys with bewilderment: “I should have thought that a pack of British boys … would have been able to put up a better show than that.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Devastating Irony of the Rescue</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The rescue is deeply ironic on multiple levels. First, the fire that attracts the ship was lit to kill Ralph — destruction, not civilisation, brings rescue. Second, the officer references <em>The Coral Island</em> directly (“Jolly good show. Like the Coral Island”), showing he expects the Victorian fantasy of civilised boys thriving. His ignorance mirrors society’s refusal to confront the truth about human nature.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Third, and most devastating: the officer is standing on the deck of a warship. He rescues the boys from their small-scale savagery only to return them to the large-scale savagery of nuclear war. Golding’s message is clear: the adult world is no different from the island. There is no true rescue from human nature.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Ralph Weeps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The novel’s final image is Ralph weeping: “Ralph wept for the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart, and the fall through the air of the true, wise friend called Piggy.” This sentence summarises the entire novel. “The end of innocence” — the boys can never return to who they were. “The darkness of man’s heart” — Golding’s central thesis, that evil is innate. “The fall through the air” of Piggy — the death of reason. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of three items used for rhetorical emphasis, often building towards a climax.">tricolon</dfn> builds in emotional weight, ending with the personal grief for Piggy that makes the abstract themes concrete.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Other boys begin to cry too, but Golding specifies that Ralph weeps for understanding, not just fear. He has learned what the others have not: that the beast is real, and it is inside them all.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Piggy’s death and the conch’s destruction symbolise the simultaneous end of reason and democracy on the island.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The rescue is deeply ironic: the fire of destruction attracts a warship, and the officer expects a <em>Coral Island</em> adventure — the adult world is equally savage.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Ralph’s final weeping (“the darkness of man’s heart”) is Golding’s thesis stated directly: evil is innate in all humans.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">The ending is the most commonly examined part of the novel. A strong essay will analyse the <strong>multiple layers of irony</strong>: the fire of destruction bringing rescue, the Coral Island reference, and the warship. Always quote Ralph’s final weeping and analyse the tricolon — “the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart, and the fall through the air of the true, wise friend called Piggy.” This single sentence connects all three major themes and demonstrates AO1 (ideas) and AO2 (methods) together.</p>
