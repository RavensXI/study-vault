--- DESCRIPTION ---
Ralph, Jack and Piggy arrive on the island — democracy, the conch, and early tensions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 1: The Sound of the Shell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with Ralph and Piggy emerging from the jungle after a plane crash. They find a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large spiral seashell used by Ralph to call assemblies; it symbolises democracy, order, and the right to speak.">conch</dfn> shell on the beach, and Piggy suggests blowing it to call any other survivors. When Ralph blows the conch, boys appear from across the island. This moment establishes two things: Ralph’s natural authority and the conch as a symbol of democratic order — whoever holds the conch has the right to speak.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jack Merridew arrives last, marching his choir in military formation, wearing black cloaks. Golding immediately signals Jack’s authoritarian instincts: he orders the choir as though they are soldiers, and his first words demand to know “where’s the man with the trumpet?” He expects adult authority — and when there is none, he assumes he should fill the role.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does each boy represent when they first appear — Ralph, Jack, and Piggy?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three central characters are established immediately: Ralph represents democracy and order, Jack represents authoritarian power and control, and Piggy represents intellect and reason. Their first actions foreshadow the entire novel’s conflict.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Election & the First Fracture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The boys hold a vote for chief. Ralph wins — not because of any particular skill, but because he holds the conch and has an attractive, calm appearance. Golding writes that there was “a stillness about Ralph as he sat that marked him out: there was his size, and attractive appearance; and most obscurely, yet most powerfully, there was the conch.” Ralph’s authority comes from <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that a person's physical appearance and manner influence how much authority others grant them, regardless of actual ability.">superficial charisma</dfn> rather than proven leadership. This is Golding’s subtle critique of democracy — the best leader is not always the one chosen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Jack is visibly humiliated by the vote. To pacify him, Ralph gives the choir to Jack as hunters. This compromise creates the fundamental division: Ralph’s group focuses on rescue (building shelters, maintaining the signal fire), while Jack’s hunters focus on killing pigs. From this moment, two competing priorities — <dfn class="term" data-def="The tension between maintaining structures that serve long-term survival (rescue) and pursuing immediate, primal desires (hunting).">civilisation versus savagery</dfn> — are built into the social structure.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Piggy is the most intelligent boy on the island but is consistently ignored, mocked, and excluded. He is overweight, asthmatic, wears glasses, and speaks with a lower-class accent. The other boys laugh at his nickname and dismiss his ideas. Ralph himself betrays Piggy’s trust by revealing his nickname after being asked not to. Golding uses Piggy to show that intelligence and reason are undervalued in society — people listen to the confident and physically strong, not the rational.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Piggy’s glasses become essential for starting the signal fire, making him practically useful. Yet this utility does not earn him respect. Golding suggests that civilised society marginalises its thinkers and rewards charisma and physical dominance instead.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Chapters 2–3: Rules, Fire & Frustration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Ralph establishes two priorities: keep the signal fire burning on the mountain (so a passing ship can rescue them) and build shelters on the beach. In Chapter 2, the boys enthusiastically build a large fire, but it spreads out of control and a boy with a mulberry-coloured birthmark on his face disappears — presumably killed. This is the first death, and it happens through carelessness rather than malice. Golding foreshadows the greater violence to come.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By Chapter 3, the initial enthusiasm has faded. Only Ralph and Simon continue building shelters. The other boys play, swim, or hunt with Jack. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A large bonfire maintained on the mountain as a distress signal for passing ships; it represents the boys' connection to civilisation and hope of rescue.">signal fire</dfn> becomes a test of commitment to rescue: maintaining it requires discipline, delayed gratification, and collective responsibility — qualities that most boys lack.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: What are Ralph's two priorities, and why do they fail?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ralph’s two rules — maintain the signal fire and build shelters — represent civilisation’s requirements: collective effort, long-term thinking, and personal sacrifice. Their failure shows how quickly people abandon shared responsibility when there is no enforcement mechanism.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jack’s Obsession with Hunting</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">In Chapter 3, Jack is described hunting alone in the forest. His appearance is already changing: he is nearly naked, his hair is longer, and he moves with “dog-like” behaviour, sniffing the air and tracking pig droppings. Golding uses animal imagery to show Jack’s regression from civilised boy to predator. Crucially, Jack has not yet killed a pig — in Chapter 1, he hesitated because of “the enormity of the knife descending and cutting into living flesh.” This hesitation disappears as the novel progresses.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Jack’s frustration at not yet killing a pig drives him further from Ralph’s priorities. Hunting offers immediate, visceral excitement; maintaining the fire and building shelters are boring, thankless tasks. Golding shows how easily humans choose primal satisfaction over communal duty.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Simon is introduced as a quiet, spiritual figure who helps Ralph build shelters when nobody else will. He also walks alone into the jungle, suggesting an affinity with nature that the other boys lack. Golding positions Simon as the novel’s moral centre — the one character who will eventually understand the true nature of the beast.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The conch establishes democratic order, but Ralph’s election is based on appearance, not ability — Golding critiques how leaders are chosen.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The division between Ralph’s fire-keepers and Jack’s hunters creates the central conflict: civilisation versus savagery.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Piggy represents intelligence marginalised by a society that values charisma and strength; his exclusion foreshadows how reason will be destroyed.</li></ul>
