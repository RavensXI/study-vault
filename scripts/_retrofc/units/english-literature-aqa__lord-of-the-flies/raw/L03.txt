The painted face, the missed ship, and the beast — civilisation begins to crumble.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 4: Painted Faces & the Missed Ship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter 4 marks the turning point. Jack paints his face with clay and charcoal to camouflage himself for hunting. But the mask does more than hide him — it <em>liberates</em> him. Golding writes that Jack looked at his reflection and was “safe from shame or self-consciousness” behind the mask. The paint frees Jack from the social norms that restrained him. He is no longer Jack Merridew, choirboy and prefect — he becomes something primal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This is when Jack finally kills his first pig. The hunters return chanting “Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A repeated phrase or song used by Jack's hunters during and after killing; it becomes increasingly violent and ritualistic as the novel progresses.">hunting chant</dfn> is rhythmic and ritualistic — it transforms killing from a practical act (getting food) into a communal ceremony. The repetition creates a hypnotic effect, pulling the boys into a collective frenzy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What two things happen in Chapter 4 that mark the turning point of the novel?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jack’s face paint removes his civilised identity, allowing him to kill without guilt. At the same time, he lets the signal fire go out, and a ship passes without seeing them. This is the novel’s turning point: hunting (savagery) has been chosen over rescue (civilisation).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">While the hunters celebrate their kill, Ralph discovers that the signal fire has gone out. A ship has passed the island — and nobody saw their signal. Ralph is furious. This moment crystallises the central conflict: Jack prioritises the thrill of the hunt over the collective need for rescue. For Golding, this choice reveals something fundamental about human nature — immediate gratification will always overpower long-term responsibility unless enforced by external authority.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Symbolism of the Face Paint</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">The face paint functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A symbolic cover or disguise that conceals a person's true identity, allowing them to act without moral restraint.">mask</dfn> that hides the individual and releases the savage. Golding describes Jack looking at his reflection: “He looked in astonishment, no longer at himself but at an awesome stranger.” The paint destroys self-awareness and personal responsibility. Once Jack is anonymous, he is capable of violence. Golding draws on the psychological concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The loss of individual identity and personal responsibility when a person becomes part of a group, often leading to extreme behaviour.">deindividuation</dfn> — people behave differently (often more aggressively) when they cannot be identified.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">As more boys paint their faces and join the tribe, they too shed their individual identities. By the end of the novel, the painted hunters are described as “savages” rather than by name. The mask does not just change behaviour — it erases personhood.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Chapter 5: Beast from Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Ralph calls an assembly to re-establish rules, but the meeting descends into chaos. The littluns (younger boys) report seeing a “beast” at night. Ralph and Piggy try to use reason: Ralph insists “there isn’t a beast!” and Piggy appeals to science and logic. But Jack exploits the fear: “If there’s a beast, we’ll hunt it down!” He offers action and excitement where Ralph offers only reassurance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Simon tries to articulate a different idea: “Maybe it’s only us.” He is suggesting that the beast is not a physical creature but something <em>within</em> the boys themselves. Nobody listens — the truth is too uncomfortable. This is one of the novel’s most important moments: Simon grasps Golding’s central theme (innate human evil), but the other boys cannot accept it.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Simon's theory about the beast and explain why nobody listens.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Simon’s suggestion that “maybe it’s only us” is Golding’s central message in miniature. The beast is not a creature — it is the capacity for evil inside every human. The boys reject this truth because it is easier to fear an external monster than to confront the darkness within themselves.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Chapter 6: Beast from Air</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A dead <dfn class="term" data-def="A pilot killed in the aerial battle of the adult war, whose body drifts onto the island and is mistaken for the beast by the terrified boys.">parachutist</dfn> lands on the mountain during the night, carried by the wind from the air battle above the island. Sam and Eric (Samneric), tending the signal fire, see the body’s silhouette rising and falling in the wind and believe it is the beast. They run back to camp, terrified.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The boys organise an expedition to find the beast. Jack leads aggressively; Ralph follows reluctantly. They explore Castle Rock (a rocky outcrop at one end of the island) and find no beast there, but Jack is excited by the defensive possibilities of the location. He says they could build a fort here — foreshadowing his later occupation of Castle Rock as the headquarters of his tribe.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Dead Parachutist — What It Means</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The dead parachutist serves multiple symbolic functions. It is a literal piece of the adult war falling onto the island — proving that the “civilised” world is just as violent as the boys’ island. It is mistaken for the beast, reinforcing the irony that the real “beast” is human violence itself. And it sits on the mountain, blocking the signal fire and the path to rescue — the adult world’s war is literally preventing the children’s salvation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Only Simon will eventually discover the truth about the parachutist, but he is killed before he can tell the others. Golding suggests that truth-tellers are destroyed by the very society they try to save.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The fear of the beast accelerates the breakdown of Ralph’s democratic order. Fear is a more powerful motivator than reason. Jack understands this instinctively — he offers protection through strength and violence, while Ralph can only offer rules and rational argument. As fear grows, more boys gravitate towards Jack.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Jack’s face paint removes civilised identity and enables violence — Golding explores how anonymity releases savagery.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The missed ship shows that immediate gratification (hunting) defeats collective responsibility (the fire) without enforcement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Simon’s insight that “maybe it’s only us” is the novel’s central truth: the beast is human nature itself.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When writing about the beast, always discuss what it <strong>symbolises</strong>, not just what the boys think it is. Strong essays distinguish between the boys’ literal fear (a physical creature) and Golding’s allegorical meaning (the beast represents innate human evil). Link Simon’s insight (“maybe it’s only us”) to the Lord of the Flies scene in Chapter 8 and to Golding’s post-war beliefs about human nature. This shows AO1 (ideas) and AO3 (context) working together.</p>
