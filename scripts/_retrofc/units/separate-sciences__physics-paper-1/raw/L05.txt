--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Domestic Electricity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK mains supply is <dfn class="term" data-def="Current that continuously changes direction, used in the UK mains supply at 50 Hz.">alternating current</dfn> (a.c.) at about 230 V and a frequency of 50 Hz. This means the current changes direction 50 times per second. Batteries supply <dfn class="term" data-def="Current that flows in one direction only, supplied by batteries and cells.">direct current</dfn> (d.c.), which always flows in the same direction.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and state the UK mains supply voltage, frequency, and whether it's a.c. or d.c., plus what type batteries supply."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>UK mains supply: alternating current (a.c.), approximately 230 V, frequency 50 Hz. Batteries supply direct current (d.c.).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Three-Pin Plug</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most appliances connect to the mains via a three-pin plug. The three wires are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Live wire</strong> (brown) — carries the alternating potential difference from the supply. It alternates between about +325 V and −325 V.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Neutral wire</strong> (blue) — completes the circuit. It is at, or close to, 0 V (earth potential).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Earth wire</strong> (green and yellow) — a safety wire connected to the metal casing of an appliance. It carries current to earth if a fault occurs, preventing electric shock.</p>

<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why the Live Wire Is Dangerous</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">The live wire is dangerous even when the switch is off, because it is still at 230 V relative to earth. If you touch it, a large potential difference exists between you and the ground, causing a current to flow through your body. This can cause serious injury or death. A switch is always connected in the live wire so that when it is off, no current can reach the appliance.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain which wire the fuse connects to, what happens when it melts, and how circuit breakers differ."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="A safety device containing a thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current exceeds a safe level.">fuse</dfn> is connected in the live wire. If too much current flows (due to a fault), the fuse wire melts, breaking the circuit and preventing overheating or fire. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An automatic switch that breaks a circuit when it detects a difference between the live and neutral currents.">circuit breaker</dfn> does the same job but can be reset instead of replaced.</p>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Electrical Power and Energy Transfer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The power of an appliance tells you how much energy it transfers per second. Two equations link power to current and voltage:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">$$P = IV$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$P = I^2 R$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Where \(P\) is power in watts, \(I\) is current in amps, \(V\) is potential difference in volts, and \(R\) is resistance in ohms.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The energy transferred by an appliance depends on its power and how long it is used:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">$$E = Pt$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Where \(E\) is energy in joules, \(P\) is power in watts, and \(t\) is time in seconds. You can also use \(E = QV\), where \(Q\) is charge in coulombs.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Energy and Cost</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">A 2 kW kettle is used for 3 minutes. Calculate the energy transferred in joules and in kilowatt-hours.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">In joules: \(E = Pt = 2000 \times 180 = 360{,}000\) J</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">In kWh: \(E = 2 \times \frac{3}{60} = 0.1\) kWh. If electricity costs 30p per kWh, the cost is \(0.1 \times 30 = 3\)p.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">The National Grid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of cables and transformers that links power stations to consumers across the UK.">National Grid</dfn> is a network of cables and transformers that distributes electricity from power stations to homes and businesses across the UK.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n24">Electricity is transmitted at very high voltages (up to 400,000 V) and low currents. Since power lost as heat in the cables equals \(P = I^2 R\), reducing the current dramatically reduces the energy wasted.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Step-Up and Step-Down Transformers</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A device that changes the voltage of an alternating current supply using electromagnetic induction.">Transformers</dfn> change the voltage of an a.c. supply. A <strong>step-up transformer</strong> increases the voltage at the power station before transmission. A <strong>step-down transformer</strong> decreases the voltage near homes to a safe 230 V.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">When voltage is increased, current decreases (assuming power stays the same: \(P = IV\)). This is why high-voltage transmission wastes less energy — the lower current means less heating in the cables.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>
