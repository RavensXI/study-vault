<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Density?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Density is a measure of how much <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of substance in a given volume.">mass</dfn> is packed into a given volume. Every material has its own characteristic density, which helps us identify different substances and predict whether they will float or sink.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the density equation using both words and symbols (ρ, m, V).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Density is calculated using the equation: \(\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}}\) or \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\)</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The unit for density is kilograms per cubic metre (kg/m³), though you might also see grams per cubic centimetre (g/cm³). To convert between these units, remember that 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Density Calculation Examples</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">A block of wood has a mass of 800 g and a volume of 1000 cm³. Its density is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$\rho = \frac{800\text{ g}}{1000\text{ cm}^3} = 0.8\text{ g/cm}^3$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Since this is less than the density of water (1.0 g/cm³), the wood will float.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Three States of Matter</h2>



<p data-narration-id="n9">Matter exists in three main states: <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter where particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement and can only vibrate.">solid</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter where particles are close together but can move past each other.">liquid</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter where particles are far apart and move randomly at high speeds.">gas</dfn>. The differences between these states depend on how the particles are arranged and how they move.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Describe how particles move in solids, liquids, and gases without looking.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In solids, particles vibrate in fixed positions. In liquids, particles can slide past each other. In gases, particles move freely and rapidly in all directions.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n11">Solids</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In a solid, particles are packed closely together in a regular pattern. They are held in place by strong forces of attraction and can only vibrate about their fixed positions. This gives solids their definite shape and volume.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Liquids</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In a liquid, particles are still close together but not in a regular arrangement. The forces between particles are weaker than in solids, allowing particles to move past each other. This means liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Gases</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In a gas, particles are far apart and move randomly at high speeds. The forces between particles are very weak. Gases have no definite shape or volume and will expand to fill any container.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Changes of State</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">When we heat or cool a substance, we can change its state. These changes are physical changes — the substance itself doesn’t change, only the arrangement and movement of its particles.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Six Changes of State</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">There are six possible changes of state:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Melting:</strong> solid → liquid (heating)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Freezing:</strong> liquid → solid (cooling)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Evaporation/Boiling:</strong> liquid → gas (heating)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Condensation:</strong> gas → liquid (cooling)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Sublimation:</strong> solid → gas (heating)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Deposition:</strong> gas → solid (cooling)</li>
</ul>
</div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Temperature During Changes of State</h3>

<p data-narration-id="n27">During a change of state, the temperature remains constant even though we continue to supply energy. This is because the energy is being used to break or form bonds between particles rather than increase their kinetic energy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the two temperature points where state changes occur and what they're called.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The temperature at which a solid melts is called its melting point. The temperature at which a liquid boils is called its boiling point. These are characteristic properties of each substance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Density Changes During State Changes</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n30">When a substance changes state, its density usually changes too. This happens because the particles become more or less tightly packed together, changing the volume while the mass stays the same.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n31">Generally, solids are denser than liquids, which are denser than gases. However, there are important exceptions — ice is less dense than liquid water, which is why ice floats.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Ice Floats on Water</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n32">Water is unusual because its solid form (ice) is less dense than its liquid form. When water freezes, the molecules arrange themselves in a hexagonal crystal structure that takes up more space than the liquid arrangement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Ice has a density of about 0.92 g/cm³ while liquid water has a density of 1.00 g/cm³. This 8% difference is enough to make ice float, which is crucial for life on Earth as it insulates water beneath from freezing completely.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Practical Applications</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n35">Understanding density and states of matter has many practical applications. Engineers use density to choose materials for different purposes — aircraft need low-density materials to reduce weight, while ship anchors need high-density materials to sink effectively.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n36">In everyday life, we use changes of state constantly: cooking involves melting butter and boiling water, while refrigeration relies on <dfn class="term" data-def="The change of state from gas to liquid, releasing energy.">condensation</dfn> and evaporation cycles.</p>

<div class="exam-tip">
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Exam Tip</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n38">When calculating density, always check your units carefully. Make sure mass is in kg and volume is in m³ for the standard SI unit of kg/m³. Remember that during changes of state, temperature stays constant — this is a common exam question.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion">
<h3 data-narration-id="n39">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n40">Density equals mass divided by volume and is measured in kg/m³</li>
<li data-narration-id="n41">The three states of matter differ in particle arrangement and movement</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">During changes of state, temperature remains constant while energy breaks or forms particle bonds</li>
</ul>
</div>
