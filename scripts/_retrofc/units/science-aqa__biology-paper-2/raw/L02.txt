--- DESCRIPTION ---
How hormones coordinate body functions, and how the pancreas controls blood glucose levels.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Endocrine System</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to control body functions. Responses are slower but longer-lasting than nervous responses.">endocrine system</dfn> is made up of glands that produce <dfn class="term" data-def="Chemical messengers produced by endocrine glands and carried in the blood to target organs, where they trigger a specific response.">hormones</dfn>. Hormones are chemical messengers secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs where they produce an effect. Compared to the nervous system, hormonal responses are <strong>slower</strong> but <strong>longer-lasting</strong> and act on a <strong>wider area</strong>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A small gland in the brain that is often called the 'master gland' because it secretes hormones that stimulate other endocrine glands to release their own hormones.">pituitary gland</dfn> in the brain is called the “master gland” because it produces hormones that act on other glands, stimulating them to release their own hormones. Key endocrine glands include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Pituitary gland</strong> (brain) — produces growth hormone, FSH, LH, ADH, TSH.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Thyroid gland</strong> (neck) — produces thyroxine, which regulates metabolic rate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Pancreas</strong> — produces insulin and glucagon to control blood glucose.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Adrenal glands</strong> (above kidneys) — produce adrenaline for the “fight or flight” response.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Ovaries</strong> — produce oestrogen and progesterone.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Testes</strong> — produce testosterone.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name which gland is called the 'master gland' and explain why hormones give slower but longer-lasting responses.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The pituitary gland is the “master gland” — it produces hormones that control other glands. Hormones travel in the blood to target organs and produce slower but longer-lasting responses than nerves.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Blood Glucose Control</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Blood glucose concentration is monitored and controlled by the <dfn class="term" data-def="An organ that monitors blood glucose levels and produces insulin (when glucose is too high) and glucagon (when glucose is too low).">pancreas</dfn>. When blood glucose rises (e.g. after eating), the pancreas releases <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the pancreas that causes cells to take up glucose from the blood, and the liver to convert glucose to glycogen for storage, lowering blood glucose levels.">insulin</dfn>. Insulin causes glucose to move from the blood into cells, and stimulates the liver to convert glucose into <dfn class="term" data-def="A storage carbohydrate found in the liver and muscles. Glucose is converted to glycogen when blood glucose is high and converted back when it is low.">glycogen</dfn> for storage. This lowers blood glucose back to normal.</p>



<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n13">When blood glucose falls too low (e.g. during exercise or between meals), the pancreas releases <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the pancreas that causes the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose, releasing it into the blood to raise blood glucose levels.">glucagon</dfn>. Glucagon causes the liver to convert glycogen back into glucose, which is released into the blood. This raises blood glucose back to normal. This is a classic example of <strong>negative feedback</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which hormone lowers glucose, which raises it, where both are made, and name the two liver conversions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Insulin lowers blood glucose (glucose → glycogen in liver). Glucagon raises blood glucose (glycogen → glucose from liver). Both are produced by the pancreas in a negative feedback loop.</p>
</div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the pancreas does not produce enough insulin. It is usually genetic and is treated with insulin injections.">Type 1 diabetes</dfn> is a condition where the pancreas produces little or no insulin. It is usually diagnosed in childhood, is not linked to lifestyle, and is treated with <strong>insulin injections</strong>. The patient must also monitor their diet and blood glucose levels.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the body’s cells no longer respond properly to insulin. It is linked to obesity and can be controlled by diet and exercise.">Type 2 diabetes</dfn> is where the body’s cells no longer respond to insulin properly (insulin resistance). It is linked to obesity and lifestyle factors. It is treated with a <strong>carbohydrate-controlled diet</strong>, increased exercise and sometimes medication. Type 2 diabetes is becoming more common worldwide due to rising obesity levels.</p>
  </div></div>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">The nervous system transmits electrical impulses along neurones for fast, short-lived, localised responses. The endocrine system uses hormones carried in the blood for slower, longer-lasting, widespread responses. Both systems work together to coordinate body functions — for example, the hypothalamus in the brain links the two systems.</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The endocrine system uses hormones (chemical messengers) in the blood; the pituitary gland is the “master gland”.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Insulin (high glucose → glycogen) and glucagon (low glucose → glucose) control blood glucose via negative feedback.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Type 1 diabetes: no insulin produced (treat with injections). Type 2: insulin resistance (treat with diet/exercise).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When explaining blood glucose control, always include the hormone, the gland that produces it, and the specific effect on the liver. A common mistake is confusing insulin and glucagon — remember: <strong>insulin</strong> = glucose goes <strong>in</strong> to cells (blood glucose decreases).</p>
