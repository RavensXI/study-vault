--- DESCRIPTION ---
How energy and biomass flow through food chains, and why transfer is inefficient.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Trophic Levels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In an ecosystem, organisms can be classified according to their <dfn class="term" data-def="The position an organism occupies in a food chain, indicating its feeding relationship.">trophic level</dfn> — their position in the food chain. Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight, which producers (plants) convert into chemical energy through photosynthesis. This energy then passes from one trophic level to the next as organisms feed on each other.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List what organisms are found at trophic levels 1, 2, 3, and 4, including the specific terms producers, primary consumers, secondar...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Trophic level 1 = <dfn class="term" data-def="Organisms (mainly plants and algae) that make their own food through photosynthesis and form the base of food chains.">producers</dfn> (plants/algae). Trophic level 2 = primary consumers (herbivores). Trophic level 3 = secondary consumers (carnivores that eat herbivores). Trophic level 4 = tertiary consumers (top predators).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total mass of living material in organisms at each trophic level.">Biomass</dfn> is the mass of living material at each trophic level. It decreases at each successive level because not all the biomass from one level is transferred to the next. This can be represented using a <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram where horizontal bars represent the biomass at each trophic level, with producers at the base.">pyramid of biomass</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Biomass Transfer Is Inefficient</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">At each trophic level, only a small proportion of the biomass is transferred to the next level. Approximately 10% of the biomass at one level is passed on. The rest is lost because:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">Not all parts of organisms are eaten (e.g. roots, bones, feathers).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">Some material is lost in <dfn class="term" data-def="Waste material produced by organisms, including faeces and urine, which contains energy not absorbed during digestion.">excretory products</dfn> such as faeces, urine, and carbon dioxide.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">A large proportion of biomass is used in <dfn class="term" data-def="The chemical process in cells that releases energy from glucose, producing carbon dioxide and water.">respiration</dfn> to release energy for life processes. This energy is ultimately lost as heat to the surroundings.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the complete formula for calculating efficiency of biomass transfer, including the multiplication by 100.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The efficiency of biomass transfer between trophic levels can be calculated using:
  $$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Biomass transferred to next level}}{\text{Biomass at previous level}} \times 100$$</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Example:</strong> Grass contains 50,000 kJ of energy. Rabbits that eat the grass contain 5,000 kJ. Calculate the efficiency of transfer.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{5000}{50000} \times 100 = 10\%$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">This means 90% of the energy was lost between these trophic levels — through respiration, excretion, and uneaten material.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Implications for Food Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Because biomass transfer is so inefficient, food chains are usually short (3–5 trophic levels). There simply isn’t enough energy left to support more levels. This has important implications for human food production.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The efficiency of food production can be improved by reducing the number of trophic levels. Eating plants directly (as <dfn class="term" data-def="A primary consumer, feeding at trophic level 2, which is the most energy-efficient way for humans to obtain food.">primary consumers</dfn>) is more efficient than eating animals that have eaten plants (secondary consumers), because less energy is lost through respiration at fewer trophic levels.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factory Farming and Energy Efficiency</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Some farmers use intensive farming methods to increase the efficiency of energy transfer to animals:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Restricting movement</strong> — animals are kept in confined spaces so they use less energy on movement, meaning more energy goes into growth (meat production).</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Controlling temperature</strong> — heated or insulated buildings mean animals lose less energy keeping warm.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>High-energy feeds</strong> — carefully formulated diets maximise growth rates.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">However, these methods raise <strong>ethical concerns</strong> about animal welfare. Many people argue that animals should be free to exhibit natural behaviours, even if this reduces farming efficiency.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why eating plants directly gives 10-20 kJ from 100 kJ of grain energy, while eating meat only gives 10 kJ from the same amo...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eating plants directly is more energy-efficient than eating meat because fewer trophic levels means less energy lost through respiration. For every 100 kJ of energy in grain, approximately 10 kJ reaches a human who eats the animal that ate the grain — but approximately 10–20 kJ reaches a human who eats the grain directly.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Trophic levels describe an organism’s position in a food chain (producers → primary consumers → secondary consumers → tertiary consumers).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Only about 10% of biomass is transferred between trophic levels — the rest is lost through respiration, excretion, and uneaten material.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Eating plants directly is more energy-efficient than eating meat. Intensive farming increases efficiency but raises ethical concerns about animal welfare.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">When calculating efficiency of biomass transfer, make sure you use the correct formula and show your working. The most common mistake is dividing the wrong way round. Remember: it’s <strong>biomass at the higher level divided by biomass at the lower level</strong>, times 100. In extended response questions about food production, always discuss the ethical implications of intensive farming alongside the efficiency benefits.</p>
