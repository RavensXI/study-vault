<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Types of Electrical Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every electrical system needs a power supply. In engineering there are two fundamentally different types: <dfn class="term" data-def="Alternating current — electricity in which the direction of current flow reverses many times per second, forming a sine wave. UK mains is 230 V AC at 50 Hz.">alternating current (AC)</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Direct current — electricity that flows continuously in one direction only. Batteries and power-supply units (PSUs) produce DC.">direct current (DC)</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">UK mains electricity is 230&thinsp;V AC at 50&thinsp;Hz. The voltage swings between a positive peak and a negative peak 50 times per second, producing a smooth sine wave. If you display mains AC on an oscilloscope you see a repeating wave; if you display battery DC you see a flat horizontal line. Mains AC is used for high-power fixed equipment (motors, heaters, industrial machinery). Batteries produce DC and are used for portable, low-power and safety-critical applications.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write three differences between mains AC and battery DC from memory, including typical voltage, waveform shape and portability.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mains AC: 230&thinsp;V, sine-wave waveform, high power, fixed location, not portable. Battery DC: typically 1.5&ndash;12&thinsp;V, flat-line waveform, lower power, portable, finite energy store. Electronic circuits (microcontrollers, sensors, logic gates) require DC &mdash; a power supply unit (PSU) converts mains AC to regulated DC.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Transformers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An electromagnetic device that steps AC voltage up or down using two coils (primary and secondary) wound on a shared iron core.">transformer</dfn> changes the voltage of an AC supply. It consists of a <strong>primary coil</strong> (input) and a <strong>secondary coil</strong> (output) wound around a shared iron core. When AC flows in the primary coil, a changing magnetic field is created in the core; this induces a voltage in the secondary coil. Transformers only work with AC &mdash; a DC current creates a static magnetic field that induces nothing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The relationship between voltages and number of turns is:
$$\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$$
where \(V_p\) is primary voltage, \(V_s\) is secondary voltage, \(N_p\) is number of primary turns and \(N_s\) is number of secondary turns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Example: a transformer has 2000 primary turns and 100 secondary turns. If the primary is connected to 230&thinsp;V AC:
$$V_s = \frac{V_p \times N_s}{N_p} = \frac{230 \times 100}{2000} = 11.5\text{ V}$$
This is a <strong>step-down transformer</strong> (output lower than input), used to produce safe low-voltage DC from mains. Step-up transformers increase voltage (more secondary turns than primary) and are used in electricity grid transmission.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit that converts AC into DC by allowing current to flow in one direction only, using diodes.">rectifier</dfn> converts AC into DC. A single diode produces <strong>half-wave rectification</strong> &mdash; it blocks every negative half-cycle, leaving only the positive pulses. A <strong>bridge rectifier</strong> uses four diodes arranged in a diamond so that both the positive and negative half-cycles contribute to the output (full-wave rectification). The output is pulsing DC, which is smoothed by a capacitor to produce a steady DC voltage.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In practice, a mains-powered PSU contains a step-down transformer, a bridge rectifier and a smoothing capacitor &mdash; this chain produces the stable DC that electronic circuits need. Many also include a voltage-regulator IC to hold the output at an exact value (e.g. 5&thinsp;V for a microcontroller).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Input Control Devices: Switches</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A component that manually or automatically makes or breaks an electrical circuit.">switch</dfn> is the simplest input control device. Push-to-make switches (normally open) complete the circuit when pressed; push-to-break switches (normally closed) interrupt the circuit when pressed. Toggle switches, rotary switches and limit switches (triggered by a physical stop on a machine) are all common in engineering applications. Switches are direct inputs &mdash; they require a human or a mechanical actuator to operate them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Relay &mdash; Electrically Controlled Switch</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An electromagnetic switch that uses a small control current to open or close a circuit carrying a much larger current.">relay</dfn> is an electromagnetic switch. A small current in the control coil creates a magnetic field that attracts an iron armature, closing (or opening) a separate set of contacts. The relay allows a low-voltage, low-current control signal (e.g. 5&thinsp;V from a microcontroller) to switch a high-voltage, high-current circuit (e.g. 230&thinsp;V AC motor). The two circuits are electrically isolated &mdash; a relay is the safe interface between control electronics and mains-powered equipment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why a relay is used between a microcontroller output and a 230 V motor, mentioning isolation and current.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A relay uses a small control current to switch a large load current, and keeps the two circuits electrically isolated. This protects sensitive control electronics (microcontrollers, sensors) from the high voltages and currents of mains-powered output devices like motors and heaters.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">Output devices convert electrical energy into other forms of energy. You need to know four types:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Electric motor</strong> &mdash; converts electrical energy into rotary mechanical energy. Used to drive pumps, conveyors, drills and fans. DC motors are common in battery-powered tools; AC motors power industrial machinery.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Solenoid</strong> &mdash; an electromagnet with a moveable iron core (plunger). When energised, the plunger is pulled into the coil, producing linear mechanical movement. Used in door locks, valve actuators and automatic dispensing machines. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An output device that uses an electromagnet to produce a linear push or pull movement of a metal plunger.">solenoid</dfn> converts electrical energy to linear mechanical motion.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Buzzer and bell</strong> &mdash; audible alarms that convert electrical energy to sound. A piezo buzzer uses a piezoelectric crystal that vibrates when current is applied. An electric bell uses an electromagnet and a striker on a spring.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Lamp (light)</strong> &mdash; converts electrical energy to light and heat. Indicator lamps (signal lights on control panels) and LED assemblies are output devices. LEDs are much more energy-efficient than filament lamps.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Choosing a Power Supply</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The choice between mains and battery depends on four factors: power requirement, portability, safety and cost. A mains supply can deliver unlimited power continuously but requires fixed wiring and is dangerous if mishandled. Batteries are self-contained and portable but have a finite energy store and must be replaced or recharged. For safety reasons, all control electronics (sensors, microcontrollers, logic circuits) should operate at low DC voltages (typically 3.3&ndash;12&thinsp;V) &mdash; mains AC must be stepped down and rectified before it can power them.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Mains AC (230 V, 50 Hz, sine wave) powers fixed high-power equipment; battery DC (low voltage, flat-line) powers portable and control electronics &mdash; a transformer steps voltage up or down and a rectifier converts AC to DC.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The transformer equation \(V_p / V_s = N_p / N_s\) links voltages to turn counts; a step-down transformer has fewer secondary turns and is used to produce safe low-voltage DC from mains.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">A relay uses a small control current to safely switch a large load current, electrically isolating the control circuit from the power circuit; output devices (motors, solenoids, buzzers, lamps) convert electrical energy to mechanical, sound or light energy.</li>
</ul>
