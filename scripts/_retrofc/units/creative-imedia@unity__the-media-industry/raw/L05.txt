<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Senior Roles in the Media Industry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Senior roles in the media industry involve managing teams, overseeing projects, and making high-level creative and strategic decisions. These professionals ensure that media products are completed on time, on budget, and to the required quality. The OCR specification identifies five senior roles you must know.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Campaign manager</strong> — plans and oversees marketing or advertising campaigns from start to finish. They coordinate the team, set timescales, manage budgets, and ensure the campaign meets the client’s objectives. A campaign manager works across all production phases. <strong>Creative director</strong> — leads the creative vision of a project or organisation. They make decisions about the overall look, feel, and message of media products, ensuring consistency across all outputs. They work closely with designers, copywriters, and other creative roles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Director</strong> — the creative lead of a film, TV programme, or other moving image production. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The creative lead of a media production who holds the overall creative vision, works with the team from pre-production through to the final edit, and ensures all technical and creative elements come together.">director</dfn> holds the creative vision throughout the entire process, from pre-production through to the final edit. They work with the script writer, appoint heads of department, select actors with casting directors, choreograph scenes (“blocking”), instruct crew on lighting, wardrobe, and make-up, and lead the editing of the final cut. <strong>Editor</strong> — a senior role that oversees the editorial direction of content, particularly in publishing and journalism. In film/TV, the editor makes creative decisions about how footage is assembled. <strong>Production manager</strong> — manages the practical and logistical aspects of a production across <em>all stages</em>. They coordinate schedules, budgets, crew, equipment, and locations.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define: a production manager manages all stages of production.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A <strong>production manager</strong> manages all stages of production. They are responsible for the practical logistics, not the creative decisions. A <strong>director</strong> holds the creative vision and makes artistic decisions. Both roles span multiple production phases.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">How Roles Contribute to Media Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Every role in the media industry contributes to the final product in a specific way. The exam may ask you to explain how a particular role contributes to a given scenario. To answer well, you need to connect the role’s responsibilities to the specific media product being discussed.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">In <strong>pre-production</strong>, the script writer creates the screenplay, the director develops the creative vision, the production manager organises the schedule and budget, and the graphic designer creates concept art. In <strong>production</strong>, the camera operator films scenes under the director’s guidance, the audio technician records sound, the photographer captures promotional stills, and actors perform. In <strong>post-production</strong>, the video editor assembles the footage, the sound editor mixes audio, VFX artists add digital effects, and the director oversees the final cut. In <strong>distribution</strong>, the finished product is converted into appropriate formats and released to audiences through cinemas, streaming platforms, or broadcasts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Freelance vs Employed Work</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">People in the media industry can work as either <dfn class="term" data-def="A self-employed worker who is hired for specific projects or contracts rather than being permanently employed by one company.">freelance</dfn> workers or as permanent employees. Many creative and technical professionals in the media industry work freelance, taking on projects for different clients. This is especially common for roles like photographers, animators, graphic designers, and web developers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Freelance advantages:</strong> flexibility to choose projects and working hours, ability to work for multiple clients, potential to earn more by setting own rates, variety of work across different sectors. <strong>Freelance disadvantages:</strong> no guaranteed income or job security, must manage own tax, insurance, and business expenses, no paid holiday or sick leave, must constantly find new clients.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Employed advantages:</strong> regular salary and job security, paid holiday, sick pay, and pension contributions, training and career development provided by employer, clear career progression pathway. <strong>Employed disadvantages:</strong> less flexibility over working hours and projects, may earn less than successful freelancers, limited variety of work, may need to follow company creative direction rather than own vision.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the camera shots/angles and their purposes.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The size and scale of a project affects how roles are allocated. In a <strong>large production</strong>, each role is performed by a specialist. In a <strong>small project</strong>, one person may perform multiple roles (e.g. a camera operator might also edit the video and mix the sound).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Career Paths in the Media Industry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The media industry offers many <dfn class="term" data-def="The sequence of job roles a person moves through over their working life, typically progressing from junior to senior positions.">career paths</dfn>. Creative iMedia provides a foundation for further study and work in media-related fields. Progression routes from this qualification include A Level Media Studies, Cambridge Technicals in Digital Media and Information Technology (Level 2 or 3), T Level qualifications in Digital Production Design and Development or Media, Broadcast and Production, and apprenticeships such as the Media and Broadcast Assistant Pathway.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">A person interested in film might start as a runner (assisting on set), progress to camera operator or assistant director, and eventually become a director or production manager. A web designer might start as a junior designer, learn coding to become a web developer, and eventually become a creative director at a digital agency. A content creator might start with a personal social media channel and progress to working for a marketing agency as a campaign manager. The transferable skills developed in Creative iMedia — creative thinking, digital presentation, planning, and problem solving — are valuable across many industries.</p>

These skills are useful not just in the media industry but in any career.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Senior roles (campaign manager, creative director, director, editor, production manager) involve managing teams and overseeing projects across multiple production phases.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Media professionals can work freelance (flexible but no guaranteed income) or employed (secure but less flexible). On small projects, one person may perform multiple roles.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Creative iMedia develops transferable skills including creative thinking, planning, digital presentation, and problem solving that support career progression in media and beyond.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">The exam often sets scenario questions where a media company is producing a specific product. When asked how a job role would contribute, always link the role’s responsibilities to the specific scenario. For example, if asked how a graphic designer contributes to a promotional campaign, don’t just describe what a graphic designer does in general — explain what they would create <em>for that campaign</em> (e.g. designing the logo, creating poster layouts, producing social media graphics).</p>
