--- DESCRIPTION ---
Types of media products from video and audio to AR/VR, and the sectors that use them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Types of Media Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The media industry produces a wide range of different <dfn class="term" data-def="Any piece of content created by the media industry, such as a video, animation, website, game, or graphic.">media products</dfn>. You need to know what these products are, how they are used, and which sectors produce them. The R093 specification lists thirteen types of product that you must be able to identify and describe. Importantly, the same product can be used by different sectors.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the audio terms and technical specifications here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The 13 media products you must know: <strong>video, audio, music, animation, special effects (SFX/VFX), digital imaging and graphics, social media platforms/apps, digital games, comics and graphic novels, websites, multimedia, eBooks, AR/VR</strong>.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Audio-Visual Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Video</strong> includes any moving image content such as films, TV programmes, online videos, and advertisements. Video is used across multiple sectors including film, television, internet, and interactive media. <strong>Audio</strong> covers sound-based content including radio broadcasts, podcasts, sound effects, and voiceovers. Audio is used in radio, internet, film, and digital publishing. <strong>Music</strong> is a specific type of audio product created for entertainment, advertising, or to accompany other media. Music is used in film, television, radio, computer games, and internet platforms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Animation</strong> involves creating the illusion of movement using a sequence of images. There are several types of animation you should know: <dfn class="term" data-def="A traditional form of animation where each frame is drawn by hand on transparent sheets called cels.">traditional/cel animation</dfn>, 2D animation, 3D animation, motion graphics animation, and stop motion animation. Animation is used in film, television, computer games, internet, and interactive media.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Traditional/cel animation</strong> — each frame is drawn by hand on transparent sheets. Disney’s early films like Snow White used this technique. <strong>2D animation</strong> — digital drawing on a flat plane, used in cartoons and explainer videos. <strong>3D animation</strong> — computer-generated models that exist in three dimensions, used in films like Toy Story and modern video games. <strong>Motion graphics</strong> — animated graphic design elements like moving text, logos, and infographics. <strong>Stop motion</strong> — physical objects are moved in small increments between individual photographs, creating movement when played in sequence. Wallace and Gromit is a well-known example.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Special Effects: SFX and VFX</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">There is an important distinction between <dfn class="term" data-def="Special effects that are physical and created during production on set, such as make-up, prosthetics, pyrotechnics, and animatronics.">SFX (special effects)</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Visual effects that are added digitally after production using computer-generated imagery (CGI), green screens, and compositing.">VFX (visual effects)</dfn>. SFX are physical effects created during production on set — examples include make-up, prosthetics, pyrotechnics/explosions, live weather effects, and animatronics. VFX are digital effects added after production using <dfn class="term" data-def="Computer-Generated Imagery — the creation of visual content using computer software, often used to create realistic environments, characters, or effects in film and games.">CGI</dfn> — examples include digital explosions, virtual backdrops, monsters/animals, and magic/superpowers.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/the-media-industry/diagram_products_in_the_media_industry.jpg" alt="Diagram: Products in the Media Industry" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define: sfx = physical effects created during production.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>SFX</strong> = physical effects created <strong>during production</strong> (on set). <strong>VFX</strong> = digital effects added <strong>after production</strong> (in post-production using CGI). Both are used in film, TV, and computer games.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Digital and Interactive Products</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Digital imaging and graphics</strong> covers photographs, illustrations, logos, and graphic designs created or edited digitally. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A digital image made up of tiny square units called pixels, where the quality depends on the number of pixels.">bitmap/raster graphic</dfn> is a digital image composed of pixels. Graphics are used across virtually all media sectors. <strong>Social media platforms/apps</strong> are online services that allow users to create and share content, such as Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. <strong>Digital games</strong> are interactive entertainment played on consoles, PCs, or mobile devices. <strong>Comics and graphic novels</strong> combine illustrations and text to tell stories — they can be physical or digital.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Websites</strong> are collections of web pages accessed through browsers, used for information, e-commerce, entertainment, and communication. <strong>Multimedia</strong> products combine multiple types of media (text, images, audio, video, animation) in a single product, such as an interactive presentation or educational app. <strong>eBooks</strong> are digital versions of books that can be read on devices like Kindle or tablets.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Augmented Reality (AR)</strong> combines computer-generated images with the real-world environment on a screen. The user sees the physical world with digital elements overlaid on top. Examples include Pokémon Go, Snapchat filters, and Google search AR features. <strong>Virtual Reality (VR)</strong> is a fully immersive computer-simulated environment that replaces the user’s real-world surroundings. The user wears a headset and experiences a completely digital world. Examples include VR gaming, virtual tours, and training simulations. Both AR and VR are used in film, computer games, and interactive media.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Products Across Sectors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A critical concept for the exam is that the same product can be used by different sectors. Video is used in film (cinema), television (broadcast), internet (streaming and social media), and interactive media (embedded in websites). Audio is used in radio (broadcast), internet (podcasts), and film (soundtracks). Understanding which products belong to which sectors — and that many products cross sector boundaries — is essential for the R093 exam.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exam structure, timings, and mark allocations.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The exam will often ask you to match products to sectors or explain why a particular product is suitable for a specific sector. Always think about the <strong>purpose</strong> of the product and the <strong>audience</strong> it is trying to reach.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">There are 13 types of media products you must know for the R093 exam, including video, audio, animation, SFX/VFX, digital games, websites, eBooks, and AR/VR.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">SFX (special effects) are physical and created during production on set, while VFX (visual effects) are digital and added after production using CGI.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The same media product can be used across different sectors — always link products to specific sectors and explain why they are suitable for the audience and purpose.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">A common exam question asks you to distinguish between SFX and VFX. Remember: SFX are <strong>physical</strong> and happen <strong>during production</strong> (on set). VFX are <strong>digital</strong> and happen <strong>after production</strong> (post-production). Do not write “CGI” or “green screen” as examples of VFX — these are <em>how</em> VFX is created, not examples of VFX itself. Instead, give examples like digital explosions, virtual backdrops, or digitally created creatures.</p>
