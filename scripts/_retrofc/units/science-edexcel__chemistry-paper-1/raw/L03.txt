<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Ionic Bonding?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When metals react with non-metals, atoms transfer electrons from one to the other. The metal atom <strong>loses</strong> electrons and becomes a positively charged ion called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses one or more electrons.">cation</dfn>. The non-metal atom <strong>gains</strong> those electrons and becomes a negatively charged ion called an <dfn class="term" data-def="A negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains one or more electrons.">anion</dfn>. The strong electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged ions is an <dfn class="term" data-def="The strong electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions.">ionic bond</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">An <dfn class="term" data-def="An atom or group of atoms that carries a positive or negative electrical charge.">ion</dfn> is simply an atom (or group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons, giving it a net charge. In a neutral atom the number of protons equals the number of electrons. Once electrons are transferred, this balance is broken and the particle carries a charge.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what happens to the electrons when a metal atom reacts with a non-metal atom to form an ionic bond?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Ionic bonds form by the <strong>transfer</strong> of electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom. The metal loses electrons to form a positive ion (cation); the non-metal gains electrons to form a negative ion (anion). Both ions end up with a full outer shell of electrons, giving them a stable electronic configuration.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Ion Formation — Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">You need to predict which ions elements form based on their position in the periodic table. The Edexcel specification limits ionic compound formation to elements in Groups 1, 2, 6 and 7:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Group 1</strong> elements (e.g. sodium, potassium) lose <strong>1 electron</strong> to form ions with a +1 charge — for example, \(\text{Na}^+\) and \(\text{K}^+\). <strong>Group 2</strong> elements (e.g. magnesium, calcium) lose <strong>2 electrons</strong> to form ions with a +2 charge — for example, \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) and \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Group 7</strong> elements (e.g. chlorine, bromine) gain <strong>1 electron</strong> to form ions with a −1 charge — for example, \(\text{Cl}^-\) and \(\text{Br}^-\). <strong>Group 6</strong> elements (e.g. oxygen, sulfur) gain <strong>2 electrons</strong> to form ions with a −2 charge — for example, \(\text{O}^{2-}\) and \(\text{S}^{2-}\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Calculating Protons, Neutrons and Electrons in Ions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When an atom forms an ion, the number of protons stays the same but the number of electrons changes. A sodium atom has 11 protons and 11 electrons. When it forms \(\text{Na}^+\), it still has 11 protons but only 10 electrons. A chlorine atom has 17 protons and 17 electrons; as \(\text{Cl}^-\) it has 17 protons and 18 electrons.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">To find the electrons in any simple ion: start with the atomic number (number of electrons in a neutral atom), then subtract the charge for positive ions or add the charge for negative ions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Naming Ionic Compounds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Ionic compounds are named using specific rules. When a compound contains just two elements, its name ends in <strong>–ide</strong>. For example, sodium and chlorine form sodium chlor<em>ide</em>; magnesium and oxygen form magnesium ox<em>ide</em>. When a compound contains a metal, a non-metal <strong>and oxygen</strong>, its name typically ends in <strong>–ate</strong>. For example, copper sulfate contains copper, sulfur and oxygen; calcium carbonate contains calcium, carbon and oxygen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what ending does a compound's name have if it contains two elements? What if it contains oxygen as well?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Names ending in <strong>–ide</strong> tell you the compound contains only two elements (e.g. sodium chloride = sodium + chlorine). Names ending in <strong>–ate</strong> tell you the compound also contains oxygen (e.g. sodium sulfate = sodium + sulfur + oxygen).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Deducing Formulae of Ionic Compounds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">To work out the formula of an ionic compound, you need to balance the charges so they cancel out to zero. For sodium chloride: \(\text{Na}^+\) has a +1 charge and \(\text{Cl}^-\) has a −1 charge, so one of each gives NaCl. For magnesium chloride: \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) needs two \(\text{Cl}^-\) ions to balance, giving \(\text{MgCl}_2\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Ionic Lattice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Ionic compounds do not exist as individual pairs of ions. Instead, billions of ions arrange themselves into a regular, repeating three-dimensional structure called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of ions held together by strong electrostatic forces.">lattice</dfn>. Each positive ion is surrounded by negative ions, and each negative ion is surrounded by positive ions. The strong <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of attraction between particles with opposite electrical charges.">electrostatic forces</dfn> of attraction between these oppositely charged ions act in all directions throughout the lattice, holding the structure firmly together.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: why do ionic compounds have high melting points? Use the words 'lattice', 'electrostatic' and 'energy'.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Ionic compounds form giant lattice structures. The strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions require a large amount of energy to overcome, which is why ionic compounds have <strong>high melting and boiling points</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Properties of Ionic Compounds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Ionic compounds share several distinctive properties, all explained by their lattice structure:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>High melting and boiling points</strong> — the strong ionic bonds throughout the lattice need a lot of energy to break. <strong>Conductivity when solid</strong> — ionic compounds do <em>not</em> conduct electricity as solids because the ions are locked in fixed positions and cannot move. <strong>Conductivity when molten or dissolved</strong> — when melted or dissolved in water, the ions become free to move and can carry an electrical current, so the compound conducts.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26">When an ionic compound dissolves in water, the lattice breaks apart and the individual ions become surrounded by water molecules. These free ions can now move through the solution towards electrodes. Positive ions (cations) move to the negative electrode and negative ions (anions) move to the positive electrode, carrying the charge and completing the circuit. This is why ionic compounds only conduct when molten or in solution — never as a solid.</p>
</div></div>
