<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Acids and Alkalis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water.">acid</dfn> is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (\(H^+\)) when dissolved in water. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A soluble base that produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) when dissolved in water.">alkali</dfn> is a soluble base that produces hydroxide ions (\(OH^-\)) when dissolved in water. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A scale from 0 to 14 measuring how acidic or alkaline a solution is.">pH scale</dfn> runs from 0 (strongly acidic) through 7 (neutral) to 14 (strongly alkaline).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Common indicators help identify whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or alkaline. <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural indicator that turns red in acid and blue in alkali.">Litmus</dfn> turns red in acid and blue in alkali. Methyl orange turns red in acid and yellow in alkali. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and pink in alkali.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What ions do acids produce? What ions do alkalis produce? What pH is neutral?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Acids produce \(H^+\) ions in solution. Alkalis produce \(OH^-\) ions in solution. A neutral solution has pH 7. Below pH 7 is acidic; above pH 7 is alkaline.</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Strong and Weak Acids</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An acid that fully dissociates (ionises) into ions in solution.">strong acid</dfn> is one that completely dissociates (breaks apart) into ions in water. Examples include hydrochloric acid (\(HCl\)), sulfuric acid (\(H_2SO_4\)), and nitric acid (\(HNO_3\)). A <dfn class="term" data-def="An acid that only partially dissociates into ions in solution.">weak acid</dfn> only partially dissociates — most of its molecules remain intact. Examples include ethanoic acid and citric acid.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The terms <strong>dilute</strong> and <strong>concentrated</strong> refer to how much acid is dissolved in a given volume of water. A dilute solution has a small amount of acid per unit volume; a concentrated solution has a large amount. The terms dilute/concentrated are about how much solute is present, while strong/weak are about how much the acid ionises.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The higher the concentration of \(H^+\) ions, the lower the pH. As the hydrogen ion concentration increases by a factor of 10, the pH decreases by 1.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Bases and Neutralisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water only.">base</dfn> is any substance that reacts with an acid to produce a salt and water only. Alkalis are bases that dissolve in water. <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction between an acid and a base that produces a salt and water.">Neutralisation</dfn> is the reaction between an acid and a base.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The ionic equation for an acid–alkali neutralisation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Acids react with different types of bases to produce salts:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Acid + metal \(\rightarrow\) salt + hydrogen</strong> (e.g. \(Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2\))</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Acid + metal oxide \(\rightarrow\) salt + water</strong> (e.g. \(CuO + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow CuSO_4 + H_2O\))</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Acid + metal hydroxide \(\rightarrow\) salt + water</strong> (e.g. \(NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O\))</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Acid + metal carbonate \(\rightarrow\) salt + water + carbon dioxide</strong> (e.g. \(CaCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2\))</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the four general equations for acid reactions with: a metal, a metal oxide, a metal hydroxide, and a metal carbonate.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The name of the salt produced depends on the acid used: hydrochloric acid makes chlorides, sulfuric acid makes sulfates, and nitric acid makes nitrates. The metal in the salt comes from the base.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Making Soluble Salts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Soluble salts can be made in two ways, depending on whether the base is soluble or insoluble.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Method 1: Using an insoluble base (e.g. metal oxide, carbonate).</strong> Add excess insoluble base to the acid. The excess base ensures all the acid has reacted. Filter off the excess solid. Evaporate the remaining solution (or leave to crystallise) to obtain the salt crystals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Method 2: Using a soluble base (alkali).</strong> A <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for measuring the exact volume of one solution needed to react with a known volume of another.">titration</dfn> must be used. Measure the acid into a flask using a pipette, add a few drops of indicator, then add the alkali from a burette until the indicator shows neutralisation. Mix the acid and alkali in exactly the right proportions (without indicator this time), then evaporate to obtain salt crystals.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n24">To prepare pure, dry hydrated copper sulfate crystals: (1) Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker. (2) Add excess copper oxide powder and stir until no more dissolves. (3) Filter off the excess copper oxide. (4) Pour the blue copper sulfate solution into an evaporating basin. (5) Heat gently on a water bath until crystals start to form, then leave to cool and crystallise. (6) Pat dry with filter paper.</p>
</div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Solubility Rules and Precipitates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Solubility rules help you predict whether a salt is soluble or insoluble in water:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>All</strong> sodium, potassium and ammonium salts are soluble. <strong>All</strong> nitrates are soluble. <strong>Most</strong> chlorides are soluble (except silver chloride and lead chloride). <strong>Most</strong> sulfates are soluble (except lead sulfate, barium sulfate and calcium sulfate). <strong>Most</strong> carbonates and hydroxides are insoluble (except those of sodium, potassium and ammonium).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">When two solutions are mixed, if the ions can form an insoluble compound, a <dfn class="term" data-def="An insoluble solid formed when two solutions containing soluble salts are mixed.">precipitate</dfn> forms. For example, mixing silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution produces an insoluble white precipitate of silver chloride.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write a list of the solubility rules from memory. Which chlorides are insoluble? Which sulfates?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>An insoluble salt can be made by mixing two solutions that contain the required ions. The precipitate is filtered off, washed with distilled water, and dried.</p>
</div>
