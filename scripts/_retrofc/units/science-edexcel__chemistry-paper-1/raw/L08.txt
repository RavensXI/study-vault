<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Electrolysis?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The decomposition of an ionic compound using electrical energy from a direct current supply.">Electrolysis</dfn> is the process of using electrical energy from a direct current (d.c.) supply to decompose (break down) an ionic compound. The ionic compound must be either molten or dissolved in water so that its ions are free to move. In this state, the compound is called an <dfn class="term" data-def="An ionic compound that is molten or dissolved in water, allowing its ions to move and conduct electricity.">electrolyte</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">During electrolysis, two <dfn class="term" data-def="Solid conductors through which electric current enters or leaves the electrolyte during electrolysis.">electrodes</dfn> are placed in the electrolyte and connected to a d.c. power supply. Positively charged ions (<dfn class="term" data-def="A positively charged ion that moves to the cathode during electrolysis.">cations</dfn>) are attracted to the negatively charged electrode (the <dfn class="term" data-def="The negative electrode in electrolysis, where cations are reduced.">cathode</dfn>). Negatively charged ions (<dfn class="term" data-def="A negatively charged ion that moves to the anode during electrolysis.">anions</dfn>) are attracted to the positively charged electrode (the <dfn class="term" data-def="The positive electrode in electrolysis, where anions are oxidised.">anode</dfn>).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Which electrode do cations go to? Which electrode do anions go to? What charge does each electrode have?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Cations (positive ions) migrate to the cathode (negative electrode). Anions (negative ions) migrate to the anode (positive electrode). Remember: <strong>Positive And Negative = PAN</strong> — Positive goes to the Anode? No! Positive goes to Negative (cathode).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Electrolysis of Specific Substances</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When electrolysing molten ionic compounds (e.g. molten lead bromide), the products are straightforward: the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal forms at the anode. Lead bromide gives lead at the cathode and bromine at the anode.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Electrolysis of solutions is more complex because water also contains ions (\(H^+\) and \(OH^-\)). The product at each electrode depends on the position of the element in the reactivity series:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>At the cathode:</strong> If the metal is more reactive than hydrogen, hydrogen gas is produced. If the metal is less reactive than hydrogen, the metal is deposited.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>At the anode:</strong> If a <dfn class="term" data-def="A halogen-containing ion such as Cl⁻, Br⁻, or I⁻.">halide</dfn> ion is present, the halogen is produced. If no halide is present, oxygen is produced from the water.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Copper chloride solution:</strong> Copper at the cathode (Cu is less reactive than H), chlorine at the anode (halide present). <strong>Sodium chloride solution:</strong> Hydrogen at the cathode (Na is more reactive than H), chlorine at the anode (halide present). <strong>Sodium sulfate solution:</strong> Hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode (no halide). <strong>Acidified water (dilute sulfuric acid):</strong> Hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Half Equations & Oxidation-Reduction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The loss of electrons by a substance during a reaction.">Oxidation</dfn> is the loss of electrons. <dfn class="term" data-def="The gain of electrons by a substance during a reaction.">Reduction</dfn> is the gain of electrons. Remember <strong>OILRIG</strong>: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">At the <strong>cathode</strong>, reduction occurs — cations gain electrons. At the <strong>anode</strong>, oxidation occurs — anions lose electrons. Half equations show what happens at each electrode separately:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For molten lead bromide:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Cathode (reduction): \(Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Anode (oxidation): \(2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-\)</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is OILRIG? Write half equations for the electrolysis of copper chloride solution from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Reduction always occurs at the cathode (cations gain electrons). Oxidation always occurs at the anode (anions lose electrons). OILRIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">When copper sulfate solution is electrolysed using <strong>copper electrodes</strong> (not inert), the copper anode dissolves and pure copper is deposited on the cathode. This is used industrially to purify copper. The impure copper is the anode, the pure copper is the cathode, and the electrolyte is copper sulfate solution. The anode loses mass and the cathode gains mass.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Cathode: \(Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu\). Anode: \(Cu \rightarrow Cu^{2+} + 2e^-\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Reactivity Series</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Metals can be ranked by their reactivity with water, dilute acids, and salt solutions. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of metals arranged in order of their reactivity, from most reactive to least reactive.">reactivity series</dfn> (most to least reactive) is: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, (hydrogen), copper, silver, gold. More reactive metals react more vigorously with water and acids, and have a greater tendency to form positive ions (cations).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction where a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound.">displacement reaction</dfn>, a more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. For example, iron displaces copper from copper sulfate solution because iron is more reactive than copper:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">$$Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu$$</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Extracting Metals from Ores</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Most metals are found in the Earth’s crust as <dfn class="term" data-def="A naturally occurring rock from which a metal can be extracted economically.">ores</dfn> — compounds that must be chemically processed to obtain the pure metal. Unreactive metals such as gold are found as uncombined elements because they do not readily react with other substances.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Extracting a metal from its ore involves <dfn class="term" data-def="The removal of oxygen from a compound; or the gain of electrons.">reduction</dfn> — removing the oxygen. The method of extraction depends on the metal’s position in the reactivity series:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Metals below carbon</strong> (e.g. iron, zinc, copper) can be extracted by heating the ore with carbon. Carbon is more reactive, so it displaces the metal. For example: \(2Fe_2O_3 + 3C \rightarrow 4Fe + 3CO_2\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Metals above carbon</strong> (e.g. aluminium, sodium) are too reactive to be extracted by carbon. They must be extracted by electrolysis of the molten ore, which is more expensive because it requires large amounts of electrical energy.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Name two metals extracted by heating with carbon and two extracted by electrolysis. Explain why the methods differ.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Metals less reactive than carbon are extracted by heating with carbon (reduction). Metals more reactive than carbon must be extracted by electrolysis. Electrolysis is more expensive due to the energy costs.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n30">Recycling metals saves energy (less mining and extraction needed), preserves finite ore reserves, and reduces landfill. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An assessment of the environmental impact of a product through its entire life: raw materials, manufacture, use, and disposal.">life-cycle assessment</dfn> evaluates the environmental impact of a product from obtaining raw materials through manufacturing, use, and disposal. These assessments help compare the true environmental cost of products and materials.</p>
</div></div>

<span>Biological Methods of Extraction</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n31"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using bacteria to extract metals from low-grade ores by producing leachate solutions containing metal compounds.">Bioleaching</dfn> uses bacteria to extract metals from low-grade ores. The bacteria produce acidic solutions that dissolve metal compounds. <dfn class="term" data-def="Using plants to absorb metal compounds from the soil; the plants are harvested and burned to obtain the metal.">Phytoextraction</dfn> uses plants that absorb metal ions from the soil through their roots. The plants are then harvested and burned, and the metal is extracted from the ash. Both methods are slower than traditional mining but are less environmentally damaging and can be used on ores with very low metal concentrations.</p>
