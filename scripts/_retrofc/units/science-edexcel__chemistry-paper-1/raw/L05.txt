--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Metallic Bonding?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Metals have a distinctive type of bonding that is different from both ionic and covalent bonding. In a metal, the atoms are arranged in a regular pattern called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of atoms or ions.">lattice</dfn>. The outer-shell electrons of each metal atom are <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrons that are free to move throughout the metallic lattice and are not associated with a single atom.">delocalised</dfn> — they are no longer attached to any particular atom and are free to move throughout the whole structure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This creates a structure of positive metal ions (cations) surrounded by a “sea” of delocalised electrons. The strong <dfn class="term" data-def="The attraction between positive metal ions and the sea of delocalised electrons in a metal.">electrostatic attraction</dfn> between the positive metal ions and the negatively charged delocalised electrons holds the structure together. This attraction is what we call a <dfn class="term" data-def="The bonding in metals, formed by the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.">metallic bond</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three things make up a metallic bond? Draw a quick labelled sketch from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons. The metal ions are arranged in a regular lattice structure.</p>
</div>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Properties of Metals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The metallic bonding model explains several key properties of metals. Most metals are shiny solids at room temperature with high melting and boiling points. This is because a large amount of energy is needed to overcome the strong electrostatic attraction between the metal ions and the delocalised electrons.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Metals are good <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to allow electricity or heat to pass through a material.">conductors</dfn> of electricity because their delocalised electrons can move freely through the lattice, carrying electrical charge. They are also good conductors of heat because the delocalised electrons transfer kinetic energy rapidly through the structure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Metals are <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a material to be hammered or pressed into shape without breaking.">malleable</dfn> (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires). When a force is applied to a metal, the layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonds, because the delocalised electrons can shift position to maintain the attraction. This is different from ionic compounds, which shatter when layers shift because ions of the same charge are forced next to each other.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three properties of metals and explain each one using the metallic bonding model.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Metals have high melting points (strong bonds), conduct electricity (delocalised electrons carry charge), and are malleable (layers slide without breaking bonds). All three properties are explained by the sea of delocalised electrons.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Most non-metals have low boiling points and are poor conductors of electricity. This is because non-metals form <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of bonding where two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.">covalent bonds</dfn> rather than metallic bonds. In simple molecular substances, the molecules are held together by weak <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between molecules; much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds.">intermolecular forces</dfn>, which require little energy to overcome. There are no delocalised electrons, so they cannot conduct electricity.</p>
</div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Comparing Types of Substance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the exam, you may need to compare the properties of ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent, and metallic substances. Each type of structure has distinctive physical properties that link directly to its bonding and structure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Ionic compounds</strong> (e.g. sodium chloride) have high melting points because of strong electrostatic forces between ions. They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water because the ions are free to move, but not when solid because ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Simple molecular substances</strong> (e.g. water, methane) have low melting and boiling points because the intermolecular forces between molecules are weak. They do not conduct electricity because they have no free charged particles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Giant covalent substances</strong> (e.g. diamond, graphite) have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken. Diamond does not conduct electricity because all electrons are involved in bonding. <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of carbon with layers of hexagonal rings; one electron per carbon atom is delocalised, making graphite a conductor.">Graphite</dfn> is unusual — it conducts electricity because each carbon atom forms only three covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that can carry charge.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A giant covalent form of carbon where each atom bonds to four others in a rigid tetrahedral structure; extremely hard.">Diamond</dfn> has each carbon atom bonded to four others in a rigid tetrahedral structure. This makes diamond extremely hard, which is why it is used in cutting tools. Graphite has carbon atoms arranged in layers of hexagons. The weak forces between the layers allow them to slide over each other, making graphite useful as a lubricant and in pencils. <dfn class="term" data-def="Molecules of carbon in the form of hollow spheres (e.g. C60) or tubes; can be used as catalysts and in drug delivery.">Fullerenes</dfn> such as \(C_{60}\) (buckminsterfullerene) are hollow carbon molecules and <dfn class="term" data-def="Cylindrical carbon molecules with excellent strength and electrical conductivity.">carbon nanotubes</dfn> are tiny cylinders of carbon atoms, both with potential uses in electronics, drug delivery, and as catalysts.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Large molecules made of many repeating smaller units (monomers) joined together in long chains.">Polymers</dfn> such as poly(ethene) are large molecules consisting of long chains of carbon atoms. Because polymer molecules are large, the intermolecular forces between them are relatively strong, giving polymers higher melting points than simple molecular substances. However, they do not conduct electricity because there are no free electrons or ions.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write a comparison table of ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent, and metallic substances covering melting point, electrical conductivity, and one example of each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four types of substance — ionic, simple molecular, giant covalent, and metallic — can be distinguished by their melting points, solubility in water, and ability to conduct electricity. These properties are explained by the type of bonding and structure present.</p>
</div>
