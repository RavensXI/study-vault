--- DESCRIPTION ---
How atoms are built from protons, neutrons and electrons, and what isotopes are.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Inside the Atom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Everything around you is made from tiny particles called <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element.">atoms</dfn>. They are incredibly small — a typical atom has a radius of about \(1 \times 10^{-10}\) m. Despite their tiny size, atoms have a definite internal structure that determines how each element behaves.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">At the centre of every atom sits a <dfn class="term" data-def="The dense, positively charged centre of an atom, made up of protons and neutrons.">nucleus</dfn>. The nucleus is extremely small compared to the overall atom — roughly 1/10,000 of the atom’s diameter — yet it contains nearly all of the atom’s mass. Around the nucleus, <dfn class="term" data-def="Negatively charged subatomic particles found in energy levels (shells) surrounding the nucleus.">electrons</dfn> move rapidly in regions called energy levels or shells.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the relative charge and relative mass of each subatomic particle?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The nucleus contains <strong>protons</strong> (relative charge +1, relative mass 1) and <strong>neutrons</strong> (relative charge 0, relative mass 1). <strong>Electrons</strong> (relative charge −1, relative mass nearly zero) orbit the nucleus in shells. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel out.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Subatomic Particles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Atoms contain three types of <dfn class="term" data-def="Particles smaller than an atom: protons, neutrons and electrons.">subatomic particles</dfn>. <dfn class="term" data-def="A positively charged particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1.">Protons</dfn> carry a positive charge and have a relative mass of 1. <dfn class="term" data-def="An uncharged particle found in the nucleus, with a relative mass of 1.">Neutrons</dfn> have no charge and also have a relative mass of 1. Electrons carry a negative charge but have a negligible mass — roughly \(\frac{1}{1836}\) of a proton’s mass.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Because protons and electrons carry equal but opposite charges, a neutral atom always contains the same number of each. If an atom has 8 protons, it also has 8 electrons. This balance makes every atom electrically neutral.</p>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the early 1800s, John Dalton proposed that atoms were solid, indivisible spheres. In 1897, J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and suggested the <dfn class="term" data-def="Thomson’s model of the atom: a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it, like plums in a pudding.">plum pudding model</dfn> — a ball of positive charge with negative electrons scattered through it. Then, in 1909–1911, Rutherford’s alpha-particle scattering experiment showed that most of an atom is empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre. Niels Bohr refined the model in 1913 by placing electrons in fixed energy levels. Finally, James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, completing the modern picture of the atom.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Atomic Number and Mass Number</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Every element has a unique <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; this defines which element it is.">atomic number</dfn> — the number of protons in its nucleus. No two different elements share the same atomic number. On the periodic table, elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.">mass number</dfn> is the total count of protons plus neutrons. To find the number of neutrons, subtract the atomic number from the mass number:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$\text{neutrons} = \text{mass number} - \text{atomic number}$$</p>
