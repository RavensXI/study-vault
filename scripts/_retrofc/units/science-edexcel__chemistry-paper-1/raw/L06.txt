<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Law of Conservation of Mass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In any chemical reaction, no atoms are created or destroyed — they are simply rearranged to form new substances. This means that the total mass of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The substances present at the start of a chemical reaction.">reactants</dfn> always equals the total mass of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The substances formed during a chemical reaction.">products</dfn>. This principle is known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that no atoms are created or destroyed in a chemical reaction, so total mass is conserved.">law of conservation of mass</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">If you carry out a reaction in a sealed (closed) container — for example, a precipitation reaction in a stoppered flask — the mass before and after the reaction will be identical. This is because nothing can enter or leave the system.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why might the mass appear to change in an open flask? Give two examples — one where mass seems to increase and one where it seems to decrease.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In an open container, mass may appear to change. If a gas is produced (e.g. \(CO_2\) from a carbonate reacting with acid), the gas escapes and the mass decreases. If a gas is taken in (e.g. when magnesium burns in air and gains oxygen), the mass of the solid product increases. In both cases, mass is actually conserved — but some of it has entered or left the container.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Relative Formula Mass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in a chemical formula; symbol Mr.">relative formula mass</dfn> (\(M_r\)) of a compound is found by adding up the relative atomic masses (\(A_r\)) of every atom in its formula. For example, for water (\(H_2O\)):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$M_r = (2 \times 1) + 16 = 18$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">For calcium carbonate (\(CaCO_3\)):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$M_r = 40 + 12 + (3 \times 16) = 100$$</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">You can calculate the <dfn class="term" data-def="The mass of a particular element in a compound as a percentage of the total relative formula mass.">percentage by mass</dfn> of an element in a compound using:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$\text{percentage by mass} = \frac{A_r \times \text{number of atoms of element}}{M_r} \times 100$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For example, the percentage by mass of oxygen in water (\(H_2O\), \(M_r = 18\)):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">$$\frac{16}{18} \times 100 = 88.9\%$$</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Balancing Equations and Reacting Masses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A balanced chemical equation shows the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. This reflects the law of conservation of mass. You can use a balanced equation to calculate the mass of reactants or products.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example — Calculating Mass from an Equation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when 10 g of calcium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">$$CaCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Step 1: Find the \(M_r\) of \(CaCO_3 = 100\) and \(CO_2 = 44\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Step 2: From the equation, 1 mole of \(CaCO_3\) produces 1 mole of \(CO_2\), so 100 g of \(CaCO_3\) produces 44 g of \(CO_2\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Step 3: Scale down: \(\frac{10}{100} \times 44 = 4.4\) g of \(CO_2\).</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Calculate the Mr of magnesium oxide (MgO) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) from memory. Check using Ar values: Mg=24, O=16, Na=23, H=1.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>To calculate reacting masses: (1) write the balanced equation, (2) find the \(M_r\) of the substances involved, (3) use the mole ratio from the equation to scale up or down from the known mass.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">The Mole</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The unit for amount of substance; one mole contains 6.02 x 10^23 particles.">mole</dfn> is a unit used to measure the amount of a substance. One mole of any substance contains exactly \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\) particles (atoms, molecules, or ions). This number is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of particles in one mole of a substance: 6.02 x 10^23.">Avogadro constant</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is numerically equal to its relative formula mass. For example, one mole of carbon (\(A_r = 12\)) has a mass of 12 g. One mole of water (\(M_r = 18\)) has a mass of 18 g.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The key equation linking mass and moles is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">$$\text{moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{M_r}$$</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Write the moles equation from memory, then calculate the number of moles in 8 g of oxygen gas (O2, Mr = 32).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>One mole of any substance contains \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\) particles (the Avogadro constant). To convert between mass and moles, use: moles = mass \(\div\) \(M_r\).</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n28">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, measured in g/dm3.">concentration</dfn> of a solution tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume. It is calculated using:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">$$\text{concentration (g/dm}^3\text{)} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (dm}^3\text{)}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">For example, if 5 g of sodium chloride is dissolved in 250 cm\(^3\) of water (= 0.25 dm\(^3\)):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">$$\text{concentration} = \frac{5}{0.25} = 20 \text{ g/dm}^3$$</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Empirical and Molecular Formulae</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.">empirical formula</dfn> shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. It can be calculated from reacting masses or percentage composition. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule of a compound.">molecular formula</dfn> shows the actual number of atoms in one molecule and can be deduced from the empirical formula and the relative molecular mass.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">For example, ethane has the molecular formula \(C_2H_6\). Its empirical formula is \(CH_3\) (dividing by the common factor of 2).</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Core Practical — Determining Empirical Formula</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n35">You can determine the empirical formula of magnesium oxide by heating a known mass of magnesium ribbon in a crucible. Weigh the crucible before and after heating. The increase in mass equals the mass of oxygen that reacted. Divide each mass by its \(A_r\) to find the mole ratio, then simplify to get the empirical formula (MgO).</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n36">Limiting Reactants</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n37">In most reactions, one reactant is used up before the other. The reactant that is completely used up is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactant that is completely used up in a reaction, limiting the amount of product formed.">limiting reactant</dfn> — it controls the amount of product formed. The other reactant is said to be in <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactant that remains after the other reactant has been completely used up.">excess</dfn>. Adding more of the excess reactant will not produce any more product.</p>
</div>
