--- DESCRIPTION ---
The 18th Amendment, the Volstead Act, speakeasies and gangsters — why banning alcohol fuelled a crime wave.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Was Prohibition?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><strong>Prohibition</strong> was the ban on making, selling, and transporting alcoholic drinks in the United States. The <strong>18th Amendment</strong> to the Constitution was passed in January 1919 and took effect on <strong>17 January 1920</strong>. The <strong>Volstead Act</strong> (October 1919) set out the rules, making it illegal to produce, sell, or move any drink with more than <strong>0.5% alcohol</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this box and name both the Amendment and Act from 1919, then what was actually banned?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Legal Framework</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <strong>18th Amendment</strong> (1919) made Prohibition part of the Constitution. The <strong>Volstead Act</strong> (1919) set the enforcement rules. Together, they banned the manufacture, sale, and transport of ‘intoxicating liquors’ — but it was <strong>never illegal to drink alcohol</strong>. People could consume it; they just could not legally get it.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Was Prohibition Introduced?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The campaign for Prohibition had been growing for decades:</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">The Temperance Movement</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Groups like the <strong>Anti-Saloon League</strong> (founded 1893) and the <strong>Women’s Christian Temperance Union</strong> (WCTU) had campaigned against alcohol for years. They argued it destroyed families, caused domestic violence, and led to poverty. These groups were well-funded and politically powerful.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Religious and Moral Arguments</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Many Protestant Christians, especially in rural areas and the South, saw alcohol as sinful. They called saloons ‘dens of vice’ and believed a ban would create a more moral, God-fearing society.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n11">Industrial Efficiency</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Industrialists like <strong>Henry Ford</strong> and <strong>John D. Rockefeller</strong> backed Prohibition. They believed sober workers would be more productive. Fewer accidents and less absenteeism meant higher profits.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Anti-German Sentiment</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">During World War One, many of America’s biggest breweries were owned by German-Americans (such as <strong>Anheuser-Busch</strong> and <strong>Pabst</strong>). Anti-German feeling during the war made it easier to target the brewing industry. Grain used for brewing could go to the war effort instead.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">How Was Prohibition Enforced?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Prohibition agents</strong> were responsible for enforcement, but the task was almost impossible from the start:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n48">
<li>Only about <strong>1,500 agents</strong> policed the entire country — a vast territory with <strong>18,700 miles</strong> of coastline and borders.</li>
<li>Agents were poorly paid (about <strong>$2,500 a year</strong>), making many easy to bribe.</li>
<li>In the first four years, <strong>1 in 12 agents</strong> were sacked for corruption.</li>
<li>Many state and local police forces were unwilling or unable to enforce the law, especially in big cities.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box, what made Ness's 'Untouchables' unique, and why was this unusual in Chicago?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Eliot Ness</div>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Eliot Ness</strong> was a young Prohibition agent in Chicago. He led a team called ‘The Untouchables’ because they refused bribes. They raided Capone’s breweries, but Ness’s team was the exception — widespread corruption undermined enforcement everywhere.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Breaking the Law: Bootlegging and Speakeasies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Instead of stopping drinking, Prohibition drove the alcohol trade underground and created a huge illegal industry:</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Bootlegging</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Bootleggers</strong> illegally made, moved, or sold alcohol. It was smuggled from <strong>Canada</strong> and <strong>Mexico</strong>, or shipped in from the Caribbean (‘<strong>rum running</strong>’). Famous rum runners like <strong>William McCoy</strong> made fortunes — he smuggled an estimated <strong>$70 million</strong> worth of whiskey in just four years. Even <strong>Joseph Kennedy</strong> (father of the future President) reportedly made money from bootlegging. Others brewed ‘<dfn class="term" data-def="Illegally home-made alcohol, often produced in hidden stills.">moonshine</dfn>’ in bathtubs or hidden stills. There were never enough agents to close down all the stills. This home-made alcohol was often deadly — <strong>98 people</strong> died from poisonous moonshine in 1920, rising to <strong>760 deaths</strong> in 1926 as illegal production spread. In total, an estimated <strong>10,000 people</strong> died from poisonous bootleg liquor during Prohibition.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Speakeasies</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Speakeasies</strong> were illegal bars that sprang up across America. By 1925, there were an estimated <strong>15,000</strong> in Detroit alone. By 1929, New York had around <strong>32,000</strong> — more than double the number of legal bars before Prohibition. Nationwide, there were roughly <strong>200,000 speakeasies</strong> by 1930. The name came from speaking quietly (‘easy’) about their location. Many hid behind secret doors, in basements, or disguised as shops. Some had concealed entrances and required a <strong>secret password</strong> to get in. The famous <strong>21 Club</strong> in New York was a speakeasy for the rich and famous. Others simply had supplies of illegal alcohol delivered to their homes.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">The Rise of Organised Crime</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Prohibition created a huge opportunity for <strong>organised crime</strong>. Criminal gangs took control of the illegal alcohol trade, making vast fortunes and wielding huge power.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Al Capone</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Alphonse ‘Al’ Capone</strong> was the most notorious gangster of the Prohibition era. From a <strong>poor immigrant background</strong>, Capone rose to control most of Chicago’s criminal underworld. His gang earned an estimated <strong>$100 million a year</strong> in total ($1.3 billion in today’s money) from bootlegging, gambling dens, brothels, and protection rackets. He had around <strong>600 gangsters</strong> under his command, as well as lawyers and accountants to manage his empire.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name at least 3 examples of Capone's power from Chicago and the estimated cost?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Al Capone’s Power</div>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Capone controlled much of Chicago through <strong>bribery and violence</strong>. He had judges, police, and politicians on his payroll — including the <strong>Chief of Police</strong> and the <strong>Mayor of Chicago</strong> — bribing officials with an estimated <strong>$75 million</strong> over his career. Capone appeared on the <strong>front cover of <em>Time</em> magazine</strong> in March 1930, opened nightclubs, and was cheered at horse races. Many poor Americans admired him as a <strong>‘Robin Hood’</strong> figure — he even opened <strong>soup kitchens</strong> to feed the homeless when the Depression began. Capone himself said: <strong>“I make my money by supplying a popular demand. If I break the law, my customers are as guilty as I am… I call myself a businessman.”</strong> Despite ordering many murders, Capone was finally convicted in <strong>1931</strong> — not for murder or bootlegging, but for <strong>tax evasion</strong>. He was sentenced to <strong>11 years</strong> in prison.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">The St Valentine’s Day Massacre</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n32">The most infamous act of gang violence was the <strong>St Valentine’s Day Massacre</strong> on <strong>14 February 1929</strong>. Gunmen working for Capone dressed as police officers and entered a garage on Chicago’s North Clark Street. They told <strong>seven members</strong> of the rival <strong>Bugs Moran</strong> gang to line up against the wall, then shot them all in the back. Moran himself arrived late and missed the attack. When asked who was behind it, Moran said: <strong>“Only Capone kills like that!”</strong> One victim survived long enough to be questioned, but kept to the gangster code of silence — his only statement was <strong>“No-one.”</strong> The massacre shocked the nation. Capone was in Florida at the time and was never charged.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Gang Wars and Violence</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Chicago was not the only city hit. The fight for control of bootlegging — known as the <strong>‘Beer Wars’</strong> — caused <strong>hundreds of murders</strong> across American cities. In Chicago alone, there were over <strong>500 gang-related murders</strong> between 1927 and 1930. Corruption was rampant — in many cities, police and politicians were on the gangsters’ payroll.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n35">Why Did Prohibition Fail?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n36">By the late 1920s, it was clear Prohibition was not working:</p>

</figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n38">The End of Prohibition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n39">In the <strong>1932 election</strong>, <strong>Franklin D. Roosevelt</strong> promised to end Prohibition. After his landslide win, the <strong>21st Amendment</strong> was ratified on <strong>5 December 1933</strong>, repealing the 18th Amendment and ending Prohibition after nearly 14 years. It remains the only time a Constitutional Amendment has been repealed.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n40" data-revision-tip="From memory, what year did the 21st Amendment happen, and what direct impact did it have on gangs?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The 21st Amendment</div>
<p data-narration-id="n41">The <strong>21st Amendment</strong> (1933) repealed Prohibition. Roosevelt reportedly said: ‘I think this would be a good time for a beer.’ Ending Prohibition created jobs, brought in tax revenue, and cut off a major income source for organised crime — though gangs simply moved into other rackets like drugs and gambling.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n45">Conclusion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n46">Prohibition was widely regarded as a failure. Introduced with genuine moral aims, it proved impossible to enforce, fuelled the rise of organised crime, corrupted law enforcement, and was ignored by millions. Rather than creating a sober society, it showed the limits of using the law to change deep-rooted social behaviour.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n47">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n49">
<li><strong>Good intentions, disastrous results:</strong> Moral, religious, and economic arguments backed Prohibition — but its consequences (organised crime, corruption, mass law-breaking) were far worse than the problems it aimed to fix.</li>
<li><strong>Al Capone and organised crime:</strong> The illegal alcohol trade made gangsters like Capone hugely wealthy, with empires that corrupted police, judges, and politicians.</li>
<li><strong>The 21st Amendment:</strong> Prohibition was repealed in 1933 — the only time a Constitutional Amendment has been undone. Its failure showed laws only work when the public supports them.</li>
</ul>
