--- DESCRIPTION ---
Mass unemployment, Hoovervilles and the Dust Bowl — the devastating human impact of the worst economic crisis in US history.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Scale of the Depression</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <strong>Great Depression</strong> was the worst economic crisis in American history, lasting from 1929 to the late 1930s. After the Wall Street Crash, the economy went into a devastating downward spiral. Businesses closed, banks failed, and millions lost their jobs, homes, and savings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Many ordinary Americans who had never bought shares still lost everything. Banks had been gambling with their customers’ deposits on the stock market. When the market crashed, the banks collapsed — and innocent savers’ money went with them. There was no government insurance to protect deposits.</p>
<figure class="diagram" data-narration-id="n4">
<img alt="Infographic showing six key statistics of the Great Depression by 1933: 13 million unemployed, industrial production down 45%, farm income dropped from $6 billion to $2 billion, 5,000 bank failures, average income fell 40%, and 2 million homeless in Hoovervilles" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/america/diagram_depression_statistics.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Unemployment and Poverty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Mass unemployment was the most devastating effect. With no welfare system, no unemployment insurance, and no safety net, losing your job meant losing everything.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">The Human Cost</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n40">
<li>By 1933, <strong>one in four workers</strong> was unemployed. In some cities it was far worse — <strong>80%</strong> in <strong>Toledo, Ohio</strong> and <strong>50%</strong> in <strong>Cleveland</strong>.</li>
<li>Those who kept jobs saw wages cut by an average of <strong>60%</strong> between 1929 and 1933.</li>
<li><strong>250,000 families</strong> had their homes repossessed in 1932 alone.</li>
<li>Millions went hungry. Breadlines and soup kitchens appeared in every major city, run by charities — not the government.</li>
<li>Suicide rates rose by <strong>30%</strong> between 1929 and 1932.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Hoovervilles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Homeless Americans built makeshift shelters on waste ground, in parks, and on town edges. These shanty towns were called <strong>‘Hoovervilles’</strong> — a bitter, mocking reference to President <strong>Herbert Hoover</strong>, whom many blamed for the crisis.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 materials used to build Hoovervilles, plus 2 'Hoover' nicknames.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Hoovervilles</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Hoovervilles were built from scrap wood, tin, cardboard, and old car parts. The largest, in <strong>Central Park, New York</strong>, housed hundreds. Others appeared in <strong>Seattle, Washington D.C., St Louis</strong>, and most major cities. Bitter nicknames spread: newspapers as blankets were <strong>‘Hoover blankets,’</strong> empty pockets turned out were <strong>‘Hoover flags,’</strong> and broken cars pulled by horses were <strong>‘Hoover wagons.’</strong></p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">President Hoover’s Response</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Herbert Hoover</strong> was President when the Crash struck. He kept insisting recovery was near, famously declaring <strong>“Prosperity is just around the corner.”</strong> To millions of starving Americans, this showed how out of touch he was.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n41">
<li>Hoover believed in <strong>‘rugged individualism’</strong> — the idea that Americans should help themselves, not rely on government handouts. He feared direct relief would make people dependent.</li>
<li>He encouraged <strong>voluntary action</strong> — asking businesses not to cut wages and urging charities to help the poor. This was nowhere near enough.</li>
<li>In <strong>1931</strong>, he invested <strong>$400 million</strong> in government building projects, including the <strong>Hoover Dam</strong>, to try to create jobs. This helped, but it was too small for the scale of the crisis.</li>
<li>In <strong>1932</strong>, he set up the <strong>Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC)</strong>, making <strong>$1,500 million</strong> available in loans to banks and businesses. Critics called this “feeding the sparrows by feeding the horse” — it helped the rich while ordinary people starved.</li>
<li>He raised tariffs with the <strong>Hawley-Smoot Tariff</strong> (1930), which actually <strong>worsened</strong> the Depression by cutting international trade as other countries retaliated.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Bonus Army, 1932</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In summer <strong>1932</strong>, about <strong>20,000 World War One veterans</strong> and their families marched on Washington D.C. They demanded early payment of a war service <strong>bonus</strong> not due until 1945. These desperate, unemployed veterans set up camp near the Capitol and called themselves the <strong>Bonus Expeditionary Force</strong> (or <strong>Bonus Army</strong>).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Without looking, who ordered the US Army to disperse the Bonus Army, and who led the troops?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Bonus Army Incident</div>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Senate rejected the bonus bill, but most veterans refused to leave. Hoover ordered the <strong>US Army</strong>, led by General <strong>Douglas MacArthur</strong>, to clear the camp. Soldiers used <strong>tear gas, bayonets, and tanks</strong> to drive out the veterans and their families, burning their shelters. Two veterans were killed and over <strong>1,000 injured</strong>, including women and children. An <strong>11-month-old baby</strong> died in the attack. The sight of the Army attacking its own war heroes horrified the nation and destroyed Hoover’s reputation.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Dust Bowl</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">While the Depression devastated cities, a separate catastrophe struck rural America. The <strong>Dust Bowl</strong> was an ecological disaster affecting the <strong>Great Plains</strong> — <strong>Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Colorado, and New Mexico</strong> — throughout the 1930s.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Causes of the Dust Bowl</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n42">
<li><strong>Over-farming:</strong> During WWI and the 1920s, farmers ploughed up vast grasslands to plant wheat and cotton, stripping the soil’s protective covering.</li>
<li><strong>Drought:</strong> A severe drought hit from 1931, lasting most of the decade. The exposed topsoil dried out completely.</li>
<li><strong>Dust storms:</strong> Huge windstorms picked up loose topsoil, creating ‘black blizzards’ that travelled hundreds of miles. On <strong>‘Black Sunday’ (14 April 1935)</strong>, dust storms turned day into night.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Impact on Farmers</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The Dust Bowl destroyed farming across a vast area. Crops failed, livestock died, and the land became useless. About <strong>2.5 million people</strong> left the Dust Bowl states. Many headed to <strong>California</strong>, where they were called <strong>‘Okies.’</strong> They found only low-paid seasonal work and faced hostility from locals.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the area (in acres) affected by the Dust Bowl at its peak, and where the dust reached.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Scale of the Dust Bowl</div>
<p data-narration-id="n24">At its peak, the Dust Bowl affected <strong>100 million acres</strong>. Dust storms reached as far as <strong>New York and Washington D.C.</strong>, dropping Great Plains soil on politicians’ desks. The disaster showed how environmental mismanagement could worsen an economic crisis.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Impact on Different Groups</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>How the Depression affected different groups</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>African Americans:</strong> Already the poorest group, they were hit hardest. ‘Last hired, first fired’ — Black unemployment reached <strong>50%</strong> in many cities, double the national average. In the South, Black sharecroppers were evicted as farm income collapsed. Racial violence continued with no government help for Black communities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Hispanic Americans:</strong> Many Mexican Americans were forced out through <strong>‘repatriation.’</strong> An estimated <strong>500,000–1 million</strong> people of Mexican descent (including US citizens) were deported or pressured to leave between 1929 and 1936.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Women:</strong> Working women were accused of ‘stealing’ men’s jobs. Some states banned married women from government jobs. However, some women found work in service roles that men refused.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Children:</strong> Many were pulled out of school to work or beg. In some mining towns, <strong>90% of children</strong> were malnourished. Thousands of teenagers became <strong>‘hobos,’</strong> riding freight trains looking for work.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Farmers:</strong> Falling prices, bank foreclosures, and the Dust Bowl devastated farming communities. Thousands lost farms that had been in their families for generations.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n31">The 1932 Election</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">By 1932, Americans had had enough of Hoover. The Democratic candidate, <strong>Franklin D. Roosevelt (FDR)</strong>, promised a <strong>“New Deal”</strong> for the American people. He pledged the <strong>“Three Rs”</strong> — <strong>Relief</strong> for the poor, <strong>Recovery</strong> for the economy, and <strong>Reform</strong> to prevent another depression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Roosevelt won in a <strong>landslide</strong>, carrying <strong>42 out of 48 states</strong>. Americans had rejected Hoover’s rugged individualism and voted for a president who promised the government would act. The stage was set for a dramatic change of direction.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n37">Conclusion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n38">The Great Depression was a catastrophe. By 1933, 13 million were unemployed, 5,000 banks had failed, and millions had lost homes and savings — including innocent savers whose banks had gambled their money away. Hoovervilles filled the cities, dust storms swept the Plains, and Hoover’s ‘rugged individualism’ left him unable to help. The Bonus Army disaster destroyed his reputation. In 1932, Roosevelt won 42 out of 48 states, promising a New Deal of Relief, Recovery, and Reform.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n39">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n43">
<li><strong>Mass suffering:</strong> 13 million unemployed by 1933 (25%), no welfare system, and innocent savers wiped out by reckless banks.</li>
<li><strong>Hoover failed:</strong> His “rugged individualism,” the Bonus Army attack, and the late RFC ($1,500 million) were not enough. His $400 million building projects barely scratched the surface.</li>
<li><strong>The Dust Bowl:</strong> An ecological disaster displacing 2.5 million people, compounding the economic crisis.</li>
</ul>
