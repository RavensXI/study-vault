--- DESCRIPTION ---
How India's location, globalisation and policy have shaped its rapid economic growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Emerging Powerhouse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1991, India faced a financial crisis with only enough foreign currency to pay for two weeks of imports. The government chose to open the economy: cutting tariffs, welcoming foreign investment, dismantling old controls. Three decades on, India is the world’s fifth-largest economy, with over 1.4 billion people. It is a classic <dfn class="term" data-def="A country undergoing rapid economic growth, transitioning from primarily agricultural to industrial and service economies.">emerging country</dfn> — but the growth has been uneven. This case study explains how India got here and what comes next.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Site, Situation and Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">India’s <strong>site</strong> — what is found at the location — covers 3.3 million km² of South Asia, with the Himalayas in the north, the Thar Desert in the west, and a vast tropical and subtropical interior. Its <strong>situation</strong> — how it relates to other places — is favourable for trade: long coastlines on the Indian Ocean, ports at Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai, and proximity to the Persian Gulf, Southeast Asia and East Africa. <strong>Connectivity</strong> has improved with new airports, mobile networks reaching 95% of the country, and a fibre-optic backbone supporting the IT industry.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Politically, India is the world’s largest democracy, with a federal structure of 28 states. Its formal Independence from Britain in 1947 left it with English-speaking elites, a robust legal system, and an inherited rail network — all useful for trade and global business. Culturally, the country has 22 official languages and immense diversity. Hinduism is the majority religion, but India is also home to large Muslim, Christian, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain populations.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall five facts about India: population, GDP rank, languages spoken, year of liberalisation and the location of two megacities.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>India: population 1.4 billion (2024); 5th-largest economy by GDP, 3rd by purchasing power; over 22 official languages; economic liberalisation began in 1991; megacities include Mumbai (financial), Delhi (political), Bengaluru (IT) and Kolkata (cultural-historical).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Globalisation Drives Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Since 1991, India’s economy has been transformed by <strong>globalisation</strong>. Average GDP growth between 2003 and 2018 was around 7% per year — second only to China among major economies. The composition of the economy has shifted dramatically. In 1990, agriculture employed about 60% of workers and produced 30% of GDP. Today agriculture is around 18% of GDP, services 50%, and industry 25%. The IT and business-services sector centred on Bengaluru exports software and back-office work to the world — Wipro, Infosys and TCS rival US tech companies.</p>

The largest sources are Singapore, the USA, Mauritius and Japan. Investment flows into IT services, telecommunications, manufacturing (Apple now assembles iPhones in Tamil Nadu), pharmaceuticals and renewable energy. The government’s ‘Make in India’ programme since 2014 has actively courted manufacturing FDI to reduce dependence on imports and create jobs for the 12 million young Indians entering the workforce each year.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Demographic and Social Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Rapid economic change has reshaped Indian society. The fertility rate has fallen from 5.4 in 1960 to 2.0 today, just below replacement. Life expectancy has risen from 41 years (1960) to 70. Around 35% of Indians now live in urban areas, up from 17% in 1950. Tens of millions are leaving farms for cities — the <strong>rural-urban migration</strong> typical of emerging economies. New regions have emerged: Bengaluru’s IT corridor, Mumbai’s financial district, Gujarat’s industrial belt — the country’s ‘<strong>core</strong>’. Other regions, especially in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, lag behind — the ‘<strong>periphery</strong>’.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two positive and two negative impacts of India's rapid economic growth on its people and environment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Around 415 million Indians escaped poverty between 2005 and 2021 (UN figures). But around 230 million still live on under $3.65 a day, and Delhi’s air pollution is among the worst in the world — a sign that growth has come at significant environmental cost.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Costs and Benefits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Benefits include rising living standards, falling extreme poverty, expansion of the middle class to around 350 million people, world-class IT companies, and improving infrastructure. Costs are real too: <strong>environmental damage</strong> — air and water pollution, especially in Delhi (PM2.5 levels routinely 10× WHO guidelines); <strong>inequality</strong> — women earn around 35% less than men, and the wealthiest 10% take more than 57% of national income; rising land and property prices; loss of farmland to urban sprawl. Globalisation has not benefited everyone equally.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">India’s rapid <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern in many emerging economies where wealthy core regions grow much faster than poorer peripheral regions, widening internal inequality.">core-periphery inequality</dfn> growth has changed its position in the world. It is part of BRICS (alongside Brazil, Russia, China, South Africa) and the G20. It now leads the Quad security partnership with the USA, Japan and Australia. Its space programme reached the moon in 2023. India is no longer just a recipient of foreign aid — it now provides aid to smaller neighbours like Sri Lanka and Bhutan. But strained relations with neighbouring China (over disputed Himalayan borders) and Pakistan still shape regional politics.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">India’s 1991 liberalisation, geographic situation and English-speaking professional class have made it a top destination for FDI and the world’s 5th-largest economy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Rapid economic change has shifted the economy from agriculture to services, lifted 415 million people out of poverty, and reshaped urban regions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Costs include environmental damage, sharp inequality between core and periphery, and rising urban pressures — growth has not been evenly shared.</li>
</ul>
