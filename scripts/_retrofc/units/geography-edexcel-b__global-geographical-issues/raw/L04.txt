--- DESCRIPTION ---
Earth's layered structure, plate boundaries and why volcanic and earthquake hazards vary.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Restless Planet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Beneath our feet, the Earth is hot and moving. The surface is broken into giant slabs of rock called <dfn class="term" data-def="Large rigid slabs of the Earth's outer layer that move slowly across the underlying mantle.">tectonic plates</dfn>. Where these plates meet, volcanic eruptions and earthquakes shake the surface. Understanding why — and where they happen — helps geographers predict, prepare and reduce risk for the billions of people who live near plate boundaries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Inside the Earth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Earth has four main layers. The <strong>inner core</strong> is solid iron and nickel at around 6,000°C. The <strong>outer core</strong> is liquid iron and nickel; circulating currents here generate Earth’s magnetic field. The <strong>mantle</strong> is hot, slow-flowing rock about 2,900 km thick; its upper part — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The hot, partially molten layer of the upper mantle on which tectonic plates slide.">asthenosphere</dfn> — is partially molten and acts like very thick treacle. The <strong>crust</strong> is the thin (5–70 km) outermost layer; its rigid uppermost part plus the very top of the mantle together form the <strong>lithosphere</strong> — the ‘plates’ themselves.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and label a sketch of the Earth's layers from inside out, with one fact about each layer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Heat from the core (released by the radioactive decay of uranium and thorium) drives <strong>convection currents</strong> in the mantle. These currents drag the lithospheric plates around at 2–10 cm per year — about as fast as your fingernails grow.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Three Types of Plate Boundary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Plates meet at three kinds of boundary. At a <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates move towards each other; one is forced down (subducted) below the other or two continents collide.">convergent boundary</dfn>, plates collide. If oceanic crust meets continental crust, the denser oceanic plate is forced down (subducted) into the mantle, melting and rising as andesitic magma to form explosive volcanoes such as those in the Andes and Cascade Range. The Pacific ‘Ring of Fire’ is a chain of these convergent boundaries.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">At a <strong>divergent boundary</strong>, plates pull apart. Magma rises into the gap and cools to form new oceanic crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge runs the length of the Atlantic; Iceland sits directly on it, with frequent fissure-style basaltic eruptions. At a <strong>conservative boundary</strong>, plates slide past each other. There is no volcanic activity, but stress builds and releases as earthquakes — the San Andreas Fault between the Pacific and North American plates is the classic example.</p>

The Hawaiian Islands sit on the moving Pacific plate above a fixed hotspot; over millions of years, a chain of volcanic islands forms as the plate drifts north-westwards. Yellowstone is another hotspot, sitting under the centre of the North American plate.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Volcanic and Earthquake Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Volcanic hazards depend on magma type. Basaltic magma (Hawaii, Iceland) is fluid and erupts as gentle lava flows; loss of life is rare though property damage can be huge. Andesitic and rhyolitic magma (Andes, Pacific Ring) is sticky; gas builds up and eruptions are explosive. Hazards include lava flows, ash falls (which collapse roofs and ground aircraft), <strong>pyroclastic flows</strong> (super-heated gas and ash travelling at 700 km/h), <strong>lahars</strong> (volcanic mudflows when heat melts ice or rain mixes with ash) and toxic gases. Mount St Helens (1980) and Mount Pinatubo (1991) showed how lethal explosive eruptions can be.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, sketch a subduction zone and label the trench, descending plate, melting magma, volcano and earthquake foci.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Earthquake magnitude is measured on the moment magnitude scale (M<sub>w</sub>): each whole-number step represents about 32× more energy. A magnitude 7 quake releases roughly 1,000× the energy of a magnitude 5.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Earthquakes and Tsunami</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Earthquakes happen when stored stress along a fault is released as seismic waves. Shallow quakes (under 70 km deep) are usually the most damaging because the energy reaches the surface concentrated. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake off Japan was a magnitude 9.0–9.1 megathrust quake at a convergent boundary — one of the largest ever recorded. It triggered a <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of huge sea waves, often triggered by an underwater earthquake or volcanic eruption.">tsunami</dfn> with waves up to 40 m high that killed around 18,000 people and destroyed the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Impacts Vary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A magnitude 7 quake in Haiti (2010) killed an estimated 230,000. A magnitude 6.9 quake in Christchurch, New Zealand (2011) killed 185. The hazard was similar; the vulnerability was not. Wealth, building codes, public education, emergency planning and political stability shape outcomes far more than magnitude alone. Developed countries can prepare; developing countries often must rely on overseas aid for short-term relief and long-term reconstruction.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">Japan’s response to the 2011 Tōhoku quake combined strict building codes, automatic train shutdowns, sea walls (some of which were overtopped), drilled school children and a Self-Defence Force ready to respond — yet still suffered 18,000 deaths because of the unprecedented tsunami. Haiti’s 2010 response was hampered by collapsed government buildings, no enforced building codes, and an over-reliance on a single airport. The contrast is not perfect but illustrates how preparation reduces — though never eliminates — tectonic risk.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Earth has a hot core, a flowing mantle and a thin crust. Heat from the core drives convection currents in the mantle, which move the lithospheric plates.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Three plate boundary types — convergent (subduction or collision), divergent and conservative — produce different patterns of volcanoes and earthquakes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Hazard severity depends on magnitude and depth, but human impact depends on vulnerability — building codes, planning and wealth determine whether people survive.</li>
</ul>
