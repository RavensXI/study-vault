--- DESCRIPTION ---
How development is measured using economic, social and political indicators across countries.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Does ‘Development’ Mean?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a country improves the economic, social and political wellbeing of its people.">Development</dfn> is the process by which a country improves the economic, social and political wellbeing of its people. It used to be measured almost entirely by income, but today geographers recognise that wealth alone misses important things. A country can have rising income while inequality, poor health or a lack of political freedom limit how that wealth helps ordinary people.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Single-Indicator Measures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The classic economic measure is <dfn class="term" data-def="Gross Domestic Product per capita: the total value of goods and services produced in a country, divided by its population.">GDP per capita</dfn>. It is easy to compare and updated yearly. The World Bank classifies countries as low income (under $1,135 per year), lower-middle, upper-middle, or high income (over $13,845). But GDP per capita has weaknesses: it ignores how income is distributed, hides the informal economy (street trading, subsistence farming), and says nothing about quality of life.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Other single indicators include literacy rate (% of adults who can read and write), life expectancy (average years a newborn is expected to live) and infant mortality rate (deaths per 1,000 live births in the first year). These tell different stories: Cuba has GDP per capita roughly a tenth of the UK’s but life expectancy almost the same, because of strong public health.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three single-indicator measures of development and one weakness of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Single-indicator measures (GDP per capita, life expectancy, literacy rate) are useful for quick comparisons but each captures only one dimension of development. They can disagree sharply about how ‘developed’ a country is.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Composite Measures: HDI</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Human Development Index, a composite index combining income, education and life expectancy on a scale of 0 to 1.">Human Development Index</dfn> was created by the UN in 1990. It combines three measures: GDP per capita (income), mean and expected years of schooling (education), and life expectancy (health). The result is a single number from 0 to 1. Norway typically tops the index (0.96), the UK is around 0.94, while many sub-Saharan African countries score below 0.5. HDI better captures development than GDP alone, but it still ignores inequality, environmental degradation and political freedom.</p>

The Nordic countries score around 0.25, the UK around 0.35 and South Africa around 0.63 — one of the most unequal societies on Earth. Two countries with the same GDP per capita can have very different living conditions if Gini differs sharply. Examiners reward students who pair GDP with Gini in their answers.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Demographic Indicators</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A country’s <strong>demographic data</strong> tells a story too. <strong>Fertility rate</strong> (births per woman) is around 1.6 in the UK and 4.5 in Niger. <strong>Death rate</strong> falls as healthcare improves. <strong>Population structure</strong> (drawn as a population pyramid) shifts from a wide-base ‘triangle’ in low-development countries (many young children, few elderly) to a ‘column’ in highly developed countries (an ageing population). Maternal and infant mortality rates fall sharply with development — from over 500 deaths per 100,000 births in the lowest-developed countries to around 5–10 in the highest.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch the population pyramid shapes for a developing, an emerging and a developed country.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three categories: <strong>developing</strong> countries (low GDP per capita, high fertility, low life expectancy — e.g. Niger, Bangladesh), <strong>emerging</strong> (rapid economic growth, falling fertility — e.g. India, Brazil), <strong>developed</strong> (high GDP, low fertility, ageing population — e.g. UK, Japan).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Theories of Why Development Differs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Two contrasting theories try to explain why development varies. <strong>Rostow’s modernisation theory</strong> (1960) argues that countries pass through five stages: traditional society (subsistence farming); preconditions for take-off (commercial farming, infrastructure); take-off (rapid growth in key industries); drive to maturity (technology spreads); high mass consumption (rich service-led economy). Investment, education and globalisation help countries climb the ladder. The UK and South Korea are examples of countries that have moved through all five stages.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Frank’s dependency theory</strong> (1967) argues the opposite: rich countries (the ‘core’) actively keep poor countries (the ‘periphery’) underdeveloped through unequal trade, foreign debt and the legacy of <strong>colonialism</strong>. The core extracts cheap raw materials and sells back expensive manufactured goods. The periphery becomes locked into supplying primary commodities. Real-world development is messy: South Korea broke dependency, but Frank’s theory still explains many post-colonial economies.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Global inequality has many causes. <strong>Historical</strong>: colonial powers extracted resources and left ex-colonies with weak institutions; <strong>environmental</strong>: tropical climates can hamper agriculture; landlocked countries struggle to trade. <strong>Economic</strong>: terms of trade often favour exporters of manufactured goods over raw commodity exporters. <strong>Political</strong>: corruption, conflict and weak rule of law deter investment. <strong>Social</strong>: poor health and education hold back productivity. No country’s development is shaped by just one factor.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Development is multi-dimensional — economic (GDP), social (health, education) and political (freedoms, governance). No single indicator captures all three.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Composite indices like HDI improve on GDP alone; the Gini coefficient adds vital information about inequality.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Rostow’s modernisation theory emphasises stages of growth; Frank’s dependency theory emphasises power imbalances. Real countries combine elements of both.</li>
</ul>
