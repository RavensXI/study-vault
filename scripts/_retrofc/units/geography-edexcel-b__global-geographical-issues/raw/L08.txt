--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why the world is urbanising rapidly and how megacities and urban primacy emerged.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Planet of City Dwellers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1950, only about 30% of people lived in towns and cities. By 2007 the world crossed a tipping point — for the first time in history, more than half of humanity was urban. Today the figure is around 57%, and the United Nations projects it will reach 68% by 2050. The next thirty years are expected to add more than 2 billion city dwellers, almost all of them in Asia and Africa. <dfn class="term" data-def="The growing proportion of a country's population that lives in towns and cities rather than rural areas.">Urbanisation</dfn> is one of the defining processes of our time, reshaping where people live, how they work and the pressures placed on the environment.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why Are Cities Growing?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Two processes drive urban growth. The first is <strong>rural-urban migration</strong> — people moving from villages to cities. The second is <strong>natural increase</strong>, where the urban population grows because births outnumber deaths inside the city itself. Migrants are typically young adults of working age, so once they arrive they have children, accelerating growth further.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">People move because of <dfn class="term" data-def="Negative pressures in rural areas that push people away, such as poverty, drought, or lack of jobs.">push factors</dfn> in the countryside and <dfn class="term" data-def="Positive attractions in cities that pull people in, such as jobs, schools and healthcare.">pull factors</dfn> in cities. Common push factors include rural poverty, mechanisation of farming reducing labour needs, drought or land degradation, and limited access to schools or hospitals. Pull factors include better-paid jobs in factories or services, brighter-lit streets and entertainment, the chance of a university education and access to clean water and electricity. The reality often falls short of the hope — many migrants end up in informal slums — but the contrast with rural conditions still makes the move worthwhile.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the world's urban share in 1950, today, and projected for 2050. Then list two push and two pull factors.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The world urban share rose from about 30% (1950) to roughly 57% (today) and is projected to hit 68% by 2050. Almost all future urban growth will happen in developing and emerging countries.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Different Speeds in Different Places</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Not every country is urbanising at the same speed. <dfn class="term" data-def="Wealthy industrialised nations such as the UK, USA and Japan with high incomes and slow urban growth.">Developed countries</dfn> are already 80% or more urban; their growth is now slow or even slightly negative as people retire to the coast or countryside. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rapidly industrialising middle-income nations such as India, Brazil, China and Indonesia.">Emerging countries</dfn> like India and Brazil are urbanising fast as factories, services and infrastructure expand. <dfn class="term" data-def="Lower-income nations where most people still depend on agriculture, such as Ethiopia and Nepal.">Developing countries</dfn> have the lowest urban share but the highest rates of urban growth. Africa is the most rural continent today but is urbanising the quickest — Lagos in Nigeria adds about 77 new residents every hour.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Cities also reshape themselves internally. <strong>Suburbanisation</strong> is the spread of housing outwards into the urban-rural fringe, common across the UK from the 1930s. <strong>Counter-urbanisation</strong> is people moving from cities to small towns or villages, which has happened in parts of southern England as remote working has spread. <strong>Re-urbanisation</strong> is the return of people to inner-city areas after regeneration — London Docklands, Manchester city centre and Liverpool waterfront are British examples. Most cities show all three processes happening at once in different neighbourhoods.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Megacities and Urban Primacy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A city with a population of more than 10 million people.">megacity</dfn> has more than 10 million inhabitants. In 1950 there were only two: New York and Tokyo. By 2025 there are around 35, and the great majority are in Asia, Africa and Latin America. Tokyo (about 37 million) and Delhi (about 33 million) lead the league. Most growth in the next twenty years will come from cities like Lagos, Dhaka, Karachi and Kinshasa — cities most British students would struggle to place on a map.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">In many countries one city stands far above the rest in size, wealth and political importance. Geographers call this <dfn class="term" data-def="When one city dominates a country economically, politically and culturally, far larger than any other.">urban primacy</dfn>. Bangkok dwarfs every other Thai city. Lima houses about a third of all Peruvians. Lagos handles most of Nigeria’s formal economy. Primacy concentrates investment but also concentrates problems — traffic, pollution, slum housing — into a single overcrowded centre.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define megacity, name three megacities outside Europe and explain what urban primacy means.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A megacity has over 10 million people. Numbers grew from 2 megacities (1950) to roughly 35 today, with most new megacities forming in Asia and Africa. Urban primacy is when one city dominates a country.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Formal vs Informal Economies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Cities in different parts of the world have different economies. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Work that is officially recognised, taxed and regulated, with contracts and protections.">formal sector</dfn> includes registered factories, offices and retail. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Unregulated, often unrecorded work such as street selling, waste picking or unregistered taxis.">informal sector</dfn> covers street trading, recycling, casual labour and home-based work. In developed countries the informal sector is small. In emerging cities like Mumbai or Lagos it employs over half the workforce. Conditions are often poor, with no contracts, sick pay or pensions, but the sector absorbs new arrivals who cannot find formal jobs immediately.</p>

For example, Lagos grew from about 7.3 million in 2000 to 15.4 million in 2024. Change = 8.1 million; 8.1 / 7.3 = 1.11; × 100 = 111% growth in 24 years. Annual rate ≈ 111 / 24 ≈ 4.6% a year. <h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The world urbanised from about 30% in 1950 to roughly 57% today, with 68% projected by 2050; almost all future growth will be in Asia and Africa.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Urban growth is driven by rural-urban migration (push and pull factors) plus natural increase among young migrant populations already in the city.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Megacities (over 10 million people) have grown from 2 in 1950 to about 35 today; many countries show urban primacy where a single city dominates everything.</li>
</ul>
