--- DESCRIPTION ---
Top-down and bottom-up strategies, and the role of TNCs, NGOs and IGOs in development.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Do You Help a Country Develop?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Development cannot be imposed; it has to take root. The big question is whether it grows best from the top down (governments and large institutions plan big projects) or from the bottom up (communities lead small-scale projects to meet local needs). In practice, most countries use a mix — but the choice shapes who benefits, who pays, and which problems get solved.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Top-Down Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Large-scale development projects planned and funded by national governments or international organisations, often using high-technology infrastructure.">Top-down development</dfn> is led by national governments, large companies or international organisations like the World Bank. Projects are usually large-scale: hydroelectric dams, motorways, ports, large hospitals or universities. They aim to deliver wide economic benefits quickly. Examples include China’s Three Gorges Dam (the world’s largest power station, displacing 1.3 million people), Egypt’s Aswan High Dam (built with Soviet funding in the 1960s) and India’s nationwide rail network.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Top-down has clear strengths. Big infrastructure can transform a country: India’s electricity grid now reaches 99% of villages thanks to government and World Bank investment. Capital-intensive projects bring jobs in construction. National economies grow. But top-down has weaknesses: high cost, often funded by foreign loans that create debt; environmental destruction (Three Gorges flooded ancient towns and damaged ecosystems); and benefits often skip the poorest, who are sometimes displaced without fair compensation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list one strength and one weakness of top-down development, with a named example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Top-down development can deliver fast, large-scale change but often displaces communities and creates debt. Bottom-up reaches the poorest but cannot scale up quickly.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Bottom-Up Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Small-scale development projects led by local communities or NGOs that use appropriate technology suited to local needs.">Bottom-up development</dfn> is led by local people, often supported by <strong>non-governmental organisations (NGOs)</strong> such as Oxfam, WaterAid or Practical Action. Projects are small, low-cost and use <dfn class="term" data-def="Technology that is suited to a country's level of skill, income and resources, often low-cost and locally repairable.">intermediate technology</dfn> — equipment that local people can use, repair and afford. Examples include hand-pump wells, solar lanterns, fuel-efficient cookstoves and rocket stoves, and micro-hydro schemes that power a single village. Practical Action’s solar-powered drip-irrigation systems in Zimbabwe let smallholder farmers double crop yields with a small one-off investment.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Bottom-up’s strengths are appropriateness and reach: projects are designed around what people actually need, give jobs to locals and rarely cause displacement. Communities own the project so it is sustained after the NGO leaves. Weaknesses: small scale — thousands of small wells will not transform a national economy the way a power grid does. Funding depends on charity. Coordination across regions is patchy.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>TNCs</strong> (transnational corporations) such as Apple, Toyota or Unilever have factories and offices in many countries. They bring foreign direct investment (FDI), jobs and technology. Critics say they extract profits home, exploit cheap labour, and avoid taxes. <strong>IGOs</strong> (intergovernmental organisations) such as the World Bank, IMF and UN fund big projects but often attach conditions like privatisation. <strong>NGOs</strong> are typically small and grassroots-focused. The same project can have all three involved — a World Bank loan funding a TNC contractor while NGOs run training schemes alongside.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Globalisation and Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Globalisation</strong> — the increasing connection of economies, cultures and movements of people — has reshaped development. Faster transport (containerisation), cheap communications (internet, mobile phones) and freer trade have allowed manufacturing to move from high-wage to low-wage countries. China rose to become the ‘world’s factory’ from the 1980s; Bangladesh produces clothes for global brands; India grew its IT services industry. Globalisation has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, but it has also created vulnerabilities: economies dependent on exports can crash when demand falls (like during the 2008 financial crisis).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three main players in development (TNCs, IGOs, NGOs) and one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>TNCs (e.g. Apple) drive globalisation by relocating production. IGOs (e.g. World Bank) fund infrastructure with strings attached. NGOs (e.g. Oxfam) deliver community-scale projects. Real development uses all three in combination.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why Some Have Benefitted More</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Globalisation has not lifted every country equally. Countries with stable governments, educated workforces, access to ports and pro-FDI policies (China, Vietnam, India, Mexico) have boomed. Countries with conflict, weak institutions, landlocked geography or heavy dependence on a single commodity (Democratic Republic of Congo, Yemen, Bolivia) have largely missed out. <strong>Selection effects</strong> matter: TNCs go where regulations favour them, not where need is greatest, so the poorest places often see the least investment.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Aid is one tool of top-down development. <strong>Bilateral aid</strong> goes directly from one government to another — the UK to Kenya, for example. <strong>Multilateral aid</strong> is pooled through organisations like the World Bank or UN and distributed by them. <strong>Tied aid</strong> comes with conditions: the recipient must spend it on goods or services from the donor country, which can lock developing countries into expensive contracts. <strong>Emergency aid</strong> after disasters (food, shelter, medicine) is short-term but vital. <strong>Development aid</strong> targets long-term needs — schools, clinics, irrigation. The UK target was to spend 0.7% of national income on aid until 2021, when it was cut to 0.5% during the pandemic. Aid critics argue it can foster dependency or be diverted by corruption; supporters point to the lives saved through global vaccination programmes funded mainly by aid.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Top-down development is large-scale, government- or IGO-led, fast but expensive and sometimes harmful to displaced communities.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Bottom-up development is small-scale, community-led, NGO-supported and appropriate to local needs — but limited in scale.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Globalisation through TNCs has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty but unevenly — countries need stable governments and infrastructure to benefit.</li>
</ul>
