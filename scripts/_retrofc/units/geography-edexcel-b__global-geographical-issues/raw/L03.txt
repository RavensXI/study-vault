--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why tropical cyclones form, where they strike and the hazards they bring to people.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Storms of Extraordinary Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A large rotating tropical storm with sustained wind speeds over 119 km/h, powered by warm ocean water.">tropical cyclone</dfn> is the most powerful storm on Earth. Called hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the western Pacific and cyclones in the Indian Ocean, they are all the same phenomenon. They can be 800 km wide, last for weeks, and pack winds over 250 km/h. They form, strengthen and dissipate in patterns geographers can predict — but every cyclone is a unique threat to the people in its path.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Where and Why They Form</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Tropical cyclones need a specific recipe: ocean water above 26.5°C, depths of at least 50 m of warm water, atmospheric instability, low wind shear (wind speed similar at different altitudes), and being at least 5° latitude away from the equator (so the Coriolis effect can spin them up). These conditions occur in summer and autumn over the warmest tropical seas: Atlantic hurricanes (June–November), Pacific typhoons (May–November) and Indian Ocean cyclones (April–December). They form in the rising arm of the Hadley cell where warm, moist air is already lifting; tracks curve westwards under the trade winds, then poleward.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four conditions a tropical cyclone needs to form.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tropical cyclones need warm ocean (26.5°C+), atmospheric instability, low wind shear and the Coriolis effect. They cannot form on the equator and they die over land or cooler water.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Anatomy of a Cyclone</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Warm, moist air rises rapidly at the storm centre, condensing into towering clouds and releasing huge amounts of energy as latent heat — this is the cyclone’s ‘fuel’. Air spirals inwards along the surface, deflected by the Coriolis effect into a rotation. Pressure at the centre drops far below normal. The <strong>eye</strong> is the calm centre — clear skies, no wind — while the <strong>eye-wall</strong> just outside it is where the most violent winds and heaviest rain are concentrated. <strong>Spiral rain bands</strong> stretch outwards for hundreds of kilometres.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Hazards They Bring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Cyclones cause damage in three main ways. <strong>High winds</strong> tear off roofs, snap trees, hurl debris and disable power lines. <strong>Intense rainfall</strong> — often 200–500 mm in a single day — causes flash flooding and triggers landslides on steep slopes. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A wall of seawater pushed onto the coast by a tropical cyclone's low pressure and strong onshore winds.">storm surge</dfn> is the most deadly: a sudden rise in sea level, often 4–9 m, that can drown coastal communities. Hurricane Katrina’s 8.5 m surge in 2005 destroyed New Orleans levees and caused most of the storm’s 1,800 deaths.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three primary hazards of a tropical cyclone and explain which is usually most deadly.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three primary hazards of a tropical cyclone are high winds, intense rainfall and storm surge. Storm surge usually causes the most deaths because of widespread coastal flooding.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why Vulnerability Varies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Two countries can be hit by similar storms but suffer hugely different outcomes. Vulnerability depends on three factors. <strong>Physical</strong>: low-lying coasts (Bangladesh, Pacific islands) and densely populated coastal cities (Manila, Miami) are at greater risk. <strong>Social</strong>: poverty forces some communities into flimsy housing or risky locations; warning systems may not reach the most isolated; some have less education about evacuation. <strong>Economic</strong>: developed countries can build sea walls, fund early-warning systems and rebuild quickly; developing countries cannot. The 2008 Cyclone Nargis killed an estimated 138,000 people in Myanmar — partly because the military government delayed international aid.</p>

In a developing country like the Philippines, response often relies on overseas aid, less robust building stock and weaker pre-disaster planning — though the Philippines has improved markedly since Typhoon Haiyan (2013, 6,300+ deaths) by setting up faster evacuation and hardened shelters.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Preparation and Response</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Effective preparation involves four layers. <strong>Forecasting</strong> uses weather satellites to track cyclones days before landfall. <strong>Warnings</strong> are issued through TV, radio, mobile alerts and sirens. <strong>Evacuation</strong> moves residents to inland shelters; coastal cities like Tampa rehearse evacuation routes. <strong>Defences</strong> include sea-walls, surge barriers and storm-resistant building codes. The Netherlands and Bangladesh have both invested heavily in surge defences. Bangladesh’s coastal cyclone-shelter network has cut deaths from over 300,000 (1970 cyclone) to under 5,000 (similar 2007 storm).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Tropical cyclones form over warm tropical oceans (26.5°C+), spinning around an eye and powered by latent heat from condensation in the eye-wall.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The three primary hazards are high winds, intense rainfall and storm surge — the surge usually kills the most people.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Vulnerability varies with physical location, social inequality and economic capacity; preparation through forecasting, warnings, evacuation and defences saves lives, as Bangladesh has shown.</li>
</ul>
