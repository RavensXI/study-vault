--- DESCRIPTION ---

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mumbai: India’s Economic Capital</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Mumbai sits on the west coast of India, on a long thin peninsula stretching into the Arabian Sea. Its population is around 22 million in the wider metropolitan area, making it one of the world’s ten largest cities. Mumbai is the financial heart of India: it hosts the Bombay Stock Exchange, the Reserve Bank of India and Bollywood, the world’s biggest film industry by output. Roughly 6% of India’s GDP is generated within Greater Mumbai — despite the city covering less than 0.1% of the country’s land area.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Site, Situation and Connectivity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Mumbai’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The actual land that a city is built on, including its physical features.">site</dfn> is unusual: the city was originally seven small islands, joined together over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries by land reclamation. Its <dfn class="term" data-def="A city's location relative to surrounding features such as mountains, rivers, ports and trade routes.">situation</dfn> is exceptional. The deep natural harbour faces the Indian Ocean and gave the British East India Company a base from which to trade with Africa, Arabia and Europe. Mumbai is now India’s busiest container port and home to its largest international airport. Mumbai’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical and digital links a city has with national and global networks.">connectivity</dfn> includes 1,400 km of suburban rail (the world’s busiest commuter system), national highways, and undersea data cables connecting India to global finance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall Mumbai's population, the share of India's GDP it produces, and one site fact and one situation fact.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mumbai is home to about 22 million people in the metropolitan area and produces roughly 6% of India’s GDP from less than 0.1% of its land. Its harbour, airport and rail network drive its global role.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Mumbai’s Urban Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Mumbai has a recognisable land-use pattern. The southern tip houses the <strong>Central Business District (CBD)</strong> — Nariman Point and Fort — with skyscrapers, the stock exchange and headquarters of major banks. North of the CBD lies the <strong>inner city</strong>, including older industrial neighbourhoods and the famous <dfn class="term" data-def="An area of poor-quality, often self-built housing where residents may lack secure property rights and basic services.">slum</dfn> of Dharavi — one of the largest slums in Asia, with around 700,000 residents on roughly 2 km². Beyond the inner city are <strong>suburbs</strong> such as Bandra and Andheri, which have been the focus of new high-rise apartment development. The <strong>urban-rural fringe</strong> includes the new airport at Navi Mumbai and growing satellite towns linked by motorway.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Population Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Mumbai’s population was about 1.5 million in 1941. By 1991 it had reached 12 million and by 2011 over 18 million. Two engines have driven growth. The first is <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between the number of births and the number of deaths in a population.">natural increase</dfn> — Mumbai has a young population so births greatly exceed deaths. The second is rural-urban migration. Migrants come from across India, especially from the poorer states of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Maharashtra. Most are pushed by drought, debt and falling rural incomes; pulled by the chance of work in textiles, construction or domestic service. Around half of Mumbai’s residents now live in informal housing.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Mumbai offers genuine opportunities. Wages for unskilled work are roughly five times higher than in rural villages. Government schools and free public hospitals serve millions. Dharavi alone produces around US$1 billion of annual output from small-scale leather, pottery and recycling workshops; many residents are second-generation entrepreneurs, not destitute. The city has a vibrant cultural scene, top universities (IIT Bombay) and access to formal-sector jobs in finance and IT for those who can train into them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Challenges of Rapid Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Growth has come at a cost. Mumbai faces severe <strong>housing shortages</strong>: an estimated 41% of residents live in slums. Property prices in the formal market are among the highest in the world — the same as central London — while incomes are far lower. Many slum residents have <strong>insecure property rights</strong>, meaning bulldozers can clear neighbourhoods with little notice. Public services struggle: clean water reaches some districts for only 2–4 hours a day, and large areas rely on shared public toilets. Traffic congestion costs the city an estimated 8% of GDP a year. Air pollution exceeds World Health Organization safe limits for most of the year, contributing to respiratory illness, especially in children.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Inequality Within One City</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Mumbai is one of the most unequal cities on Earth. Antilia, the home of the businessman Mukesh Ambani, is a 27-floor private skyscraper reportedly worth US$2 billion. It overlooks neighbourhoods where families pay 1,000 rupees a month (roughly £10) for a 10 m² tin-roofed shack. Two reasons explain the contrast: India’s rapid growth has rewarded skilled, English-speaking professionals far more than unskilled migrants; and the city’s land scarcity (built on a peninsula) has driven property prices to extreme levels, locking poorer residents out of formal housing. Politicians struggle to manage the gap because slum dwellers are also a powerful voting block.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name three challenges of rapid growth in Mumbai with a specific statistic for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Around 41% of Mumbai’s residents live in slums. Water reaches some districts only 2–4 hours per day. Traffic congestion costs the city about 8% of GDP a year. Air pollution exceeds WHO safe limits for most of the year.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Mumbai is India’s economic capital with about 22 million residents and roughly 6% of India’s GDP, anchored by a deep natural harbour and high-density rail network.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Its structure runs from a financial CBD at the southern tip, through the inner city (including Dharavi), out to fast-growing suburbs and the urban-rural fringe.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, education, entrepreneurship) but also slums, water and transport stress, pollution and extreme inequality.</li>
</ul>
