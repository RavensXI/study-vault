--- DESCRIPTION ---
How global atmospheric circulation transfers heat and creates arid and high-rainfall zones.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Planet on the Move</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Sun heats the Earth unevenly. The equator receives the most concentrated solar energy because the Sun’s rays strike it directly, while the poles receive weaker, slanting rays. Without a way to move that heat away from the equator, tropical regions would keep getting hotter and the poles colder. The atmosphere — the thin layer of gases surrounding Earth — works as a giant heat-redistribution system. So do the oceans. Together they make the planet liveable from the tropics to the Arctic Circle.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Heat moves around the globe through a system called <dfn class="term" data-def="The worldwide pattern of air movement that transfers heat from the equator towards the poles.">global atmospheric circulation</dfn>. Warm air rises near the equator, travels at altitude towards the poles, sinks where it cools, and flows back along the surface. This creates large loops of moving air called <dfn class="term" data-def="A large-scale loop of moving air that redistributes heat between the equator and the poles.">circulation cells</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Three Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">There are three circulation cells in each hemisphere: the Hadley, Ferrel, and Polar cells. The <strong>Hadley cell</strong> stretches from the equator to about 30° latitude. Hot air rises at the equator, cools and releases its moisture as heavy rainfall, then sinks at around 30° as warm, dry air. This sinking air creates the world’s great deserts — the Sahara, the Arabian, the Atacama and the Australian. The <strong>Ferrel cell</strong> sits between 30° and 60° latitude. Air rises at 60° (forming temperate low-pressure systems and rain) and sinks at 30°. The <strong>Polar cell</strong> covers 60° to the poles, with cold dry air sinking over the icy poles.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch the three circulation cells, labelling rising and sinking air at the equator, 30°, 60° and the poles.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rising air = low pressure = high rainfall. Sinking air = high pressure = arid conditions. The world’s deserts cluster around 30°N and 30°S because that is where Hadley-cell air sinks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Why Pressure Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The weight of the atmosphere pressing on the Earth's surface, measured in millibars or hectopascals.">Atmospheric pressure</dfn> is the weight of air pushing down on the surface. Where air is rising, it pulls air in from surrounding regions and pressure is low. Low pressure brings cloud, rainfall and unsettled weather. Where air is sinking, it pushes outwards and pressure is high — bringing clear skies and dry conditions. This explains why the equator is wet and 30° latitude is dry. It also explains why the UK, sitting under the rising arm of the Ferrel cell at 50–55°N, has changeable, often rainy weather.</p>

This is the <strong>Coriolis effect</strong>. It bends winds in the Hadley cell from northerly into the north-east <strong>trade winds</strong>, and the Ferrel cell winds into the prevailing south-westerlies that bring weather systems to the UK from the Atlantic.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Oceans Move Heat Too</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The atmosphere does about half of the global heat transfer. The other half is done by <dfn class="term" data-def="Continuous, directed movements of seawater that move heat around the world's oceans.">ocean currents</dfn>. Warm surface currents, such as the North Atlantic Drift, carry tropical heat polewards. Cold deep currents return colder water back towards the equator. The North Atlantic Drift gives the British Isles winters that are unusually mild for their latitude — 5–10°C warmer than parts of Canada at the same latitude. This linked atmospheric and oceanic system is sometimes called the ‘global conveyor belt’.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the three circulation cells in order from equator to pole and one consequence of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Heat is moved by winds (atmosphere) AND by currents (oceans). Together these create the climate zones — tropical, arid, temperate, polar — which determine where rainforests, deserts and tundra exist.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why This Matters for Geography</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Almost everything else in this course depends on this idea. Tropical cyclones form in warm, moist Hadley-cell air. Deserts exist because of sinking air at 30°. The world’s tropical rainforests grow under the equatorial rising air. UK weather changes day to day because the country sits where the Ferrel cell meets the polar front. If global circulation shifts — for example because of climate change — rainfall patterns shift with it, with consequences for farming, water supply and where people can live.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Uneven heating of the Earth drives global atmospheric circulation; the three cells in each hemisphere (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar) move heat from equator to poles.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Rising air = low pressure = wet (equator, 60°); sinking air = high pressure = dry (30°, poles). This pattern locates the world’s rainforests and deserts.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Ocean currents like the North Atlantic Drift add a second heat-transfer system, giving the UK milder winters than its latitude alone would predict.</li>
</ul>
