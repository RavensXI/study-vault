--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Key Themes in Journey’s End</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your modern-text essay will always focus on a character, theme, or relationship. Themes run through the entire play, so your essay must range across all three acts — not just one scene. This lesson brings together the play’s major themes and shows you how to structure them into exam-ready arguments. Each theme is supported by key quotations that you should memorise for the closed-book exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Heroism and Duty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Sherriff presents <dfn class="term" data-def="Brave or noble conduct, traditionally associated with military valour but questioned and complicated throughout the play.">heroism</dfn> not as a grand quality but as the quiet, exhausting act of carrying on. Stanhope is called the best company commander in the battalion, yet he is an alcoholic who can barely function without whisky. Osborne’s heroism lies in his calm decency and willingness to lead the raid despite knowing the danger. Even Trotter is heroic in his own way — his determination to focus on the next meal rather than on fear is a survival strategy that keeps him and others grounded.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The play redefines heroism: it is not the absence of fear, but the decision to continue despite it. Stanhope tells Hibbert that every officer is afraid. The difference between them is not bravery but <dfn class="term" data-def="A sense of moral obligation to serve one’s country and fulfil one’s responsibilities, especially in wartime.">duty</dfn> — Stanhope’s sense of duty keeps him at the front even when every instinct tells him to leave. Sherriff asks whether this version of heroism is admirable or simply a form of entrapment imposed by social expectations.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write from memory the two key Stanhope quotations about endurance and forgetting.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sherriff redefines heroism as endurance, not glory. Stanhope’s heroism is inseparable from his damage. He drinks to endure what most cannot face.e play suggests that the real heroism of WWI was not found in dramatic charges but in the daily, grinding act of carrying on when every part of you wants to stop.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Fear and Coping</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Every character in the play is afraid, but each copes differently. Stanhope uses alcohol. Osborne uses routine and calm. Trotter uses food and humour. Hibbert tries to escape. Raleigh, at first, does not understand fear because he has not yet experienced the reality of the front. Sherriff uses these contrasting responses to argue that <dfn class="term" data-def="An overwhelming feeling of distress or terror, often caused by an awareness of danger.">fear</dfn> is universal — the only question is how you manage it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Stanhope–Hibbert confrontation is the play’s most direct exploration of this theme. Stanhope threatens to shoot Hibbert, then admits: “I feel the same — exactly the same.” This moment strips away the idea that courage is a fixed character trait. Instead, Sherriff presents it as a daily choice, made harder by the absence of any support system for psychological damage. The military offered no treatment for what we now call <dfn class="term" data-def="A mental health condition triggered by traumatic events, characterised by flashbacks, anxiety, and emotional withdrawal. Known as shell shock during WWI.">PTSD</dfn>; the only options were to endure, to drink, or to break.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">All the officers in <em>Journey’s End</em> are from the middle or upper-middle class, educated at <dfn class="term" data-def="Fee-paying secondary schools in England, historically associated with the upper and upper-middle classes, which trained boys for leadership roles.">public schools</dfn>. Stanhope and Raleigh attended the same school; Osborne was a schoolmaster. The public-school system trained boys for leadership through sport, discipline, and the suppression of emotion. These values — the stiff upper lip, team spirit, duty before self — are both the source of the officers’ strength and the cause of their suffering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Trotter is the exception: he comes from a lower-middle-class background and is less polished than the others. His promotion to replace Osborne after the raid subtly suggests that the class system is breaking down under the pressure of war — competence matters more than background. The Colonel, meanwhile, represents the upper echelons of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The rigid ranking system of the military, where orders flow from senior officers to junior officers and then to soldiers.">military hierarchy</dfn>, issuing orders from behind the lines with little regard for the cost to the men who carry them out.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Friendship and Brotherhood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The bonds between the men are the play’s emotional core. Stanhope and Osborne share a deep, unspoken understanding — Osborne is the only person Stanhope trusts. The Stanhope–Raleigh relationship is more complicated: it is built on a school friendship that the war has distorted. Stanhope describes Osborne as “the one man I could trust — my best friend — the one man I could talk to as man to man,”t the Stanhope he finds is a damaged stranger who sees Raleigh as a threat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Sherriff shows that <dfn class="term" data-def="A bond of loyalty and mutual support between people who share a common experience, especially among soldiers.">camaraderie</dfn> is both the men’s greatest comfort and the source of their greatest pain. Osborne’s death devastates Stanhope precisely because they were close. Stanhope’s tenderness with dying Raleigh shows that the friendship was real all along, but the war made it impossible to express. The play suggests that love and loss in wartime are inseparable.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of being pointless or producing no useful result, a key theme in WWI literature that questions whether the war’s enormous human cost achieved anything meaningful.">Futility</dfn> pervades the play. The raid is carried out at great cost — Osborne dies — to capture a single German prisoner whose information changes nothing. The German attack comes anyway. Raleigh dies. The dugout is destroyed. The play ends without resolution, suggesting that the suffering was meaningless.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Sherriff does not make this argument through speeches or moralising. Instead, he shows it through structure: the play ends where it began, with the sound of shells and the darkness of the dugout. Nothing has been gained. The cycle of duty, fear, loss, and death simply continues. This structural futility mirrors the wider futility of the Western Front, where millions died for gains measured in yards.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and name two themes that overlap in the play, then explain how they connect to each other.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The play’s themes work together: heroism is undermined by futility, friendship is destroyed by loss, and the class system that creates leaders also destroys them. The best exam essays explore these connections rather than treating themes in isolation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Exam Technique — The Modern-Text Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A typical modern-text essay question gives you a short quotation from the play and asks you to write an essay. You will often choose one question from two. The question will focus on a character, theme, or relationship. It is a closed-book exam, so you must memorise quotations. Here is a step-by-step approach:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Step-by-Step Essay Structure</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step 1 — Read the quotation carefully.</strong> Identify the character, theme, or idea it relates to. Underline key words in the question.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Step 2 — Plan three to four paragraphs.</strong> Each paragraph should make a different point about the question. Aim to cover different parts of the play (not just one act). Each paragraph needs: a point, a quotation (embedded, not bolted on), analysis of language, and a link to context (AO3).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Step 3 — Use the quotation as a springboard.</strong> Start your first paragraph by engaging with the given quotation. Analyse its language closely. Then move outwards to other parts of the play.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Step 4 — Embed context into analysis.</strong> Do not write a separate “context paragraph.” Instead, weave AO3 into every paragraph: “Stanhope’s reliance on alcohol reflects the widespread use of whisky among officers who had no other support for what we now recognise as PTSD.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Step 5 — Conclude briefly.</strong> Sum up your argument in two to three sentences. Do not introduce new evidence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n25">The assessment objectives for the modern-text essay typically include: <strong>AO1</strong> — your response to the text, supported by quotations and references; <strong>AO3</strong> — understanding of context (historical, social, cultural); and <strong>AO4</strong> — spelling, punctuation, and grammar alongside accurate use of literary terminology. Allow roughly 45–50 minutes for this question. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact mark allocation and the balance between AOs.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n28">The play’s themes — heroism, fear, class, friendship, and futility — are interconnected. The best essays explore these connections rather than treating them in isolation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Sherriff redefines heroism as endurance rather than glory, and presents fear as universal — the only difference between characters is how they cope.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">For the Section B essay: use the given quotation as a springboard, range across the whole play, embed context into every paragraph, and spend 45–50 minutes. Memorise 10–12 short, versatile quotations.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For the closed-book exam, memorise 10–12 short quotations that cover the play’s key themes and characters. The best quotations are short (under 10 words), versatile (usable for multiple themes), and contain interesting language to analyse. For example, “It’s the only way to forget” works for essays on Stanhope, on fear, on coping, or on the psychological effects of war. Always analyse individual word choices: why “forget” rather than “cope”? Why “the only way” rather than “a good way”?</p>
