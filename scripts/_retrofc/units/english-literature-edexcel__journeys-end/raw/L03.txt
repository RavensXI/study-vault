<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Act 2 — The Pressure Builds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 2 of <em>Journey’s End</em> deepens the tensions established in Act 1. The German attack is now imminent, and the Colonel arrives with orders for a dangerous trench raid. Stanhope must send men across no man’s land to capture a German prisoner for intelligence. This act also contains two of the play’s most important scenes: Stanhope’s decision to censor Raleigh’s letter, and Hibbert’s attempt to leave the front line by claiming illness.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Sherriff structures Act 2 around the theme of <dfn class="term" data-def="The obligation to follow orders and carry out one’s responsibilities, even when the personal cost is devastating.">duty</dfn> versus self-preservation. Every character is forced to choose between obeying orders and protecting themselves or the people they care about. The raid hangs over the act like a sentence of death, and the audience watches the men respond in different ways: Osborne with quiet acceptance, Stanhope with fierce control, Hibbert with desperate evasion, and Raleigh with naïve willingness.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: who orders the raid, who objects, and what does this reveal about the military hierarchy?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Colonel orders the raid to capture a German soldier for intelligence about the expected attack. Stanhope objects to sending Osborne on such a dangerous mission, but the Colonel insists. This reveals the military hierarchy’s disregard for individual lives — decisions made from behind the lines directly cause the deaths of men the audience has come to care about.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Censorship Scene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">One of Act 2’s most revealing scenes occurs when Stanhope insists on reading Raleigh’s letter home before it is sent. He has been dreading this moment — convinced that Raleigh will describe his <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of examining and suppressing information in letters, communications, or publications, used during wartime to prevent sensitive details reaching the enemy or demoralising civilians.">censorship</dfn> of his own letters was a real practice during WWI, and Sherriff uses it here to create dramatic irony.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When Osborne reads the letter aloud, it contains nothing but admiration: Raleigh describes Stanhope as “the best company commander in the battalion” and says how proud he is to serve under him. Stanhope is stunned and ashamed. The moment is devastating because it reveals the gap between Stanhope’s tortured self-image and how others actually see him. He has been so consumed by guilt and fear that he cannot recognise loyalty when it is offered.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What the Censorship Scene Reveals About Stanhope</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Stanhope’s insistence on censoring the letter exposes his deepest fear: not the Germans, not death, but being seen as he truly is. His identity is split between the heroic schoolboy Raleigh remembers and the alcoholic wreck he believes himself to be. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation in which the audience knows something that a character does not, creating tension or emotional impact.">dramatic irony</dfn> is powerful — the audience already suspects that Raleigh’s letter will be positive, but Stanhope cannot imagine it. His shame has so distorted his perception that he is incapable of trusting anyone’s good opinion of him.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">After hearing the letter, Stanhope quietly says he will not censor it after all. This small moment of grace is one of the few times in the play where Stanhope lets his guard down. Sherriff shows that beneath the toughness, Stanhope is still capable of being moved — but the war has made vulnerability feel like a threat.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Hibbert’s Attempt to Leave</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Second Lieutenant Hibbert claims he has <dfn class="term" data-def="Persistent, severe headaches that Hibbert claims to suffer from, which Stanhope believes are a fabricated excuse to leave the front line.">neuralgia</dfn> (severe headaches) and asks to be sent to the rear. Stanhope sees through the excuse immediately and confronts Hibbert in one of the play’s most intense scenes. He tells Hibbert that every officer is afraid — including himself — and threatens to shoot him if he tries to leave.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">This scene is crucial because it shows Stanhope at his most complex. He is simultaneously cruel (threatening to shoot a fellow officer) and honest (admitting his own fear). When he says that he, too, has considered pretending to be ill, he strips away the mask of the fearless leader. The scene also raises an uncomfortable moral question: is Hibbert a coward, or simply a man who has reached his breaking point? Sherriff refuses to give a simple answer. Hibbert’s fear is real, even if his excuse is not.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Stanhope threaten to do to Hibbert, and what does he then admit about his own feelings?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stanhope tells Hibbert: “If you went — and left Osborne and Trotter and Raleigh and all the other men who are staying here — I’d rather spare you than any of them.” threatens to shoot Hibbert if he leaves, but then admits his own terror. The scene ends with both men agreeing to go on together. Sherriff shows that courage is not the absence of fear but the decision to carry on despite it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Raid Preparations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Colonel selects Osborne and Raleigh to lead the raiding party. Stanhope objects, not wanting to risk Osborne, but the Colonel dismisses his concerns. This moment reveals the callousness of military command: the Colonel is more interested in capturing a prisoner than in the lives of the men he sends. He offers Osborne a box of cigars as a reward, a detail that highlights the grotesque disparity between the commanders behind the lines and the men who face death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The night before the raid, Osborne and Raleigh sit together in the dugout. Osborne reads aloud from <em>Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland</em>, and they talk about England, home, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Trivial, ordinary, or everyday matters, used in the play to contrast with the extraordinary danger the characters face.">mundane</dfn> things. This quiet domestic scene, set against the knowledge that one or both may die tomorrow, is one of Sherriff’s most effective dramatic techniques. The contrast between the ordinariness of their conversation and the horror of what awaits them creates unbearable tension.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Osborne and Alice in Wonderland</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Osborne’s choice of reading material is significant. <em>Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland</em> is a children’s book about a nonsensical world where normal rules do not apply. There is a clear parallel with the war: the officers have fallen into their own nightmarish wonderland where death is random and authority makes no sense. The book also connects to Osborne’s former life as a schoolmaster — it is a reminder of the civilised, orderly world he has left behind.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">By having Osborne read a children’s book just before he goes to his death, Sherriff emphasises his innocence and gentleness. It also functions as a form of escape: Osborne retreats into a fantasy world rather than facing the reality of the next day. The scene is profoundly moving because it shows a good man quietly preparing to die.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Themes in Act 2</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Act 2 brings several key themes into sharp focus. The conflict between duty and self-preservation is dramatised through Hibbert. The gap between the military command and the men on the ground is shown through the Colonel’s casual ordering of the raid. The theme of <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between how things appear on the surface and the reality beneath, a key theme in the play where characters hide their true feelings behind masks of composure.">appearance versus reality</dfn> deepens as Stanhope’s fear is contrasted with his outward authority, and Raleigh’s admiring letter reveals the disconnect between Stanhope’s self-image and how he is perceived. Every scene in Act 2 tightens the pressure towards the inevitable catastrophe of the raid.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">The censorship scene creates dramatic irony: Stanhope expects criticism in Raleigh’s letter but finds only admiration, revealing how guilt and shame have distorted his self-perception.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Stanhope–Hibbert confrontation explores the fine line between courage and cowardice — Stanhope threatens to shoot Hibbert, then admits his own fear.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Osborne reading <em>Alice in Wonderland</em> before the raid creates a poignant contrast between innocence and impending death, and symbolises the absurdity of war.</li></ul>
