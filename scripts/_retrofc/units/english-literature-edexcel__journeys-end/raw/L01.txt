<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The War Behind the Play</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">R.C. Sherriff’s <em>Journey’s End</em> (1928) is one of the most important plays ever written about the First World War. Unlike the patriotic propaganda that dominated British culture during 1914–1918, Sherriff’s play presents the war honestly — showing the fear, boredom, exhaustion, and psychological damage that officers endured in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrow ditch dug into the ground used as a defensive position on the Western Front, where soldiers lived and fought.">trenches</dfn>. Sherriff himself served as an officer in the 9th East Surrey Regiment and was wounded at Passchendaele in 1917. The play draws directly on his own experience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For the Edexcel exam, understanding this context is essential. AO3 requires you to show how the play’s meaning is shaped by the historical period in which it was written and set. Every character, every silence, and every moment of dark humour in the dugout is rooted in the reality of the Western Front.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what year was the play set, where was the dugout, and what real offensive was about to begin?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sherriff served as a captain in the trenches and was seriously wounded at Passchendaele in 1917. The play is set in a dugout near Saint-Quentin in March 1918, just before the real German Spring Offensive that nearly broke the Allied lines.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Trench Warfare on the Western Front</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">By 1915 the Western Front had become a continuous line of trenches stretching from the English Channel to Switzerland. Soldiers lived in cramped, muddy, rat-infested conditions. The front-line trenches were supported by communication trenches, reserve trenches, and supply lines. Men rotated between front-line duty, support duty, and brief rest periods behind the lines.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The play captures the monotony and tension of trench life. The characters spend most of their time waiting — eating, talking, sleeping — punctuated by sudden violence when a raid or bombardment begins. This rhythm of boredom and terror is central to the play’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of realistic detail in art and literature to represent ordinary life accurately, without idealisation.">naturalism</dfn>. Sherriff does not glamorise war; he shows what it actually felt like to sit in a dugout knowing an attack was coming.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The war began in August 1914 and was expected to end quickly. Instead, both sides dug in, and the Western Front barely moved for four years. Key battles include the Somme (1916), where 20,000 British soldiers died on the first day, and Passchendaele (1917), where Sherriff was wounded. By March 1918 — when the play is set — the German army launched its Spring Offensive (Operation Michael), hoping to win before American reinforcements arrived. The play’s final bombardment mirrors this real attack.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The average life expectancy of a junior officer on the front line was just six weeks. Officers were expected to lead from the front, set an example of calm courage, and never show fear to their men. This pressure is exactly what destroys Stanhope in the play.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Officer Class</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">All the main characters in <em>Journey’s End</em> are officers — men from the middle and upper classes who were educated at <dfn class="term" data-def="Fee-paying secondary schools in England, historically associated with the upper and upper-middle classes, which trained boys for leadership roles.">public schools</dfn>. Stanhope and Raleigh both attended the same school; Osborne was a schoolmaster. The British Army’s officer corps was drawn almost entirely from this social class. They were trained to lead by example, suppress emotion, and maintain a stiff upper lip.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Sherriff shows how these ideals of honour and <dfn class="term" data-def="A sense of moral obligation to serve one’s country and fulfil one’s responsibilities, especially in wartime.">duty</dfn> both sustained and crushed the men. Stanhope’s sense of duty keeps him at the front even as alcohol becomes his only coping mechanism. Osborne’s quiet decency makes his death in the raid all the more devastating. The play asks whether the values these men were raised with are adequate for the horrors they face.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three expectations placed on officers in WWI that Sherriff explores through Stanhope.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The officer class was expected to show courage, self-control, and leadership. Sherriff explores how these ideals both hold the men together and mask the psychological damage beneath the surface. Stanhope’s alcoholism is tolerated because he still does his duty.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Shell Shock and Psychological Damage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A term used during WWI for the psychological trauma caused by prolonged exposure to combat, now known as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).">Shell shock</dfn> was one of the most controversial issues of the war. Thousands of soldiers developed symptoms including nightmares, trembling, inability to speak or move, and emotional breakdown. The military establishment was slow to recognise it as a genuine condition — many sufferers were accused of cowardice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Sherriff never uses the term “shell shock” in the play, but its effects are everywhere. Stanhope drinks to forget. Hibbert invents physical ailments to escape. Even the steady Osborne reveals quiet anxiety. The play shows that psychological damage was not a sign of weakness but an inevitable consequence of prolonged exposure to combat. When Stanhope says, “It’s the only way to forget,” he is describing a coping mechanism shared by thousands of real officers.</p>

    <span>The Play’s 1928 Reception</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">When <em>Journey’s End</em> premiered in December 1928, a decade after the Armistice, audiences were ready to confront the reality of the war. The play was an immediate sensation — transferring to the West End in January 1929 and running for nearly 600 performances. Critics praised its honesty and restraint. Unlike earlier war literature that either glorified combat or descended into bitterness, Sherriff presented ordinary men doing their best in impossible circumstances.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The play appeared alongside other landmark works of war literature: Erich Maria Remarque’s <em>All Quiet on the Western Front</em> (1929), Siegfried Sassoon’s <em>Memoirs of an Infantry Officer</em> (1930), and Wilfred Owen’s posthumous poetry. Together, these works created a cultural shift in how Britain remembered the war — moving from patriotic pride to a more honest reckoning with its human cost.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Sherriff’s Dramatic Approach</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Sherriff chose to set his entire play inside a single dugout over a period of just four days (Monday to Thursday). There are no battle scenes on stage, no grand speeches, and no villains. The war is experienced through the men’s conversations, their silences, and the sounds of shelling from above. This stripped-back approach forces the audience to focus on the human cost of war rather than its spectacle. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical surroundings and location in which a play takes place, used by playwrights to create atmosphere and reflect themes.">setting</dfn> itself becomes a kind of trap — the men cannot leave, and the audience shares their confinement.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Sherriff served in the trenches and was wounded at Passchendaele — the play draws directly on his own experience of the Western Front.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The officer class was expected to suppress fear and show courage; the play explores how these ideals both sustain and damage the characters.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Shell shock (now PTSD) is central to the play, even though the term is never used — Stanhope’s drinking, Hibbert’s feigned illness, and Osborne’s quiet anxiety all reflect its effects.</li></ul>

AO3 (context) is worth up to 20 of the 40 content marks, so you must weave historical context into every paragraph — not bolt it on at the end. Instead of writing “soldiers suffered in WWI,” connect context to specific moments: “Stanhope’s alcoholism reflects the widespread reliance on alcohol among officers who had no other way to cope with prolonged front-line duty.”</p>
