--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Characters in Journey’s End</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sherriff populates his dugout with a carefully chosen group of characters who represent different responses to war. For the Edexcel exam, you need to be able to write about how each character is presented, what they reveal about the play’s themes, and how Sherriff uses them to explore the effects of conflict on individuals. Remember that Section B tests AO1 (your response and use of quotations) and AO3 (context), so always link character analysis to the historical period.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Captain Stanhope</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Stanhope is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central character in a literary work, around whom the main action revolves.">protagonist</dfn> and the most complex character in the play. He is twenty-one years old, has been at the front for three years without leave, and is the longest-serving officer in the battalion. He is a brilliant and dedicated commander — the Colonel calls him “the best company commander in the battalion” — but he is also an alcoholic who drinks to suppress the psychological damage of prolonged combat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Stanhope’s central conflict is between the person he was (a popular, heroic schoolboy) and the person the war has made him (a traumatised, dependent wreck). His fear about Raleigh’s letter reveals this split: he is terrified that his fiancée Madge will learn the truth. His line “You think there’s no limit to what a man can bear?” is the play’s most direct expression of psychological breaking point.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Stanhope's quotation about why he drinks and explain what it reveals about his character.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stanhope’s alcoholism is not weakness but survival. He says: “It’s the only way to forget.” Sherriff presents a young man who has been pushed beyond his limits by a war that offers no support for mental health. His fierce loyalty, his tenderness with the dying Raleigh, and his refusal to leave the front all show that duty and damage can coexist.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Lieutenant Osborne — “Uncle”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Osborne is the oldest officer in the company, a former schoolmaster and rugby player, and the emotional heart of the group. He is nicknamed “uncle” because of his paternal, caring nature. He defends Stanhope to Hardy, reassures Raleigh, reads <em>Alice in Wonderland</em>, and organises earwig races — small acts of kindness that hold the group together.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Osborne represents the <dfn class="term" data-def="The values and behavioural code of the English upper-middle class, emphasising composure, decency, and self-sacrifice.">gentlemanly ideal</dfn>: calm, decent, selfless. His death during the raid is the play’s emotional turning point. Sherriff kills the most universally loved character to show that war does not spare the good. Osborne’s reading of <em>Alice in Wonderland</em> before the raid connects his innocence to the absurdity of the world he is about to enter — a world where decency offers no protection.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Second Lieutenant Raleigh</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Raleigh is the youngest and most inexperienced officer, fresh from England and eager to serve. He hero-worships Stanhope from their school days and pulled strings to be posted to his company. Raleigh functions as the audience’s entry point into the world of the trenches: his naïveté mirrors our own unfamiliarity, and his gradual loss of innocence mirrors the broader disillusionment caused by the war.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Raleigh admires Stanhope deeply, though it is Stanhope who later describes Osborne as “the one man I could trust — my best friend”” establishes the schoolboy bond that Stanhope fears the war has destroyed. His refusal to eat at the dinner after Osborne’s death shows genuine moral feeling, but also reveals his inability to understand the coping mechanisms that front-line soldiers need. His death on Osborne’s bed completes his journey from innocent to casualty, and Stanhope’s tenderness in that scene finally reconciles the two men.</p>

    <span>Second Lieutenant Trotter</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Trotter is a cheerful, uncomplicated officer from a lower-middle-class background. He talks constantly about food, draws circles on a chart to count down the hours of each duty period, and provides <dfn class="term" data-def="Humour or light-hearted moments in an otherwise serious or tragic work, providing temporary emotional relief for the audience.">comic relief</dfn> through his blunt, good-natured personality. His coping mechanism is to focus on small, tangible pleasures — the next meal, the next rest period — rather than thinking about the bigger picture.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Trotter is often underestimated in exam answers, but he is important. He represents the ordinary soldier who survives by not thinking too deeply. His promotion to replace Osborne after the raid shows that the war values endurance over sensitivity. Sherriff uses Trotter to contrast with the more emotionally complex Stanhope and Osborne — his simplicity is both his strength and his limitation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Second Lieutenant Hibbert</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hibbert is the most controversial character in the play. He claims to have <dfn class="term" data-def="Sharp, intense pain along a nerve, which Hibbert uses as an excuse to leave the front line.">neuralgia</dfn> and asks to be sent to the rear, but Stanhope sees through his excuse and threatens to shoot him. Hibbert represents the fear that every soldier felt but most suppressed. His attempt to escape is not simply cowardice; it is a human response to intolerable conditions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">What makes Hibbert interesting is that Stanhope admits he has felt the same way. The difference between them is not that Stanhope is braver — it is that Stanhope’s sense of duty is stronger. Sherriff blurs the line between courage and cowardice, suggesting that both are responses to the same impossible situation. Hibbert challenges the audience to consider whether they would behave any differently.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and compare: how do Stanhope and Hibbert each cope with fear, and what is the key difference?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hibbert and Stanhope are two sides of the same coin. Both are terrified. Stanhope stays because of duty and alcohol; Hibbert tries to leave because he has no coping mechanism. Sherriff asks whether the line between courage and cowardice is really as clear as the military system demands.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Mason and the Colonel — Supporting Characters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Mason</strong> is the officers’ cook and <dfn class="term" data-def="A soldier who serves as a personal servant to an officer, handling domestic duties like cooking and cleaning.">batman</dfn>. He provides gentle comic relief through his complaints about the quality of food (“a yellow sort of stuff, sir”) and his earnest attempts to make the best of terrible supplies. Mason humanises the dugout and reminds the audience that even in war, people cling to the routines of normal life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>The Colonel</strong> represents the military hierarchy. He orders the dangerous raid, dismisses Stanhope’s objections about sending Osborne, and sends champagne to celebrate the capture of a prisoner — apparently indifferent to Osborne’s death. The Colonel is not a villain, but his detachment from the human cost of his orders illustrates the gap between those who give commands and those who carry them out.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Together, these characters form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small group that represents the wider population, allowing the playwright to explore broad social themes through a limited number of individuals.">cross-section</dfn> of the officer class: the heroic wreck (Stanhope), the steady mentor (Osborne), the innocent newcomer (Raleigh), the cheerful pragmatist (Trotter), the man at breaking point (Hibbert), and the detached authority (the Colonel). Each responds to the war differently, and Sherriff uses their contrasts to explore the full range of human responses to conflict.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Stanhope is the play’s most complex character — a brilliant officer whose alcoholism is a survival mechanism, not a weakness. His line “It’s the only way to forget” encapsulates the play’s view of psychological damage.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Osborne, Raleigh, Trotter, Hibbert, and Mason each represent different responses to war, from quiet decency to desperate evasion to cheerful pragmatism.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Sherriff blurs the line between courage and cowardice — the Stanhope–Hibbert confrontation shows that both men are afraid, and the only difference is their coping mechanism.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Character questions in Section B often ask you to explore how a character is <em>presented</em> — this means discussing Sherriff’s choices as a playwright, not just what the character does. Always include: what the character says (close quotation analysis), what others say about them, their dramatic function (e.g. Raleigh as audience surrogate), and how they connect to AO3 context (the officer class, shell shock, the military hierarchy).</p>
