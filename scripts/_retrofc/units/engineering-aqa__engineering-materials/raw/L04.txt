--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Cost and Supply Shape Material Choice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Even when a material is technically perfect for an application, it may still be the wrong choice if it is too expensive, difficult to source or unavailable in the right form. An engineer must always balance performance against cost, lead time and how much waste will be produced during manufacture. <dfn class="term" data-def="The standard physical shapes and dimensions in which a material is commercially supplied, such as sheet, bar, tube, rod, angle section, wire and powder.">Stock forms</dfn> are the standard physical formats in which materials are sold: sheet, bar, rod, tube, angle section, channel, wire, powder and granule. Using a standard stock form avoids the cost of special-order processing and means material can be ordered quickly from a distributor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list six different stock forms, then write one material that is commonly supplied in each form.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Standard stock forms include: sheet (aluminium, mild steel), bar (square, rectangular, round &mdash; steel, brass), tube (circular, square &mdash; steel, aluminium), angle section (steel structural), rod, wire (copper, steel), granule and powder (plastics for injection moulding). Off-the-shelf standard sizes minimise lead times and reduce cost.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Factors That Determine Material Cost</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Raw material cost is driven by several factors. The rarity or abundance of the ore or feedstock matters: aluminium is abundant but energy-intensive to refine, so it is more expensive per kilogram than mild steel. Copper is moderately scarce and copper prices fluctuate with global demand from the electrical and electronics industries. Gold and platinum are very expensive because of their rarity and the complexity of mining and refining. The degree of processing also increases cost: a sheet of aluminium that has already been rolled, heat-treated and precision-cut to tolerance costs more than the equivalent mass of aluminium ingot. Any material that has been <dfn class="term" data-def="A material that has been processed by machining, heat treatment or surface treatment before it is supplied, reducing the amount of work needed by the engineer but increasing the purchase price.">pre-machined or treated</dfn> will carry a premium over raw stock.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The reduction in unit cost that occurs when purchasing materials or components in larger quantities, because fixed setup, delivery and handling costs are spread across more items.">Economies of scale</dfn> (also called bulk pricing or price breaks) mean that the unit cost of a material falls as the quantity ordered rises. A small workshop ordering 10 steel bars pays a higher price per bar than a large manufacturer ordering 10,000 bars of the same specification. This is because the supplier&rsquo;s delivery, handling, warehousing and order-processing costs are spread across a larger number of units. Engineers ordering for a prototype batch need to be aware that their per-unit material cost will be much higher than the production-run cost, which affects how accurately they can predict the final product price.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Not all materials are equally available everywhere. <dfn class="term" data-def="The time between placing an order for a material or component and receiving it ready for use in manufacture.">Lead time</dfn> is the delay between ordering and receiving a material. For standard stock held in a local distributor&rsquo;s warehouse, lead time might be next-day delivery. For specialist alloys, custom-profile extrusions or material in unusual thicknesses, lead time can be weeks or months. Global supply chains mean that a political crisis, port disruption or natural disaster in a producing country can suddenly make a material unavailable or very expensive. Engineers are increasingly required to consider whether a second-choice material could be substituted if the primary supply chain was disrupted &mdash; this is called dual-sourcing or supply-chain resilience planning.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Comparing Material Costs: Three Examples</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Understanding relative cost is as important as knowing absolute price. Consider these comparisons:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Copper vs gold for electrical contacts.</strong> Both are excellent conductors, but gold is roughly 60,000 times more expensive per kilogram than copper. Copper is used for all general-purpose electrical wiring and contacts. Gold is reserved only for high-reliability micro-contacts (spacecraft, medical implants) where its superior corrosion resistance justifies the premium cost and where the contact area is tiny so the total mass of gold is very small. For a consumer power cable, using gold would make the product impossibly expensive to market.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Timber vs structural steel for a roof beam.</strong> Structural timber (C24 grade) costs significantly less per metre of beam than an equivalent steel RSJ, and timber is a renewable resource. For a domestic roof span up to about 6 metres, timber is the standard choice. For longer spans or where fire resistance is critical, steel is preferred despite its higher cost, because its strength-to-cross-section ratio and non-combustibility justify the premium. The engineer must calculate whether the saving on material cost outweighs the higher fabrication cost of steel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Polymer housing vs metal housing for a handheld tool.</strong> Injection-moulded nylon or ABS housing is far cheaper to produce in large quantities than an equivalent aluminium alloy die-casting: the tooling cost is similar, but the cycle time is shorter and no machining or surface treatment is needed. For small quantities (fewer than a few hundred units) machined aluminium may actually be cheaper because expensive injection-mould tooling cannot be amortised over enough units.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in three sentences why a small prototype batch has a higher per-unit material cost than a production batch of the same component.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Economies of scale reduce unit cost as quantity rises because fixed costs (delivery, handling, tooling, setup) are spread over more parts. This is why prototype unit costs are always much higher than production-run unit costs &mdash; an important factor when giving clients a production-price estimate.</p>
</div>

    <span>Calculating Parts per Sheet and Material Waste</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common calculation question involves working out how many parts fit on a standard sheet and what the resulting material cost per part is. The critical trap is dividing sheet area by part area and treating the result as the number of parts. In practice, parts must be laid out in a rectangular grid (or an efficient nesting pattern) and you cannot use partial parts. The correct method is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Step 1 &mdash; Divide the sheet length by the part length (round DOWN to the nearest whole number) to find how many fit along the length. Step 2 &mdash; Divide the sheet width by the part width (round DOWN) to find how many fit across the width. Step 3 &mdash; Multiply the two whole numbers to find total parts per sheet. Step 4 &mdash; Divide the sheet cost by the number of parts to find material cost per part. The waste (offcut) area is the difference between the sheet area and the total area of all the cut parts. Minimising this waste is a key goal of production planning &mdash; nesting software on CNC cutting machines optimises layouts automatically.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Material cost is determined by rarity, processing energy, pre-treatment and quantity ordered; economies of scale mean unit cost falls as order volume rises, so prototype batches always cost more per part than production runs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Standard stock forms (sheet, bar, tube, rod, wire, angle) allow quick delivery from distributors and avoid custom-processing costs; lead time is a key availability factor alongside price.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Sheet-yield calculations require rounding DOWN to whole parts along each dimension before multiplying &mdash; dividing total areas overestimates the part count and underestimates waste.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">For sheet-yield calculation questions, always round DOWN when calculating how many parts fit along each dimension &mdash; never round up, because you cannot use a fraction of a part. Show your working in two steps (length fit &times; width fit) before multiplying. Missing this step and dividing areas directly is the most common error in these calculation questions and costs the final accuracy mark. For &lsquo;give two reasons why material X costs more than material Y,&rsquo; name a specific mechanism (rarity, processing energy, lead time) rather than just writing &lsquo;it is harder to get.&rsquo;</p>
