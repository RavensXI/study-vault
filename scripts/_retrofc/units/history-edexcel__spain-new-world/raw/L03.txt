--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Landfall, October 1492</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the early hours of 12 October 1492 a Pinz&oacute;n lookout sighted the white cliffs of an island in the Bahamas. Columbus called it San Salvador (&lsquo;Holy Saviour&rsquo;); its inhabitants called it Guanahani. The first thing Columbus did on landing was plant a Castilian banner and read out a formal claim to the island in the name of Ferdinand and Isabella &mdash; in Spanish, to people who did not speak it. Within hours, curious Tainos came to look at the strangers. Columbus noted in his journal that they were &lsquo;very gentle&rsquo;, naked, generous with food and gifts, and unfamiliar with iron weapons. He also noted, with intense interest, that some wore small pieces of gold in their nostrils.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: the date Columbus reached the Bahamas, the name of the first island, and the name of the Indigenous nation he met.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Columbus made landfall at Guanahani in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492, met the Taino people, and immediately fixed on the small gold ornaments they wore as evidence that wealth lay nearby.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Tainos and the Caribs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The peoples Columbus encountered were not one group. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Arawak-speaking Indigenous peoples of the Greater Antilles, organised into chiefdoms led by caciques; the first Indigenous nation Columbus encountered.">Tainos</dfn> were Arawak-speaking, lived in the Bahamas and the Greater Antilles (Cuba, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica) and were organised into chiefdoms led by hereditary leaders called <dfn class="term" data-def="The hereditary chief of a Taino community, responsible for political leadership, ritual life and relations with neighbouring peoples.">caciques</dfn>. They farmed cassava, sweet potato, maize and tobacco, fished, traded across the islands in large canoes, and lived in villages of thatched houses around a central plaza. They had no metal tools, no horses, no wheels and no immunity to European diseases.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Indigenous peoples of the Lesser Antilles, who resisted Spanish colonisation more militarily than the Tainos did and were stereotyped by Spanish observers as warlike.">Caribs</dfn>, by contrast, lived further south in the Lesser Antilles. The Tainos described them to Columbus as raiders who attacked Taino villages, and Columbus seized on this to label them &lsquo;cannibals&rsquo; &mdash; a word he popularised that derives from his garbled spelling of &lsquo;Carib&rsquo;. The cannibal label was important: Spanish law eventually allowed enslavement of Indigenous peoples judged hostile or anthropophagous, and the Carib stereotype gave Spanish slavers a legal opening that they would later exploit. Edexcel students should treat the &lsquo;cannibal&rsquo; framing as a feature of the Spanish source perspective, not a neutral description.</p>

Columbus reached lands new to Europeans, by accident rather than by design &mdash; he died in 1506 still believing he had touched the outer islands of Asia. The Edexcel 2026 specification deliberately uses the verb &lsquo;reached&rsquo; rather than &lsquo;discovered&rsquo;, and rewards students who treat Indigenous peoples as actors in the story rather than as background to a Spanish achievement.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Looking for Gold</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">For nine weeks Columbus zig-zagged through the Bahamas, along the north coast of Cuba (which he insisted was the Asian mainland) and across to the large island he named La Espa&ntilde;ola (Hispaniola, modern Haiti and the Dominican Republic). At every stop he asked the same question: where does the gold come from? Tainos politely pointed him further on, partly to be helpful, partly to move the strangers along. Small gold ornaments could be traded for hawk&rsquo;s bells, glass beads, scraps of cloth or broken pottery shards &mdash; an exchange that bewildered the Spanish, who did not realise how rare and ritually loaded these European materials were for Taino communities. The journal entries from this period read like a man trying to convince Isabella, in real time, that he had found Asia.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Christmas Eve and the Wreck of the Santa Mar&iacute;a</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Disaster struck on the night of 24&ndash;25 December 1492. The flagship Santa Mar&iacute;a ran aground on a reef off the north coast of Hispaniola. The local cacique, Guacanagar&iacute;, sent canoes to help unload her cargo &mdash; without his help, the loss would have been complete. Columbus took the wreckage as providence: God, he wrote, had wanted him to leave a settlement here. The Santa Mar&iacute;a&rsquo;s timbers were used to build a wooden fort, which Columbus called <dfn class="term" data-def="The first European settlement in the Americas, built from the wreck of the Santa Mar&iacute;a on the north coast of Hispaniola in December 1492.">La Navidad</dfn> (&lsquo;Christmas&rsquo;).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">La Navidad: The Template</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Thirty-nine men were left at La Navidad under Columbus&rsquo;s deputy, Diego de Arana. Their orders were to seek gold, treat the Tainos well, and wait for the relief fleet that Columbus would bring back from Spain. Columbus sailed for home on 4 January 1493 with the Pinta and the Ni&ntilde;a, leaving the small garrison behind. The settlement was the first European colony in the Americas. It was also a model for everything that followed: a small Spanish garrison, total dependence on local goodwill, an unspoken assumption that Indigenous peoples would feed and tolerate them, and an explicit hunger for gold.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">Reconstructing Taino views is hard because almost no Taino-language sources survive. Later writers like Bartolom&eacute; de las Casas, who knew Hispaniola well, suggest that Taino caciques such as Guacanagar&iacute; saw the Spaniards initially as useful trading partners and possible allies against rival chiefdoms &mdash; not as conquerors. The European trinkets exchanged for gold ornaments were rare prestige goods in Taino terms. Treating Indigenous peoples as actors with their own strategies, not as background to a Spanish achievement, is exactly the analytical move Edexcel rewards.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the Spanish settlement founded by Columbus in December 1492, the date, and the name of the Taino cacique who helped him.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>La Navidad was founded on the north coast of Hispaniola in December 1492 from the wreck of the Santa Mar&iacute;a. The local Taino cacique Guacanagar&iacute; helped salvage the cargo. Thirty-nine men were left behind under Diego de Arana when Columbus sailed home in January 1493.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The First Encounter from Both Sides</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">From the Taino perspective in late 1492, the Spaniards were exotic visitors with strange ships, beards, animals and weapons &mdash; not yet conquerors. Trade was useful and the strangers seemed, at first, to need help. From the Spanish perspective, the Tainos were a docile, naked people whose lack of metal weapons and whose interest in beads &lsquo;proved&rsquo; their innocence and their suitability for conversion. Both perspectives, in their different ways, were wrong about what was about to happen. When Columbus returned in November 1493, he would find La Navidad burnt and all 39 men dead &mdash; killed after they kidnapped Taino women, raided villages and turned on Guacanagar&iacute;&rsquo;s rivals. The template the colonists laid down in 1492 collapsed within twelve months.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Columbus made landfall at Guanahani (San Salvador) on 12 October 1492 and met the Tainos &mdash; an Arawak-speaking people organised into chiefdoms under caciques.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">He explored the Bahamas, the north coast of Cuba and Hispaniola, hunting for gold and labelling the more militarised Caribs to the south &lsquo;cannibals&rsquo; &mdash; a category Spanish slavers would later exploit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">After the Santa Mar&iacute;a wrecked on Christmas Eve 1492, Columbus founded La Navidad with the help of the cacique Guacanagar&iacute; and left 39 men under Diego de Arana &mdash; the first European settlement in the Americas, and a template that would not survive the year.</li>
</ul>
