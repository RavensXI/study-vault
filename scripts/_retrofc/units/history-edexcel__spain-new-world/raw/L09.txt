Cortes’s rule over New Spain, the consequences for the Aztecs, and the Pacific base at Panama.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Conqueror to Governor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tenochtitlan had fallen on 13 August 1521. Two years later, in October 1523, Emperor Charles V appointed <dfn class="term" data-def="Spanish conquistador (1485&ndash;1547) who led the 1519&ndash;21 conquest of the Aztec Empire and became Governor and Captain-General of New Spain in 1523.">Hernan Cortes</dfn> as <dfn class="term" data-def="A senior royal title combining military and civil command, given to Cortes in 1523 over the new colony of New Spain.">Captain-General</dfn> and Governor of New Spain. The appointment confirmed Cortes&rsquo;s victory at the expense of his old rival Diego Velazquez, the governor of Cuba who had originally tried to recall the expedition. For the next five years, Cortes governed an enormous, half-conquered territory at almost ten thousand kilometres from Madrid &mdash; a distance that gave him real local power, but that also made the crown deeply nervous.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in what year was Cortes appointed Governor of New Spain, and in what year was he recalled?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cortes ruled New Spain as Governor and Captain-General from 1523 to 1528. Charles V then recalled him to Spain and replaced his personal rule with the <em>audiencia</em> &mdash; a royal court of judges directly answerable to Madrid.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Rebuilding Mexico City</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Cortes&rsquo;s first project as governor was to rebuild the ruined Aztec capital. The siege of 1521 had levelled Tenochtitlan; surviving Indigenous labourers were now forced to clear the rubble of their own city and build a Spanish capital on top. Mexico City rose on the same lake island grid &mdash; the cathedral was placed on the site of the Templo Mayor, the viceregal palace on the ruins of Montezuma&rsquo;s palace, and the Plaza Mayor over the old Aztec ceremonial square. The geography of central Mexico City today is the geography of Tenochtitlan with Spanish stone laid over Mexica foundations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Encomiendas and the New Aristocracy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Cortes also rewarded his men. He granted <dfn class="term" data-def="A Spanish grant giving a settler the right to demand tribute and labour from a named Indigenous community in return for &lsquo;protection&rsquo; and Christian instruction.">encomiendas</dfn> &mdash; rights to Indigenous tribute and labour &mdash; to his Spanish soldiers, in many cases recycling the Aztec tribute lists wholesale. The maize, cotton and labour that had once flowed to Tenochtitlan now flowed to Spanish encomenderos instead. Crucially, the people doing the carrying did not change. The encomienda was not a new economy; it was the old Aztec tribute economy with new owners on top. Franciscan and Dominican friars arrived from 1524 onwards to convert the Indigenous population &mdash; the <em>Twelve Apostles of Mexico</em>, Franciscans who landed in May 1524, are the most famous group &mdash; and missions began alongside the encomiendas.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mostly, no &mdash; not at first. The encomienda inherited the Aztec tribute system. The same Nahua communities that had paid maize, cotton and labour to Tenochtitlan now paid them to Spanish encomenderos. What changed was the destination of the tribute and the layer of Catholic worship laid on top. New economic structures &mdash; silver mining, the trans-Pacific Manila trade, the sugar economy &mdash; would come later. Top-band answers note this continuity: in the short term, the conquest changed who was at the top, not how the bottom worked.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Why Cortes Was Recalled</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">By 1526 Cortes had marched south on a long, costly expedition to Honduras (1524&ndash;26) hunting down a rebellious lieutenant; the journey took him out of the capital for almost two years and his enemies used the absence well. Rivals at the Spanish court accused him of corruption, of withholding the king&rsquo;s share of treasure, of executing the captured Aztec emperor Cuauhtemoc on the Honduras march in 1525, and &mdash; most damaging of all &mdash; of behaving like an independent prince. Charles V had a simple rule: a conquistador who built a kingdom of his own was a problem. In 1528 Cortes was recalled to Spain. He was given titles and estates (the marquisate of the Valley of Oaxaca) but stripped of the governorship. Royal power in New Spain was handed to the first <dfn class="term" data-def="A royal court of judges that exercised executive and judicial authority in a Spanish colony, directly answerable to the crown in Madrid.">audiencia</dfn> in 1528, and from 1535 to a Viceroy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two reasons Charles V recalled Cortes in 1528, and what replaced him.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cortes was recalled in 1528 because the crown distrusted any conquistador who became too powerful and independent. He was replaced first by the audiencia (royal court) in 1528 and then by the Viceroy of New Spain in 1535.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Consequences for the Aztecs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">For the Mexica and the wider Nahua world, the consequences of conquest unfolded on several timescales. The immediate consequences were the most visible: the demolition of Tenochtitlan, the execution of priests and rulers, the burning of <dfn class="term" data-def="A pre-Columbian Mexican manuscript book made of folded bark paper, recording religious, historical or astronomical knowledge.">codices</dfn>, the systematic dismantling of the temple priesthood. The medium-term consequences were demographic. Central Mexico&rsquo;s population is estimated to have fallen from somewhere between 15 and 25 million people in 1519 to perhaps 1 to 2 million by 1600 &mdash; the deadliest collapse in recorded human history. Smallpox in 1520, measles in 1531, typhus in 1545 and again in 1576: each wave killed a generation of people who had no immunity, while forced labour, malnutrition and the loss of land made survivors easier to kill the next time.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">But the long-term picture also shows survival. The Nahuatl language continued to be spoken by millions and was written down in alphabetic script by Indigenous scribes (often working with Franciscans). Nahua codex-painting traditions adapted to Catholic subjects rather than disappearing. Local cabildos &mdash; town councils &mdash; preserved a layer of Indigenous self-government under the Spanish empire. Catholic worship absorbed Indigenous symbolism: the cult of the Virgin of Guadalupe (from 1531) drew on the older shrine of Tonantzin at the same hilltop. The conquest was catastrophic, but it did not erase Indigenous Mexico.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Panama: A Pacific Gateway</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">While Cortes was reorganising New Spain, a second front was opening on the Pacific. In 1513 Vasco Nunez de Balboa had crossed the isthmus and become the first European to see the Pacific Ocean from the Americas. The settlement at Darien was rough and unhealthy, but the strategic prize was huge: a base from which Spanish ships could push south down the Pacific coast of South America. In 1519 Pedrarias Davila &mdash; the same governor who had Balboa beheaded that year &mdash; founded Panama City on the Pacific side of the isthmus. Goods and treasure crossed the narrow neck of land overland by mule train; ships unloaded on one ocean and reloaded on the other.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The significance of Panama was that it gave Spain a continental Pacific port two thousand kilometres south of Mexico. From Panama, Francisco Pizarro, Diego de Almagro and the priest Hernando de Luque organised the expeditions of 1524, 1526 and finally 1530&ndash;32 that would bring down the Inca empire. Without Panama, the conquest of Peru &mdash; and the silver economy that would underwrite Spanish power for the rest of the century &mdash; could not have happened on the timetable that it did.</p>

The conquest of the Inca, which begins in 1532, is the direct continuation of this lesson &mdash; Pizarro launches from Panama, copies Cortes&rsquo;s coalition-and-capital playbook, and arrives in Peru just as smallpox and a civil war have already broken Inca leadership. The two halves of mainland Spanish America fit together.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Cortes was appointed Governor and Captain-General of New Spain in 1523, rebuilt Mexico City on the ruins of Tenochtitlan and granted encomiendas to his men. He was recalled in 1528 because Charles V distrusted independent conquistadors and replaced him first with the audiencia and from 1535 with a Viceroy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The consequences for the Aztecs ran across three timescales: immediate destruction of the temple state, medium-term demographic collapse from smallpox, measles and typhus (population falling from 15&ndash;25 million in 1519 to 1&ndash;2 million by 1600), and long-term survival of Nahuatl language, codex-painting and Indigenous local government under Spanish rule.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Panama City &mdash; founded by Pedrarias Davila in 1519 &mdash; gave Spain a Pacific gateway. From it, Pizarro launched the expedition that would bring down the Inca empire from 1532, making Panama the strategic link between the two halves of Spanish America.</li>
</ul>
