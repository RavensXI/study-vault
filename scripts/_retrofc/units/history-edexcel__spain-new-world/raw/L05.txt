Columbus’s later voyages, the founding of Santo Domingo and the catastrophe of smallpox.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">After La Navidad: Columbus Returns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Columbus sailed back into Hispaniola in November 1493, he found La Navidad burnt to the ground and all 39 of the men he had left behind dead. The Taino cacique Guacanagar&iacute; reported &mdash; through gestures and a few translated words &mdash; that the garrison had quarrelled with each other, kidnapped Taino women and turned on the cacique&rsquo;s rivals, who had then wiped them out. The first Spanish settlement in the Americas had collapsed inside twelve months. Columbus did not turn back. His second voyage was a colonising expedition on a scale his first had not been: 17 ships, around 1,200 men, livestock, seeds, tools, soldiers and priests. Spain had decided that the Indies were worth occupying, not just exploring.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many ships and men sailed on Columbus&rsquo;s second voyage, and what did he find when he reached La Navidad?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Columbus&rsquo;s second voyage of 1493 carried 17 ships and around 1,200 men. He arrived to find La Navidad destroyed and all 39 settlers dead &mdash; killed by Tainos after the garrison kidnapped women and turned on Guacanagar&iacute;&rsquo;s rivals.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Three More Voyages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Columbus made four voyages in total. His third (1498) reached the South American mainland near the mouth of the Orinoco &mdash; though Columbus, ever stubborn, insisted he had touched another peninsula of Asia. His fourth (1502&ndash;04) explored the coasts of Honduras and Panama in a desperate hunt for a strait through to the East. By the time he died in 1506, he had still not accepted the truth: the lands he had reached were not Asia but a continent unknown to medieval Europe. The crisp word for these voyages is &lsquo;reached&rsquo;, not &lsquo;discovered&rsquo; &mdash; the Tainos, the Caribs and the peoples of the South American coast had lived there for thousands of years, and Norse settlers had reached Newfoundland around the year 1000.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Santo Domingo, 1496</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1496 Columbus&rsquo;s younger brother <dfn class="term" data-def="Christopher Columbus&rsquo;s brother; appointed governor of Hispaniola in his absence and founder of Santo Domingo in 1496.">Bartholomew Columbus</dfn> founded a new settlement on the south coast of Hispaniola: Santo Domingo. Built around a sheltered river mouth, it became the first permanent European city in the Americas and the capital of Spanish operations in the New World for the next thirty years. Every later Spanish expedition into the Caribbean and onto the mainland &mdash; the conquest of Cuba, Balboa&rsquo;s push to the Pacific, Cortes&rsquo;s Mexican expedition &mdash; was launched, supplied or supervised through Santo Domingo. The first stone cathedral, the first university, and the first royal court (audiencia) in the Americas were all built there.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">La Navidad had been a wooden fort built from a wreck on the north coast. Santo Domingo was deliberately sited on the south coast for safer harbours, closer to gold-bearing rivers, and away from the territory of caciques the Spanish had alienated. Where La Navidad relied on Indigenous goodwill, Santo Domingo was planned as a permanent Spanish town with its own walls, port, governor and church &mdash; a colonial city, not a frontier outpost.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Columbus as Governor: The Brutal Years</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Columbus served as governor of Hispaniola from 1494 to 1500. The picture is grim. To force gold from the Tainos, he imposed a tribute system: every Taino over fourteen on the gold-bearing parts of the island had to deliver a small amount of gold dust every three months, or have their hands cut off. Settlers seized Tainos as forced labour, kidnapped women, and put down resistance with mass executions. Columbus also faced settler revolts, because Spaniards who had expected easy riches found tropical disease, hunger and an island that did not, in fact, contain Asia&rsquo;s spice markets. Royal commissioners began to receive complaints &mdash; from settlers and friars alike &mdash; that the governor was tyrannising both his Indigenous subjects and his own colonists.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">In 1500 the Crown sent the royal commissioner <dfn class="term" data-def="Royal commissioner sent in 1500 to investigate Columbus&rsquo;s rule on Hispaniola; arrested Columbus and shipped him home in chains.">Francisco de Bobadilla</dfn> to investigate. Bobadilla arrested Columbus and his brothers, put them in chains, and shipped them back to Spain. Columbus was eventually freed and his estates restored, but he was never again given the governorship of Hispaniola. The first European empire in the Americas had begun &mdash; under Spanish rule, not Columbus&rsquo;s personal rule.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Treatment of the Tainos</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">For the Tainos of Hispaniola, the consequences of Spanish settlement were catastrophic. Forced labour to find gold left fields untended; villages were broken up; sexual violence and the seizure of women destroyed family structures; those who resisted were enslaved or killed; many took their own lives rather than submit. Estimates of the pre-contact Taino population on Hispaniola range from around 250,000 to over a million; by 1514 a Spanish census counted around 30,000 Taino tributaries left. By 1540 the Tainos of Hispaniola were essentially extinct as an independent people. This collapse was already well underway BEFORE smallpox arrived &mdash; a critical point that examiners flag again and again.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the estimated Taino population on Hispaniola in 1492 and in 1514, and name three Spanish causes of the collapse other than disease.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Taino population of Hispaniola fell from an estimated 250,000&ndash;1,000,000 in 1492 to around 30,000 by 1514. The collapse was caused by forced labour, sexual violence, dispossession and warfare &mdash; AND, after 1518, by smallpox.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Smallpox Reaches Hispaniola, 1518</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In late 1518 or early 1519, a ship arrived at Santo Domingo carrying European sailors infected with <dfn class="term" data-def="A highly contagious viral disease causing fever, rash and high mortality; introduced to the Americas by Europeans in 1518&ndash;19, with no inherited Indigenous immunity.">smallpox</dfn>. Indigenous peoples in the Americas had no inherited immunity to the disease. The result was an epidemic of horrifying speed: in some districts, mortality reached 50&ndash;90% within weeks. From Hispaniola, smallpox spread to Puerto Rico, Cuba, the mainland, and ahead of the conquistadors all the way to Mexico and Peru. Disease became the deadliest weapon Spain ever wielded in the Americas &mdash; far deadlier than steel or gunpowder &mdash; even though Spaniards did not know they were carrying it.</p>

    <span>Misconception: was smallpox a separate &lsquo;natural&rsquo; disaster?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Students often treat smallpox as bad luck &mdash; a separate natural disaster overlaid on conquest. The historians&rsquo; consensus is that the two cannot be cleanly separated. Forced labour, malnutrition, dispossession and warfare made Indigenous communities vastly more vulnerable to epidemics than they would otherwise have been. Spanish demands stripped fields of farmers, broke up families and concentrated workers in unhealthy conditions. When smallpox arrived, it found populations already weakened by Spanish rule. Treating disease as part of conquest &mdash; alongside steel, horses and the encomienda &mdash; rather than as something separate is the analytical move that wins the top band.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Columbian Exchange</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Smallpox was the deadliest part of a much wider biological reshuffle that historians now call the <dfn class="term" data-def="The biological exchange of plants, animals and diseases between the Americas and Eurasia after 1492 &mdash; including smallpox, measles and influenza moving west, and crops like maize and the potato moving east.">Columbian Exchange</dfn>. European diseases (measles, influenza, typhus) crossed west; Indigenous American crops (maize, the potato, tomatoes, tobacco, cassava) crossed east; Spanish horses, pigs, cattle and sheep were introduced to the Americas, and the rats and weeds that travelled with them transformed Caribbean ecologies. The Caribbean settlements were the laboratory in which all of this happened first &mdash; and where the deadliest costs of the exchange fell on the Tainos.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Why It Mattered</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">By 1520 the Caribbean had become the template for everything Spain would do across the Americas: a permanent capital city, a violent gold-tribute economy, a labour system that destroyed the Indigenous workforce, and an unintended biological catastrophe that swept ahead of every Spanish expedition into the mainland. When Cortes left Cuba for Mexico in 1519, the model he carried in his head &mdash; small Spanish elite, Indigenous labour force, gold first &mdash; had been forged on Hispaniola. So had its consequences.</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Bartholomew Columbus founded Santo Domingo in 1496, the first permanent European city in the Americas and the capital of Spanish operations for the next thirty years.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Columbus&rsquo;s rule as governor of Hispaniola (1494&ndash;1500) imposed a brutal gold-tribute system; he was arrested by Francisco de Bobadilla in 1500 and shipped home in chains.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The Taino population of Hispaniola collapsed from hundreds of thousands in 1492 to around 30,000 by 1514, driven by forced labour, sexual violence and dispossession &mdash; AND, after 1518, by smallpox.</li>
</ul>
