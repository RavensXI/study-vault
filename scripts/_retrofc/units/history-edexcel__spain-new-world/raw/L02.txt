How Columbus won Isabella’s backing in 1492, where he meant to go, and what really happened.

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Genoese Outsider with a Big Idea</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Christopher Columbus was born in Genoa around 1451, the son of a wool weaver. He went to sea young, sailed as far as Iceland and the Gulf of Guinea, and read the geographers of his day with intense, sometimes wishful, attention. His central idea was simple but radical: sail west across the open Atlantic and you would reach the eastern edge of Asia &mdash; specifically Cipango (Japan) and the wealthy lands described by Marco Polo. Columbus was right that the Earth was a sphere; he was wrong about the size. He underestimated the planet&rsquo;s circumference by about a third. The land mass he eventually reached &mdash; the Caribbean &mdash; sat roughly where his calculations placed Japan.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: where Columbus thought he was going, why he thought it was reachable, and which monarch finally backed him.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Columbus believed he could reach Asia by sailing west; his estimate of the Earth&rsquo;s size was a third too small. After six years of refusal in Portugal and Castile, Queen Isabella signed the Capitulations of Santa Fe in April 1492.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Six Years of Rejection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Columbus first pitched his westward plan to King John II of Portugal in 1485. Lisbon&rsquo;s royal experts &mdash; the best in Europe &mdash; calculated his maths and rejected him. Portugal also had its own better-grounded plan: keep going down the African coast. Columbus moved to Spain in 1485 and found himself queuing behind the Reconquista. Ferdinand and Isabella granted him an audience in 1486 but their commission of scholars studied his plan for years and concluded the Atlantic was simply too wide. Columbus pitched again in 1491 and was rejected again. He was preparing to leave for France in early 1492 when the royal treasurer of Aragon, <dfn class="term" data-def="The royal treasurer of the Crown of Aragon who lobbied Isabella to back Columbus and helped raise the financing for the 1492 voyage.">Luis de Sant&aacute;ngel</dfn>, intervened.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Sant&aacute;ngel argued that the cost was small, the potential reward huge, and the political risk of letting Columbus go to France enormous. Isabella, fresh from the surrender of Granada in January 1492, agreed. The deal was signed at Santa Fe near Granada in April 1492 &mdash; the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The agreement signed at Santa Fe in April 1492 between Columbus and the Spanish crown, granting Columbus the titles of Admiral of the Ocean Sea and Viceroy of any lands found.">Capitulations of Santa Fe</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Isabella&rsquo;s Mixed Motives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Why did Isabella finally say yes? Three motives ran together, and exam answers should keep them separate, not flatten them into one. The first was religious: she wanted to spread Christianity, both to bring &lsquo;new souls&rsquo; into the Church and to fund a future crusade to retake Jerusalem. The second was economic: gold from Asia, even via a small share of trade, would refill a treasury drained by the Granada war. The third was political: Portugal&rsquo;s lead in African and Asian exploration was an embarrassment. A successful Spanish westward route would leapfrog Lisbon overnight. Sant&aacute;ngel&rsquo;s pitch worked because it spoke to all three at once.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">No. Neither Isabella nor Columbus had any idea the Americas existed. Isabella sponsored a westward voyage to <em>Asia</em>. Columbus reached lands that were new to Europeans &mdash; not new to the people who lived in them. Indigenous peoples had inhabited the Americas for at least 15,000 years, and Norse seafarers under Leif Erikson had reached Newfoundland around the year 1000. Columbus himself died in 1506 still convinced he had reached the outer islands of Asia. The crisp Edexcel verb is &lsquo;reached&rsquo;, not &lsquo;discovered&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Terms Columbus Won</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For a Genoese commoner, the Capitulations were extraordinary. Columbus secured the title <dfn class="term" data-def="The hereditary title of supreme Spanish naval commander, granted to Columbus under the Capitulations of Santa Fe in 1492.">Admiral of the Ocean Sea</dfn>, the right to be Viceroy and Governor of any lands he found, ten per cent of all profits, the right to invest in (and earn from) any future voyage, and hereditary status for his heirs. The Crown bore most of the costs, with the Pinz&oacute;n family of Palos contributing experienced sailors. The terms reflected how unlikely the venture seemed: Isabella was paying little upfront and risked nothing if Columbus failed. The Capitulations would later cause endless legal disputes when the lands turned out to be vastly more than anyone expected.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Voyage Itself, August&ndash;October 1492</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Three ships left the port of Palos on 3 August 1492: the small carrack Santa Mar&iacute;a (Columbus&rsquo;s flagship), and two smaller caravels, the Pinta (under Mart&iacute;n Alonso Pinz&oacute;n) and the Ni&ntilde;a. About 87 men sailed, mostly Castilians and a handful of foreign sailors. The fleet stopped at the Canary Islands &mdash; Spanish-owned since the 1480s &mdash; for repairs and fresh food, then turned west on 6 September. From there, no European had sailed back.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">The voyage stretched to 33 days of open Atlantic. By October the crews were close to mutiny: the trade winds had pushed them so far that no one believed they could ever sail home into them. Columbus kept two logs &mdash; an honest one for himself and a falsified one showing shorter distances to share with the men. False sightings of land from rope-tricks and Sargasso weed kept morale flickering. Around two in the morning on 12 October, a lookout on the Pinta sighted the white cliffs of an island in the Bahamas. Columbus called it San Salvador; its Indigenous name was Guanahani.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: the date Columbus left Palos, the date of landfall, and the names of the three ships.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Columbus left Palos on 3 August 1492 with three ships &mdash; Santa Mar&iacute;a, Pinta and Ni&ntilde;a &mdash; and 87 men. After 33 days of open Atlantic crossing and near-mutiny, landfall was made at Guanahani in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why It Mattered Even Before Anyone Knew What It Was</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Columbus had not reached Asia. He had reached lands neither he nor Isabella knew existed. Yet the political fact was clear at once: Spain could now claim a westward route, sponsored by the Crown, with a Castilian flag planted in lands beyond Portuguese reach. Within a year Pope Alexander VI would issue Inter caetera dividing the &lsquo;new&rsquo; world between Spain and Portugal. Within two, the Treaty of Tordesillas would redraw that line. The voyage of 1492 mattered not because Columbus understood it &mdash; he didn&rsquo;t &mdash; but because it kicked off the diplomatic and religious machinery that turned an accident into an empire.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Columbus pitched a westward route to Asia for six years before Luis de Sant&aacute;ngel persuaded Isabella to sign the Capitulations of Santa Fe in April 1492.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Isabella&rsquo;s motives mixed religion (spreading Christianity), economics (gold and trade) and politics (outflanking Portugal); they reinforced each other rather than competing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The 1492 voyage took 33 days and ended with landfall at Guanahani on 12 October &mdash; not Asia, but a foothold from which Spain could claim a whole new western world.</li>
</ul>
