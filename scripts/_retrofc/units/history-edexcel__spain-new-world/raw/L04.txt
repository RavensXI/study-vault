How Spain and Portugal divided the world in 1494 with papal backing — and why it mattered.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Diplomatic Crisis Made by Columbus</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Columbus sailed into Lisbon harbour on his return voyage in March 1493, King John II of Portugal was waiting. The Portuguese crown understood almost immediately that something had gone wrong for them. For nearly a century Portugal had invested in the African route to Asia: forts at El Mina, navigation schools at Sagres, the rounding of the Cape of Good Hope by Bartolomeu Dias in 1488. A westward Castilian voyage that claimed to have reached Asian islands threatened all of that overnight. King John warned Columbus that under previous treaties the new lands belonged to Portugal &mdash; an opening shot in what could easily have become a Iberian war.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: the year of Inter caetera, the year of Tordesillas, and the distance the line was moved between them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The papal bull Inter caetera (1493) drew a line 100 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands; the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) moved it to 370 leagues west &mdash; far enough to give Portugal an unintended claim on Brazil in 1500.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Papal Solution: Inter Caetera (1493)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Ferdinand and Isabella moved fast. Their man in Rome was Rodrigo Borgia, who had become Pope Alexander VI just the previous year. Pope Alexander was Spanish-born, had owed his elevation partly to Spanish votes, and had no wish to side with Portugal. In May 1493 he issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal papal decree, sealed with a lead bulla; the chief instrument by which medieval popes pronounced on disputes between Catholic kingdoms.">papal bull</dfn> Inter caetera, drawing a line of demarcation from pole to pole, 100 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Everything west of the line was reserved for Spain; everything east, including the African route, was Portugal&rsquo;s. The bull explicitly framed Spanish exploration as a duty to spread Christianity, allowing Ferdinand and Isabella to present any seizure of land as a religious mission rather than naked conquest.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">For Spain, this was a gift: a religiously underwritten title to half the unknown world before they had any clear idea what was in it. For Portugal, the line was unacceptable. King John II protested that the boundary cut too close to Portuguese-held islands and ignored Portuguese knowledge that land lay in the western Atlantic closer than Inter caetera assumed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Treaty of Tordesillas, 7 June 1494</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Rather than risk war, the two crowns negotiated directly. Their representatives met at Tordesillas, a small town in Castile on the river Duero. The resulting <dfn class="term" data-def="The June 1494 treaty between Spain and Portugal that moved the line of demarcation to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands, dividing the non-European world between the two crowns.">Treaty of Tordesillas</dfn>, signed on 7 June 1494, moved the line significantly westward, to 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. In return, Portugal accepted Spanish title to everything west of the new line. The pope ratified the new line in 1506. From a Castilian point of view, this looked like a concession; from John II&rsquo;s point of view, it was a victory bought with quiet inside knowledge.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Tordesillas only divided the &lsquo;new world&rsquo; between Spain and Portugal &mdash; it had no consultation with anyone living in the lands divided. The Indigenous peoples of the Americas, the Tainos, Mexica, Inca, Quechua and dozens more, were not party to the treaty and would not have recognised either crown&rsquo;s authority. The treaty also said nothing about the rest of Europe: France, England and the Netherlands all eventually rejected its legitimacy and built their own American empires anyway. So Tordesillas was decisive between two crowns &mdash; and irrelevant to most of the people whose lives it affected.</p>
  </div></div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Alexander VI &mdash; Rodrigo Borgia &mdash; was Spanish-born from Valencia. He had owed his elevation in 1492 partly to Spanish votes in the conclave, was a controversial figure even by the standards of the Borgia family, and depended on Ferdinand and Isabella&rsquo;s political backing in Italian disputes. Issuing Inter caetera in their favour cost him nothing politically and earned him Spanish gratitude. Critics at the time and since have argued that the bull was less a neutral religious decision than a political favour &mdash; a reminder that papal authority in this period operated inside European power politics, not above them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Cabral Surprise (1500)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Why did King John II push so hard for the line to be moved further west? Portugal had been gathering reports for years that land lay much closer to the African coast in the southern Atlantic than the original bull assumed. Six years after Tordesillas, Pedro &Aacute;lvares Cabral was sailing for India when his fleet, taking the wide southern Atlantic curve to catch the trade winds, made landfall in 1500 on what is now Brazil &mdash; east of the new line. Brazil therefore fell to Portugal entirely by Tordesillas&rsquo; definition. Whether Cabral&rsquo;s landing was an accident or a deliberate confirmation of insider Portuguese knowledge is still debated. What is not debated is that the renegotiated line gave Portugal a foothold in the Americas it would otherwise not have had.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why Tordesillas Mattered for the Period</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For the option as a whole, Tordesillas matters in three ways. First, it gave Spain unchallenged Catholic title (within Europe) to everything west of the line, which Cortes, Pizarro and the encomenderos would later use to justify conquest. Second, it embedded a religious framing that Spanish colonialism never abandoned: the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="A statement read in Spanish to Indigenous peoples before military attack, declaring that the pope had granted their lands to Spain and that resistance to Christian rule would be met with war.">Requerimiento</dfn>&rsquo; (drafted 1513) was based on the papal donation. Third, it became the template for later imperial agreements &mdash; European powers dividing the rest of the world among themselves without consulting any of its inhabitants. Tordesillas is therefore not just a date to remember; it is the legal scaffolding for the whole Spanish enterprise that follows.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three ways the Treaty of Tordesillas shaped the rest of the unit, with one detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tordesillas underwrote Spanish claims throughout the conquest era: it justified Spanish title in Catholic eyes, framed conquest as a Christian mission (used in the 1513 Requerimiento), and inadvertently gave Portugal Brazil after Cabral&rsquo;s 1500 landing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A Treaty No One Else Recognised</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">It is worth ending on the limits of Tordesillas. France&rsquo;s King Francis I famously demanded to see &lsquo;the clause in Adam&rsquo;s will&rsquo; that gave the world to Spain and Portugal. England, the Netherlands and France all eventually built American empires in defiance of the treaty &mdash; the very fact that they had to defy it shows how serious a tool of legitimacy it had become for Madrid and Lisbon. Indigenous peoples never recognised it. Within the Catholic Iberian world, however, Tordesillas gave Spanish empire-builders a piece of paper that turned conquest into mission, and that paper would be cited in encomienda awards, viceroyalty grants and missionary orders for the next 250 years.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Pope Alexander VI&rsquo;s 1493 bull Inter caetera drew a line 100 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands; Spain got everything west, Portugal everything east.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">King John II of Portugal forced the renegotiation that produced the Treaty of Tordesillas (7 June 1494), moving the line to 370 leagues west &mdash; an apparent concession that handed Portugal Brazil after Cabral&rsquo;s 1500 landing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Tordesillas was the legal scaffolding for the whole Spanish enterprise that followed; it underwrote conquest in Catholic eyes but had no force with non-Catholic Europe and was ignored by every Indigenous nation in the Americas.</li>
</ul>
