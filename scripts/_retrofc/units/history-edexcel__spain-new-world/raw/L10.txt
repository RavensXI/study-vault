--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Pizarro exploited an Inca civil war to capture Atahualpa at Cajamarca, and why Cuzco fell.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reconnaissance, 1528</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Spanish ships had been pushing south down the Pacific coast of South America from Panama since the early 1520s. The expedition that mattered was the third one. In 1528 Francisco Pizarro &mdash; an illegitimate, illiterate veteran from Trujillo in Spain who had been with Balboa when the Pacific was first reached &mdash; landed briefly on the Inca coast at Tumbes. He saw a wealthy, organised society he had not expected. He saw enough cloth, gold and well-built towns to know there was a major civilisation to the south. He returned to Spain in 1529 and, in the Capitulation of Toledo (signed by Queen Isabella of Portugal as regent in July 1529), secured the legal and royal authority to lead a full conquest expedition.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Death of Huayna Capac</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">While Pizarro was lobbying in Spain, the Inca empire was destroying itself. Around 1527, the Inca emperor <dfn class="term" data-def="Inca emperor (r. c1493&ndash;1527) whose death &mdash; probably from smallpox spreading south from Mexico through Indigenous trade routes ahead of the Spanish &mdash; triggered the Inca civil war.">Huayna Capac</dfn> died suddenly in the northern city of Quito, alongside his named heir. Spanish chroniclers and modern epidemiologists agree the most likely cause was smallpox &mdash; the disease that had reached Tenochtitlan in 1520 had moved south through Indigenous trade routes faster than the Spaniards themselves, and arrived in the Andes years before the conquistadors did. The empire suddenly had no recognised emperor. Two of Huayna Capac&rsquo;s sons claimed the throne instead: <dfn class="term" data-def="Inca emperor in Cuzco; defeated and killed by his half-brother Atahualpa just before the Spanish arrival in 1532.">Huascar</dfn>, the legitimate heir based in the southern capital Cuzco, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Inca emperor; victor of the Inca civil war over Huascar; captured by Pizarro at Cajamarca on 16 November 1532 and executed in July 1533.">Atahualpa</dfn>, his half-brother in the north, who had the loyalty of the empire&rsquo;s veteran armies.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who Huayna Capac&rsquo;s two rival heirs were, where each was based, and which won the civil war.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Huayna Capac&rsquo;s death (c.1527, probably from smallpox arriving ahead of the Spaniards) split the Inca empire between Huascar in Cuzco (legitimate) and Atahualpa in Quito (military). Atahualpa won the civil war just weeks before Pizarro arrived.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Inca Civil War, 1529&ndash;32</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Atahualpa&rsquo;s veteran northern army marched south against Cuzco. The campaign culminated in spring 1532 with Atahualpa&rsquo;s general Quizquiz capturing Huascar at the battle of Quipaipan. Indigenous chronicles describe massacres of Huascar&rsquo;s family and supporters &mdash; the empire was, in effect, two warring states. Atahualpa was on his way south to be crowned in Cuzco when news reached him that strange bearded men had landed on the coast.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Pizarro&rsquo;s 1530&ndash;32 Expedition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Pizarro sailed from Panama in January 1531 with about 168 men, 62 horses and a handful of cannon. He brought his half-brothers Hernando, Juan and Gonzalo Pizarro, his partner <dfn class="term" data-def="Pizarro&rsquo;s partner in the conquest of Peru; later rival; executed in 1538 after losing the conquistador civil war.">Diego de Almagro</dfn>, the Dominican friar Vicente de Valverde and the Indigenous interpreter Felipillo. They landed at the Bay of San Mateo and marched down the coast through the early months of 1532, founding the first Spanish town in Peru, San Miguel de Piura. In September 1532 they began the climb up into the Andes towards the highland city of Cajamarca, where Atahualpa was resting his army at the hot springs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Battle of Cajamarca, 16 November 1532</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Battle of <dfn class="term" data-def="The northern highland town in Peru where, on 16 November 1532, Pizarro&rsquo;s 168 Spaniards ambushed Atahualpa and his unarmed retinue, killing thousands and seizing the emperor.">Cajamarca</dfn> on 16 November 1532 may be the most disproportionate military encounter in recorded history. Atahualpa, surrounded by thousands of unarmed retainers in ceremonial dress, came out to meet Pizarro in the central plaza of the town. Friar Valverde read out the Requerimiento &mdash; the formal Spanish demand that the emperor accept the Catholic faith and Spanish authority &mdash; through the interpreter Felipillo. When Atahualpa picked up the Bible Valverde handed him, did not understand what to do with it, and dropped it, Pizarro&rsquo;s men gave the prepared signal. Hidden cavalry, foot soldiers with steel swords, and small cannon attacked the unarmed retinue. Thousands of Inca died in the plaza. Atahualpa was seized.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Atahualpa offered an enormous ransom: a room about 22 feet by 17 feet filled once with gold and twice with silver. Indigenous porters carried the metal in for months. The treasure was eventually melted down and distributed among the Spaniards (worth tens of millions of pounds in modern terms). Pizarro had Atahualpa garrotted in the plaza of Cajamarca on 26 July 1533 anyway &mdash; condemned on charges of plotting against the Spanish, idolatry and the murder of Huascar. The execution had no clear justification under Spanish law and Charles V later expressed displeasure.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">No &mdash; Pizarro arrived at the moment a civil war and an undocumented epidemic had already shattered Inca leadership. The historian Matthew Restall&rsquo;s &lsquo;Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest&rsquo; argues that the conquest of Peru, like the conquest of Mexico, was a coalition victory over a divided empire, not a Spanish solo achievement. Smallpox killed Huayna Capac. The civil war split the Inca army in two. Atahualpa&rsquo;s execution removed the empire&rsquo;s only recognised authority. Indigenous peoples Atahualpa had crushed in the civil war &mdash; the Cañari, the Huanca and Huascar&rsquo;s old loyalists &mdash; allied with the Spanish. Top-band judgements weigh disease, civil war, surprise at Cajamarca, Spanish horses and steel, and Indigenous allies against each other rather than collapsing the conquest into one cause.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Cuzco and Lima</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">After Atahualpa&rsquo;s execution, the Spaniards marched on Cuzco, the imperial capital, and entered the city in November 1533. They installed Manco Inca &mdash; another son of Huayna Capac &mdash; as a puppet emperor and looted the temple of Coricancha, melting down gold panels that had once lined its walls. In January 1535 Pizarro founded a new Spanish capital, Lima, on the Pacific coast &mdash; chosen so that Spanish ships could resupply quickly without climbing the Andes. The Inca empire&rsquo;s political centre had shifted from a highland Indigenous city to a coastal Spanish one.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Inca Revolt and Siege of Cuzco, 1536&ndash;37</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The puppet, however, did not stay quiet. In 1536 Manco Inca slipped out of Cuzco and raised an army of perhaps 100,000 Indigenous warriors. From May 1536 to early 1537 he besieged Cuzco, which was held by a Spanish garrison under Hernando and Juan Pizarro. The siege of Cuzco was the closest the Inca came to driving the Spanish out: the city burned, Juan Pizarro was killed, and the small Spanish force was reduced to fighting from inside the cathedral and the fortified hills above the city. The siege was finally broken when reinforcements arrived from Almagro returning out of Chile and from <dfn class="term" data-def="Spanish puppet Inca emperor (r. 1533&ndash;36 as puppet, then 1536&ndash;44 in resistance) who led the 1536&ndash;37 revolt and founded the rump Neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba.">Manco Inca&rsquo;s</dfn> Indigenous enemies switched sides. Manco himself retreated into the eastern jungle and founded a rump <dfn class="term" data-def="The mountain refuge in the eastern Andes where Manco Inca founded a rump Neo-Inca state that lasted until the Spanish destroyed it in 1572.">Vilcabamba</dfn> state that lasted until 1572.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the puppet emperor who led the 1536&ndash;37 siege of Cuzco, and where his rump state survived until 1572.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Manco Inca, installed by Pizarro as a puppet in 1533, raised an army of about 100,000 warriors and besieged Cuzco for ten months in 1536&ndash;37. He retreated to Vilcabamba, where the Neo-Inca state survived until 1572.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The conquest of Peru gave Spain a second continental empire to match New Spain. The Andean economy was reorganised around silver mining (Potosi was discovered in 1545, three years after the New Laws). Encomiendas were granted to conquistadors. The Quechua-speaking world entered a long, painful adjustment to Spanish rule. But the conquest also planted the seed of its own crisis: Pizarro and Almagro had been partners but were now rivals, and the conquistadors&rsquo; thirst for encomiendas would soon collide with Madrid&rsquo;s attempt to centralise authority. The 1546 conquistador rebellion against the New Laws &mdash; led by Pizarro&rsquo;s younger brother Gonzalo &mdash; was already in formation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">The two great conquests are remarkably similar. Both pivoted on a small Spanish force exploiting an empire weakened from inside &mdash; for Cortes the Tlaxcalan resentment and the Toxcatl rebellion; for Pizarro the Inca civil war and the death of Huayna Capac. Both depended on smallpox arriving ahead of or alongside the Spaniards (Tenochtitlan in 1520, the Andes around 1527). Both used the capture of a capital and a puppet emperor as the political endgame &mdash; Cuauhtemoc and Manco Inca played the same role. Both ended with conquistador civil wars over the spoils &mdash; Velazquez vs Cortes, Almagro vs Pizarro. Top-band judgement answers can use the comparison itself: the conquest of empires of millions by forces of hundreds is not explained by Spanish military superiority alone, but by a recurring pattern of Indigenous division, disease and coalition warfare.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Smallpox arrived in the Andes ahead of the Spaniards and killed Huayna Capac around 1527, triggering an Inca civil war between Huascar in Cuzco and Atahualpa in Quito. Atahualpa won the war just weeks before Pizarro arrived in 1532.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">At the Battle of Cajamarca on 16 November 1532, Pizarro&rsquo;s 168 men ambushed Atahualpa&rsquo;s unarmed retinue, killed thousands and seized the emperor. Despite paying an enormous gold and silver ransom, Atahualpa was garrotted on 26 July 1533.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The Spanish entered Cuzco in November 1533 and Pizarro founded Lima in 1535. Manco Inca&rsquo;s 1536&ndash;37 siege of Cuzco came close to reversing the conquest before Indigenous and Spanish reinforcements broke it; the rump Neo-Inca state survived at Vilcabamba until 1572.</li>
</ul>
