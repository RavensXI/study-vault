<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Atom Economy and Percentage Yield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In industrial chemistry, two key measures tell us how efficiently a reaction uses its raw materials. <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of the total mass of all products that is the desired useful product, calculated from the balanced equation.">Atom economy</dfn> tells you what fraction of the mass of the reactants ends up as the wanted product rather than waste. A high atom economy means less waste is generated, lowering environmental impact and production costs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{atom economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product}}{\text{sum of } M_r \text{ of all products}} \times 100$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The actual yield of a product expressed as a percentage of the theoretical maximum yield predicted by the balanced equation.">Percentage yield</dfn> compares the actual amount of product made to the theoretical maximum:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Percentage yield is often below 100% because of incomplete reactions, side reactions, reversible reactions that do not go to completion, or product lost during purification. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction, limiting how much product can form. The other reactant remains in excess.">limiting reactant</dfn> determines the theoretical yield &mdash; once it runs out, the reaction stops regardless of how much of the other reactant remains.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: atom economy measures waste from the equation; percentage yield measures efficiency in the lab. Write both equations from memory, including units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Atom economy measures what proportion of reactant atoms become the desired product (from the balanced equation). Percentage yield measures how much product was actually made compared to the theoretical maximum. Both should be as high as possible for economic and environmental reasons.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Life Cycle Assessments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A systematic analysis of the environmental impact of a product at every stage of its existence, from raw material extraction through to disposal.">life cycle assessment</dfn> (LCA) evaluates the environmental impact of a product at every stage of its life. The four main stages are: (1) extracting and processing raw materials, (2) manufacturing, (3) using the product (including transport and packaging), and (4) disposing of the product at the end of its useful life.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What LCAs Consider</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">LCAs consider: energy consumption at each stage, raw material use, water use, emissions to air and water (including greenhouse gases and toxic substances), waste produced, and the potential for recycling or reuse at the end of life. However, some factors &mdash; such as visual impact, noise, or local ecosystem disruption &mdash; are difficult to quantify precisely. This means LCAs involve some subjective judgement, and different analysts can reach different conclusions if they weight factors differently. Companies sometimes use selective LCA data to support marketing claims, so evaluating LCA evidence critically is important.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Recycling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Recycling reduces the need to extract new raw materials, saves energy, and reduces the volume of waste going to landfill. Factors that affect decisions about recycling include: the cost of collection and sorting, the energy needed to process recycled material compared to making new material, the market value of the recycled product, and public willingness to separate and recycle materials.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Metals are particularly worth recycling because mining and refining metal ores is very energy-intensive. Recycling aluminium, for example, uses only a small fraction of the energy required to extract aluminium from its ore (bauxite) by electrolysis. Glass and paper can also be recycled effectively, though the economic viability depends on collection costs, sorting complexity and market conditions.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: name two reasons recycling metals is particularly worthwhile, and state one factor that makes recycling less economical.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Recycling conserves finite resources, reduces energy use, and cuts landfill waste. Metals such as aluminium are especially valuable to recycle because extracting them from ore by electrolysis is very energy-intensive. The overall benefit must weigh the energy and cost of collection and processing.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Extracting Metals from Low-Grade Ores</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Traditional smelting requires high concentrations of metal ore and generates significant environmental impact. Where ore is too dilute (low-grade) to make smelting economically viable, alternative biological methods can be used. Two techniques are particularly important: <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique that uses certain plants to absorb dissolved metal compounds from soil through their roots. The plants are then harvested and the metal extracted from the ash.">phytomining</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique that uses bacteria to produce acidic solutions that dissolve metal compounds from low-grade ores. The dissolved metal can then be extracted by displacement or electrolysis.">bioleaching</dfn>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Phytomining and Bioleaching in Detail</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Phytomining</strong> uses plants that can absorb and accumulate metal ions from the soil through their root systems. These plants, sometimes called hyperaccumulators, concentrate metal ions in their leaves and stems. The plants are then harvested and burned; the resulting ash contains a much higher concentration of the metal compound than the original soil. The metal is then extracted from the ash, often by smelting or chemical treatment. The process is slow (growing the plants takes time) but has a lower environmental impact than traditional opencast mining.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Bioleaching</strong> uses bacteria that produce acidic solutions (leachate) by reacting with metal sulfide ores. The leachate dissolves the metal compounds out of the ore. The resulting metal-rich solution (often called the &lsquo;pregnant leach solution&rsquo;) can then have the metal extracted from it either by displacement (adding a more reactive metal) or by electrolysis. Bioleaching is also slower than conventional extraction but requires less energy input and can process waste heaps and low-grade ores that would otherwise be uneconomical to use.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Explain to someone: how does phytomining differ from bioleaching? What do they have in common? Which stage of each process actually extracts the metal?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Phytomining uses plants to absorb metal ions; the plants are burned and the metal extracted from the ash. Bioleaching uses bacteria to produce acidic solutions that dissolve metals from low-grade ores; the metal is then removed by displacement or electrolysis. Both are slower but more sustainable alternatives to conventional smelting for low-grade ores.</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing Extraction Methods</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Traditional <strong>reduction with carbon</strong> is used for metals below carbon in the reactivity series (such as iron, zinc and copper). Carbon displaces the metal from its oxide when heated at high temperatures. <strong>Electrolysis</strong> is required for metals above carbon in the reactivity series (such as aluminium) because they cannot be displaced by carbon. Electrolysis uses large amounts of electrical energy, making it expensive. By contrast, phytomining and bioleaching require very little energy but are slow, making them best suited to low-grade or dilute ore bodies where faster methods are not viable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Sustainability in Chemistry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The principles of sustainable chemistry combine all the ideas above: maximising atom economy to reduce waste, conducting LCAs to guide product design, recycling where possible to conserve finite resources, and using biological or lower-energy extraction methods where appropriate. Chemistry plays a central role in meeting the needs of the present without depleting resources that future generations will need.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Atom economy measures the proportion of reactant mass that becomes the desired product; percentage yield measures how much of the theoretical maximum was actually obtained. Both should be maximised in green chemistry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Life cycle assessments evaluate the environmental impact of a product from raw material extraction through to disposal. They are partly subjective and must be interpreted critically.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Phytomining (using plants) and bioleaching (using bacteria) are sustainable alternatives to conventional smelting for low-grade copper ores, requiring less energy but more time.</li>
</ul>
