--- DESCRIPTION ---
Collision theory, factors affecting reaction rate, and practical methods for measuring rate.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Rate of Reaction?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed in a chemical reaction.">rate of reaction</dfn> tells us how quickly a reaction happens. A fast reaction, such as an explosion, is over in a fraction of a second. A slow reaction, such as the rusting of iron or the weathering of stone, takes months or years. In industry, controlling the rate of a reaction is vital &mdash; too fast can be dangerous, too slow is uneconomical.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Rate can be measured in several ways: the volume of gas produced per unit time (using a gas syringe or measuring cylinder over water), the mass of a reaction mixture lost per unit time (as gas escapes, measured using a balance), or the time taken for a colour change or for a solution to become opaque. The choice depends on the particular reaction and what can be conveniently measured.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Collision Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that reactions can only occur when particles collide with sufficient energy and the correct orientation.">Collision theory</dfn> states that for a reaction to occur, the reacting particles must: (1) <strong>collide</strong> with each other, (2) collide with sufficient energy (at least the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum energy that reacting particles must have in order to collide successfully and react.">activation energy</dfn>), and (3) collide with the correct <strong>orientation</strong>. Not every collision leads to a reaction &mdash; most are unsuccessful because the particles bounce off without reacting. The rate of reaction depends on the <strong>frequency of successful collisions</strong>: collisions that have both enough energy and the right orientation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="For 3-mark explain questions on rate factors, always include: (1) what changes about the particles, (2) how this affects collision frequency or energy, (3) the effect on rate. Cover this and practise writing one factor using all three steps.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy (the activation energy) and the correct orientation. Anything that increases the <strong>frequency</strong> or <strong>energy</strong> of successful collisions increases the rate of reaction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Factors Affecting Rate of Reaction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Four main factors affect the rate of a chemical reaction: temperature, concentration, surface area, and catalysts.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Temperature, Concentration, Surface Area &mdash; Detailed Explanations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Temperature:</strong> Increasing the temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more frequently. More importantly, a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or exceeding the activation energy, so a much larger fraction of collisions are successful. Even a small increase in temperature produces a significant increase in rate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Concentration</strong> (for solutions) and <strong>pressure</strong> (for gases): Increasing concentration means more particles in the same volume, so they collide more frequently. For gases, increasing pressure pushes particles closer together, with the same effect. More collisions per unit time &rarr; more successful collisions &rarr; faster rate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Surface area:</strong> Breaking a solid into smaller pieces increases the <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of exposed surface to the volume of a solid, which increases when the solid is broken into smaller pieces.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn>. A greater surface area means more of the solid is exposed to the other reactant, so collisions occur more frequently. Powdered marble chips react faster with hydrochloric acid than large lumps, even with the same total mass. In extreme cases, a fine powder of combustible material in air can cause a dust explosion.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Catalysts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy, without being used up itself.">catalyst</dfn> increases the rate of a reaction without being used up. It provides an <strong>alternative reaction pathway</strong> with a lower activation energy, so a greater proportion of collisions are successful. On a reaction profile, the catalyst lowers the peak (activation energy) without changing the energy levels of the reactants or products. Each catalyst is specific to a particular reaction &mdash; iron catalyses the Haber process but would not catalyse the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (which uses manganese dioxide).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Catalysts lower the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway. They are NOT used up. They do NOT change the overall energy change or the amount of product formed. Cover this and write three facts about catalysts from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Catalysts speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy. They are not used up and are not changed at the end of the reaction. <dfn class="term" data-def="Biological catalysts (proteins) that speed up reactions in living organisms by providing a specific active site.">Enzymes</dfn> are biological catalysts found in all living organisms.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Interpreting Rate of Reaction Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Rate graphs typically plot volume of gas (or mass lost) on the y-axis against time on the x-axis. The <strong>gradient</strong> (steepness) of the line at any point shows the rate at that moment &mdash; a steeper gradient means a faster rate. The line levels off when one reactant is used up (the reaction is complete). Two curves on the same axes that level off at the same point but with different steepnesses represent the same reaction with the same amount of reactant, but at different rates (e.g. different temperatures or concentrations).</p>

    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Gas syringe:</strong> collect gas in a syringe and record volume at regular intervals.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Displacement of water:</strong> collect gas over water in an inverted measuring cylinder.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Mass loss:</strong> place the flask on a balance; record mass at regular intervals as gas escapes through cotton wool.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Disappearing cross:</strong> for the sodium thiosulfate and acid reaction, time how long it takes for a cross viewed through the solution to disappear as a precipitate of sulfur forms.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">In all methods, change only one variable at a time and keep all other conditions constant to make it a fair test. Look for anomalous results in your data and exclude them when calculating means.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Rate of reaction measures how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. Collision theory: particles must collide with enough energy (activation energy) and correct orientation for a reaction to occur.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Increasing temperature, concentration (or pressure for gases) and surface area all increase the rate by increasing the frequency and/or energy of successful collisions. Catalysts lower the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway &mdash; they are not used up.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Rate graphs: steeper gradient = faster rate; the line levels off when a reactant is used up. PAG C5 involves measuring rate by gas collection (syringe or displacement) or mass loss. Change only one variable at a time for a fair test.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">For <strong>3-mark Explain</strong> questions about factors affecting rate, always give a three-step answer: (1) state what changes (e.g. temperature increases), (2) link to collision theory (e.g. particles have more kinetic energy, collide more frequently and with more energy), (3) give the consequence for rate (e.g. more successful collisions per second, so rate increases). Generic responses like &lsquo;particles collide more&rsquo; without explaining <em>why</em> collisions increase will only score 1 or 2 marks. A common mistake is saying catalysts are &lsquo;used up&rsquo; &mdash; they are not, and stating this will lose marks.</p>
