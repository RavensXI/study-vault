Acid-base reactions, the pH scale, neutralisation, preparation of pure dry salts and titration calculations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Acids, Bases and the pH Scale</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) when dissolved in water, with a pH below 7.">acid</dfn> is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (\(H^+\)) when dissolved in water. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that can neutralise an acid. Insoluble bases react with acids to form a salt and water.">base</dfn> is a substance that neutralises an acid. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A soluble base that produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in solution, with a pH above 7.">alkali</dfn> is a soluble base — it produces hydroxide ions (\(OH^-\)) when dissolved in water.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A scale from 0 to 14 measuring how acidic or alkaline a solution is. Below 7 = acidic, 7 = neutral, above 7 = alkaline.">pH scale</dfn> runs from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most alkaline), with 7 being exactly neutral. pH is measured using universal indicator (which changes colour) or a pH probe (which gives a precise digital reading).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Write the pH ranges for acidic, neutral and alkaline solutions, and state what type of ion each produces in water.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Acids: produce \(H^+\) ions, pH 0–6. Neutral: pH 7. Alkalis: produce \(OH^-\) ions, pH 8–14. The neutralisation reaction: \(H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O\).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Neutralisation Reactions</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction between an acid and a base that produces a salt and water. The H⁺ and OH⁻ ions combine to form water.">Neutralisation</dfn> occurs when an acid reacts with a base, producing a salt and water. The key reaction types are:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Acid + metal</strong> → salt + hydrogen gas. E.g. \(Mg + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow MgSO_4 + H_2\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Acid + metal oxide</strong> → salt + water. E.g. \(CuO + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow CuSO_4 + H_2O\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Acid + metal hydroxide</strong> → salt + water. E.g. \(NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Acid + metal carbonate</strong> → salt + water + carbon dioxide. E.g. \(CaCO_3 + 2HCl \rightarrow CaCl_2 + H_2O + CO_2\)</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n11">The name of the salt produced depends on the acid used: hydrochloric acid (HCl) makes <strong>chlorides</strong>, sulfuric acid (\(H_2SO_4\)) makes <strong>sulfates</strong>, and nitric acid (\(HNO_3\)) makes <strong>nitrates</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Predict the salt formed from each acid: HCl → chlorides, H₂SO₄ → sulfates, HNO₃ → nitrates. Practice: magnesium + hydrochloric acid → what salt?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>HCl → chlorides. \(H_2SO_4\) → sulfates. \(HNO_3\) → nitrates. Salt name = metal name + acid ending. Example: sodium + sulfuric acid → sodium sulfate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Making Pure Dry Salts</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Method 1 — Making a Soluble Salt from an Insoluble Base (Core Practical)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">To make copper(II) sulfate crystals from copper(II) oxide and sulfuric acid (a core practical):</p>
    <ol>
      <li data-narration-id="n15">Warm dilute sulfuric acid in a beaker.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n16">Add excess copper(II) oxide (black powder) and stir until no more dissolves — the excess ensures all acid is used up.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n17">Filter the mixture to remove the excess copper(II) oxide.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n18">Evaporate the blue filtrate gently until crystals begin to form, then leave to crystallise fully.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n19">Pat the blue crystals dry with filter paper.</li>
    </ol>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The independent variable is the amount of acid used; the thermometer measures temperature (it does not control it — the water bath controls temperature).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Method 2 — Making an Insoluble Salt by Precipitation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Some salts are insoluble and cannot be made by neutralisation. They are made by <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction between two solutions that forms an insoluble solid (precipitate) which can be filtered off.">precipitation</dfn>: mix two solutions whose ions react to form the insoluble salt. For example, to make barium sulfate: mix barium chloride solution with sodium sulfate solution — barium sulfate precipitates immediately. Filter, wash with distilled water and dry.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">$$BaCl_2 + Na_2SO_4 \rightarrow BaSO_4 + 2NaCl$$</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Titration</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for accurately measuring the volume of one solution needed to react completely with a known volume of another solution.">Titration</dfn> is used to find the exact volume of acid needed to neutralise a known volume of alkali (or vice versa). The apparatus required includes a <dfn class="term" data-def="A long, graduated glass tube with a tap at the bottom, used to deliver precise volumes of a solution.">burette</dfn> (to deliver the acid) and a <dfn class="term" data-def="A glass tube of fixed volume used to measure an exact volume of a solution.">pipette</dfn> (to measure the exact volume of alkali). An indicator such as phenolphthalein or methyl orange shows when the endpoint is reached.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Titration Procedure (Core Practical)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">1. Rinse the burette with the acid solution, then fill it and record the initial reading to 0.05 cm³. 2. Use the pipette to measure an exact volume of alkali into a conical flask; add a few drops of indicator. 3. Add the acid from the burette drop by drop, swirling the flask. As the endpoint approaches, add half-drops. 4. Stop when the indicator changes colour permanently. Record the final burette reading. 5. Calculate the volume added (titre = final − initial). Repeat until two titres agree within 0.10 cm³; these are concordant results. Average the concordant results.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Using Titration Data in Calculations</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n26">From a titration, use: \(n = c \times V\) where \(n\) = moles, \(c\) = concentration (mol/dm³), \(V\) = volume (dm³). Convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Example:</strong> 25.0 cm³ of NaOH solution of concentration 0.10 mol/dm³ required 20.0 cm³ of HCl solution. Find the concentration of the HCl.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n28">Moles NaOH \(= 0.10 \times 0.025 = 0.0025\) mol. From NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O, the ratio is 1:1, so moles HCl = 0.0025 mol. Concentration HCl \(= \frac{0.0025}{0.020} = 0.125\) mol/dm³.</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>

  <p data-narration-id="n34">A key distinction for the exam is between strong and weak acids: strength refers to the degree of ionisation in water, not concentration. A concentrated weak acid like ethanoic acid has a lower pH than a dilute weak acid, but both ionise only partially. When making soluble salts by neutralisation, use an indicator to find the exact equivalence point; when using an insoluble base, add excess and then filter to remove it — no indicator is needed because the excess base is simply removed by filtration.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Acids produce H⁺ ions (pH below 7); alkalis produce OH⁻ ions (pH above 7). Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. HCl gives chlorides, H₂SO₄ gives sulfates, HNO₃ gives nitrates.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Soluble salts: add excess insoluble base to acid, filter, evaporate, crystallise. Insoluble salts: mix two solutions → precipitation, filter, dry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Titration uses a burette and pipette to find exact volumes at neutralisation. Calculation: moles = concentration × volume (dm³). Average concordant titres only.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">On 6-mark 'describe a method' questions for making a soluble salt, examiners want: (1) add excess insoluble base to the acid; (2) filter to remove excess solid; (3) evaporate gently and crystallise. Crucially, a thermometer in the experiment <em>measures</em> temperature — the water bath <em>controls</em> it. Mixing these up is the most common mark loss in core practical questions. For titration calculations: always convert cm³ to dm³ before using \(n = c \times V\). Concordant results means two titres that agree within 0.10 cm³ — the anomalous (rough) result is excluded from the average.</p>
