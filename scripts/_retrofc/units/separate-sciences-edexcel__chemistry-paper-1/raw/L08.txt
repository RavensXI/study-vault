--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Electrolysis?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The decomposition of an ionic compound by passing an electric current through it when molten or dissolved in solution.">Electrolysis</dfn> is the process of using an electric current to break down an ionic compound. The compound being broken down is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water because its ions are free to move.">electrolyte</dfn>, and it must be either molten or dissolved so that the ions are free to move.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The electrolyte is connected to two solid conductors called <dfn class="term" data-def="Solid conductors that carry current into and out of the electrolyte during electrolysis.">electrodes</dfn>. The <strong>cathode</strong> is the negative electrode; the <strong>anode</strong> is the positive electrode. The memory aid <strong>PANIC</strong> helps: <em>Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode</em>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Name which electrode attracts positive ions, which attracts negative ions, and what happens (gain or loss of electrons) at each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Electrolysis: positive ions (cations) move to the <strong>cathode</strong> (negative) and <strong>gain</strong> electrons — this is reduction. Negative ions (anions) move to the <strong>anode</strong> (positive) and <strong>lose</strong> electrons — this is oxidation. PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Electrolysis of Molten Ionic Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">When a molten ionic compound is electrolysed, only the ions from that compound are present. For molten lead(II) bromide (\(PbBr_2\)):</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Cathode:</strong> \(Pb^{2+}\) ions gain electrons → lead metal deposited. \(Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Anode:</strong> \(Br^-\) ions lose electrons → bromine gas released. \(2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-\)</li>
</ul>

<div class="higher-only">
  <p data-narration-id="n9">These equations showing what happens at each electrode are called <dfn class="term" data-def="An equation showing the oxidation or reduction reaction at a single electrode during electrolysis, including electrons.">half-equations</dfn>. The electrons lost at the anode must equal the electrons gained at the cathode.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11">When an ionic compound is dissolved in water, \(H^+\) and \(OH^-\) ions from water are also present. This means there is competition at each electrode between different ions:</p>

<div class="higher-only">
  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Discharge Rules for Aqueous Electrolysis</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>At the cathode:</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li data-narration-id="n13">If the metal cation is <em>less reactive than hydrogen</em> (e.g. Cu²⁺, Ag⁺) → the metal is deposited.</li>
        <li data-narration-id="n14">If the metal cation is <em>more reactive than hydrogen</em> (e.g. Na⁺, Zn²⁺) → hydrogen gas is produced instead.</li>
      </ul>
      <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>At the anode:</strong></p>
      <ul>
        <li data-narration-id="n16">If a <dfn class="term" data-def="An ion of a halogen (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻). Halide ions are preferentially discharged at the anode in electrolysis.">halide ion</dfn> is present (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) → the halogen is produced (e.g. chlorine gas).</li>
        <li data-narration-id="n17">If no halide is present → oxygen is produced from the discharge of \(OH^-\) ions.</li>
      </ul>
    </div></div>
  </div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Aluminium Extraction by Electrolysis</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n19">Aluminium is too reactive to be extracted by reduction with carbon, so it is extracted by electrolysis of molten <dfn class="term" data-def="Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), the ore from which aluminium is extracted by electrolysis.">aluminium oxide</dfn> (\(Al_2O_3\)).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Pure aluminium oxide has a very high melting point (over 2000 °C). To reduce energy costs, it is dissolved in molten <dfn class="term" data-def="A mineral (Na₃AlF₆) mixed with aluminium oxide to lower the melting point, reducing the energy cost of electrolysis.">cryolite</dfn>, which lowers the melting point to around 950 °C.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Cover this. State the formula for aluminium oxide, the mineral used to lower the melting point, what forms at each electrode, and why the carbon anodes need replacing.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aluminium extraction: electrolysis of \(Al_2O_3\) dissolved in molten cryolite (lowers melting point). At cathode: \(Al^{3+} + 3e^- \rightarrow Al\). At anode: oxygen released reacts with the carbon anodes, burning them away — they need regular replacement.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Half-equations for Aluminium Extraction</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Cathode (reduction):</strong> \(Al^{3+} + 3e^- \rightarrow Al\)</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Anode (oxidation):</strong> \(2O^{2-} \rightarrow O_2 + 4e^-\)</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n24">The oxygen produced at the anode reacts with the carbon (graphite) anodes at high temperature, forming carbon dioxide. This means the anodes gradually burn away and must be replaced regularly — a significant running cost.</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Core Practical: Electrolysis of Copper Sulfate Solution</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n26">This core practical investigates the electrolysis of aqueous copper sulfate solution, comparing the effect of inert electrodes (carbon) versus active electrodes (copper).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Core Practical Method and Observations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>With inert (carbon) electrodes:</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n28">Cathode: copper metal deposits (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu). The cathode gains mass.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n29">Anode: oxygen gas is produced (no halide present). The copper sulfate solution gradually becomes paler as Cu²⁺ ions are removed.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>With copper electrodes (active):</strong></p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n31">Cathode: copper still deposits — cathode gains mass.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n32">Anode: the copper anode dissolves (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻). The anode loses mass. The Cu²⁺ concentration in solution remains roughly constant, making this the basis of copper purification and electroplating.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n33">To test the gas at the anode: a glowing splint would relight if it were oxygen. To test for copper, observe the reddish-brown deposit at the cathode. Weigh the electrodes before and after to quantify mass changes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Common Misconceptions and Exam Pitfalls</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n38">A frequent error is assuming that the positive electrode always produces oxygen — this only applies when no halide ions (such as chloride) are present in solution. When chloride ions are present in higher concentration, chlorine gas forms at the anode instead. Likewise, students sometimes confuse electrolytic cells with electrochemical cells: in an electrolytic cell, electrical energy is put <em>in</em> to drive a non-spontaneous chemical change, whereas in a galvanic cell, chemical energy is converted <em>out</em> to electrical energy. Always identify the type of electrode material and the ions present in solution before predicting products.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Electrolysis breaks down ionic compounds using electricity. Positive ions move to the cathode (reduction); negative ions move to the anode (oxidation). PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">In aqueous solutions: cathode produces the metal (if less reactive than H) or hydrogen gas; anode produces a halogen (if halide present) or oxygen. Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite to lower the melting point.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">The core practical electrolysing copper sulfate solution with inert electrodes deposits copper at the cathode and releases oxygen at the anode. With copper electrodes, the anode dissolves to replenish Cu²⁺ ions — the basis of electroplating.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n39">For half-equations, examiners check: (1) correct formula for the product, (2) the correct number of electrons, (3) the equation is balanced for both atoms and charge. At the cathode, electrons are added to the left side (reduction); at the anode, electrons appear on the right side (oxidation). PANIC helps: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode. For aqueous electrolysis questions, state the discharge rule clearly: at the cathode, hydrogen is produced if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen; at the anode, the halogen is discharged if a halide is present, otherwise oxygen. In the core practical on electrolysis of copper sulfate, using copper electrodes changes the outcome — the copper anode dissolves and replenishes the Cu²⁺ in solution rather than depositing copper, so the solution concentration stays roughly constant. Inert electrodes (carbon/platinum) give pure copper depositing at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.</p>
