--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ions form, electron transfer and sharing, dot-and-cross diagrams and metallic delocalised electrons.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Ionic Bonding</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">When a metal reacts with a non-metal, electrons are <strong>transferred</strong> from the metal atom to the non-metal atom. The metal atom loses one or more electrons to form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A positively charged ion formed when a metal atom loses one or more electrons.">positive ion</dfn> (cation), and the non-metal atom gains those electrons to become a <dfn class="term" data-def="A negatively charged ion formed when a non-metal atom gains one or more electrons.">negative ion</dfn> (anion). Both ions then have full outer electron shells and are stable.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The strong electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged ions is called an <dfn class="term" data-def="The strong electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.">ionic bond</dfn>. In sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium loses one electron to become \(Na^+\) and chlorine gains one electron to become \(Cl^-\). The resulting ions pack together in a regular three-dimensional arrangement called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic forces in all directions.">giant ionic lattice</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what does each atom do in ionic bonding, what charge does each ion carry, and what holds them together?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ionic bonding: metal atoms lose electrons (form positive ions) and non-metal atoms gain electrons (form negative ions). Strong electrostatic attraction between the opposite charges holds ions together in a giant ionic lattice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Naming Ionic Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The name of an ionic compound gives useful information about which ions are present. When a metal combines with a non-metal element only, the non-metal ion takes an <strong>-ide</strong> ending — for example, sodium <em>chloride</em>, magnesium <em>oxide</em>, potassium <em>iodide</em>. When the compound contains oxygen as part of a polyatomic ion, it usually takes an <strong>-ate</strong> ending — for example, sodium <em>sulfate</em>, copper(II) <em>nitrate</em>, calcium <em>carbonate</em>. Recognising these endings tells you instantly whether a simple or complex ion is involved.</p>

    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n8">Draw Na with one outer electron (dot). Draw Cl with seven outer electrons (crosses).</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n9">Show the dot crossing into Cl's outer shell, so Cl now has eight electrons. Draw square brackets around each ion and write the charge outside: \([Na]^+\) and \([Cl]^-\).</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">For magnesium oxide, Mg loses two electrons (2,8 → 2,8, 0 becomes \(Mg^{2+}\)) and oxygen gains two (2,6 → 2,8, becomes \(O^{2-}\)).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Covalent Bonding</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">When non-metals bond with other non-metals, atoms <strong>share</strong> pairs of electrons rather than transferring them. Each shared pair of electrons is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A bond formed when two non-metal atoms share one or more pairs of electrons, giving each atom a full outer shell.">covalent bond</dfn>. Both atoms count the shared pair as part of their own outer shell, so both achieve a full, stable electron configuration.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Common examples:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14">\(H_2\) — a single covalent bond (one shared pair).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15">\(O_2\) — a double covalent bond (\(O=O\), two shared pairs).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">\(H_2O\) — two single covalent bonds (oxygen bonds to two hydrogen atoms).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">\(CH_4\) — four single covalent bonds (carbon bonds to four hydrogen atoms).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">\(NH_3\) — three single covalent bonds plus one lone pair on nitrogen.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Drawing Dot-and-Cross Diagrams for Covalent Molecules</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">For water (\(H_2O\)): draw oxygen in the centre with six outer-shell electrons. Draw a hydrogen on each side with one electron. Show one dot from O and one cross from each H overlapping in a shared pair. Each H now has a full shell of two; oxygen has a full shell of eight.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">For methane (\(CH_4\)): carbon has four outer electrons. Each of the four hydrogen atoms contributes one electron. Show four shared pairs around carbon, giving it eight; each hydrogen has two.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Only draw outer-shell electrons. Never add electrons that aren't there or remove electrons to make the diagram look neater — the total electron count must balance.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Metallic Bonding</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n23">In metals, the outer electrons leave their parent atoms and become <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrons that have left their atoms and are free to move throughout a metal structure, forming a 'sea' that holds the lattice together.">delocalised electrons</dfn> — free to move throughout the entire metal structure. What remains are positive metal ions. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and the surrounding sea of delocalised electrons.">metallic bond</dfn> is the strong electrostatic attraction between these positive ions and the surrounding sea of electrons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why metals conduct electricity and are malleable, linking each property to the sea of delocalised electrons.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Metallic bonding: positive metal ions in a lattice surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons. The electrons carry charge → metals conduct electricity. Layers of ions can slide over each other → metals are malleable and ductile.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing the Three Types of Bonding</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Ionic:</strong> electrons transferred, metal + non-metal, giant ionic lattice, high melting point, conducts when molten or dissolved.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Covalent:</strong> electrons shared, non-metal + non-metal, simple molecules (low m.p.) or giant covalent (very high m.p.), mostly poor conductors.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Metallic:</strong> delocalised electrons, metal atoms only, giant metallic lattice, good conductors, malleable, generally high melting points (though mercury is liquid at room temperature).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n33">The key exam skill is justifying properties from structure: ionic compounds have high melting points because many strong electrostatic attractions must be overcome simultaneously. Simple covalent molecules like water have low boiling points because intermolecular forces, not covalent bonds, are broken during boiling. Giant covalent structures like diamond and silicon dioxide have very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken throughout the entire lattice.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Ionic bonding: electrons transfer from metal to non-metal, forming oppositely charged ions held in a giant lattice by electrostatic attraction. -ide compounds contain a simple non-metal ion; -ate compounds contain an oxygen-bearing polyatomic ion.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Covalent bonding: non-metals share electron pairs so each atom achieves a full outer shell. Dot-and-cross diagrams show only outer shell electrons, with shared pairs in the overlap region.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Metallic bonding: delocalised electrons move freely between positive metal ions, making metals good conductors of electricity and heat and giving them the malleability to be shaped.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">For 4-mark explain questions on bonding, always link structure to property with a causal 'because' — for example: 'ionic compounds have high melting points <em>because</em> there are strong electrostatic forces between oppositely charged ions acting in all directions, so a large amount of energy is needed to overcome them.' One mark for the claim, one for the scientific reason. For dot-and-cross diagrams, examiners check that (1) only outer shells are shown, (2) shared pairs sit in an overlap between atoms for covalent or are fully transferred with charges for ionic, and (3) the electron count balances. Remember: -ide endings indicate a binary ionic compound (two elements only); -ate endings indicate a polyatomic ion containing oxygen.</p>
