Exothermic and endothermic reactions, bond energies, reaction profiles and activation energy.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy in Chemical Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All chemical reactions involve energy changes. When chemical bonds are broken, energy must be <strong>absorbed</strong>; when new bonds are formed, energy is <strong>released</strong>. The overall energy change depends on the balance between these two processes. If more energy is released than absorbed, the reaction is <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to rise.">exothermic</dfn>. If more energy is absorbed than released, it is <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to fall.">endothermic</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in which type of reaction does the temperature of the surroundings rise, and in which does it fall?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Bond breaking is always endothermic (energy absorbed); bond making is always exothermic (energy released). The overall reaction type depends on which process releases or absorbs more energy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Exothermic Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In an exothermic reaction, the energy released when new bonds form is <strong>greater</strong> than the energy needed to break the original bonds. This surplus energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat, so the temperature of the surroundings <strong>rises</strong>. Everyday examples include combustion (burning fuels), neutralisation reactions between acids and alkalis, and the oxidation of metals. Hand warmers contain iron powder that reacts exothermically with oxygen from the air, steadily releasing warmth.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Combustion &mdash; A Key Exothermic Example</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">When methane burns in oxygen, new C&mdash;O and H&mdash;O bonds form in the products carbon dioxide and water. These new bonds release more energy than is needed to break the original C&mdash;H and O=O bonds. The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">$$CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The reaction is strongly exothermic &mdash; this is why burning fuels release heat and light.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Endothermic Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In an endothermic reaction, the energy needed to break bonds in the reactants is <strong>greater</strong> than the energy released when new bonds form. Energy is therefore absorbed from the surroundings and the temperature falls. Thermal decomposition reactions are typically endothermic &mdash; for example, heating copper carbonate:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$CuCO_3 \rightarrow CuO + CO_2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Sports injury cold packs exploit this principle: ammonium nitrate dissolves endothermically in water, absorbing heat from the injured area and producing an immediate cooling effect.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write one exothermic example and one endothermic example from everyday life, stating which way temperature changes for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Exothermic: temperature of surroundings rises (e.g. combustion, neutralisation, hand warmers). Endothermic: temperature of surroundings falls (e.g. thermal decomposition, dissolving ammonium nitrate, cold packs).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Reaction Profiles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing how energy changes during a chemical reaction, from reactants through a transition state to products.">reaction profile</dfn> (also called an energy profile diagram) shows the energy of the system as reactants are converted to products. The vertical axis shows energy; the horizontal axis shows the &lsquo;progress of reaction&rsquo; (sometimes called the reaction coordinate). Every reaction profile has a &lsquo;hump&rsquo; &mdash; reactants must reach the top of this energy barrier before products can form.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Reading Exothermic and Endothermic Profiles</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Exothermic profile:</strong> The reactants start at a higher energy level than the products. The difference in height between reactants and products is the energy released to surroundings (−ΔH). The &lsquo;hump&rsquo; above the reactants represents the activation energy required to start the reaction.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Endothermic profile:</strong> The products sit at a higher energy level than the reactants. Energy has been absorbed from the surroundings (+ΔH). The activation energy is the height from the reactants up to the peak of the hump.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A catalyst lowers the height of the activation energy hump, allowing more collisions to be successful at the same temperature &mdash; this is shown on a profile as a lower peak, with the reactant and product energy levels unchanged.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Activation Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum energy that reacting particles must have for a collision to result in a chemical reaction.">activation energy</dfn> is the minimum energy that particles must possess for a collision to result in a reaction. Even strongly exothermic reactions require an initial energy input to break the first bonds and get the reaction started &mdash; a match is needed to ignite methane gas, for example. On a reaction profile, activation energy is the vertical distance from the reactant energy level to the peak of the curve.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Catalysts Affect Activation Energy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy. It is not used up in the reaction.">catalyst</dfn> provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy. On a reaction profile, this appears as a lower peak. The overall energy change (&Delta;H) between reactants and products remains identical; only the barrier height changes. This means more collisions at a given temperature have enough energy to react, so the rate increases.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
  <h2 data-narration-id="n22">Bond Energy Calculations</h2>
  <p data-narration-id="n23">Every chemical bond has a characteristic <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of energy (in kJ/mol) needed to break one mole of a particular type of bond in the gas phase, or the energy released when that bond forms.">bond energy</dfn> (in kJ/mol). By summing the bond energies of all bonds broken in the reactants and all bonds made in the products, the overall energy change for a reaction can be calculated:</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n24">$$\Delta H = \text{Energy to break bonds} - \text{Energy released making bonds}$$</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n25">A positive ΔH means energy is absorbed overall (endothermic); a negative ΔH means energy is released overall (exothermic).</p>

  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Worked Example: Bond Energy Calculation</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n26">Calculate the energy change for: \(H_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2HCl\)</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n27">Bond energies: H&mdash;H = 436 kJ/mol; Cl&mdash;Cl = 242 kJ/mol; H&mdash;Cl = 431 kJ/mol</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Step 1 &mdash; bonds broken:</strong> 1 &times; H&mdash;H (436) + 1 &times; Cl&mdash;Cl (242) = 678 kJ/mol</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Step 2 &mdash; bonds made:</strong> 2 &times; H&mdash;Cl (2 &times; 431) = 862 kJ/mol</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Step 3 &mdash; overall change:</strong> \(\Delta H = 678 - 862 = -184\) kJ/mol</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n31">The negative value confirms the reaction is exothermic &mdash; 184 kJ/mol is released to the surroundings.</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>

  <div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this and practise: bonds broken minus bonds made gives ΔH; negative = exothermic, positive = endothermic. Can you recall that rule without looking?">
    <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
    <p>ΔH = energy to break bonds &minus; energy released making bonds. Negative ΔH = exothermic (energy released); positive ΔH = endothermic (energy absorbed). Always include the sign and units (kJ/mol) in your answer.</p>
  </div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Exothermic reactions release energy to the surroundings (temperature rises); endothermic reactions absorb energy (temperature falls). Bond breaking is always endothermic; bond making is always exothermic.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Reaction profiles show the energy of reactants and products; the peak represents the transition state and the activation energy is the height from reactants to that peak.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Bond energy calculations: ΔH = energy to break bonds &minus; energy released making bonds. Negative ΔH = exothermic; positive ΔH = endothermic.</li>
</ul>
