From Dalton's solid spheres to Chadwick's neutron — how evidence changed our model of the atom.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Atoms, Elements and Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">All matter is made of tiny particles called <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest particle of an element that can exist. Atoms cannot be created or destroyed in chemical reactions.">atoms</dfn>. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A pure substance made of only one type of atom. Each element has a unique atomic number.">element</dfn> is a pure substance containing only one type of atom &mdash; for example, copper contains only copper atoms and chlorine contains only chlorine atoms. When atoms of different elements join together chemically, they form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance formed when two or more different elements are chemically bonded together in fixed proportions.">compound</dfn>. In a compound, the atoms are held by chemical bonds and can only be separated by chemical reactions. Mixtures, by contrast, contain more than one substance not chemically bonded, and can be separated by physical means.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state what makes a substance an element versus a compound. Give one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All matter is made of atoms. Elements contain only one type of atom. Compounds contain atoms of two or more different elements chemically bonded together in fixed proportions. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Structure of the Atom</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The atom has a central <dfn class="term" data-def="The central part of an atom, containing protons and neutrons. It carries an overall positive charge and contains almost all of the atom's mass.">nucleus</dfn> containing <strong>protons</strong> (positively charged) and <strong>neutrons</strong> (no charge). Electrons (negatively charged) orbit the nucleus in <strong>shells</strong> (energy levels). In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the charges balance.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The relative masses and charges of the three subatomic particles are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Proton:</strong> relative mass 1, charge +1.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Neutron:</strong> relative mass 1, charge 0.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Electron:</strong> relative mass approximately 0 (very small compared to protons and neutrons), charge &minus;1.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Two numbers describe every atom. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It is unique to each element and defines what element the atom is.">atomic number</dfn> (proton number) is the number of protons in the nucleus and determines which element the atom is. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.">mass number</dfn> is the total number of protons plus neutrons. The number of neutrons in a neutral atom = mass number &minus; atomic number.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Isotopes and Relative Atomic Mass</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers.">Isotopes</dfn> are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. For example, carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Both are carbon because they have atomic number 6. Isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties (same electron arrangement) but different physical properties (different masses).</p>
    <div class="higher-only">
      <p data-narration-id="n12">Because naturally occurring elements often consist of a mixture of isotopes, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The weighted mean mass of an atom of an element, taking into account the relative mass and abundance of each naturally occurring isotope.">relative atomic mass</dfn> (Aᵣ) is calculated as a weighted mean. For example, chlorine has two isotopes: chlorine-35 (about 75% abundant) and chlorine-37 (about 25% abundant). Its relative atomic mass is approximately:
      $$A_r = \frac{(75 \times 35) + (25 \times 37)}{100} = \frac{2625 + 925}{100} = 35.5$$</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and define isotopes. Then explain why isotopes of the same element have the same chemical properties but may differ in physical properties.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Isotopes have the same proton number (atomic number) but different neutron numbers (different mass numbers). Relative atomic mass is the weighted mean mass, taking account of the abundance of each isotope. Isotopes have identical chemistry because they have the same electron arrangement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Development of the Atomic Model</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Our model of the atom has changed repeatedly as new experimental evidence came to light. This history shows how scientific models are provisional &mdash; they are the best explanation of available evidence and are revised when new evidence demands it.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n16">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1803&ndash;1808</div>
    <h4>Dalton&rsquo;s Atomic Theory</h4>
    <p>John Dalton proposed that all matter was made of tiny indivisible spheres called atoms. A key claim of Dalton&rsquo;s model was that <strong>all atoms of a given element are identical</strong> in mass and properties, and that atoms of different elements differ in mass. He also proposed that atoms cannot be created or destroyed in chemical reactions and that compounds are formed by the combination of atoms in fixed whole-number ratios. The existence of isotopes later showed that not all atoms of an element are identical in mass &mdash; refining Dalton&rsquo;s claim.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1897</div>
    <h4>Thomson &mdash; Discovery of the Electron and the &lsquo;Plum Pudding&rsquo; Model</h4>
    <p>J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and concluded that atoms contained smaller, negatively charged particles. He proposed the &lsquo;plum pudding&rsquo; model: the atom is a diffuse sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it like plums in a pudding. This model retained the idea that the atom is a solid structure but revealed that atoms have internal structure.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1909&ndash;1911</div>
    <h4>Rutherford &mdash; the Gold Foil Experiment</h4>
    <p>Ernest Rutherford, working with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, fired alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil. Most particles passed straight through, but a small number were deflected at large angles, and a very few bounced almost straight back. This was inconsistent with the plum pudding model. Rutherford concluded that most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in a tiny, dense, positively charged <strong>nucleus</strong>, with a large amount of empty space around it. Electrons orbit the nucleus at a distance.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1913</div>
    <h4>Bohr &mdash; Electrons in Shells</h4>
    <p>Niels Bohr proposed that electrons orbit the nucleus in specific, fixed energy levels (shells) rather than anywhere in the space around the nucleus. This explained the discrete emission spectra of elements. Each shell can hold a set number of electrons (the first holds up to 2, the second up to 8, and so on).</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1932</div>
    <h4>Chadwick &mdash; Discovery of the Neutron</h4>
    <p>James Chadwick discovered the neutron, confirming that the nucleus contains uncharged particles as well as protons. This explained why atoms are heavier than the number of protons alone would suggest, and why isotopes of the same element exist.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why the Plum Pudding Model Was Replaced</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The plum pudding model predicted that all alpha particles would be deflected only slightly by the diffuse positive charge. Rutherford&rsquo;s gold foil experiment showed that a small fraction bounced back sharply &mdash; something the plum pudding model could not explain. A new model was needed: the nuclear model, in which almost all the mass and all the positive charge are concentrated in a tiny nucleus at the centre of a largely empty atom. Rutherford&rsquo;s result is a classic example of how a single experiment can overturn an established model in science.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Atoms consist of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in shells. Atomic number = protons; mass number = protons + neutrons; neutrons = mass number &minus; atomic number.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Relative atomic mass is the weighted mean mass, accounting for isotope abundance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The atomic model evolved: Dalton (solid spheres) &rarr; Thomson (plum pudding) &rarr; Rutherford (nuclear model from gold foil experiment, 1909&ndash;1911) &rarr; Bohr (electrons in shells, 1913) &rarr; Chadwick (neutron, 1932).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">The gold foil experiment comes up regularly &mdash; be ready to explain the result (most particles pass through; a small number are deflected; very few bounce back) and connect each observation to the nuclear model (mostly empty space; positively charged nucleus; dense, concentrated nucleus). For isotope questions, remember: same element = same atomic number = same proton number. For relative atomic mass calculations, always show your working and check your weighted mean makes sense (it should lie between the two isotope masses).</p>
