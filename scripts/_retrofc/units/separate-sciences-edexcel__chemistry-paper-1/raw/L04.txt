<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Properties of Ionic Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Ionic compounds form <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions held together by strong electrostatic forces in all directions.">giant ionic lattices</dfn>. The strong electrostatic forces between the ions act in all directions, which gives ionic compounds a characteristic set of properties:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n3"><strong>High melting and boiling points</strong> — a large amount of energy is needed to overcome the many strong bonds acting throughout the lattice.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water</strong> — the ions are then free to move and carry charge. They do <strong>not</strong> conduct when solid because the ions are fixed in position.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Brittle</strong> — if an external force shifts one layer of ions, identical charges align and repel each other strongly, shattering the crystal.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write one property of ionic compounds and then explain it fully in terms of the giant ionic lattice.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ionic compounds: high melting points (strong forces in all directions), conduct when molten or dissolved (ions free to move), do not conduct when solid (ions fixed), brittle (layers shift → repulsion).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Properties of Simple Molecular Substances</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Small molecules formed by covalent bonds between non-metal atoms. They have strong bonds within molecules but weak intermolecular forces between them.">Simple molecules</dfn> (e.g. \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\), \(O_2\)) have <strong>strong</strong> covalent bonds within the molecule but weak <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between separate molecules. Much weaker than covalent bonds and require little energy to overcome.">intermolecular forces</dfn> between molecules. This gives them:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Low melting and boiling points</strong> — little energy is needed to overcome the weak intermolecular forces (not the covalent bonds themselves).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Do not conduct electricity</strong> — no free electrons or ions to carry charge.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n11">As molecules get larger, their intermolecular forces become stronger, so boiling points increase with molecular size. This is why large hydrocarbons are liquid or solid at room temperature while small ones (e.g. methane) are gases.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="A common error: students say covalent bonds break when a simple molecular substance melts. Correct this — intermolecular forces are what breaks, not covalent bonds.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, the weak intermolecular forces are overcome — the strong covalent bonds within molecules remain intact. This explains why simple molecules have low melting points despite having strong internal bonds.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Giant Covalent Structures</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A structure where many atoms are joined by covalent bonds in a continuous network. Very high melting points because many strong covalent bonds must be broken.">Giant covalent structures</dfn> have millions of atoms connected by covalent bonds in a continuous network. They have <strong>very high melting points</strong> because many strong covalent bonds must be broken to separate the atoms. Examples include diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In <dfn class="term" data-def="A giant covalent allotrope of carbon where each atom bonds to four others in a rigid tetrahedral lattice. Very hard, high melting point, does not conduct electricity.">diamond</dfn>, each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds arranged in a rigid tetrahedral pattern. This gives diamond:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n16">Extreme hardness — the rigid lattice resists deformation.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n17">A very high melting point — all four bonds per atom must be broken.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n18">No electrical conductivity — all four outer electrons are used in bonding, leaving none free to carry charge.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">In <dfn class="term" data-def="A giant covalent allotrope of carbon where each atom bonds to three others in flat hexagonal layers. Soft, slippery and conducts electricity.">graphite</dfn>, each carbon atom bonds to three others in flat hexagonal layers. The fourth outer electron from each carbon becomes <strong>delocalised</strong> — free to move along the layers, allowing graphite to conduct electricity. The layers are held together only by weak intermolecular forces, so they slide easily over one another, making graphite soft and slippery — useful as a lubricant and in pencil leads.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Graphene, Fullerenes and Carbon Nanotubes</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="A single layer of graphite — a one-atom-thick sheet of carbon atoms in a hexagonal arrangement. Extremely strong, lightweight and conducts electricity and heat very well.">Graphene</dfn> is a single layer of graphite: a one-atom-thick sheet of hexagonally arranged carbon atoms. Scientists Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov isolated it at the University of Manchester in 2004, work for which they received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2010. Graphene is extraordinarily strong relative to its mass and is an excellent conductor of electricity and heat.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="Carbon molecules in the shape of hollow spheres or tubes. The simplest is buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀), a sphere of 60 carbon atoms.">Fullerenes</dfn> are carbon molecules shaped as hollow spheres or tubes. <strong>Buckminsterfullerene</strong> (\(C_{60}\)) — 60 carbon atoms arranged like a football — was first made in 1985 by Harold Kroto, Robert Curl and Richard Smalley, who were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1996.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">Carbon <dfn class="term" data-def="Cylindrical fullerene structures with exceptional strength and electrical conductivity. Used in electronics, composite materials and drug delivery research.">nanotubes</dfn> are cylindrical fullerenes with remarkable tensile strength and electrical conductivity. They are used in electronics and as reinforcing agents in composite materials.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Compare diamond (4 bonds, no conductivity) with graphite (3 bonds, delocalised electrons, conducts). Then state what graphene is and when it was first isolated.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Diamond: 4 bonds per carbon, rigid, hard, no conductivity. Graphite: 3 bonds per carbon + 1 delocalised electron, layers slide, conducts electricity. Graphene: single graphite layer — isolated 2004, strongest known material per unit mass.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Polymers</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="Very large molecules made by joining many smaller molecules (monomers) together in long chains via covalent bonds.">Polymers</dfn> are very large molecules made from many repeating units called monomers, joined by covalent bonds. Examples include poly(ethene) and poly(propene). The long tangled chains experience stronger intermolecular forces than simple molecules, so polymers are solid at room temperature. They do not conduct electricity and generally have moderate melting points depending on chain length and degree of cross-linking.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n31">When comparing allotropes in the exam, always link structure to property: graphite conducts because delocalised electrons move between layers, whereas diamond does not because all four outer electrons are covalently bonded. Fullerenes and graphene are studied for nanotechnology applications — fullerene cages can carry drug molecules, while graphene's exceptional strength comes from the hexagonal arrangement of carbon atoms in a single layer.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Ionic compounds form giant lattices — high melting points, conduct when molten or dissolved, brittle. Simple molecular substances have low melting points because only weak intermolecular forces need overcoming.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Giant covalent structures (diamond, graphite, graphene) have very high melting points. Diamond is the hardest natural material; graphite conducts electricity due to delocalised electrons and has layers that slide.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Graphene (isolated 2004) is a single graphite layer — extremely strong and conductive. Fullerenes include C₆₀ (buckminsterfullerene, made 1985) and carbon nanotubes, with applications in electronics and materials science.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">A very common error on 6-mark questions: students say 'covalent bonds break when graphite or diamond melts'. For graphite, the weak intermolecular forces between layers are overcome on melting, not the covalent bonds — this is why graphite still has a very high melting point. For simple molecules, the intermolecular forces are overcome but the covalent bonds within the molecule stay intact. Always name the type of force being overcome, not just 'bonds'. For graphene/fullerenes questions, examiners want the year 1985 (buckminsterfullerene) and 2004 (graphene isolation) — but if unsure, paraphrase: 'discovered in the late twentieth century'.</p>
