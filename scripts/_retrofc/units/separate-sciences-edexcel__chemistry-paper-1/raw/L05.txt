--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Mole?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The unit chemists use to count atoms, molecules and ions. One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles.">mole</dfn> is the chemist's counting unit. Just as a dozen always means 12, one mole always contains \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\) particles — whether those particles are atoms, molecules or ions. This enormous number is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of particles in one mole of any substance, equal to 6.02 × 10²³.">Avogadro's constant</dfn> (\(N_A\)).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The key link between moles and mass is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The mass in grams of one mole of a substance, numerically equal to its relative formula mass (Mr).">molar mass</dfn>. For any element, the molar mass in grams equals its relative atomic mass (\(A_r\)). For compounds, add up the \(A_r\) values of all the atoms in the formula to get the <dfn class="term" data-def="The sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in a formula unit of a substance.">relative formula mass</dfn> (\(M_r\)).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Write the mole equation triangle from memory, then use it to calculate: how many moles are in 56g of iron (Ar = 56)?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>\[\text{moles} = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g/mol)}}\] One mole of any element has a mass equal to its relative atomic mass in grams. For CO₂: \(M_r = 12 + (2 \times 16) = 44\,\text{g/mol}\).</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Q:</strong> Calculate the number of moles in 22 g of carbon dioxide (\(CO_2\), \(M_r = 44\)).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Step 1 — Find \(M_r\): \(12 + (2 \times 16) = 44\,\text{g/mol}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Step 2 — Apply the formula: \(\text{moles} = \frac{22}{44} = 0.5\,\text{mol}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Q:</strong> What mass of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, \(M_r = 40\)) contains 0.25 mol?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Rearrange: \(\text{mass} = \text{moles} \times M_r = 0.25 \times 40 = 10\,\text{g}\).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Using Moles in Balanced Equations</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11">A balanced chemical equation shows the <strong>molar ratio</strong> of reactants to products. For example:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">$$2\,Mg + O_2 \rightarrow 2\,MgO$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The numbers in front (coefficients) tell you that 2 moles of magnesium react with 1 mole of oxygen to produce 2 moles of magnesium oxide. To find the mass of a product from a known mass of reactant: (1) calculate moles of reactant, (2) use the ratio to find moles of product, (3) convert moles of product to mass.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Mass from Balanced Equation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Q:</strong> What mass of MgO is produced when 12 g of Mg burns completely? (\(A_r\): Mg = 24, O = 16)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Step 1 — Moles of Mg: \(\frac{12}{24} = 0.5\,\text{mol}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Step 2 — Ratio from equation: 2 mol Mg : 2 mol MgO → 1:1 ratio, so moles of MgO = 0.5 mol.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Step 3 — Mass of MgO: \(M_r(\text{MgO}) = 24 + 16 = 40\,\text{g/mol}\). Mass \(= 0.5 \times 40 = 20\,\text{g}\).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
  <h2 data-narration-id="n18">Limiting Reactants</h2>

  <p data-narration-id="n19">In most reactions, one reactant runs out before the others. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactant that is completely used up first in a reaction, limiting the amount of product that can be formed.">limiting reactant</dfn> determines the maximum amount of product. To identify it: calculate the moles of each reactant and compare to the stoichiometric ratio. Whichever reactant would be exhausted first is the limiting reactant; the other is in excess.</p>

  <div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Examiners frequently test limiting reactants. Calculate moles of both reactants, divide by their coefficients in the balanced equation — the smaller value reveals the limiting reactant.">
    <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
    <p>The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. The amount of product formed is always calculated from the moles of the limiting reactant, not the excess reactant.</p>
  </div>

  <h2 data-narration-id="n21">Percentage Yield</h2>

  <p data-narration-id="n22">In practice, reactions rarely produce the theoretical maximum amount of product. Some product may be lost during transfer between vessels, some reactions may not go to completion, or unwanted side reactions may occur. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of the maximum theoretical yield that is actually obtained in a reaction.">percentage yield</dfn> compares the actual yield to the theoretical maximum:</p>

  <p data-narration-id="n23">$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$</p>

  <p data-narration-id="n24">Industrial chemists aim to maximise percentage yield to reduce waste and cost. Factors lowering yield include incomplete reactions, reversible reactions that reach equilibrium before completion, and losses during filtration or evaporation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Worked Example: Percentage Yield</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Q:</strong> A reaction has a theoretical yield of 50 g of aspirin. The chemist isolates 38 g. Calculate the percentage yield.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n26">\(\text{percentage yield} = \frac{38}{50} \times 100 = 76\%\)</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n27">In an industrial context, a 76% yield would be assessed against the cost of raw materials and any waste disposal requirements before deciding if the process is commercially viable.</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>

  <h2 data-narration-id="n28">Atom Economy</h2>

  <p data-narration-id="n29"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how much of the total mass of reactants is converted into useful product. High atom economy means less waste.">Atom economy</dfn> measures how efficiently a reaction converts reactants into the desired product. Unlike percentage yield (which measures what you got versus what you could get), atom economy is a property of the reaction equation itself:</p>

  <p data-narration-id="n30">$$\text{atom economy} = \frac{\text{sum of } M_r \text{ of desired products}}{\text{sum of } M_r \text{ of all products}} \times 100$$</p>

  <p data-narration-id="n31">A reaction where all atoms end up in the desired product has an atom economy of 100%. Addition reactions (where everything combines into one product) are ideal. Reactions that produce many by-products have low atom economy and generate chemical waste — an important issue in green chemistry and industrial sustainability.</p>

  <div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Remember: atom economy uses masses from the balanced equation, not actual amounts in the lab. Percentage yield uses actual vs theoretical amounts. They measure different things.">
    <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
    <p>Atom economy = (\(M_r\) of useful products ÷ \(M_r\) of all products) × 100. High atom economy is desirable — less waste, lower cost, more sustainable. A 100% atom economy means every atom of reactant ends up in the desired product.</p>
  </div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">One mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles. Moles = mass ÷ molar mass (\(M_r\)). \(M_r\) is found by adding up relative atomic masses for all atoms in the formula.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Balanced equations give molar ratios. The limiting reactant runs out first and determines the theoretical yield. Percentage yield = (actual ÷ theoretical) × 100.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Atom economy = (\(M_r\) of desired products ÷ \(M_r\) of all products) × 100. High atom economy means less waste — important for industrial sustainability and green chemistry.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">The most common calculation error on moles questions is forgetting to divide mass by \(M_r\) — instead students just write down the mass. Always state the equation, substitute values, and give a final answer with units. For percentage yield: the theoretical yield must be calculated via balanced equation first, then divide actual by theoretical and multiply by 100. For atom economy: use the balanced equation and the \(M_r\) values of products only — not reactants. In 6-mark 'describe a method' questions on making a salt, examiners explicitly look for: independent variable, dependent variable, control variables, repeats, and how data will be analysed — missing any of these caps the mark.</p>
