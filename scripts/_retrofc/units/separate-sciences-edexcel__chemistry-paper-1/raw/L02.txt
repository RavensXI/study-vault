How Mendeleev arranged the elements, electronic configuration, and trends in Groups 0, 1 and 7.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Mendeleev Organised the Elements</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Before atomic structure was understood, chemists tried to organise elements by their properties. Dmitri <dfn class="term" data-def="The Russian chemist (1834–1907) who arranged elements in order of atomic weight, grouped those with similar properties in columns, and left gaps for undiscovered elements — creating the foundation of the modern periodic table in 1869.">Mendeleev</dfn> (1834&ndash;1907) published his periodic table in 1869. He arranged elements in order of <strong>atomic weight</strong> and grouped elements with similar chemical properties in the same column. Crucially, he left <strong>gaps</strong> where no known element fitted the pattern, predicting that new elements would be discovered to fill them. When elements such as gallium and germanium were later discovered and their properties closely matched his predictions, the scientific community accepted his table.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Mendeleev also swapped the positions of some elements so that their chemical properties aligned with their group, even if this meant they were not in strict order of atomic weight. This was a bold move, but it was vindicated when atomic numbers (rather than atomic weights) were later found to be the true basis for ordering the elements.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain in one minute: why did leaving gaps in his table make Mendeleev's contribution so significant?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mendeleev&rsquo;s table (1869) organised elements by atomic weight, grouped similar properties in columns, and left gaps for undiscovered elements. The accurate prediction of the properties of those missing elements was key evidence that the table was correct.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Modern Periodic Table</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The modern periodic table arranges all known elements in order of <strong>atomic number</strong> (number of protons). Elements in the same vertical column share the same <dfn class="term" data-def="A vertical column in the periodic table. Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outermost shell and similar chemical properties.">group</dfn>. Elements in the same horizontal row share the same <dfn class="term" data-def="A horizontal row in the periodic table. Elements in the same period have the same number of occupied electron shells.">period</dfn>. Metals are on the left and in the centre of the table; non-metals are on the right.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The arrangement of electrons in an atom across its energy levels (shells). The first shell holds up to 2 electrons; the second and third each hold up to 8.">Electronic configuration</dfn> determines chemical behaviour. The first electron shell holds up to 2 electrons; the second and third each hold up to 8. You can write the electronic configuration of an atom from its atomic number &mdash; for example, sodium (atomic number 11) is written as 2, 8, 1 (2 electrons in shell 1; 8 in shell 2; 1 in shell 3). Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outermost shell, which is why they share similar chemical properties.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Group 0 &mdash; The Noble Gases</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 0 of the periodic table (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon). They have full outer electron shells and are very stable and unreactive.">noble gases</dfn> (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon) have full outer electron shells. This makes them extremely stable and chemically <strong>unreactive</strong> &mdash; they do not normally form compounds. Going down Group 0, the <strong>boiling points increase</strong> (from &minus;269&deg;C for helium to &minus;62&deg;C for radon) because the atoms become larger, with stronger intermolecular forces between them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Group 1 &mdash; The Alkali Metals</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 1 of the periodic table (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium). They all have one electron in their outer shell and are highly reactive metals.">alkali metals</dfn> (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) each have <strong>one electron</strong> in their outer shell. They react with water to produce a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas &mdash; for example:
$$2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Going <strong>down</strong> Group 1, <strong>reactivity increases</strong>. This is because the outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus, so the electrostatic attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker. The outer electron is more easily lost, making the metal more reactive. Going down the group, melting and boiling points <strong>decrease</strong>, and density generally increases.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this box and explain, without looking, why Group 1 reactivity increases down the group. Use the words outer electron, nucleus and attraction.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Group 1 metals have one outer electron and react vigorously with water. Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more easily lost. This is the opposite trend to Group 7.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Group 7 &mdash; The Halogens</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 7 of the periodic table (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine). They have seven outer electrons and react by gaining one electron to form a negative ion.">halogens</dfn> (F, Cl, Br, I, At) have <strong>seven electrons</strong> in their outer shell. They exist as diatomic molecules (e.g. Cl<sub>2</sub>, Br<sub>2</sub>). Going <strong>down</strong> Group 7, <strong>reactivity decreases</strong>. This is because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so the atom has a weaker pull on an incoming electron from a reaction partner &mdash; it is harder to gain the extra electron needed to complete the outer shell.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Physical state at room temperature changes down the group: fluorine is a pale yellow gas; chlorine is a yellow-green gas; bromine is an orange liquid; iodine is a grey-black solid. Boiling points increase down the group.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Halogen Displacement Reactions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A more reactive halogen can <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction in which a more reactive halogen takes the place of a less reactive halogen in a solution of one of its salts.">displace</dfn> a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt. For example, chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution:
    $$Cl_2 + 2KBr \rightarrow 2KCl + Br_2$$
    The solution turns orange as bromine is formed. This reaction confirms the reactivity order: fluorine &gt; chlorine &gt; bromine &gt; iodine. Iodine cannot displace chlorine from a chloride solution because iodine is less reactive than chlorine.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Transition Metals &mdash; a Brief Overview</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The <strong>transition metals</strong> occupy the central block of the periodic table. Compared with Group 1 metals they are much <strong>less reactive</strong>, have <strong>higher melting points</strong>, are <strong>harder and denser</strong>, and typically form <strong>coloured compounds</strong>. Many transition metals and their compounds act as <strong>catalysts</strong> &mdash; for example, iron is used as the catalyst in the industrial Haber process for making ammonia. Transition metals can form ions with different charges (e.g. iron can form Fe<sup>2</sup>&plus; and Fe<sup>3</sup>&plus;).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Group 7 reactivity decreases down the group because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, making it harder for the atom to attract and gain an electron. This is the opposite of Group 1, where losing one electron gets easier down the group.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Dmitri Mendeleev (1834&ndash;1907) organised elements by atomic weight, left gaps for undiscovered elements and grouped similar properties in columns &mdash; his predictions of missing elements confirmed his table. The modern table uses atomic number.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Electronic configuration follows the pattern 2, 8, 8 ... for the first three shells. Group number = outer electrons (groups 1&ndash;7). Group 0 elements have full outer shells and are unreactive noble gases.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Group 1 reactivity increases down the group (outer electron further from nucleus, more easily lost). Group 7 reactivity decreases down the group (harder to gain an electron from a more distant outer shell). A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.</li>
</ul>

For displacement reactions, you must state which halogen is more reactive and explain why the displacement does (or does not) occur.
