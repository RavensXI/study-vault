Reactivity series, displacement reactions, redox, metal extraction by reduction or electrolysis, and alloy properties.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Reactivity Series</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Metals can be arranged in order of their chemical reactivity — this is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of metals arranged in order of their reactivity. More reactive metals displace less reactive metals from compounds and react more vigorously with water and acids.">reactivity series</dfn>. From most to least reactive: potassium (K), sodium (Na), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), aluminium (Al), <em>carbon (C)</em>, zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), <em>hydrogen (H)</em>, copper (Cu), silver (Ag), gold (Au). Carbon and hydrogen are included as non-metal reference points.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">A more reactive metal will <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction where a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in a solution of its compound, releasing the less reactive metal.">displace</dfn> a less reactive metal from a solution of its compound. For example, iron displaces copper from copper(II) sulfate solution:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">$$Fe + CuSO_4 \rightarrow FeSO_4 + Cu$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The solution changes from blue to colourless, and a copper-coloured solid forms on the iron. This is evidence that a displacement reaction has occurred.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Write the full reactivity series from K to Au with carbon and hydrogen in their correct positions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reactivity series (most → least reactive): K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, C, Zn, Fe, H, Cu, Ag, Au. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its compound. Metals above carbon must be extracted by electrolysis; those below carbon can be extracted by reduction with carbon.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Oxidation and Reduction (Redox)</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Displacement reactions are <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction in which both oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously.">redox reactions</dfn> — one species is oxidised while another is reduced. The memory aid <strong>OIL RIG</strong> helps keep the definitions straight: <strong>O</strong>xidation <strong>I</strong>s <strong>L</strong>oss (of electrons), <strong>R</strong>eduction <strong>I</strong>s <strong>G</strong>ain (of electrons).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">In the iron/copper sulfate displacement reaction: iron atoms lose electrons to become \(Fe^{2+}\) ions — iron is <dfn class="term" data-def="A process in which a substance loses electrons (or gains oxygen). In the reactivity context, the more reactive metal loses electrons.">oxidised</dfn>. Copper(II) ions gain electrons to become copper atoms — copper is <dfn class="term" data-def="A process in which a substance gains electrons (or loses oxygen). In the reactivity context, the less reactive metal ion gains electrons.">reduced</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain of electrons. In every redox reaction, if one thing is oxidised, something else must be reduced.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain. In displacement reactions, the more reactive metal (higher in the series) is oxidised; the less reactive metal ion is reduced. Both changes happen simultaneously.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Extracting Metals from Their Ores</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Most metals are found in the Earth's crust combined with other elements in <dfn class="term" data-def="A rock or mineral from which a metal can be extracted economically.">ores</dfn>. The method of extraction depends on the metal's position in the reactivity series:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Metals less reactive than carbon</strong> (e.g. iron, zinc, copper) — extracted by <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction in which oxygen is removed from a metal oxide, using carbon (or carbon monoxide) as the reducing agent.">reduction with carbon</dfn>. Carbon (or carbon monoxide) is more reactive than these metals under smelting conditions, so it removes the oxygen from the metal oxide.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Metals more reactive than carbon</strong> (e.g. aluminium, sodium, potassium) — must be extracted by <strong>electrolysis</strong>. This requires large amounts of electricity, making it much more expensive.</li>
</ul>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Iron is extracted from iron(III) oxide (\(Fe_2O_3\)) in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large industrial furnace in which iron ore, coke and limestone are converted into molten iron using hot air blasted in from the bottom.">blast furnace</dfn>. Coke (carbon) burns in hot air to form carbon dioxide, which then reacts with more coke to form carbon monoxide (CO). The carbon monoxide reduces the iron oxide:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">$$Fe_2O_3 + 3CO \rightarrow 2Fe + 3CO_2$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Limestone (\(CaCO_3\)) is added to remove acidic silicon dioxide impurities as liquid slag (calcium silicate), which is tapped off separately from the molten iron.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Rusting and Corrosion</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="The corrosion of iron by water and oxygen to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust).">Rusting</dfn> occurs when iron or steel is exposed to both water and oxygen. The overall reaction produces hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust). Rusting is an oxidation process — iron is oxidised. Preventing rusting is commercially vital: methods include <dfn class="term" data-def="Coating iron with a thin layer of zinc to protect it from rusting. Zinc acts as a sacrificial metal.">galvanising</dfn> (zinc coating), painting, oiling, or using a sacrificial metal (attaching a more reactive metal that corrodes preferentially).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Alloys</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
  <p data-narration-id="n21">A pure metal has a regular lattice of identical atoms that can slide over each other easily, making the metal soft. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually other metals or carbon) to improve its properties.">alloy</dfn> contains atoms of different sizes, which distort the lattice and make it harder for layers to slide. This makes alloys harder and stronger than the pure metals they contain.</p>

  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Common Alloys and Their Uses</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Steel:</strong> iron + carbon (and sometimes other metals). Low-carbon steel is malleable; high-carbon steel is harder and more brittle; stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel) is corrosion-resistant.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Bronze:</strong> copper + tin. Harder than copper alone; used for statues, bells and bearings.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Brass:</strong> copper + zinc. Harder than copper; used for musical instruments and decorative fittings.</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Gold alloys (carat gold):</strong> pure gold is too soft for jewellery; it is alloyed with silver or copper. 18-carat gold contains 18 parts gold in 24 parts alloy (75% gold).</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>

  <div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this. Explain why alloys are harder than pure metals in terms of different-sized atoms and lattice distortion.">
    <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
    <p>Alloys are harder than pure metals because the different-sized atoms disrupt the regular metallic lattice, preventing layers from sliding easily over each other.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="collapsible">
    <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
      <span>Electroplating</span>
      <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
    </button>
    <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
      <p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="A process that uses electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of metal, improving its appearance or corrosion resistance.">Electroplating</dfn> uses electrolysis to deposit a thin layer of metal onto an object. The object to be plated acts as the cathode (negative electrode), the plating metal acts as the anode (positive electrode), and both are placed in a solution containing ions of the plating metal. Metal ions are reduced at the cathode and deposited onto the object; the anode gradually dissolves to replenish the ions. Applications include chrome plating of car parts, silver plating of cutlery, and tin plating of steel cans.</p>
    </div></div>
  </div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Reactivity series (K to Au): a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its compound. OIL RIG — the more reactive metal is oxidised (loses electrons), the less reactive metal ion is reduced (gains electrons).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Metals below carbon in the reactivity series are extracted by reduction with carbon (e.g. iron in the blast furnace). Metals above carbon (e.g. aluminium) need electrolysis — more expensive due to high electricity use.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Alloys are harder than pure metals because atoms of different sizes distort the lattice, preventing layers sliding. Rusting needs both water and oxygen; galvanising (zinc coating) and electroplating are protective methods.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">OIL RIG is essential for redox questions — examiners want the electron transfer stated explicitly, not just 'iron lost electrons' without saying whether that is oxidation or reduction. For extraction questions: always check the metal's position relative to carbon — if it is above carbon it needs electrolysis, if below it can be reduced. For alloy questions: the key word is 'distort the lattice' — saying layers cannot 'slide' without explaining the different atomic sizes earns fewer marks. Electroplating and galvanising are Triple-only topics (not in Combined Science) — in your exam, these will be worth focused marks if they appear.</p>
