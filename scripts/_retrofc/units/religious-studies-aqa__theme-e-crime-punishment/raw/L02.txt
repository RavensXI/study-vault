<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Aims of Punishment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When someone breaks the law, society must decide how to respond. <dfn class="term" data-def="A penalty imposed on someone who has committed a crime, intended to maintain justice and social order.">Punishment</dfn> serves several purposes, and religious believers often disagree about which aim should be prioritised. The three main aims studied in GCSE Religious Studies are retribution, deterrence and reformation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Punishment that is designed to make the criminal pay for what they have done; an ‘eye for an eye’ approach.">Retribution</dfn></strong> means making the criminal “pay” for what they have done. It is based on the idea that wrongdoing deserves a proportionate response. The Old Testament teaches “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth” (Exodus 21:24), suggesting that punishment should match the severity of the crime. In Islam, the Qur’an records the principle of proportional retaliation as it was ordained for the Children of Israel: ‘We ordained for them: a life for a life, an eye for an eye’ (5:45 — a Qur’anic record of a ruling originally given to the Children of Israel in the Torah). Islamic jurisprudence recognises a similar principle of qiṣāṣ (proportional retribution).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Punishment that aims to discourage the criminal and others from committing crimes by demonstrating the consequences.">Deterrence</dfn></strong> aims to discourage crime by showing the consequences of breaking the law. If people see that criminals are punished severely, they may be less likely to commit crimes themselves. Both Christianity and Islam support the idea that clear consequences help maintain social order, though they disagree about how harsh those consequences should be.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="From memory, name the three aims of punishment and write one religious teaching that supports each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Punishment that aims to change a criminal’s behaviour so they become a law-abiding member of society.">Reformation</dfn></strong> focuses on helping criminals change their behaviour and become productive members of society. Many Christians prioritise reformation, following Jesus’s teaching about forgiveness and second chances. The Parable of the Lost Son (Luke 15:11–32) shows God welcoming back those who have strayed, suggesting that criminals can be redeemed. Islam also supports reformation — the Qur’an teaches: “But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, indeed Allah will turn to him in forgiveness” (5:39).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Treatment of Criminals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">There are several ways in which criminals can be punished. Each method serves different aims and raises different ethical questions.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Prison, Corporal Punishment and Community Service</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A building where people are held as punishment for a crime, usually for a set period of time.">Prison</dfn></strong> removes criminals from society to protect the public, and provides time for reformation through education and rehabilitation programmes. However, critics argue that prisons can be “universities of crime,” where offenders learn new criminal skills. Many religious organisations, including Prison Fellowship (a Christian charity), work within prisons to support rehabilitation and spiritual growth.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Physical punishment inflicted on the body, such as caning, flogging or beating.">Corporal punishment</dfn></strong> means inflicting physical pain on the body — such as caning, flogging or beating. It is banned in the UK but remains legal in some countries. Some Muslims argue that corporal punishment is supported by the Qur’an for certain offences, but others argue that it is outdated and contradicts the principle of mercy. Most Christians oppose corporal punishment, arguing that Jesus taught forgiveness rather than physical retribution.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Unpaid work done in the local community as a form of punishment, often seen as an alternative to prison.">Community service</dfn></strong> requires offenders to do unpaid work that benefits the local community. It can serve both deterrence (the offender must give up their time) and reformation (the work may help them develop skills and a sense of responsibility). Many religious believers support community service as a humane alternative to prison, particularly for less serious offences.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Death Penalty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal execution of a person as punishment for a crime, also known as capital punishment.">death penalty</dfn> (capital punishment) is the most extreme form of punishment — the state-sanctioned execution of a person convicted of a serious crime. It was abolished in the UK in 1965 but remains legal in many countries, including parts of the USA, China, Iran and Saudi Arabia.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write two arguments for and two against the death penalty, using religious teachings.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two key ethical principles apply to the death penalty debate. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The ethical principle that the best action is the one that produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people.">principle of utility</dfn> (utilitarianism) argues that the morality of an action depends on its consequences — if the death penalty deters crime and protects society, it produces the greatest good for the greatest number. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that all human life is holy and belongs to God, and therefore must never be deliberately destroyed.">sanctity of life</dfn> principle argues that all human life is sacred and belongs to God, so no human being has the right to take another’s life, regardless of what they have done.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Arguments For and Against the Death Penalty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Arguments for:</strong> It acts as the ultimate deterrent — people may be less likely to commit serious crimes if they know they could be executed. It provides retribution — some argue that the most serious crimes (such as serial murder or terrorism) deserve the ultimate punishment. It permanently protects society from the most dangerous criminals. The principle of utility may support it if it saves more lives than it takes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Arguments against:</strong> It violates the sanctity of life — only God has the right to take life. Innocent people may be wrongly convicted and executed, and this cannot be undone. It does not allow for reformation — the criminal can never change or make amends. It reduces the state to the level of the murderer. Evidence suggests that the death penalty does not actually deter crime more effectively than long prison sentences.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Contrasting Beliefs on the Death Penalty and Corporal Punishment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Christianity:</strong> Most modern Christian denominations oppose the death penalty. Pope Francis declared in 2018 that the death penalty is “inadmissible” and called for its worldwide abolition. The Church of England also opposes it. Christians argue that Jesus taught forgiveness (“Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing” — Luke 23:34) and that the death penalty denies the possibility of reformation. However, some Christians point to the Old Testament, which prescribed death for certain offences, as evidence that God sometimes sanctions extreme punishment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Islam:</strong> Islamic law (Shari’ah) permits the death penalty for certain serious offences, including murder and apostasy. However, the Qur’an also encourages mercy: “But whoever forgoes it as charity, it is an expiation for him” (5:45), suggesting that forgiveness is preferred. In practice, many Muslim scholars argue that the conditions for applying the death penalty under Shari’ah are extremely strict and rarely met. Regarding corporal punishment, some forms are prescribed in Islamic law (such as flogging for certain offences), but many modern Muslim scholars argue these should be understood in their historical context.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Key similarity:</strong> Both religions teach the sanctity of life and emphasise that mercy and forgiveness are virtues. Both encourage reformation of criminals where possible.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Key difference:</strong> Most mainstream Christian denominations now firmly oppose the death penalty, seeing it as incompatible with Jesus’s teachings on forgiveness. Islamic law technically permits it for certain offences, though many Muslim scholars argue that mercy should prevail and that the conditions for its application are extremely difficult to meet in practice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The three main aims of punishment are retribution (making criminals pay), deterrence (discouraging crime) and reformation (helping criminals change).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The death penalty raises key ethical questions about the sanctity of life versus the principle of utility; most Christians oppose it, while Islamic law permits it under strict conditions but encourages mercy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Corporal punishment is banned in the UK but debated in religious contexts — most Christians oppose it, while some Muslims argue it is permitted by Shari'ah under strict conditions.</li>
</ul>
