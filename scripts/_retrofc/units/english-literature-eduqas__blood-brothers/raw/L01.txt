<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Russell’s Liverpool</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The playwright of Blood Brothers, born in Whiston near Liverpool in 1947, who left school at 15 and later became a hairdresser before studying drama.">Willy Russell</dfn> grew up in a working-class family on Merseyside. He left school with no qualifications, worked as a hairdresser and in a factory, and only began writing plays in his twenties after attending evening classes. His personal experience of the class system — watching how opportunity was distributed by postcode and income, not talent — drives every page of <em>Blood Brothers</em>. Russell once said he wanted to write plays “for the people I grew up with, not the people who run the National Theatre.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding Russell’s background is essential for the Eduqas C720U Component 2 exam. The play is not a neutral story — it is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A play or novel that delivers a clear social or political message, aiming to change the audience’s attitudes or beliefs.">polemic</dfn> against class inequality. Russell wanted audiences to leave the theatre angry at a system that gives one twin everything and the other nothing, despite identical genes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three facts about Russell’s life shaped Blood Brothers?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Russell left school at 15, worked manual jobs, and studied as a mature student. He drew directly on his experience of class division in Liverpool. Blood Brothers is autobiographical in spirit: Russell <em>was</em> the Mickey figure — a working-class boy whose life chances were limited by the system, not by ability.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Thatcherism & the 1980s</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The musical version of <em>Blood Brothers</em> premiered in London’s West End in 1983, four years into Margaret Thatcher’s government. <dfn class="term" data-def="The political ideology of Margaret Thatcher’s Conservative government (1979–1990), emphasising free markets, individual responsibility, privatisation and reduced state welfare.">Thatcherism</dfn> reshaped Britain. Traditional industries — steel, shipbuilding, coal mining — were allowed to collapse. Unemployment trebled between 1979 and 1983, hitting 3.3 million. The north of England, Scotland and Wales suffered disproportionately, while London and the south-east boomed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Liverpool was devastated. The city lost a quarter of its manufacturing jobs in five years. Youth unemployment in Toxteth reached over 50%. In 1981, the Toxteth riots erupted — young people, largely Black and working-class, clashed with police over joblessness, racism and heavy-handed policing. Thatcher’s own cabinet minister, Geoffrey Howe, privately recommended a policy of “managed decline” for Liverpool — essentially abandoning the city.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">In the play, the Johnstones live on a <dfn class="term" data-def="Social housing built and managed by the local council, typically for low-income families; associated in the play with poverty, overcrowding and limited opportunity.">council estate</dfn> in inner-city Liverpool. The houses are cramped, the streets are rough, and Sammy is already on the path to crime. The Lyons family live in a large private house in the suburbs — clean, quiet, with a garden and space. When the Johnstones are relocated to the countryside (Skelmersdale), it initially seems like an escape, but the lack of jobs and opportunity follows them.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Russell uses these contrasting settings to make his political point visible. The audience can <em>see</em> the class divide in the physical space. Eddie’s world has books, a private school, and a bedroom of his own. Mickey’s world has shared beds, hand-me-down clothes, and a mother who struggles to feed seven children. The settings are not just backdrops — they are the argument.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Welfare State Under Attack</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">After World War Two, the Labour government built a <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government-funded healthcare, education, housing and social security designed to protect citizens from poverty; established in Britain after 1945.">welfare state</dfn> — the NHS, council housing, free education, unemployment benefit. The idea was that no child’s future should be determined by their parents’ wealth. By the 1980s, Thatcher’s government was dismantling this safety net. Council housing was sold off under Right to Buy. Benefits were cut. The gap between rich and poor widened dramatically.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Russell shows the consequences through Mrs Johnstone. She is not lazy or irresponsible — she works as a cleaner for Mrs Lyons, she loves her children fiercely, she sings to them. But the system offers her nothing. When she falls behind on the hire purchase payments, the milkman and catalogue man come to repossess her belongings: “We’re not going to take them all, love. We’ll leave you one, you can swap each week.” Russell makes the audience feel the humiliation of poverty — and understand that it is structural, not personal.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three ways Thatcherism connects to events in Blood Brothers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thatcher said “there is no such thing as society.” Russell’s entire play is an argument against this. Blood Brothers insists that society — its class system, its housing, its education, its job market — determines who thrives and who is destroyed. Mickey’s unemployment, depression and imprisonment are not personal failures; they are the predictable consequences of structural inequality.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The ‘Nature vs Nurture’ Experiment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Blood Brothers is structured as a <dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific and philosophical debate about whether human character is determined by genetics (nature) or upbringing and environment (nurture).">nature versus nurture</dfn> experiment. Two genetically identical boys are placed in different environments. One receives wealth, education, confidence and opportunity. The other receives poverty, overcrowding, a failing school and a brother heading for prison. The outcome is devastating — and Russell’s argument is clear: nurture wins. The system, not the person, is to blame.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Narrator’s closing question makes this explicit: “And do we blame superstition for what came to pass? Or could it be what we, the English, have come to know as class?” Russell does not leave the audience guessing. The answer is class. Superstition is a red herring — a way for society to avoid confronting the real cause of inequality.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Russell grew up working-class in Liverpool and left school at 15 — Blood Brothers is driven by his personal experience of class inequality.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The play was written during Thatcherism: mass unemployment, deindustrialisation, council estate poverty and the dismantling of the welfare state provide its political context.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Narrator’s final question (“superstition or class?”) makes Russell’s argument explicit: the class system, not fate, determines who lives and who dies.</li></ul>
