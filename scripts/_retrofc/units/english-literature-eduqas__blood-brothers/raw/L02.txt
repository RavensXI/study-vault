<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Mother With Nothing Left</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 1 opens with <dfn class="term" data-def="The twins’ biological mother, a warm, superstitious working-class woman abandoned by her husband and struggling to raise seven children alone.">Mrs Johnstone</dfn> already at breaking point. Her husband has left her for a younger woman. She has seven children to feed on a cleaner’s wage. The milkman, the catalogue man and the finance company are all chasing debts. When she discovers she is expecting twins, her reaction is not joy but terror: she cannot afford the children she already has.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Russell establishes Mrs Johnstone’s character through her opening song, “Marilyn Monroe.” She compares herself to the glamorous film star, remembering when her husband told her she was “sexier than Marilyn Monroe.” The song is simultaneously comic and devastating — it reveals a woman whose youthful dreams have been crushed by poverty, abandonment and relentless childbearing. The audience is positioned to feel <dfn class="term" data-def="The audience’s emotional identification with a character’s suffering or situation.">sympathy</dfn> for her from the very start. Russell does not want us to judge Mrs Johnstone — he wants us to judge the system that failed her.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does the Marilyn Monroe song reveal about Mrs Johnstone’s character and circumstances?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Marilyn Monroe” establishes Mrs Johnstone as a woman whose dreams have been destroyed by poverty. She was once full of hope; now she is abandoned, in debt, and facing an impossible choice. The song uses dramatic irony — the audience knows Marilyn Monroe died tragically, foreshadowing Mrs Johnstone’s own tragic fate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mrs Lyons: Manipulation & Desperation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The wealthy, childless employer of Mrs Johnstone who persuades her to give up one of her twins; she becomes increasingly paranoid and unstable.">Mrs Lyons</dfn> is Mrs Johnstone’s employer — a wealthy, childless woman whose husband works abroad for long periods. When she learns that Mrs Johnstone is expecting twins, she seizes the opportunity. She tells Mrs Johnstone: “Give one to me.” The line is chillingly direct. Mrs Lyons does not ask; she instructs. Russell uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="A grammatical mood that gives commands or orders; Mrs Lyons’s imperative language reveals her assumption of authority over Mrs Johnstone.">imperative</dfn> to reveal the power imbalance between employer and employee, between the middle class and the working class.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Mrs Lyons’s argument is calculated. She points out that Mrs Johnstone “already has seven” and that the child would have a better life with her — a big house, a good school, everything money can buy. She promises that Mrs Johnstone can still see the child every day because she works in the Lyons household. The logic seems almost reasonable, which is precisely what makes it so dangerous. Russell shows how the middle class uses rational-sounding arguments to exploit the vulnerability of the poor.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">To seal the pact, Mrs Lyons makes Mrs Johnstone swear on the Bible — a <dfn class="term" data-def="An object, action or idea that represents something beyond its literal meaning; the Bible oath symbolises the irreversible nature of the pact.">symbol</dfn> of absolute, unbreakable commitment. For Mrs Johnstone, who is deeply superstitious, this transforms a desperate agreement into a sacred vow. The Bible oath is the play’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The event or decision that sets the main action of a plot in motion and cannot be undone.">catalyst</dfn> — the moment that sets the entire tragedy in motion. Once the oath is sworn, there is no going back.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Mrs Lyons then invents the <dfn class="term" data-def="Irrational beliefs in supernatural forces; Mrs Johnstone believes in omens like shoes on the table and broken mirrors.">superstition</dfn> that if the twins ever discover they are brothers, they will both die. “They say that if either twin learns he was one of a pair, they shall both immediately die.” This is a lie — Mrs Lyons fabricates it to ensure Mrs Johnstone never tells the truth. But Mrs Johnstone believes it absolutely, because her world is one where superstition fills the gap left by powerlessness. When you have no control over your life, you look for patterns — even irrational ones — to explain why bad things happen.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Narrator’s Warning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who speaks directly to the audience, commenting on the action, foreshadowing the tragedy, and functioning as a figure of fate or conscience.">Narrator</dfn> appears immediately after the pact is made. He warns: “Shoes upon the table / An’ a spider’s been killed.” This is the first of many <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary device in which the writer gives hints or clues about events that will happen later in the story.">foreshadowing</dfn> interventions. The Narrator links the pact to a chain of superstitious omens — shoes on the table (bad luck), a broken mirror (seven years of misfortune), a magpie (sorrow). Each omen signals that disaster is coming.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Narrator’s role here is complex. On one level, he reinforces the superstition — his ominous tone makes the audience feel that the pact truly is cursed. On another level, Russell uses the Narrator to create <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension and emotional engagement.">dramatic irony</dfn>. We know the twins will die because we saw it in the opening scene. Every moment of hope or happiness that follows is undercut by our knowledge of the ending. The Narrator is not telling us the future — he is reminding us that we already know it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three ways Mrs Lyons manipulates Mrs Johnstone in Act 1.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Lyons uses three tactics to secure the pact: (1) she exploits Mrs Johnstone’s poverty (“you already have seven”), (2) she makes her swear on the Bible, and (3) she invents a superstition about the twins dying if they learn the truth. Each tactic reflects the middle class’s power over the working class — financial, religious, and psychological.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Twins Are Born: Separation & Its Consequences</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">When the twins are born, Mrs Johnstone immediately regrets her decision. She tries to keep both babies, but Mrs Lyons takes Edward. The stage direction describes Mrs Johnstone reaching out for her child — a physical, visceral image of loss. Russell makes the audience feel the cruelty of the separation. This is not a fair exchange between equals; it is a transaction in which a desperate woman is coerced into giving up her child.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Mrs Lyons then fires Mrs Johnstone to prevent her from seeing Edward. The promise that Mrs Johnstone could “see him every day” is broken immediately. Russell shows how the powerful break promises to the powerless without consequence. Mrs Johnstone has no legal rights, no contract, no power to challenge her employer. The class system protects Mrs Lyons and punishes Mrs Johnstone.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">From this point, the two boys grow up in entirely different worlds. Edward is raised in wealth, security and education. Mickey is raised in poverty, chaos and deprivation. The nature versus nurture experiment has begun — and the audience watches it unfold with the devastating knowledge of how it will end.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Mrs Johnstone is established through “Marilyn Monroe” as a sympathetic figure crushed by poverty, abandonment and debt — Russell ensures the audience blames the system, not the mother.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Mrs Lyons manipulates Mrs Johnstone using three tactics: financial exploitation, the Bible oath, and an invented superstition — each reflecting the middle class’s power over the working class.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The Narrator’s “shoes upon the table” refrain begins the pattern of foreshadowing that creates dramatic irony throughout the play.</li></ul>
