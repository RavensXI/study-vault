<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 9: Dr Lanyon’s Narrative</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Lanyon’s letter, opened after his death, reveals what killed him. Jekyll (as Hyde) sent Lanyon instructions to collect chemicals from the laboratory. When Lanyon witnesses Hyde drink the potion and transform into Jekyll before his eyes, the shock destroys him: “My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me; the deadliest terror sits by me at all hours.” Lanyon’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A first-person account of events from the perspective of a character who witnessed them.">testimony</dfn> gives the reader the first direct description of the transformation — and confirms that Jekyll and Hyde are the same person.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Stevenson delays this revelation until Chapter 9 of 10 — a structural choice that maximises impact. The reader has spent eight chapters constructing theories (blackmail? murder? a secret relative?), and the truth is more disturbing than any of them. Lanyon’s horror is not just at the physical transformation but at its implications: if a respectable doctor contains a murderer, what might <em>anyone</em> contain? The truth about duality is literally lethal to the Victorian mind.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why Stevenson delays the revelation until the penultimate chapter.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson delays the truth until Chapter 9, making the reader experience the same process of discovery as the characters. This delayed <dfn class="term" data-def="The point at which a crucial piece of hidden information is made known to the reader or characters.">revelation</dfn> maximises shock: eight chapters of mystery resolve into a truth more disturbing than any theory. Lanyon’s death proves that the Victorian world cannot survive the knowledge of its own duality.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Chapter 10: Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jekyll’s confession is the novella’s final chapter and its richest for analysis. He describes his life before the experiment: “I was in no sense a hypocrite; both sides of me were in dead earnest.” He was genuinely good <em>and</em> genuinely drawn to vice. The experiment did not create evil — it separated it from the restraints of conscience. Hyde was “pure evil” because he was Jekyll without the moral part: “Edward Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil.”</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jekyll’s Confession: Key Quotations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Jekyll describes his initial pleasure in being Hyde: “I felt younger, lighter, happier in body… I knew myself, at the first breath of this new life, to be more wicked, tenfold more wicked… and the thought, in that moment, braced and delighted me like wine.” The language of pleasure and intoxication (“lighter,” “delighted,” “wine”) reveals that Jekyll <em>enjoyed</em> being Hyde. He does not present himself as a victim of his experiment but as someone who chose to indulge his darkest impulses and found it addictive.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Hyde is smaller than Jekyll because, as Jekyll explains, “the evil side of my nature… was less robust and less developed than the good.” This is because Jekyll had spent his life exercising virtue and suppressing vice. But once released, evil grows: “I began to be tortured with throes and longings, as of Hyde struggling after freedom.” The metaphor of addiction is unmistakable — Hyde is a craving that intensifies with every indulgence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Failure of the Experiment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jekyll’s experiment fails for a specific reason: he cannot create a potion that separates the <em>good</em> side. The transformation only works one way — it releases evil but cannot isolate virtue. Jekyll explains: “Had I approached my discovery in a more noble spirit… all must have been otherwise.” He acknowledges that his motives were selfish from the start — he wanted freedom from moral responsibility, not genuine self-improvement. The novella argues that you cannot escape moral accountability by splitting yourself in two.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jekyll’s Death and Hyde’s Suicide</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Jekyll’s final transformation is permanent because the original salt that powered the potion was impure — he cannot replicate it. He runs out of the crucial ingredient and knows that the next transformation will be his last. His statement ends with the knowledge that “this is the last time… that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts.” When Utterson breaks down the door, he finds Hyde dead by poison. Jekyll and Hyde die together because they are, and always were, one person.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The suicide is significant: Hyde chooses death rather than face justice. Even Hyde’s final act is selfish — he kills himself to avoid consequences, not out of remorse. Jekyll’s tragedy is that he created something he could not control and could not undo. The novella ends not with moral redemption but with destruction — a warning, not a comfort.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write Jekyll’s explanation of why Hyde is smaller and what this reveals about the nature of evil.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hyde is smaller than Jekyll because “the evil side of my nature was less robust and less developed than the good.” Jekyll has spent years exercising virtue, so evil is stunted. But once released, Hyde grows stronger: the novella argues that evil is not a fixed quantity but a force that expands with indulgence. Repression does not destroy evil — it only stores it.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Stevenson delays the revelation until Chapter 9 to maximise impact — the reader discovers the truth alongside Lanyon, making the shock shared.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Jekyll enjoyed being Hyde: the language of pleasure and intoxication reveals that the experiment was driven by desire, not science, making Jekyll morally responsible.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Hyde is smaller because evil was ‘less developed,’ but grows with indulgence — the novella argues that repression stores evil rather than destroying it.</li></ul>
