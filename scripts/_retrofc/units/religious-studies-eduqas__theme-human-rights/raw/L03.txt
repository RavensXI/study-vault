<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Wealth, Poverty, and Religious Ethics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Religious traditions do not uniformly condemn wealth, but they do consistently place ethical demands on how it is acquired and used. Two forms of poverty frame the issue. <dfn class="term" data-def="Poverty in which a person lacks the basic necessities for survival — food, clean water, shelter, and clothing.">Absolute poverty</dfn> is the state of lacking basic necessities for survival. <dfn class="term" data-def="Poverty defined in relation to the living standards of the wider society — having significantly less than the average person in that context.">Relative poverty</dfn> is being significantly worse off than others in the same society. Both forms exist in Britain and globally. Religious traditions draw on scripture and teaching to explain why poverty matters morally and how believers should respond through individual action and institutional charity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Christianity: Wealth as Stewardship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Christianity teaches that wealth is not wrong in itself, but its acquisition and use carry moral weight. Several Gospel parables directly address wealth. Luke 16:19&ndash;31, the parable of the rich man (Dives) and Lazarus, describes a wealthy man who ignores a poor man at his gate; after death their situations are dramatically reversed. The parable implies that ignoring the poor has eternal consequences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Luke 12:13&ndash;21, the parable of the rich fool, criticises a man who builds larger barns to store surplus grain and says to himself &ldquo;You have plenty of good things laid up for many years. Take life easy; eat, drink and be merry&rdquo; &mdash; only to die that very night. The parable warns against treating wealth as the ultimate security. Mark 10:21&ndash;31 records Jesus telling a rich young man to sell everything, give to the poor, and follow him. Mark 12:41&ndash;44, the story of the widow&rsquo;s offering, praises a poor widow who gives two small coins, more than the wealthy who give from their surplus. These passages together suggest that what matters is not the amount given but the sacrifice and the spirit behind the giving.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Christian Aid</strong> and <strong>CAFOD</strong> (the Catholic Agency for Overseas Development) are major British faith charities that apply these teachings internationally. Christian Aid works across more than 50 countries, campaigning against the causes of poverty as well as providing emergency relief. CAFOD, the official Catholic development agency for England and Wales, works in partnership with local organisations worldwide, motivated by Catholic social teaching on solidarity and the preferential option for the poor. The <strong>Trussell Trust</strong>, which operates a network of food banks across Britain, was founded as a Christian charity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three Christian parables about wealth from this lesson, giving the Gospel reference for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key Gospel passages on wealth: Luke 16:19&ndash;31 (rich man and Lazarus &mdash; ignoring poverty has eternal consequences), Luke 12:13&ndash;21 (rich fool &mdash; wealth is not ultimate security), Mark 10:21&ndash;31 (sell everything and give to the poor), Mark 12:41&ndash;44 (widow&rsquo;s mite &mdash; sacrifice matters more than amount).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Judaism: Tzedakah and the Eight Levels of Giving</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Hebrew term meaning 'righteousness' or 'justice'; the Jewish obligation to give to those in need, understood as a matter of justice rather than generosity.">Tzedakah</dfn> &mdash; charitable giving &mdash; is a core Jewish obligation, not merely an optional act of kindness. It is rooted in the understanding that wealth ultimately belongs to God and that sharing it is an act of justice. The twelfth-century philosopher <strong>Maimonides</strong> described eight levels of tzedakah, from the lowest (giving reluctantly and inadequately) to the highest (helping someone become financially self-sufficient, thereby removing their need for charity altogether).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>World Jewish Relief</strong> is the primary British Jewish charity working to alleviate poverty, operating in Eastern Europe and other regions affected by poverty and conflict. HIAS supports refugees globally. These organisations embody tzedakah in action, working on the principle that Jewish memory of persecution creates a specific obligation to protect the vulnerable. Concern for the <em>ger</em> (the stranger) is also a repeated Torah commandment, grounding Jewish poverty relief in theological duty.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Islam: Zakat and the Purification of Wealth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Islam&rsquo;s approach to wealth includes both a theological and a practical dimension. Qur&rsquo;an 2:177 describes true righteousness as including giving wealth to those in need: to relatives, orphans, the needy, travellers, those who ask, and for the freeing of captives. This is not voluntary generosity &mdash; it describes the character of a truly righteous person.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The third Pillar of Islam — the obligatory annual payment of 2.5% of accumulated wealth above a minimum threshold (nisab), given to the poor and other specified recipients.">Zakat</dfn> is the third Pillar of Islam: every Muslim who holds wealth above a minimum threshold (<em>nisab</em>) for a full lunar year must give 2.5% to those in need. Zakat is not charity but a religious obligation &mdash; it purifies the rest of one&rsquo;s wealth by acknowledging that a portion belongs to the poor. <strong>Islamic Relief</strong> and <strong>Muslim Aid</strong> are major British Islamic charities that channel zakat and voluntary donations to emergency relief and long-term development projects worldwide, working in conflict zones and areas of extreme poverty.</p>

Unlike some traditions that view wealth with suspicion, Hinduism acknowledges artha as a proper human goal, provided it is pursued within the constraints of <em>dharma</em> (righteousness). Wealth acquired unethically or used selfishly, however, generates negative karma.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Hindu practice of charitable giving, particularly the giving of food, clothing, or money to those in need, seen as a spiritual act of generosity.">Dana</dfn> &mdash; charitable giving &mdash; is a virtuous act in Hinduism, generating good karma and expressing the understanding that the divine is present in every person. <strong>Food for Life Global</strong>, founded within the Vaishnava (ISKCON) tradition, provides free vegetarian meals to people in need worldwide, making it one of the world&rsquo;s largest food relief programmes. It operates on the principle that food is spiritual and sharing it with all people is an act of worship.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Sikhism: Sewa and Langar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Sikh teaching presents wealth as a trust from God. The Guru Granth Sahib at ang 1246 contains teachings on the duty of giving to those in need. <dfn class="term" data-def="Selfless service, a central Sikh practice of serving others without expectation of reward, seen as an act of devotion to God.">Sewa</dfn> &mdash; selfless service &mdash; is one of the three pillars of Sikhism (alongside <em>naam japna</em> and <em>kirat karo</em>). Giving generously and without discrimination is expected of all Sikhs. The <em>langar</em> &mdash; the free community kitchen in every gurdwara &mdash; is a daily, practical expression of sewa: anyone, regardless of wealth, religion, or status, is welcome to eat there at no cost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Khalsa Aid</strong> is a British Sikh humanitarian organisation that provides emergency food, water, and shelter in disaster zones and conflict areas worldwide, explicitly motivated by the Sikh teaching of sewa and the principle that every human being deserves dignity. The Guru Granth Sahib at ang 42 also contains teachings linking righteous giving to spiritual advancement.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Buddhism: The Middle Way and Right Livelihood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Buddhism&rsquo;s approach to wealth is shaped by the story of Siddhartha Gautama himself. Born into wealth, he abandoned it; after extreme asceticism failed him, he found the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Buddha's teaching that the path to liberation lies between extreme indulgence and extreme asceticism — neither excessive wealth nor extreme poverty is the ideal.">Middle Way</dfn> &mdash; neither excessive wealth nor extreme poverty. The Four Noble Truths identify <em>tanha</em> (craving or attachment) as the root of suffering. Attachment to wealth &mdash; treating it as a source of lasting happiness &mdash; leads to dukkha.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The fifth step of the Noble Eightfold Path, requiring that one's livelihood does not cause harm to oneself or others.">Right Livelihood</dfn> &mdash; the fifth step of the Eightfold Path &mdash; requires earning a living in ways that do not cause harm. <em>Dana</em> (generosity and charitable giving) is one of the ten Buddhist perfections (<em>paramis</em>) and the first step on the path. Triratna Buddhists in Britain practise dana in their communities. <strong>Karuna Trust</strong> is a British Buddhist charity that works to relieve poverty and social exclusion, particularly among Dalit communities in South Asia &mdash; addressing both material poverty and the structural discrimination that causes it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write from memory: name five faith charities covered in this lesson, their tradition, and one country or region where they work.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key faith charities: <strong>CAFOD</strong> and <strong>Christian Aid</strong> (Christian &mdash; overseas development and emergency relief), <strong>World Jewish Relief</strong> (Jewish &mdash; Eastern Europe and conflict regions), <strong>Islamic Relief</strong> and <strong>Muslim Aid</strong> (Islamic &mdash; disaster zones globally), <strong>Khalsa Aid</strong> (Sikh &mdash; humanitarian relief worldwide), <strong>Karuna Trust</strong> (Buddhist &mdash; Dalit communities, South Asia), <strong>Food for Life</strong> (Hindu/ISKCON &mdash; global food relief).</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n23">Humanists UK and secular organisations argue that poverty should be addressed on the basis of rational ethics and compassion, without the need for religious motivation. The utilitarian argument &mdash; associated with philosophers like Peter Singer &mdash; holds that if we can prevent suffering at relatively little cost to ourselves, we have a moral obligation to do so. Singer has argued that wealthy individuals in developed nations are morally obligated to give significantly more to effective anti-poverty organisations than most currently do. Secular organisations such as Oxfam and Shelter work alongside faith charities to address poverty, driven by a shared moral commitment to human wellbeing regardless of theological grounding.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Christianity warns against making wealth an ultimate goal (Luke 12:13&ndash;21; Mark 10:21&ndash;31) and against ignoring poverty (Luke 16:19&ndash;31). Faith charities Christian Aid, CAFOD, and the Trussell Trust embody these teachings in Britain and globally.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Judaism&rsquo;s tzedakah (justice-giving; Maimonides&rsquo; eight levels) and Islam&rsquo;s zakat (obligatory 2.5% on surplus wealth; Qur&rsquo;an 2:177) both make poverty relief a religious duty rather than personal preference. World Jewish Relief, HIAS, Islamic Relief, and Muslim Aid put these into practice.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Sikhism&rsquo;s sewa (selfless service; Guru Granth Sahib, angs 42 and 1246) is embodied in langar and Khalsa Aid; Hinduism&rsquo;s artha and dana are practised through Food for Life; Buddhism&rsquo;s Right Livelihood and dana are embodied in Karuna Trust. Humanists ground poverty relief in rational ethics and compassion.</li>
</ul>
