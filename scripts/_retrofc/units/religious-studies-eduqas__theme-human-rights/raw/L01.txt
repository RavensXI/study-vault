<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Human Rights and the Dignity of Every Person</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Basic rights and freedoms to which all human beings are entitled, regardless of nationality, sex, religion, or any other status.">Human rights</dfn> are the basic entitlements that belong to every person by virtue of being human. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), adopted by the United Nations in 1948, provides the foundational international statement of these rights. Article 1 declares that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. Article 18 protects <dfn class="term" data-def="The right to hold, change, and express one's religious beliefs, protected by Article 18 of the UDHR and Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights.">freedom of religion or belief</dfn>, including the freedom to change one&rsquo;s religion and to express it in worship, teaching, practice, and observance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Religious traditions have their own deep accounts of why human life possesses special dignity &mdash; accounts that both support and complicate their relationship to modern human rights frameworks. In this lesson we examine what each tradition teaches about human dignity, how that teaching shapes social action, and how religious communities navigate tensions between personal conviction and civil law.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Christianity: Imago Dei and Agape</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The foundational Christian teaching on human dignity is <dfn class="term" data-def="Latin phrase meaning 'image of God'; the biblical teaching (Genesis 1:26-27) that every human being reflects the image of God and therefore possesses inherent dignity.">imago Dei</dfn> &mdash; the belief, rooted in Genesis 1:26&ndash;27, that God created human beings in His image and likeness. This means every person, regardless of race, gender, ability, or status, carries the imprint of the divine and is owed respect. The Catholic Catechism, paragraphs 1929&ndash;1933, develops this into a doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="The conviction that every person possesses an inherent worth that must be respected in social, political, and economic life.">social justice</dfn>, stating that every person is made in the image of God and that human society must be organised to protect and promote this dignity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In practice, Christianity expresses human dignity through <em>agape</em> &mdash; selfless, unconditional love for others as God loves them. Christian charities, advocacy organisations, and individuals are motivated by agape to work for human rights. Catechism paragraphs 2493&ndash;2499 address freedom of expression and social communication, while paragraphs 1905&ndash;1912 outline the <dfn class="term" data-def="The sum of social conditions that allow individuals and communities to reach their full potential; a key Catholic social teaching concept.">common good</dfn> as the framework within which individual rights are exercised. An example of conflict between personal religious conviction and law might include a conscientious objector or a Christian registrar who sought to decline officiating at same-sex ceremonies &mdash; cases where courts have had to balance individual conscience against the rights of others.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Without looking, recall what Genesis 1:26-27 teaches and name one Catholic Catechism paragraph range that develops the doctrine of human dignity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Genesis 1:26&ndash;27 teaches that God created human beings in His image (<em>imago Dei</em>). Catholic Catechism 1929&ndash;1933 develops this into a teaching on social justice: every person&rsquo;s dignity must be respected and protected in social and political life.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Judaism: Tzedakah and the Value of Each Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Judaism also grounds human dignity in Genesis 1:26&ndash;27: each person is <em>b&rsquo;tselem Elohim</em> &mdash; in the image of God &mdash; and therefore every human life is of infinite value. The Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 states that whoever saves a single life, it is as if they have saved an entire world. This principle of the infinite worth of each individual underpins Jewish human rights ethics.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Hebrew term meaning 'righteousness' or 'justice'; in practice it refers to the obligatory giving of charity and the pursuit of social justice in Jewish tradition.">Tzedakah</dfn> &mdash; charitable giving as an act of justice rather than mere generosity &mdash; is a central practice through which Jews promote human dignity. Maimonides, the twelfth-century Jewish philosopher, described eight levels of tzedakah ranging from reluctant giving to the highest level: helping someone become self-sufficient. Jewish organisations such as World Jewish Relief and HIAS (formerly the Hebrew Immigrant Aid Society) work globally to support refugees and those affected by poverty and persecution, embodying <em>tzedakah</em> in action.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jews have also faced direct conflicts between religious conviction and civil law &mdash; for example, when laws of the state have required actions incompatible with <em>halakha</em>, or conversely when religious dress or practice has been restricted by law. Jewish history is shaped by the experience of persecution, making religious freedom (Article 18 UDHR) particularly resonant.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Islam: Fitra, Ummah, and Equality Before God</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Islam teaches that every human being is born with <dfn class="term" data-def="The innate natural disposition towards goodness and recognition of God with which every person is created, according to Islamic teaching.">fitra</dfn> &mdash; a pure, natural disposition towards goodness and recognition of God. This gives every person inherent dignity from birth. Qur&rsquo;an 5:32 states that whoever saves a life, it is as if they have saved all of humanity &mdash; a verse that grounds Islamic human rights ethics in the sanctity of life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The worldwide community of Muslims, bound together by shared faith; a key Islamic social and ethical concept.">ummah</dfn> &mdash; the global Muslim community &mdash; expresses solidarity and mutual responsibility. Muslims are called to promote justice (<em>adl</em>) and forbid wrongdoing (<em>nahi anil munkar</em>). Islamic human rights work includes organisations such as Islamic Relief and Muslim Aid, which operate in disaster zones and areas of poverty worldwide. The concept of censorship and religious extremism is also addressed within Islamic ethics: scholars across traditions condemn acts of violence committed in the name of religion, emphasising that Islam teaches the sanctity of life. Islamophobia &mdash; prejudice and discrimination against Muslims &mdash; is a recognised form of religious discrimination that conflicts with Article 18&rsquo;s protection of religious freedom.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Hinduism: Atman, Dharma, and Human Dignity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In Hindu teaching, every person possesses an <dfn class="term" data-def="The eternal self or soul in Hindu philosophy, understood as a spark of the divine (Brahman) present in every living being.">atman</dfn> &mdash; an eternal soul that is ultimately one with Brahman, the universal divine reality. The recognition that Brahman dwells in all people (<em>Brahman in all</em>) grounds human dignity in Hindu thought. This belief motivates <em>dharma</em> &mdash; righteous action and ethical duty &mdash; as the framework for human social life. Dharma in action may include volunteering, charitable giving, and advocacy for the oppressed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The tension between personal religious conviction and civil law arises in Hinduism around practices such as ritual slaughter, caste-based restrictions, or temple access. Modern Hindu organisations such as the Ramakrishna Mission have emphasised service to humanity (<em>seva</em>) as worship of God, grounding social justice work in theology.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Sikhism: Khalsa and the Equality of All</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Sikh teaching holds that every human being is created by God and possesses equal worth. The Guru Granth Sahib opens by describing God (<em>Waheguru</em>) as the one who created all and is present in all. The Khalsa &mdash; the community of initiated Sikhs founded by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699 &mdash; was established partly to resist oppression and stand for the rights of the downtrodden. <dfn class="term" data-def="Selfless service, a central Sikh practice of serving others without expectation of reward, seen as an act of worship.">Sewa</dfn> (selfless service) is a primary means by which Sikhs act for human dignity. British Sikh organisations such as Khalsa Aid provide humanitarian relief in disaster zones worldwide, embodying sewa in action.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Freedom of religious expression has been directly contested for Sikhs in Britain &mdash; for example, through legal cases around the right to wear the <em>kara</em> (steel bangle) and <em>turban</em> in schools and workplaces. These cases illustrate the tension between Article 18 UDHR rights and institutional rules, ultimately largely resolved in favour of religious accommodation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what does Qur'an 5:32 teach, and what is the parallel Jewish teaching from Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Qur&rsquo;an 5:32 teaches that saving one life is like saving all of humanity. The Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 teaches the same principle: whoever saves a single life, it is as if they have saved an entire world. Both ground human rights ethics in the infinite worth of every person.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Buddhism: Dhamma, Compassion, and Freedom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Buddhist teaching holds that all sentient beings are capable of suffering (<em>dukkha</em>) and therefore deserving of compassion (<em>karuna</em>). The Five Precepts and the Eightfold Path provide the ethical framework for respecting others&rsquo; lives and freedoms. <dfn class="term" data-def="The Buddhist teaching and ethical path; the natural law or truth by which all things are governed, in Pali spelling used in Theravada Buddhism.">Dhamma</dfn> in action may involve non-violent activism, meditation as a tool for reducing social harm, and supporting refugees and the dispossessed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Buddhist charities such as Karuna Trust work to address the needs of Dalits and marginalised communities in Britain and abroad. The tension between personal conviction and law has arisen for Buddhists around meditation centres, dietary practices, and in some countries the right to establish monastic communities. Buddhism, with its commitment to non-violence (<em>ahimsa</em>), condemns religious extremism and the use of violence in the name of faith.</p>

Religious believers may support restrictions on content they regard as blasphemous or deeply offensive, while also claiming the right to freely express their own faith. <dfn class="term" data-def="Extreme, often violent, forms of religious belief that reject pluralism and may involve the use of force to impose religious goals.">Religious extremism</dfn> &mdash; the use of violence or coercion in the name of religion &mdash; is condemned across all six traditions covered in this course. Leaders across Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Sikhism, and Buddhism have publicly stated that their traditions do not justify terrorism or violence against civilians.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">However, the line between expressing sincere religious conviction and inciting hatred can be contested. Cases where street preachers, religious broadcasters, or community leaders have been prosecuted illustrate the tension between Article 18 (religious freedom) and laws against hate speech. Most religious traditions teach a balance: the right to express faith freely, combined with the responsibility not to harm others in doing so.</p>

Humanists UK and Amnesty International both campaign for freedom of religion and belief, arguing that no one should face persecution for their beliefs or non-belief. Humanists are concerned that some religious teachings restrict the rights of others (e.g. women, LGBTQ+ people) and argue that human rights must be universal, not subject to religious exceptions.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">All six traditions ground human dignity in a relationship with the divine: Christians and Jews use <em>imago Dei</em> (Genesis 1:26&ndash;27); Islam uses <em>fitra</em> (innate goodness) and Qur&rsquo;an 5:32 (saving one life = saving all); Hinduism uses <em>atman</em>; Sikhism uses God&rsquo;s presence in all; Buddhism uses the capacity for suffering and enlightenment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Article 18 of the UDHR (1948) protects freedom of religion or belief. Conflicts between personal religious conviction and civil law arise in every tradition, and courts typically balance Article 18 rights against the rights of others.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">All six traditions condemn religious extremism. Humanists ground human rights in reason and the capacity for flourishing rather than religious authority, and support the UDHR as a universal secular framework.</li>
</ul>
