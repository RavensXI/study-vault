--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reading scatter relationships using the full correlation vocabulary, and why association is not the same as cause.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Correlation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When two variables tend to change together, we say there is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A statistical relationship between two variables, where changes in one tend to be associated with changes in the other.">correlation</dfn> between them. Correlation is about pattern, not proof. Statisticians have a precise vocabulary for describing correlations, and using the right term in the right context is one of the most reliable ways to pick up marks in interpretation questions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Direction: Positive, Negative and Zero</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A correlation where, as one variable increases, the other tends to increase too.">Positive correlation</dfn> means that as one variable increases, the other tends to increase too. Hours of revision and exam score typically show positive correlation: students who revise more tend to score higher.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A correlation where, as one variable increases, the other tends to decrease.">Negative correlation</dfn> means that as one variable increases, the other tends to decrease. Distance from the city centre and house price often show negative correlation: properties further from the centre tend to cost less (though this depends heavily on context).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Zero correlation (no correlation) means there is no consistent relationship between the two variables. The points on a scatter graph are scattered randomly with no discernible pattern.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the three directions of correlation. Then give one real-world example of each direction from a different context than the lesson examples.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three directions: positive correlation (both variables increase together), negative correlation (one increases as the other decreases), zero correlation (no consistent pattern). The word &ldquo;moderate&rdquo; for correlation strength is NOT used in this specification &mdash; use strong, weak, or no correlation only.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Strength: Strong, Weak and No Correlation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The <em>strength</em> of a correlation describes how closely the points cluster around a straight line. The specification defines precise thresholds for strength based on the value of the correlation coefficient \(r\):</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Strong correlation:</strong> \(|r| \geq 0.6\) (i.e. \(r \geq 0.6\) or \(r \leq -0.6\))</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Weak correlation:</strong> \(0.2 \leq |r| < 0.6\) (i.e. between 0.2 and 0.6 in magnitude)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>No correlation:</strong> \(|r| < 0.2\) (i.e. between \(-0.2\) and \(0.2\), not including these endpoints)</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n13">These thresholds are fixed by the specification. Do not use the word &ldquo;moderate&rdquo; &mdash; the specification explicitly excludes this term. Do not make up your own thresholds. Use strong, weak, or no correlation as described above.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Interpolation and Extrapolation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using a line of best fit to estimate a value within the range of the original data.">Interpolation</dfn> means using the line of best fit to estimate a value that falls within the range of the data you collected. If your data covers ages 20 to 60 and you estimate for age 35, that is interpolation. Interpolation is generally considered reliable when the correlation is strong.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using a line of best fit to estimate a value beyond the range of the original data.">Extrapolation</dfn> means extending the line beyond the range of your data to estimate a value. If your data covers ages 20 to 60 and you try to estimate for age 80, that is extrapolation. Extrapolation is much less reliable &mdash; the relationship seen within the data range may not continue outside it. The line of best fit might predict a negative house price if extended far enough, which is obviously impossible.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain the difference between interpolation and extrapolation, and say which one is less reliable and why. Write this without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Interpolation (within the data range) is generally more reliable. Extrapolation (beyond the data range) is risky because the relationship seen in the data may not continue outside it. The further you extrapolate, the less reliable the estimate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Causation vs Association</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">This is the most important idea in this lesson &mdash; and one of the most commonly examined. Finding a strong correlation between two variables does NOT mean that one causes the other. The specification explicitly states: correlation does not necessarily imply causation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Consider this: there is a strong positive correlation between ice cream sales and drowning incidents over time. Does ice cream cause drowning? No. Both variables share a common cause: hot weather. People buy more ice cream in hot weather, and more people swim in hot weather, leading to more drowning incidents. The ice cream and the drowning are both responses to the temperature &mdash; they did not cause each other.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">The hidden factor that explains both variables is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A third variable that is associated with both the explanatory and response variables and may account for an apparent correlation between them.">lurking variable</dfn> (also called a confounding variable). The specification says you should know that there are multiple factors that may interact. Any time you see a strong correlation, a good statistician asks: &ldquo;Is there a third variable that could explain this?&rdquo;</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n23">The correct language is: &ldquo;There is a strong positive correlation between X and Y. This suggests X and Y are associated, but this does not mean X causes Y. There could be a lurking variable that explains both.&rdquo; Using the word &ldquo;associated&rdquo; rather than &ldquo;causes&rdquo; shows the examiner you understand the distinction.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
  <h2 data-narration-id="n24">Interpreting Spearman's Rank and Pearson's PMCC (Higher)</h2>
  <p data-narration-id="n25">At Higher tier, you need to interpret calculated values of two correlation coefficients. <dfn class="term" data-def="A correlation coefficient that measures the strength and direction of the relationship between the rank orders of two variables; denoted r_s.">Spearman's rank correlation coefficient</dfn> measures the strength and direction of correlation between the rank orders of two variables &mdash; not the raw values. It works for any data that can be ranked, including ordinal data.</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="A correlation coefficient that measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables; denoted r.">Pearson's product moment correlation coefficient</dfn> (PMCC) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables. The key distinction: Spearman's measures correlation of rank orders, whereas Pearson's measures the linear relationship itself. For a straight-line relationship between continuous variables, Pearson's is more appropriate; for ranked or ordinal data, Spearman's is preferred.</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n27">The formula for Spearman's rank will always be given in the question &mdash; you do not need to memorise it. What you do need to do is interpret the resulting value using the same thresholds above: \(|r_s| \geq 0.6\) = strong, \(0.2 \leq |r_s| < 0.6\) = weak, \(|r_s| < 0.2\) = no correlation.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Correlation describes the direction (positive, negative, zero) and strength (strong, weak, none) of the relationship between two variables. Strength thresholds: strong if \(|r| \geq 0.6\), weak if \(0.2 \leq |r| < 0.6\), no correlation if \(|r| < 0.2\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Interpolation (estimating within the data range) is more reliable than extrapolation (estimating beyond it) &mdash; the relationship seen in the data may not continue outside the observed range.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Correlation does not imply causation &mdash; a strong association between two variables may be explained by a third lurking variable that influences both. Always consider alternative explanations before claiming a causal link.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">On &lsquo;Compare in Context&rsquo; and &lsquo;Describe&rsquo; questions about scatter graphs or correlation coefficients, always (1) state the direction (positive/negative/zero) and (2) state the strength using the correct threshold (strong if \(|r| \geq 0.6\), weak if \(0.2 \leq |r| < 0.6\)). Then interpret in context &mdash; what does this mean for the specific variables? The single most common error is using the word &ldquo;causes&rdquo; instead of &ldquo;associated with&rdquo; &mdash; this loses a mark on any question that rewards knowledge of the correlation/causation distinction.</p>
