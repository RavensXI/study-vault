--- DESCRIPTION ---
How abstraction, decomposition and algorithmic thinking turn big problems into solvable steps.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Every day, computers help us solve problems that would take a person hours &mdash; finding the quickest route home, sorting thousands of songs, or checking millions of records in seconds. But a computer cannot work anything out for itself. Before a problem can be handed to a machine, a human has to work out exactly how it will be solved. <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach to solving problems in a clear, logical way so that the solution could be carried out by a computer.">Computational thinking</dfn> is the name we give to this way of tackling problems, and it is the foundation of everything a programmer does.</p><p data-narration-id="n2">Computational thinking is built on three main principles: <strong>abstraction</strong>, <strong>decomposition</strong> and <strong>algorithmic thinking</strong>. Used together, they turn a large, messy, real-world problem into something precise enough for a computer to handle. This lesson looks at each principle in turn and then shows how they work together.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">Abstraction</h2><p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing or hiding unnecessary detail from a problem so that only the important information needed to solve it remains.">Abstraction</dfn> means removing or hiding the details that do not matter, so that you can focus on the information that does. Almost every problem contains detail that is not needed to solve it. Filtering that detail out makes the problem simpler and much easier to work with.</p><p data-narration-id="n5">A classic example is the London Underground map designed by Harry Beck. A real, geographically accurate map of the tunnels would be cluttered and confusing. Beck&rsquo;s design throws away the true distances and the exact positions of the stations, and keeps only what a passenger actually needs: the order of the stations, which line each one is on, and where the lines connect. As geography the map is &lsquo;wrong&rsquo;, yet it is far more useful for planning a journey &mdash; that is abstraction at work.</p><p data-narration-id="n6">Abstraction is everywhere in computing. When you drive a car, the steering wheel and pedals let you control the vehicle without knowing how the engine works &mdash; the mechanical detail is hidden from you. In a program, giving a value a sensible name such as <strong>score</strong> or <strong>playerName</strong> is abstraction too: the name lets you think about what the data means while hiding the binary and memory details underneath.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down in your own words what abstraction removes and what it keeps."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Abstraction removes <em>unnecessary</em> detail while keeping the key details needed to solve the problem. It does not mean throwing everything away &mdash; it means deciding what matters and what can be ignored.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n8">Decomposition</h2><p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Breaking a large, complex problem down into smaller, more manageable sub-problems that are easier to solve.">Decomposition</dfn> means breaking a big, complicated problem into smaller parts. Each smaller part is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A smaller, more manageable problem produced by breaking down a larger one.">sub-problem</dfn>, and each one can be solved on its own. Trying to solve one huge problem all at once is hard; solving a series of small, clear problems is far more manageable.</p><p data-narration-id="n10">Imagine you are asked to write a quiz app. As one giant task it feels overwhelming, and it is difficult to even know where to begin. Once it is decomposed, it becomes a list of smaller jobs that you can tackle one at a time.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Decomposing a quiz app</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n11">A quiz app might break down into these sub-problems:</p><ul><li data-narration-id="n12">store the questions along with their correct answers</li><li data-narration-id="n13">display one question at a time to the player</li><li data-narration-id="n14">collect the player&rsquo;s answer and check whether it is correct</li><li data-narration-id="n15">keep a running score and show the final result at the end</li></ul><p data-narration-id="n16">Each sub-problem is small enough to solve, test and fix on its own. Decomposition also lets a team split the work between them, and a solved sub-problem &mdash; such as keeping score &mdash; can often be reused in a completely different program.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n17">Algorithmic thinking</h2><p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Working out the logical, step-by-step sequence of instructions needed to solve a problem.">Algorithmic thinking</dfn> is working out the exact steps needed to solve a problem, in the right order. The result of this thinking is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A precise sequence of steps or set of rules that solves a problem or completes a task.">algorithm</dfn> &mdash; a clear set of instructions that reliably leads to the correct result.</p><p data-narration-id="n19">You already use algorithms in everyday life. A recipe is an algorithm: a list of steps, in order, that turns raw ingredients into a finished meal. Directions to a friend&rsquo;s house are an algorithm too. If the steps are muddled, or a step is missing, you end up with the wrong result.</p><p data-narration-id="n20">Algorithmic thinking means being precise. A computer follows instructions exactly, so a vague step like &lsquo;check the answers&rsquo; is not enough. You have to spell each step out: get the player&rsquo;s answer, compare it with the correct answer, and if they match, add one to the score. Spotting repeated patterns &mdash; such as doing that same check for every question &mdash; is an important part of thinking algorithmically.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the two things every algorithm must have to work correctly."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>An algorithm must have its steps in the correct <em>order</em> and each step must be <em>precise</em>. A computer does exactly what it is told, so an unclear or out-of-order step will produce the wrong output.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n22">Putting the three together</h2><p data-narration-id="n23">The three principles are at their most powerful when combined. Suppose you want a program that finds the highest score in a class. <strong>Decomposition</strong> breaks the task into reading the scores, comparing them, and showing the result. <strong>Abstraction</strong> lets you ignore who sat where or when the test was taken &mdash; only the scores themselves matter. <strong>Algorithmic thinking</strong> gives you the ordered steps: treat the first score as the highest so far, then check each remaining score and replace the highest whenever you find a bigger one.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Common mistakes to avoid</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n24">Watch out for these common misunderstandings:</p><ul><li data-narration-id="n25">Abstraction is <em>not</em> the same as decomposition &mdash; abstraction removes detail, while decomposition breaks a problem into parts.</li><li data-narration-id="n26">An algorithm is the finished set of steps; algorithmic thinking is the process of working those steps out.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Abstraction keeps the important detail &mdash; it does not simply mean &lsquo;making things shorter&rsquo;.</li></ul></div></div></div><p data-narration-id="n28">Master these three principles and you have the core toolkit of a computer scientist &mdash; a dependable way to take any problem, however large, and shape it into something a computer can solve.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n31">Abstraction removes unnecessary detail so you can focus on the information that matters.</li><li data-narration-id="n32">Decomposition breaks a large problem into smaller sub-problems that are easier to solve, test and share.</li><li data-narration-id="n33">Algorithmic thinking works out the precise, ordered steps that form an algorithm to solve the problem.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">Read the command word carefully. &lsquo;State&rsquo; or &lsquo;identify&rsquo; wants a short, direct answer, while &lsquo;describe&rsquo; and &lsquo;explain&rsquo; want you to develop the point &mdash; and &lsquo;explain&rsquo; usually means giving a reason or a consequence. A very common error is to define a term when the question asks you to <em>apply</em> it. If a question describes a particular program or system, use that context in your answer instead of writing a general definition.</p>
