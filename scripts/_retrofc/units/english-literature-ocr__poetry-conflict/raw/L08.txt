<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Technique: The Conflict Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your poetry paper will typically include a comparison question on your anthology poems. The question names one poem and asks you to compare a theme with one of your choice. Balance AO1 (response), AO2 (methods), and AO3 (context) across 4–5 paragraphs. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for exact mark weightings and timings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How should you split your time in the conflict poetry comparison?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Split your time roughly: a short planning stage (choose poem, identify 3–4 points, select quotations), the main writing stage (4–5 paragraphs), and a final checking stage (add any missing AO2 terminology or AO3 context). The plan saves time by preventing mid-essay confusion about what to write next.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Quick Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Identify the theme. Underline the key word in the question.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Choose your comparison poem. Write it down immediately.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Note 3–4 comparison points with a quotation from each poem and the method you will analyse.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Q:</strong> Compare how the reality of conflict is presented in ‘Bayonet Charge’ and one other poem.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Choice:</strong> The Charge of the Light Brigade — both about soldiers charging, different eras.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Point 1:</strong> Opening — Hughes: in medias res, chaos. Tennyson: dactylic metre, momentum. Context: post-war vs Victorian honour.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Point 2:</strong> Attitude to duty — Hughes: “luxuries” dropped. Tennyson: “do and die.” Shift in values.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Point 3:</strong> Imagery — Hughes: hare as objective correlative. Tennyson: “valley of Death” (biblical). Different emotional registers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Point 4:</strong> Form — Hughes: enjambment = chaos. Tennyson: regular stanzas = memorial. AO2 focus.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Quotation Bank: Conflict Collection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Memorise 2–3 short <dfn class="term" data-def="Short memorable phrases from a text that can be embedded in analysis.">quotations</dfn> per poem. Short quotes (3–5 words) are easier to remember and more effective when embedded in your sentence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: cover the quotation bank and write two quotations per poem for at least four conflict poems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Embed quotations within your sentence: “Hughes presents patriotic values as ‘luxuries’ discarded under fire” is stronger than “Hughes writes: ‘King, honour, human dignity, etcetera / Dropped like luxuries.’” The first keeps your analysis flowing; the second interrupts it with a block quote.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">Spend 5 minutes planning: choose poem, 3–4 comparison points, quotations. Stick to your plan.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Write 4–5 paragraphs using alternating comparison, hitting AO1, AO2, and AO3 in each.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Memorise 2–3 short quotations per poem and embed them in your sentences.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">In the final 5 minutes, re-read and check: Did I compare in every paragraph? Use quotations from both poems? Name methods? Embed context at least 3 times? If anything is missing, add a sentence. These additions can push you up a mark band.</p>
