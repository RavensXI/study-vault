How to compare conflict poems across centuries, linking attitudes to their historical contexts.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Comparing Across Time Periods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Conflict collection spans from Blake’s Romantic-era protest (1794) through Victorian war poetry (Tennyson, 1854) to contemporary responses (Duffy, Agard, Hughes). Comparing poems from different eras is essential for AO3 because it shows you understand <em>why</em> attitudes to conflict change over time. The key shift is from conflict as something honourable and necessary to conflict as something brutal, unjust, and psychologically devastating.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is the key shift in attitudes to conflict from the 19th century to the 20th century?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The key shift is from <strong>conflict as duty and honour</strong> (Tennyson’s memorial to brave soldiers) to <strong>conflict as trauma and injustice</strong> (Hughes’s individual terror, Duffy’s critique of indifference). The two World Wars shattered the myth of glorious combat. Post-colonial poets like Agard expanded “conflict” to include cultural and racial struggles.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Three Eras of Conflict Poetry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Romantic Era (Blake, 1794):</strong> Blake challenged <dfn class="term" data-def="The established order of society — Church, State, monarchy — and its power over ordinary people.">the establishment</dfn> through protest poetry. “London” attacks institutional corruption. Conflict here is social and political — the powerful against the powerless. Blake’s radical politics anticipated later movements for workers’ rights and democracy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Victorian Era (Tennyson, 1854):</strong> Tennyson wrote as Poet Laureate — an official voice. His duty was to honour the soldiers, even while acknowledging the military “blunder” that caused their deaths. The poem reflects a society that valued <dfn class="term" data-def="Unquestioning compliance with orders, especially in a military context.">obedience</dfn>, duty, and sacrifice. Criticising the war itself would have been almost unthinkable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Modern Era (Hughes, Duffy, Agard, Rumens):</strong> After two World Wars, Vietnam, and the Troubles, poets had no illusions about war’s glory. Hughes strips away patriotism to reveal animal terror. Duffy exposes comfortable indifference. Agard and Rumens expand “conflict” to include racial, cultural, and identity struggles. The modern poets question rather than memorialise.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Tennyson vs Hughes on the Soldier</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Tennyson’s soldiers are a collective (“the six hundred”) defined by obedience: “Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die.” The parallel structure reduces them to a single function, but the imperative “Honour the Light Brigade” elevates that function to something sacred. Tennyson writes within a Victorian culture that valued duty above individual feeling.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Hughes’s soldier is a nameless individual whose patriotic ideals — “King, honour, human dignity, etcetera” — are “dropped like luxuries” under fire. The dismissive “etcetera” is devastating: it reduces Tennyson’s sacred values to an afterthought. Hughes writes after 1945, when the myth of glorious sacrifice had been permanently destroyed by industrialised warfare.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Context link:</strong> The century between them transforms the soldier from celebrated hero to traumatised individual. This shift reflects a society that has witnessed the mechanised slaughter of two World Wars and can no longer accept the idea that dying for your country is automatically noble.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Embedding Context in Conflict Comparisons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When comparing conflict poems across time, use context to explain <em>why</em> the poets differ. Do not just list historical facts — connect them to specific textual evidence. Three effective strategies:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>1. Explain method through context:</strong> “Tennyson’s dactylic metre honours the soldiers through rhythm, reflecting his Laureate’s duty to memorialise; Hughes’s enjambment recreates chaos, reflecting a post-war distrust of orderly narratives about conflict.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>2. Explain attitude through context:</strong> “Blake’s anger at ‘charter’d streets’ reflects Romantic radicalism — a belief that institutions corrupt natural human freedom. Agard’s anger at ‘Dem tell me’ reflects post-colonial awareness — a recognition that education itself was a tool of Empire.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>3. Explain genre through context:</strong> “Tennyson writes a <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem honouring the dead, often with a public, ceremonial tone.">memorial poem</dfn> because his society expected poets to commemorate sacrifice. Duffy writes an accusatory poem because her society has the luxury of distant observation, not direct participation.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write: One embedded context sentence for each of the three strategies (method, attitude, genre).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Context in conflict poetry is most effective when it explains a <strong>shift in attitude</strong>: from duty to trauma, from honour to disillusionment, from national pride to individual suffering. Always link this shift to specific historical events (World Wars, post-colonial independence) and their effect on how poets understood conflict.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Contextual Traps to Avoid</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Avoid:</strong> “In the Victorian era, life was hard” (too vague). “Tennyson was born in 1809” (biography, not context). “All war poets hated war” (not true — Tennyson honours it). “Hughes fought in World War I” (he did not — he imagines a WWI soldier). Always check your facts and make context <em>specific</em> to the poem.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The Conflict collection spans Romantic, Victorian, and Modern eras — each brings different attitudes shaped by different historical experiences of conflict.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The key shift is from conflict as duty/honour (Tennyson) to conflict as trauma/injustice (Hughes, Duffy), expanded by post-colonial poets to include cultural and racial conflict (Agard, Rumens).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Embed context by explaining <em>why</em> poets differ: link their methods, attitudes, and genres to specific historical circumstances.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">The strongest contextual comparisons explain a <strong>shift</strong>: “While Tennyson, writing in 1854, could still present military obedience as noble, Hughes, writing after two World Wars, reduces patriotic values to ‘luxuries’ discarded under fire. This shift reflects a century of disillusionment with the myth of glorious sacrifice.” This kind of comparative context earns both AO1 and AO3 marks simultaneously.</p>
