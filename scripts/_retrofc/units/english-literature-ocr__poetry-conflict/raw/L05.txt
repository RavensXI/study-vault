--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Form, Structure & Language in Conflict Poetry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">AO2 requires you to analyse <em>how</em> poets present conflict, not just what they say about it. The Conflict collection uses an extraordinary range of methods: <dfn class="term" data-def="The stressed and unstressed syllable pattern in a line of poetry.">metre</dfn> that mimics galloping horses or marching feet, sound devices that recreate the noise of battle, and structural choices that mirror chaos or rigid military order.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: What three areas of method does AO2 assess, with one conflict poem example for each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO2 assesses: <strong>Form</strong> (e.g. Tennyson’s dactylic metre mimicking cavalry), <strong>Structure</strong> (e.g. Hughes’s in medias res plunging the reader into action, Agard’s dual-voice layout), and <strong>Language</strong> (e.g. Blake’s “mind-forg’d manacles” metaphor, Duffy’s religious simile for the darkroom).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Form: Shape & Sound</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Form in conflict poetry often <em>enacts</em> its subject. Tennyson’s dactylic metre (DAH-duh-duh) in “The Charge of the Light Brigade” sounds like galloping hooves. By contrast, Hughes’s “Bayonet Charge” uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Lines that run on into the next without punctuation, creating momentum.">enjambment</dfn> and irregular line lengths to create the breathless, stumbling movement of a soldier running across no man’s land.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Blake’s “A Poison Tree” uses AABB <dfn class="term" data-def="A pair of lines that rhyme with each other.">couplets</dfn> and simple diction to create a nursery-rhyme quality. This innocent form makes the murderous content more disturbing. Agard’s “Half-caste” deliberately avoids standard punctuation and uses phonetic spelling, making the form itself an act of resistance against English literary conventions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Refrain</strong> (Tennyson): Repeated “valley of Death” and “the six hundred” create a memorial, ritual quality.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Volta</strong> (Duffy): The shift in “War Photographer” from the darkroom’s solemnity to the readers’ indifference creates a damning structural contrast.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Language: Imagery, Sound & Diction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Conflict poets use <dfn class="term" data-def="Words that imitate the sounds they describe.">onomatopoeia</dfn>, harsh consonants, and violent imagery to recreate the sensory experience of conflict. Hughes’s “bullets smacking the belly out of the air” uses the plosive “b” sounds and violent verb “smacking” to make the reader <em>hear</em> gunfire. Blake’s “blood down palace walls” uses visceral imagery to link the State directly to soldiers’ deaths.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Diction — the <em>level</em> of language — also conveys meaning. Compare Tennyson’s elevated, formal register (“Honour the charge they made!”) with Agard’s deliberately informal, oral register (“Explain yuself”). The formality reflects Tennyson’s role as Poet Laureate; Agard’s informality is a rejection of the literary establishment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write one example each of onomatopoeia, violent imagery, and contrasting diction from three different conflict poems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The strongest AO2 analysis connects <strong>sound to meaning</strong>: plosive consonants recreate violence, sibilance suggests stealth or suffering, dactylic metre mimics movement. Never just name a device — explain what it makes the reader <em>experience</em> and why the poet chose it.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Form in conflict poetry often enacts its subject: dactylic metre for cavalry, enjambment for chaos, couplets for deceptive simplicity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Structural features (in medias res, dual voices, refrain, volta) control the reader’s experience and should be analysed as deliberate choices.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Language analysis must connect sound to meaning — explain what the reader hears, sees, or feels, not just name the device.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Never write “The poet uses alliteration to make the poem interesting.” Instead, specify <em>which</em> sounds, quote them, and explain the <em>effect</em>: “Hughes’s plosive alliteration in ‘bullets smacking the belly’ forces the reader to hear the percussive violence of gunfire, making the experience visceral rather than abstract.”</p>
