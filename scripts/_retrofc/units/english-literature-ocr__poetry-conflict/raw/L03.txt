<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Inner Conflict & Anger</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not all conflict in the OCR anthology takes place on a battlefield. Some of the most powerful poems explore <dfn class="term" data-def="A struggle that takes place within a person’s mind — between opposing emotions, desires, or beliefs.">inner conflict</dfn> — the battles fought inside the mind. These poems present anger, frustration, moral dilemma, and the struggle between what we feel and what we are expected to feel. Studying them alongside war poems reveals that “conflict” is a far broader concept than military combat.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three forms does inner conflict take in the anthology?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Inner conflict in the anthology takes three main forms: <strong>moral conflict</strong> (struggling with right and wrong), <strong>emotional conflict</strong> (anger, grief, or frustration that cannot be expressed), and <strong>identity conflict</strong> (tension between who you are and who society expects you to be). These internal battles can be as destructive as physical warfare.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">A Poison Tree — William Blake (1794)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Blake’s deceptively simple poem uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="A short story with a moral lesson, often using symbolic characters or objects.">allegory</dfn> of a growing tree to show how suppressed anger becomes deadly. The opening stanza establishes the contrast: “I was angry with my friend: / I told my wrath, my wrath did end. / I was angry with my foe: / I told it not, my wrath did grow.” Expressed anger dissolves; suppressed anger grows.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The tree is “watered” with fears and “sunned” with smiles — Blake inverts natural imagery to show how <dfn class="term" data-def="The outward concealment of true feelings, often through politeness or pretence.">deceit</dfn> nourishes resentment. The “apple bright” that grows is a clear allusion to the Garden of Eden: suppressed anger becomes temptation, and the foe who eats it is found “outstretched beneath the tree” — dead. The final image is chilling because the speaker seems <em>glad</em>: “In the morning glad I see.”</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Blake personifies anger as a living thing that needs nurturing. “Watered” with tears and “sunned” with “soft deceitful wiles” uses the semantic field of gardening — but what grows is poison, not beauty. The regular AABB rhyme and simple diction give the poem a nursery-rhyme quality that makes the dark content more disturbing — like a children’s story with a murderous ending.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Half-caste — John Agard (1996)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Agard’s poem is an angry, humorous response to the offensive term “half-caste.” The speaker dismantles the word by applying its logic to art: “Explain yuself / wha yu mean / when yu say half-caste / yu mean when Picasso / mix red an green / is a half-caste canvas?” If mixing colours in painting is genius, why is mixing races considered incomplete? The <dfn class="term" data-def="Writing that represents the sounds of spoken dialect rather than standard spelling.">phonetic spelling</dfn> (“yu,” “dem,” “wha”) asserts the speaker’s Caribbean identity — refusing to adopt “proper” English is itself an act of resistance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The poem builds through <dfn class="term" data-def="Repetition of words or phrases for emphasis and rhythmic effect.">repetition</dfn> of “Explain yuself” — a challenge that demands the listener justify their prejudice. The ending shifts from humour to power: “But yu must come back tomorrow / wid de whole of yu eye / an de whole of yu ear / an de whole of yu mind.” The implication is devastating — anyone who uses “half-caste” is the one who is incomplete, seeing with half an eye and thinking with half a mind.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: How does Agard use the word 'half' against those who use the term 'half-caste'?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Agard turns the insult back on the user: if the speaker is “half-caste,” then they can only offer “half a hand,” “half a dream,” and “half a shadow.” But the final demand for “de whole of yu mind” implies that it is the <em>prejudiced person</em> who is incomplete — they see the world with only half their understanding.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Comparing Inner Conflict Poems</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Blake vs Agard: Suppressed vs Expressed Anger</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Similarity:</strong> Both poems explore anger as a response to injustice. Both use deceptively simple language and repetitive structures to deliver serious messages.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Difference:</strong> Blake’s anger is suppressed and becomes deadly — the poem warns against hiding true feelings. Agard’s anger is openly expressed as a tool of resistance and empowerment — speaking out dismantles prejudice. Blake’s speaker is complicit in the harm; Agard’s is fighting it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Context:</strong> Blake wrote during the Romantic period, challenging the suppression of emotion by polite society. Agard writes as a British-Caribbean poet challenging racial prejudice in 1990s Britain. Both use poetry as a weapon against social hypocrisy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Blake’s “A Poison Tree” uses garden allegory and biblical allusion to warn that suppressed anger becomes deadly — the poem’s nursery-rhyme form makes the dark content more disturbing.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Agard’s “Half-caste” uses phonetic spelling, repetition, and absurd logic to dismantle racial prejudice, turning the insult “half” back on the user.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Inner conflict poems broaden the definition of “conflict” beyond warfare to include emotional suppression, identity struggles, and social injustice.</li></ul>

When comparing, always identify <em>what</em> the conflict is, <em>how</em> the poet presents it (methods), and <em>why</em> it matters in context (AO3).</p>
