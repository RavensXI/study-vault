<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Social Conflict & Identity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Conflict collection includes poems that explore social and political struggles: the conflict between the powerful and the powerless, between cultural identity and forced conformity, and between individual freedom and systemic oppression. These poems broaden “conflict” beyond warfare to include the daily battles of people fighting for recognition, equality, and justice.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three types of social conflict appear in the anthology?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Social conflict poems in the anthology explore three types: <strong>class conflict</strong> (inequality between rich and poor, powerful and powerless), <strong>racial and cultural conflict</strong> (the struggle to assert identity against prejudice), and <strong>political conflict</strong> (resistance to tyranny, oppression, or unjust authority).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">London — William Blake (1794)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Blake’s “London” presents a devastating portrait of social oppression in the late 18th-century city. The speaker walks through “charter’d streets” near the “charter’d Thames” — the repetition of <dfn class="term" data-def="Officially mapped, controlled, or restricted by legal charter.">charter’d</dfn> suggests that even the natural river has been claimed and controlled by the powerful. In every face the speaker sees “marks of weakness, marks of woe” — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive lines or clauses.">anaphora</dfn> of “marks” brands suffering onto the city’s people like a physical scar.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The poem builds through a catalogue of victims: the chimney sweeper’s “cry” indicts the Church (“every blackening church appalls”), the soldier’s sigh “runs in blood down palace walls,” and the “youthful Harlot’s curse” blights the “Marriage hearse.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="Placing two contrasting ideas next to each other for dramatic effect.">juxtaposition</dfn> of “Marriage” and “hearse” (a funeral carriage) suggests that even love and family are corrupted by a society built on exploitation.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Quotation: “mind-forg’d manacles”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">“<strong>In every cry of every Man, / In every Infant’s cry of fear, / I hear / … the mind-forg’d manacles I hear.</strong>” The metaphor “mind-forg’d manacles” is Blake’s most famous image. “Manacles” are handcuffs — but these are “mind-forg’d,” meaning the chains are mental, not physical. Blake argues that oppression is maintained not just by law and force, but by people accepting their own subjugation. The word “forg’d” carries a double meaning: both “made” (as a blacksmith forges metal) and “faked” (forged like a counterfeit document). The chains are manufactured, artificial — they could be broken.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Checking Out Me History — John Agard (1996)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Agard’s poem is a protest against the Eurocentric curriculum that taught him about Dick Whittington and Florence Nightingale but not about Toussaint L’Ouverture (who led the Haitian Revolution) or Nanny de Maroon (who resisted British slavery in Jamaica). The poem alternates between two voices: the <strong>imposed history</strong> (“Dem tell me / Dem tell me / Wha dem want to tell me”) in standard layout, and the <strong>reclaimed history</strong> in italicised, lyrical stanzas celebrating Black heroes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The refrain “Dem tell me” exposes education as a form of control — “dem” (they) decided what history was worth knowing. The poem’s climax is defiant: “But now I checking out me own history / I carving out me identity.” The active verbs “checking out” and “carving” present the speaker taking control of their own narrative. “Carving” suggests both sculpture (creating something permanent) and effort (it requires force to carve).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: What three Black historical figures does Agard celebrate, and what did each achieve?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Agard celebrates: <strong>Toussaint L’Ouverture</strong> (led the Haitian Revolution, the first successful slave revolt), <strong>Nanny de Maroon</strong> (escaped slave who led guerrilla resistance against the British in Jamaica), and <strong>Mary Seacole</strong> (Jamaican nurse who cared for soldiers in the Crimean War but was overlooked in favour of Florence Nightingale). Each represents history that the British curriculum erased.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Comparing Social Conflict Poems</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Blake vs Agard: Systems of Control</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Similarity:</strong> Both poets expose how powerful institutions (Church, State, education) control ordinary people. Both use repetition as a structural device to hammer home systemic oppression. Both argue that awareness is the first step to freedom.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Difference:</strong> Blake’s poem is despairing — the “mind-forg’d manacles” suggest people are trapped by their own acceptance. Agard’s is empowering — “I carving out me identity” shows the speaker breaking free. Blake observes oppression; Agard actively resists it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Context:</strong> Blake wrote during the Industrial Revolution when workers had almost no rights. Agard writes as a post-colonial poet whose education was shaped by the British Empire. Both challenge the systems that keep people ignorant of their own oppression.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Blake’s “London” uses “charter’d,” “mind-forg’d manacles,” and the “Marriage hearse” to present a city where every institution — Church, State, family — perpetuates suffering.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Agard’s “Checking Out Me History” uses dual voices, phonetic spelling, and active verbs to reclaim erased Black history and assert identity against a Eurocentric curriculum.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Both poems expose systemic control, but Blake is despairing while Agard is defiantly empowered.</li></ul>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Social conflict poems work brilliantly alongside war poems for comparison — both explore power, oppression, and resistance. When comparing, focus on who holds power, how it is maintained, and whether the poem ends with hope or despair. This “power dynamic” analysis impresses examiners because it shows you understand the poems’ political messages (AO1 + AO3).</p>
