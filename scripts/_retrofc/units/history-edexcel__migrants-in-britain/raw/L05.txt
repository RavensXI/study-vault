--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Kind of Migrant Story</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between c1500 and c1700, England changed in ways that reshaped who came and why. The country became a Protestant nation after Henry VIII&rsquo;s break with Rome (1534), the cloth industry pulled in skilled labour, and global trading companies and <dfn class="term" data-def="Privately funded armed shipping that attacked enemy vessels with royal permission, common in Elizabethan England.">privateering</dfn> tied English ports to Africa, the Americas and Asia. New types of migrant arrived: Protestant <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who has been forced to leave their country to escape war, persecution or natural disaster.">religious refugees</dfn> from Catholic Europe, and small but real numbers of Africans &mdash; some free, some unfree &mdash; in Tudor and Stuart parishes. The continuity matters too: the legal label of &lsquo;alien&rsquo; carried over from medieval England, and most migrants still arrived through the south-east ports.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three early modern migrant groups, with one defining feature for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three early modern migrant groups dominate this period: Flemish and Walloon Protestant refugees from the 1560s, Huguenots fleeing France after 1685, and Africans &mdash; including the trumpeter John Blanke at the Tudor court &mdash; documented in parish, court and household records.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Flemish and Walloon Refugees: Sandwich and Canterbury</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In the 1560s, Protestant Flemings and Walloons fleeing Spanish Catholic persecution in the Low Countries became the first wave of religious refugees in Protestant England. Elizabeth I and the Privy Council encouraged the arrivals because they brought valuable cloth-making skills the English economy needed. In 1561, Sandwich (Kent) was authorised to take in twenty-five master weavers and their families. Canterbury followed soon after. By the 1580s, Sandwich&rsquo;s migrant population numbered several thousand &mdash; perhaps a third of the town. The arrivals founded their own <dfn class="term" data-def="Self-governing Protestant churches established by Flemish, Walloon and other refugees in 16th- and 17th-century England, with their own ministers and services in their own languages.">stranger churches</dfn>, ran their own poor relief, and used the chancel of St Peter&rsquo;s in Sandwich for Walloon-language services.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Their economic impact was disproportionate to their numbers. Flemish weavers introduced the &lsquo;new draperies&rsquo; &mdash; lighter, brighter, more fashionable cloths &mdash; which Sandwich and Canterbury exported across northern Europe. They drained marshland for market gardens around Sandwich, growing celery, carrots and cauliflowers on a commercial scale for the first time in England. Local guilds sometimes resented the competition, but corporation records show town authorities defending the strangers&rsquo; right to trade because of the customs revenue and tax they generated. The Flemish and Walloon experience set a pattern: religious refugees could be welcomed when their skills met an English economic need.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Huguenots: Spitalfields after 1685</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A century later, France produced its own wave of Protestant refugees. In 1685, Louis XIV revoked the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1598 royal decree that had granted French Protestants (Huguenots) limited religious freedom; revoked by Louis XIV in 1685.">Edict of Nantes</dfn>, ending the limited religious tolerance French Protestants had enjoyed since 1598. Around 50,000 Huguenots came to England, the largest single religious migration of the period. Many settled in Spitalfields on the eastern edge of London, where the silk-weaving trade was already established. Charles II encouraged the arrivals through royal proclamations promising <dfn class="term" data-def="A grant by the English crown that gave a foreign-born person partial legal rights, falling short of full naturalisation but easing trade restrictions.">denization</dfn> &mdash; a partial legal status that let migrants own property and trade without becoming full subjects. Public collections in parish churches raised funds for the most destitute new arrivals.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">In Spitalfields, Huguenot weavers transformed silk production. They built tall houses with broad upper-floor windows to let in the light needed to work the patterned brocades and damasks they specialised in. By 1700, around 25,000 Huguenots lived in Spitalfields and its surrounding parishes. Beyond silk, Huguenot migrants reshaped finance (founding members of the Bank of England in 1694 included Huguenot families), the army (officers serving William III), and clock-making, gunsmithing and jewellery in London&rsquo;s West End. Within two generations the community had largely assimilated, leaving stranger churches, family names and skilled trades as their lasting mark.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Africans in Tudor and Stuart Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Far smaller in number, but historically important, was the African presence in Tudor and Stuart Britain. The historian Miranda Kaufmann&rsquo;s work on parish registers and household accounts has identified more than 360 Africans in England between 1500 and 1640, recorded as servants, court entertainers, divers, sailors and household members. They include <dfn class="term" data-def="African trumpeter at the courts of Henry VII and Henry VIII; appears in royal payment rolls between 1507 and 1512 and in the Westminster Tournament Roll of 1511.">John Blanke</dfn>, a royal trumpeter at the courts of Henry VII and Henry VIII, who appears in payment rolls and the 1511 Westminster Tournament Roll. The presence of these Africans in 1500&ndash;1640 dismantles the older idea that Black British history begins with Windrush in 1948.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Other named individuals include Diego, an African mariner who sailed with Francis Drake during the 1577&ndash;80 circumnavigation, and Cattelena of Almondsbury, an independent Black woman in Bristol who in 1625 owned her own cow and small property &mdash; a rare snapshot of an ordinary working life. Status was uneven: some Africans were enslaved, but courts in this period (notably the 1569 Cartwright case) accepted the principle that England had no laws sanctioning slavery on its own soil. The picture was contradictory &mdash; English merchants were beginning to profit from the Atlantic slave trade abroad, while parish registers in England were treating individual Africans as full members of their communities.</p>

  <p>In 1685, Louis XIV&rsquo;s revocation of the Edict of Nantes triggered the largest single religious migration to England before the 20th century &mdash; around 50,000 Huguenots, most settling in Spitalfields and reshaping silk-weaving, finance and skilled trades.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Flemish and Walloon Protestant refugees arrived from the 1560s, especially in Sandwich and Canterbury, where they transformed cloth-making (the &lsquo;new draperies&rsquo;) and market gardening; they were the first major wave of religious refugees in England.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Around 50,000 Huguenots came after Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, settling especially in Spitalfields where they remade silk-weaving and contributed to the Bank of England in 1694.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Africans were present in Tudor and Stuart England in numbers larger than once believed &mdash; over 360 named individuals, including John Blanke and Cattelena of Almondsbury &mdash; documented in parish, court and household records.</li>
</ul>
