--- DESCRIPTION ---
Pogroms drive Eastern European Jews to Whitechapel; sweatshops, synagogues and the 1905 Aliens Act.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why the East End?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between 1881 and 1914, around 150,000 Eastern European Jewish migrants settled in Britain, the great majority in the East End of London. They arrived fleeing waves of <dfn class="term" data-def="An organised, often state-tolerated mob attack on a Jewish community, especially in the Russian Empire from 1881 onwards.">pogroms</dfn> &mdash; organised violent attacks on Jewish communities in the Russian Empire that began after the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in March 1881. New Russian laws (the &lsquo;May Laws&rsquo; of 1882) restricted where Jews could live, study and trade. The East End offered three things at once: cheap lodging in Whitechapel, Spitalfields and Stepney; a thriving garment trade that needed cheap labour; and an existing small Anglo-Jewish community whose synagogues and welfare societies could absorb new arrivals. Migration in this period was driven by religious push and economic pull working together.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three reasons Eastern European Jewish migrants settled in the East End in this period.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Around 150,000 Eastern European Jewish migrants came to Britain between 1881 and 1914, settling overwhelmingly in the East End. They were driven by Russian pogroms (push), pulled by the East End garment trade (economic), and supported by an existing Anglo-Jewish community whose synagogues and welfare societies absorbed the arrivals.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Sweatshops and the Garment Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most migrants entered the <dfn class="term" data-def="A small workshop, often in cramped tenement rooms, where workers laboured for low wages over long hours; a defining feature of the East End garment trade in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.">sweated trades</dfn> &mdash; tailoring, cap-making, boot- and shoe-making, cabinet-making &mdash; that flourished in the East End. By 1900 around 60% of London tailoring was done in the East End, much of it by Jewish migrants. The work was piece-rate, performed in small workshops in tenement back rooms, where men, women and children worked 12-hour days for very low wages. Trade unions including the Hebrew Cabinet Makers&rsquo; Association and the Jewish Tailors&rsquo; Union organised through the 1880s and 1890s, demanding limits on hours and minimum rates. The 1889 London Dock Strike and the wider East End labour movement drew Jewish workers into mainstream British trade unionism.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Beyond the workshops, Jewish migrants reshaped the East End&rsquo;s street life. Yiddish theatres opened in Whitechapel; the <dfn class="term" data-def="Jewish newspaper published in London from 1889, written in Yiddish; one of several Yiddish-language papers serving the East End community.">Yiddish press</dfn> ran multiple daily papers including the <em>Yidisher Ekspres</em>; kosher butchers, bakers and synagogues filled streets that had been Huguenot two centuries earlier (the Brick Lane synagogue had begun life as a Huguenot chapel before becoming a Methodist chapel and then, from 1898, the Spitalfields Great Synagogue). Mutual-aid societies, hevra (small synagogues), and the Jewish Board of Guardians provided welfare. The community also produced a generation of socialist and trade-union activists, including the journalist Morris Winchevsky, who became central voices in early British labour politics.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Role of the Media</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">From the 1880s, sections of the British press treated Jewish migration as a national problem. Newspapers like the <em>Daily Mail</em> (founded 1896) and the older <em>Daily News</em> ran scare stories about &lsquo;destitute aliens&rsquo; flooding the East End and undermining native wages. Cartoons depicted Jewish migrants in stereotyped, often antisemitic terms. A Royal Commission on Alien Immigration (1902&ndash;03) investigated, hearing evidence from Jewish community leaders, social workers and trade unionists alongside hostile politicians. The press response was often more inflammatory than the Commission&rsquo;s findings. The role of the popular press in shaping public attitudes &mdash; well before radio or television &mdash; was a defining feature of late-Victorian and Edwardian migration debates.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A common assumption is that hostile tabloid coverage of migrants began in the late 20th century. The pattern is much older. The 1880s and 1890s saw the British press routinely framing Eastern European Jewish migrants as a public-health and economic threat. Headlines about &lsquo;the alien invasion&rsquo; appeared two decades before the Aliens Act. The new mass-circulation press (the <em>Daily Mail</em> from 1896) accelerated the pattern, but the template was set earlier. Strong answers identify the press as a third factor (alongside push and pull) shaping the Jewish migrant experience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Aliens Act 1905</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In 1902 the <dfn class="term" data-def="Anti-immigration pressure group founded in 1901 in the East End by Major William Evans-Gordon, MP for Stepney; lobbied for restrictions on poor Jewish migration that became the 1905 Aliens Act.">British Brothers&rsquo; League</dfn> was founded by <dfn class="term" data-def="Conservative MP for Stepney 1900&ndash;1907 and founder of the British Brothers&rsquo; League; the most prominent parliamentary advocate for the 1905 Aliens Act.">Major William Evans-Gordon</dfn>, the Conservative MP for Stepney. Its rallies in the East End demanded an end to Jewish immigration. The campaign succeeded: the Conservative government&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="Britain&rsquo;s first peacetime immigration control law, passed under Prime Minister Arthur Balfour in 1905; aimed at restricting poor Jewish migrants from Eastern Europe while exempting wealthy migrants and political refugees.">Aliens Act 1905</dfn> &mdash; Britain&rsquo;s first peacetime immigration control &mdash; gave immigration officers power to refuse entry to migrants who could not support themselves, who were judged to be of bad character, or who had a criminal record. It exempted political refugees in principle, and wealthy migrants who could pay their passage in cabin class were not subject to inspection at all. The Act was administered by the new Aliens Inspectorate at the major ports.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">The 1905 Act did not stop Jewish migration outright. Numbers continued to rise until the First World War, partly because the Liberal government from 1906 administered the Act loosely &mdash; Home Secretary Herbert Gladstone instructed inspectors to give migrants the benefit of the doubt. But the Act marked a clear break from the 19th-century tradition of free entry. It established the principle that Britain could close its doors at will. Later acts &mdash; the 1914 Aliens Restriction Act, the 1919 Aliens Restriction Act, the 1962 and 1968 Commonwealth Immigrants Acts &mdash; built on the 1905 framework.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: the Aliens Act 1905 was a general immigration control</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A frequent error is to describe the 1905 Aliens Act as a general cap on foreign immigration. It was nothing of the kind. The Act&rsquo;s exemptions for wealthy migrants and (in principle) political refugees made clear that the target was poor Jewish migrants from Eastern Europe specifically. Migrants travelling cabin class were not even inspected. The British Brothers&rsquo; League&rsquo;s rallies in Stepney made the antisemitic intent visible at the time. Strong answers identify the Act as the first piece of British immigration law shaped explicitly by hostility to one specific group.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Impact and Legacy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1914 the East End had been remade by Jewish migration. Synagogues, Yiddish theatres, kosher food shops, and trade-union halls clustered along Whitechapel Road, Brick Lane and Commercial Street. The community produced doctors, lawyers, writers and politicians within a generation. Mutual-aid networks (the Jewish Board of Guardians, hevra synagogues, friendly societies) modelled welfare provision that fed into wider British social policy. At the same time, the 1905 Aliens Act and the press hostility that produced it set a template for 20th-century immigration politics. Both legacies &mdash; the cultural transformation of east London and the legal-political restriction of free entry &mdash; can be traced back to the same three decades.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the year 1905 is a turning point in the British migration story.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1905 Aliens Act was Britain&rsquo;s first peacetime immigration control. It targeted poor Eastern European Jewish migrants specifically, exempted wealthy migrants entirely, and established the principle &mdash; new to Britain &mdash; that the state could refuse entry to migrants at will.</p>
</div>

</ul>
