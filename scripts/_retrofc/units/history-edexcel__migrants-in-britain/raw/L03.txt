--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jorvik as a trading hub: Coppergate archaeology, Anglo-Scandinavian culture and the Viking economy of the north.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Eboracum to Jorvik</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the Heathen Army captured York in 866, the city was already centuries old. The Romans had founded <em>Eboracum</em> as a legionary fortress in 71 CE; under the Anglo-Saxons it became <em>Eoforwic</em>, a centre of Northumbrian kingship and Christian learning. The Vikings did not create York from nothing. What they did was rename it <dfn class="term" data-def="The Viking name for York between 866 and 954, used for the city as the capital of the Viking kingdom of York.">Jorvik</dfn>, expand it dramatically, and turn it into one of the largest trading towns in northern Europe. Studying York is the spec&rsquo;s case study for the medieval period &mdash; a chance to see the four key factors (institutions, religion, economic influences, attitudes) all working at once on a single named place.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three names of York under Romans, Anglo-Saxons and Vikings, with one detail about each phase.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>York was Roman <em>Eboracum</em>, then Anglo-Saxon <em>Eoforwic</em>, before becoming Viking <em>Jorvik</em> after 866. Vikings inherited and expanded an existing city; they did not invent it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">A Capital City and a Royal Court</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From 866 Jorvik became the capital of the Viking kingdom of York &mdash; a real political institution with its own kings, coinage and courts. The most famous king was <dfn class="term" data-def="Norwegian-born king of York, ruled 947&ndash;948 and 952&ndash;954; killed at the Battle of Stainmore in 954, ending Viking rule of York.">Eric Bloodaxe</dfn>, who reigned from 947&ndash;948 and again from 952&ndash;954. His expulsion and death at Stainmore in 954 ended independent Viking kingship in England, but Norse culture and population remained dominant in the city for generations. Jorvik issued silver pennies stamped with the names of its kings, including Cnut and the Anglo-Scandinavian moneyer Hildulf &mdash; physical evidence that institutions of government were running smoothly in the hands of Viking-descended rulers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Coppergate and the Archaeology of Daily Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Most of what we know about ordinary life in Jorvik comes from the <dfn class="term" data-def="A street in central York whose 1976&ndash;81 excavation uncovered remarkable preservation of Viking-era timber buildings, leather, textiles and craft workshops.">Coppergate</dfn> excavation, carried out by the York Archaeological Trust between 1976 and 1981. Waterlogged conditions had preserved timber buildings, leather shoes, textiles, hair combs and even fragments of food in extraordinary detail. The street name itself comes from Old Norse <em>kopari-gata</em> &mdash; &lsquo;the street of the cup-makers&rsquo;. Coppergate showed Jorvik as a working town of densely packed wooden houses, narrow alleys, workshops, latrines and back yards full of crafts.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Excavated workshops produced amber jewellery, glass beads, leather shoes, ironwork and bone combs &mdash; the everyday output of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The skilled artisans whose workshops on Coppergate produced jewellery, leather goods, ironwork, combs and glassware in Viking York.">Coppergate craftsmen</dfn>. Coin hoards and Arabic dirhams found in the city show York&rsquo;s reach into a wider trading world. The economic factor pull is overwhelming here: Jorvik was a magnet because work and wealth were available.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A frequent error is to treat Vikings as the founders of York. They were not. Roman <em>Eboracum</em> had been a legionary capital for centuries, and Anglo-Saxon <em>Eoforwic</em> was already an important Northumbrian centre with an archbishop and a school of learning. The Viking transformation came in scale and character: street layout was reshaped, the population grew rapidly, and the city was wired into a North Sea trading network. Strong answers credit the existing Roman and Anglo-Saxon foundations and then explain what Vikings added on top.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">A Trading Hub for Northern Europe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jorvik&rsquo;s role in <dfn class="term" data-def="Long-distance trading systems linking towns and peoples through exchange of goods, money and people.">international trade networks</dfn> made it one of the wealthiest towns in early medieval Britain. Norse merchants linked York to Dublin, Bergen, Birka in Sweden and the Russian rivers that ran down to the Black Sea and the Caliphate of Baghdad. Excavations have turned up silk fragments from the eastern Mediterranean, cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean, amber from the Baltic and silver dirhams minted under Abbasid caliphs. The York merchants exported wool, cloth, leather and fine craftwork in return.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">By the time of Domesday Book in 1086, York had a population estimated at around 9,000 people &mdash; second only to London in England. This was the result of a long process of <dfn class="term" data-def="Migration into a town or city, especially the growth of a settled urban population from rural and overseas migrants.">urban migration</dfn> that had begun under the Anglo-Saxons and accelerated under the Vikings. Population growth fed economic growth, which in turn pulled in more migrants &mdash; a self-reinforcing cycle visible across the period.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">It is easy to picture Viking York as a self-contained northern English town. The archaeology tells a different story. Silver dirhams minted in the Abbasid Caliphate of Baghdad were carried up the Russian river system, through the Baltic, and into York merchants&rsquo; purses. Cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean ended up in Coppergate workshops. Silk fragments from the eastern Mediterranean were stitched into local clothing. Strong answers identify Jorvik as a node in a transcontinental trading network, not just a regional capital &mdash; this is what makes the economic factor so striking in the Viking period.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">A Mixed Anglo-Scandinavian Culture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By the eleventh century, Viking and Anglo-Saxon residents were difficult to tell apart in everyday life. Historians describe Jorvik&rsquo;s shared way of life as <dfn class="term" data-def="The mixed Anglo-Saxon and Scandinavian culture that developed in northern and eastern England in the tenth and eleventh centuries.">Anglo-Scandinavian</dfn>. Norse personal names appear alongside English names in church records; Christian crosses use Norse decorative styles such as the &lsquo;ring chain&rsquo; pattern; the city&rsquo;s archbishops, including Wulfstan II in the early eleventh century, served Norse-descended communities. Religious institutions were not destroyed by Viking arrival &mdash; they adapted, and the Church became one of the routes by which Norse migrants were absorbed into wider English society.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three things the Coppergate excavation tells us about everyday life in Jorvik.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Coppergate excavation (1976&ndash;81) revealed Jorvik as a densely populated town of wooden houses, leather and textile workshops, and trade goods from as far as the Indian Ocean and the Caliphate &mdash; making the case for Viking York as a major economic and cultural hub, not just a military capital.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">York was Roman <em>Eboracum</em>, then Anglo-Saxon <em>Eoforwic</em>, before becoming Viking Jorvik after 866 &mdash; the Vikings expanded an existing city, they did not invent it.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Coppergate excavation (1976&ndash;81) showed Jorvik as a thriving working town: densely packed wooden houses, craft workshops in amber, leather, bone and ironwork, and trade goods reaching from the Baltic to the Indian Ocean.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">By the eleventh century an Anglo-Scandinavian culture had taken hold &mdash; shared churches, mixed personal names, Norse-decorated Christian crosses &mdash; showing how migration could transform a city's economy and identity over a few generations.</li>
</ul>
