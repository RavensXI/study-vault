--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why migration is the British story: factors, change and continuity, and how to read the question stem.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Migration Is the Long British Story</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Britain has been shaped by people arriving from elsewhere for at least twelve hundred years. Vikings, Normans, medieval Jewish communities, Flemish weavers, Huguenots, Irish famine migrants, Eastern European Jewish refugees, Caribbean Windrush passengers and Ugandan Asians are all part of the same long thread. The historian David Olusoga has argued that Britain&rsquo;s story cannot be told without these arrivals: hostility and welcome have run alongside each other in every period. The thematic study you are about to begin asks you to track that story across four periods &mdash; medieval, early modern, eighteenth and nineteenth century, and modern Britain &mdash; and to spot what changed, what stayed the same, and which factors drove each shift.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four periods of the thematic study, with one example migrant group from each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The thematic study covers four periods: c800&ndash;c1500 (medieval), c1500&ndash;c1700 (early modern), c1700&ndash;c1900 (industrial), and c1900&ndash;present (modern). Examiners reward answers that move between periods within a single paragraph rather than treating migration as a 20th-century story only.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Four Key Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Edexcel specification names four <dfn class="term" data-def="The four forces the specification asks you to use when explaining change in migration: institutions, religion, economic influences, and attitudes in society.">key factors</dfn> that drive change in migration: <strong>institutions</strong> (government and Church), <strong>religion</strong>, <strong>economic influences</strong>, and <strong>attitudes in society</strong>. Most exam questions are really asking which of these mattered most at a given moment. The 1290 expulsion of England&rsquo;s Jewish population, for example, mixes institutions (a royal decree from Edward I) with religion (Christian hostility) and economics (royal debts owed to Jewish lenders). The 1948 British Nationality Act mixes institutions (Parliament) with end-of-Empire politics and post-war labour shortages. Strong answers name the factor explicitly.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Alongside the four factors, historians use <dfn class="term" data-def="The reasons people leave their country of origin (push) and the reasons they choose a particular destination (pull).">push and pull factors</dfn> to explain why people moved at all. Push factors drove people away from home &mdash; famine in Ireland in the 1840s, pogroms in the Russian Empire from 1881, the expulsion of Asians from Uganda in 1972. Pull factors drew them to Britain &mdash; jobs in the cloth industry, royal protection for moneylenders, post-war NHS recruitment, the legal right to enter under the 1948 Nationality Act. Edexcel guidance is clear: focus mainly on what pulled people TO Britain, but show some awareness of what pushed them away from home.</p>

The thematic study explicitly demands twelve centuries of continuous migration. The historian Miranda Kaufmann has documented over sixty parish baptisms of Africans in Tudor England between 1500 and 1640; Hanseatic merchants ran a trading enclave in London from the medieval period; Huguenots reshaped Spitalfields silk weaving after 1685. Top-band thematic essays move between at least two of the four periods within a single paragraph. Restricting yourself to the post-1948 story will cap your mark.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Change, Continuity and Turning Points</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The thematic study is not a list of arrivals; it is a study of <dfn class="term" data-def="Concepts historians use to analyse the past, including causation, consequence, change, continuity, similarity, difference and significance.">second-order concepts</dfn> &mdash; the analytical ideas historians use to make sense of the past. The most important here are <strong>change</strong> (how migration patterns altered between periods), <strong>continuity</strong> (what stayed the same &mdash; the legal idea of the &lsquo;alien&rsquo;, religious refugee waves, hostility from press and politicians), and <strong>turning points</strong> (the 1290 expulsion, the 1685 Huguenot wave, the 1905 Aliens Act, the 1948 British Nationality Act, the 1962 Commonwealth Immigrants Act). Knowing your turning points by date is what lets you write across periods quickly under exam pressure.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">It is a mistake to assume there is a fixed group of &lsquo;indigenous Britons&rsquo; against which everyone else is foreign. Each successive wave &mdash; Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, Normans, Huguenots, Irish, Jewish, Caribbean &mdash; was at first treated as &lsquo;foreign&rsquo; and was later folded into the idea of &lsquo;British&rsquo;. Britishness is a constructed identity, not a biological one. This is why historians like Robert Winder describe migration as the engine of British history rather than its disruption.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sources and the Notting Hill Site</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Edexcel paper splits into a thematic study (worth 36 marks) and a historic environment (worth 16 marks). For Notting Hill, c1948&ndash;c1970, you will work with both <dfn class="term" data-def="Documents, photographs, oral memoirs or records produced in or about a specific local area.">local sources</dfn> &mdash; for example the West Indian Gazette, council and Metropolitan Police records, oral memoirs &mdash; and <dfn class="term" data-def="Documents and records produced at the level of the country as a whole, such as national newspapers, government records and census data.">national sources</dfn> like Times and Guardian coverage, government immigration files, census data and Pathé reports. The 8-mark source-utility question asks you to weigh up content, provenance and your own knowledge together; saying &lsquo;it&rsquo;s biased so it&rsquo;s not useful&rsquo; is explicitly capped by examiners.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">Edexcel question stems are signposted. &lsquo;Describe two features&rsquo; (4 marks) wants two precise features with one developed detail each &mdash; not a paragraph of context. &lsquo;Explain why&rsquo; (8 marks) wants at least two reasons developed with specific period detail. &lsquo;Make and defend a judgement&rsquo; (16 marks) wants a clear, signposted judgement supported across at least two periods, using both stimulus points AND a self-generated factor. The 12-mark historic environment essay wants explicit links between Notting Hill specifics (a named street, a named individual) and wider context (housing shortage, colour bar, end of Empire). Spotting which factor &mdash; institutions, religion, economy, attitudes &mdash; the question is steering you towards is the single most valuable skill in the unit.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four key factors that drive change in migration are institutions, religion, economic influences and attitudes in society. Almost every exam question is steered towards one or two of these &mdash; naming the factor explicitly is what unlocks the higher mark band.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Migration is the long British story: four periods c800&ndash;present, with hostility and welcome running alongside each other in every period &mdash; not just a 20th-century phenomenon.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Four key factors drive change: institutions, religion, economic influences and attitudes in society. Push and pull factors explain why people moved; Edexcel asks you to focus mainly on pull factors to Britain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Top-band answers use second-order concepts (change, continuity, turning points), span at least two periods within a paragraph, and signpost a judgement clearly &mdash; weaving the named site of Notting Hill into the wider thematic story.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">For the 8-mark &lsquo;Explain Why&rsquo; question, name at least two reasons drawn from different key factors (for example one institutional, one economic) rather than two versions of the same point. For the 16-mark judgement question, signpost your view in the introduction (&lsquo;The most important factor was&hellip;&rsquo;) and reinforce it in the conclusion &mdash; relying only on the two stimulus points without a self-generated factor of your own is explicitly capped. SPaG marks reward consistent use of period-specific terminology (Danelaw, Huguenot, Aliens Act, Commonwealth) so weave these in throughout.</p>
