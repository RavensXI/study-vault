<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Britain after the War: Labour, Empire and Notting Hill</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Notting Hill of the 1950s sat at the meeting point of three national stories. The first was post-war reconstruction: the Blitz had destroyed thousands of London homes, and labour was needed for the rebuild, the new National Health Service and the public transport system. The second was the end of Empire: the 1948 British Nationality Act gave citizens of the colonies and Dominions the right to live and work in Britain, and the docking of the <em>Empire Windrush</em> at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 became the symbolic start of large-scale Caribbean migration. The third was a wider cultural change &mdash; what would later be called the &lsquo;Swinging Sixties&rsquo; was visible in west London nightlife by the end of the 1950s. Notting Hill held all three together. Caribbean migrants were doing the labour the rebuild needed; the colour bar in housing concentrated them in Notting Hill; and a small but vocal group of white Londoners refused to accept the change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three national stories that converged on Notting Hill in 1958, and one piece of evidence for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Notting Hill riots of 1958 cannot be understood without the wider context: post-war labour demand pulled Caribbean migrants to London, the 1948 British Nationality Act gave them the right to come, and the colour bar in housing concentrated them in Notting Hill, where a fascist revival under Oswald Mosley targeted them directly the following year.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The 1958 Notting Hill Riots</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In the late August Bank Holiday weekend of 1958, groups of young white men &mdash; including <dfn class="term" data-def="Subculture of working-class young men of the 1950s, originally identified by Edwardian-style suits, drainpipe trousers and quiffs; some Notting Hill teddy boys took part in coordinated attacks on Black homes during the August 1958 riots.">teddy boys</dfn> from the surrounding boroughs &mdash; carried out a series of coordinated attacks on Caribbean homes around Bramley Road and Latimer Road. Attacks ran across nine consecutive nights from 30 August to 5 September. Crowds of up to 400 people, armed with iron bars, knives, milk bottles and homemade petrol bombs, smashed windows and tried to set fire to houses where Caribbean families lived. The Metropolitan Police arrested around 140 people; ultimately nine white men were sentenced to four years each by Mr Justice Salmon, who used his sentencing remarks to condemn the attacks &mdash; an unusually direct judicial response for the period. Caribbean residents organised defence committees to protect homes, but the police later acknowledged in the Met file MEPO 2/9838 that the violence was largely white-led rather than two-sided.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: the 1958 attacks were a two-sided race riot</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">A common error is to describe the 1958 events as a clash &lsquo;between the two communities&rsquo;. Contemporary press accounts often used the phrase &lsquo;race riot&rsquo;, which suggested two groups in conflict on equal terms. The contemporary record does not support that framing. The Metropolitan Police file MEPO 2/9838, opened to the public in the 2000s, recorded that crowds of up to 400 white men, including teddy boys travelling in from outside the area, attacked named houses where Caribbean families lived. Caribbean residents formed defence committees to protect those homes &mdash; a reactive rather than coordinated counter-attack. Strong answers describe the events as coordinated white attacks on Black homes, with Caribbean residents defending themselves, not as a symmetric riot.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Murder of Kelso Cochrane (May 1959)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the early hours of 17 May 1959, an Antiguan carpenter named <dfn class="term" data-def="Antiguan-born carpenter, aged 32, murdered by a gang of white youths near the junction of Southam Street and Golborne Road in Notting Hill on 17 May 1959; despite extensive police investigation, no one was ever convicted.">Kelso Cochrane</dfn> was attacked by a group of six white youths near the junction of Southam Street and Golborne Road, around the corner from Notting Hill. He was stabbed in the chest and died later in hospital. He was thirty-two years old. The murder was widely understood at the time as a racist killing &mdash; coming nine months after the riots and at a moment of high anti-immigrant feeling. The Metropolitan Police investigated for several months but no one was charged; the case remains formally unsolved. His funeral procession through Notting Hill on 6 June 1959 was the largest the area had seen, with around 1,200 mourners. Local Caribbean leaders, including the founders of the West Indian Gazette, used the funeral and the press coverage to demand stronger anti-discrimination law. Mark Olden&rsquo;s 2011 research on the case identified a likely suspect from contemporary police records, but the Crown Prosecution Service has never reopened the case.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Oswald Mosley and the Union Movement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Within weeks of the 1958 riots, the former British Union of Fascists leader <dfn class="term" data-def="Former leader of the British Union of Fascists in the 1930s; relaunched his Union Movement in 1948 and stood as the Union Movement candidate in the North Kensington constituency, including Notting Hill, at the 1959 general election on a platform of forced repatriation; received 8% of the vote.">Oswald Mosley</dfn> announced he would stand in the constituency of North Kensington at the 1959 general election under the banner of the Union Movement. Mosley campaigned door-to-door across Notting Hill on a platform of compulsory repatriation of Black and Caribbean residents and a ban on further Commonwealth migration. His campaign meetings drew crowds in the hundreds and his name appeared on local walls. At the October 1959 election, Mosley received 2,821 votes &mdash; about 8% of the constituency total &mdash; finishing third. The result was much higher than commentators had predicted and showed that openly anti-immigrant politics had a meaningful local base in west London. Mosley failed to win the seat, but his campaign signalled to mainstream politicians that immigration could be made into a vote-winning issue &mdash; a signal that fed into the 1962 Commonwealth Immigrants Act and the wider hardening of British immigration policy through the 1960s.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A common simplification is to describe Mosley&rsquo;s 1959 campaign as a fringe joke that nobody took seriously. The vote share tells a different story. Mosley&rsquo;s 8% in North Kensington was an extraordinarily high vote for an openly fascist candidate in post-war Britain &mdash; comparable to results that might decide a seat in a tight election. He finished third, ahead of the Liberal candidate. The campaign was a serious local result that fed into mainstream political calculation about immigration, and it should not be filed alongside obvious fringe candidacies. Strong answers note the eight per cent and explain its significance in a constituency where Caribbean migration had concentrated.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Policing in Notting Hill: Sus, Raids and the Drift to the Mangrove Crisis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">From the late 1950s the Metropolitan Police&rsquo;s relationship with Caribbean Notting Hill was already tense, and that tension hardened through the 1960s. A central tool was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1824 Vagrancy Act offence of being a &lsquo;suspected person loitering with intent&rsquo;; revived heavily by the Metropolitan Police in the 1960s and 1970s and used disproportionately against young Black men, including in Notting Hill, until repealed in 1981.">sus law</dfn> &mdash; the 1824 Vagrancy Act offence of being a suspected person loitering with intent. By the late 1960s the Met used sus heavily against young Black men in Notting Hill, despite there being no specific complaint of any actual offence. Caribbean cafes, shebeens and the Mangrove Restaurant on All Saints Road were repeatedly raided on drugs warrants, often returning no evidence. The pattern of repeated raids on the Mangrove between January 1969 and the summer of 1970 produced the protest march of August 1970 and led directly to the Mangrove Nine trial of 1971 (covered in the next lesson). The Met&rsquo;s policing of Caribbean Notting Hill in the late 1960s was not yet on the legal record as institutionally racist &mdash; that judicial recognition would come in 1971 &mdash; but the daily experience for residents was of a force that treated them as suspects rather than as members of the public it served.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why the murder of Kelso Cochrane mattered politically as well as personally.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1958 riots, the May 1959 murder of Kelso Cochrane, Mosley&rsquo;s 8% vote at the October 1959 election and the Met&rsquo;s repeated use of sus and drugs raids together produced the political climate that turned Notting Hill into the centre of British Black activism by 1970. Each event built on the last; none should be read in isolation.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The August&ndash;September 1958 attacks on Caribbean homes around Bramley Road and Latimer Road were largely white-led and coordinated &mdash; not a two-sided race riot &mdash; with crowds of up to 400 men attacking named streets across nine nights.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Kelso Cochrane&rsquo;s unsolved murder in May 1959 and Oswald Mosley&rsquo;s 8% vote in North Kensington at the October 1959 general election together politicised Notting Hill and signalled to mainstream politicians that anti-immigrant politics had a working local base.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Through the 1960s the Metropolitan Police used the sus law and repeated drugs raids on Caribbean cafes, shebeens and the Mangrove on All Saints Road, building the resentment that produced the 1970 Mangrove protest and the 1971 trial.</li>
</ul>
