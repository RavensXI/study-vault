--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Three Migrant Stories, One Period</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between c1066 and c1500, three very different migrant groups shaped England: the Normans, who arrived as a conquering elite; Jewish communities, brought across by William the Conqueror as royal financiers; and European traders and craftsmen, including the Hanseatic merchants of London&rsquo;s Steelyard. Reading them side by side teaches the heart of the thematic study &mdash; that migration in the same period can be elite, forced, religious or economic all at once. The four key factors (institutions, religion, economic influences, attitudes) all worked differently for each group.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three medieval migrant groups, with one defining feature for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three medieval migrant groups dominate this period: the Normans (elite military migration after 1066), Jewish communities (brought as financiers, expelled in 1290) and European traders (including Hanseatic merchants at the London Steelyard).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Normans After 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Norman Conquest of 1066 was a wave of <dfn class="term" data-def="Migration of a small ruling class who replace existing elites in landownership, government and the Church.">elite migration</dfn>. <dfn class="term" data-def="Duke of Normandy who conquered England in 1066 and ruled as William I until 1087.">William the Conqueror</dfn> distributed land to perhaps 200 Norman barons, displacing almost the entire Anglo-Saxon landowning class. Domesday Book (1086) records that around 95% of land was held by Norman lords by William&rsquo;s death. The Norman elite was not, however, a single ethnic group: it included Bretons from western France, Flemings from modern Belgium, and other northern French speakers. Calling them simply &lsquo;the French&rsquo; misses how mixed the conquering force actually was.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Norman migration reshaped landownership, the towns, the wool industry and the Church. Romanesque cathedrals replaced Anglo-Saxon ones at Durham, Canterbury and Winchester; the Domesday survey put royal taxation on a new institutional footing; the legal idea of feudal tenure took hold. Norman French became the language of court and law, while ordinary people continued to speak English. Over the centuries the two languages mingled to produce Middle English &mdash; one of the deepest impacts of any wave of migration on this island.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Jewish Communities and the Crown</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">William the Conqueror also brought the first Jewish migrants to England, encouraging them to settle in London and other towns. Jewish communities played a crucial economic role because Christian teaching in this period prohibited Christians from charging interest on loans. Without Jewish moneylenders, the crown could not finance castle-building, military campaigns and royal administration. <dfn class="term" data-def="Twelfth-century Lincoln financier whose loans to the crown and great barons made him one of the wealthiest men in England.">Aaron of Lincoln</dfn> (d. 1186) became one of the wealthiest men in England by lending to the crown, the great barons and even Cistercian monasteries.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Jewish migrants in England held the unique legal status of belonging directly to the king. They were taxed through <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavy royal tax levied directly on Jewish communities; rates rose sharply in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries.">tallage</dfn> &mdash; a special tax that could be raised at royal will, unlike the more limited taxes paid by other subjects. This made Jewish communities both protected and exposed: protected by royal interest, but exposed when popular hostility flared. In 1190, antisemitic violence climaxed in the massacre of Jews at Clifford&rsquo;s Tower in York. Hundreds of Jewish people died after taking refuge there. The events of 1190 sit alongside Aaron of Lincoln&rsquo;s wealth as evidence that medieval Jewish life in England mixed royal protection with episodes of devastating violence.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">A frequent error is to describe medieval English Jews as freely tolerated until 1290 and then suddenly expelled. The reality was harsher throughout. Heavy royal taxation increased steadily after 1200; the massacres of 1190 (most famously at York) showed how thin royal protection could be; from the 1270s, Edward I tried and failed to make Jewish moneylending profitable for the crown by replacing it with new taxes. The 1290 <dfn class="term" data-def="Edward I's 1290 royal decree expelling England's Jewish population, the first national expulsion of Jews in medieval Europe.">Edict of Expulsion</dfn> was the endpoint of a long deterioration, not a sudden reversal of an otherwise tolerant period.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">European Traders and the Hanseatic Steelyard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Alongside Norman lords and Jewish financiers, European traders and craftsmen made up the third strand of medieval migration. Many Flemish weavers settled in Norwich, London and the south-east, where the wool industry pulled in skilled labour. The most famous example of European trade migration was the Hanseatic Steelyard in London. <dfn class="term" data-def="Merchants of the Hanseatic League, a powerful trading confederation of north German and Baltic cities active from the 13th to the 17th century.">Hanseatic merchants</dfn> ran a fortified trading enclave on the north bank of the Thames, near London Bridge, granted special privileges by Henry III in 1281. From the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Hanseatic League's fortified trading enclave on the north bank of the Thames in London, granted special privileges from 1281.">Steelyard</dfn>, they imported timber, furs, wax, fish and grain from northern Europe and exported English wool and cloth.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Like Jewish migrants, Hanseatic merchants held a special legal status &mdash; the medieval status of <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval and early modern legal status of someone not born under the English crown, often subject to higher taxes and restrictions on landownership and trade.">alien</dfn>. Aliens paid higher customs duties, could not own land freely, and lived under their own community rules in defined enclaves. The Steelyard merchants ran a self-governing community with their own warehouses, dining hall and even a small chapel. They prospered until 1598, when Elizabeth I finally revoked their privileges &mdash; but for over three centuries they were the most visible foreign-trading community in London.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Black Death and Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Black Death of 1348&ndash;49 killed perhaps a third of the English population and reshaped migration patterns. Survivors moved from villages to towns where labour was suddenly scarce and wages were rising. Skilled craftsmen, especially weavers and dyers, were actively recruited from the Low Countries to fill gaps. The plague did not stop migration &mdash; it concentrated it in growing towns and increased demand for European craftspeople. By 1450, around 1.5% of England&rsquo;s population was made up of recorded aliens, with concentrations in London, Norwich and Southampton.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1290, Edward I expelled England&rsquo;s entire Jewish population by royal decree &mdash; the first national expulsion of Jews in medieval Europe. England would have no recognised Jewish community again until Cromwell allowed readmission in 1656.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Norman migration after 1066 was elite military migration: a small ruling class of perhaps 200 barons (including Bretons and Flemings) replaced the entire Anglo-Saxon landowning elite and reshaped law, language and the Church.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Jewish migrants were brought to England by William the Conqueror to provide moneylending the Christian Church forbade Christians from doing; they faced steady taxation, the 1190 York massacre, and final expulsion in 1290 under Edward I.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Hanseatic merchants at the Steelyard and Flemish weavers in towns made up the third strand &mdash; bound by the legal status of &lsquo;alien&rsquo;, integrated into the wool and cloth economy, and only fully expelled (Hanseatic) or absorbed (Flemish) by the early modern period.</li>
</ul>
