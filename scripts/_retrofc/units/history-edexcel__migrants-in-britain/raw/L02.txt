--- DESCRIPTION ---
Raids to settlement: Viking arrivals, the Danelaw boundary, and the lasting mark on northern England.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Raid to Settlement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Britain&rsquo;s first wave of recorded large-scale migration in the period c800&ndash;c1066 came from Scandinavia. The earliest Viking raids on Lindisfarne in 793 are famous, but the decisive shift was the arrival of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A coalition of Scandinavian armies that landed in East Anglia in 865 and conquered much of eastern and northern England, leading to permanent Viking settlement.">Heathen Army</dfn> in 865. Within a decade this force had taken Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia. The pull factors were clear: Britain offered fertile land, weak political resistance after years of internal Anglo-Saxon warfare, and a coastline studded with rich monasteries. The push factors included population pressure in Scandinavia and the political fragmentation of Norse societies. By the 870s, large numbers of Danish and Norwegian families were arriving not as raiders but as settlers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences how Viking activity changed between 793 and 878.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vikings did not arrive only as raiders. After the Heathen Army landed in 865, large numbers came as settlers and farmers, and by the 870s entire Scandinavian families were establishing themselves in eastern and northern England.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Treaty of Wedmore and the Danelaw</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In 878, after defeating the Viking leader Guthrum at the Battle of Edington, <dfn class="term" data-def="King of Wessex 871&ndash;899; defeated Guthrum at Edington and negotiated the Treaty of Wedmore in 878.">Alfred the Great</dfn> agreed the Treaty of Wedmore. Guthrum was baptised as a Christian and accepted a boundary running roughly along the Roman road of Watling Street, from London to Chester. North and east of that line, Scandinavian law and custom would prevail. This area became known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of north and eastern England under Danish law and Scandinavian settlement after 878, including Yorkshire, the East Midlands and East Anglia.">Danelaw</dfn>. It was not a separate Viking kingdom but a recognised zone of Norse settlement and legal custom inside England.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Within the Danelaw, the unit of local government was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Scandinavian unit of local government in the Danelaw, equivalent to the Anglo-Saxon hundred.">wapentake</dfn> &mdash; the equivalent of the Anglo-Saxon hundred elsewhere in England. Disputes were settled at wapentake meetings rather than at hundred courts. Place names still mark the difference: villages ending in <em>-by</em> (Whitby, Grimsby, Selby) and <em>-thorpe</em> (Scunthorpe, Mablethorpe) were Norse settlements; place names ending in <em>-tun</em> were Anglo-Saxon. Roughly 1,500 modern English place names in the Danelaw counties are Scandinavian in origin.</p>

After 865, Viking newcomers cleared land, married into Anglo-Saxon families, paid taxes, attended local courts and converted to Christianity (Guthrum did so as part of the Treaty of Wedmore). The Danelaw was a place of farming villages, market towns and trade, not a series of beach raids. Treating Vikings as &lsquo;just raiders&rsquo; misses the migration story the spec is asking about.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Anglo-Saxons as Earlier Migrants</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">It is also a mistake to treat Anglo-Saxons as the original &lsquo;native&rsquo; population against whom Vikings were foreign. The Anglo-Saxons were themselves migrants &mdash; descendants of Angles, Saxons and Jutes who arrived from northern Germany and Denmark in the fifth and sixth centuries. By the 800s they were &lsquo;British&rsquo; in identity, but only a few centuries earlier their ancestors had also been arrivals. This is exactly the pattern you saw in lesson one: each wave of newcomers was treated as &lsquo;foreign&rsquo; at first and folded into &lsquo;British&rsquo; later. Both Anglo-Saxons and Vikings spoke languages from the same Germanic family, which is why Old English and Old Norse mingled rather than clashed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Cnut and the Anglo-Scandinavian Crown</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Viking influence reached its political peak in 1016 when <dfn class="term" data-def="Danish king who became king of England in 1016 and ruled an Anglo-Scandinavian empire including England, Denmark and Norway until 1035.">Cnut</dfn> became king of England, ruling an empire that also included Denmark and Norway. Cnut governed England by working with Anglo-Saxon institutions: he kept the existing system of shires and ealdormen, issued law codes that drew on earlier Anglo-Saxon traditions, and gave generously to English monasteries. By the time of his death in 1035, Anglo-Scandinavian England had a stable shared culture. The institutions key factor (royal government and Church) had absorbed the migrants rather than been overturned by them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The lasting impact of Viking migration runs deep. In language, hundreds of common English words are Norse in origin: <em>sky</em>, <em>law</em>, <em>egg</em>, <em>knife</em>, <em>they</em>, <em>them</em> and <em>their</em>. In law, the wapentake survived in northern England into the Middle Ages, and aspects of trial by jury developed from Scandinavian custom. In economy, Viking trade networks linked York and Lincoln to Dublin, Bergen and the Black Sea &mdash; bringing silver, slaves, amber and ironwork through English ports. None of this would have happened if migration had stopped at the raid stage.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down three lasting impacts of Viking settlement on England, one for language, one for law and one for the economy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Viking migration left lasting marks on language (around 1,500 Scandinavian place names; everyday words like <em>sky</em>, <em>law</em>, <em>they</em>), on law (the Danelaw, the wapentake, early forms of trial by jury) and on the economy (trade networks linking York and Lincoln to Dublin, Bergen and beyond).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">Viking activity moved from raid to settlement: the Heathen Army of 865 brought permanent Scandinavian families to eastern and northern England, not just raiders.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The Treaty of Wedmore (878) created the Danelaw &mdash; a recognised zone of Norse law and settlement, governed locally by wapentakes and visible in place names ending <em>-by</em> and <em>-thorpe</em>.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Cnut&rsquo;s reign from 1016 produced a stable Anglo-Scandinavian England; Viking migration permanently shaped English language, law and trade rather than being a temporary disruption.</li>
</ul>
