--- DESCRIPTION ---
Potential difference, Ohm's law, and the rules for series and parallel circuits.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Current, Potential Difference and Resistance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of flow of electric charge; measured in amperes (A). One ampere means one coulomb of charge flows past a point every second.">Electric current</dfn> is the rate of flow of charge. In a metal conductor, it is the movement of free electrons driven by a power supply. Current (symbol \(I\)) is measured in amperes (A) using an ammeter connected in series.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The work done per unit charge as charge moves between two points in a circuit; measured in volts (V). Also called voltage.">Potential difference</dfn> (symbol \(V\), measured in volts) is the work done per unit charge as it moves between two points in the circuit. It is the &lsquo;push&rsquo; that drives current around the circuit. A voltmeter measures potential difference and is connected in parallel across a component.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The opposition to the flow of current in a component; measured in ohms (&Omega;).">Resistance</dfn> (symbol \(R\), measured in ohms, \(\Omega\)) is the opposition to current flow. For many components at constant temperature, current is proportional to potential difference &mdash; this is <dfn class="term" data-def="The relationship for ohmic conductors stating that potential difference equals current multiplied by resistance: V = I &times; R.">Ohm&rsquo;s law</dfn>:
$$V = I \times R$$
</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the relationship between V, I and R, then rearrange it to make I the subject.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Potential difference \(V\) = current \(I\) &times; resistance \(R\). Units: volts = amperes &times; ohms. Rearranged: \(I = V / R\) and \(R = V / I\). This applies to resistors at constant temperature (ohmic conductors).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Series Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected end-to-end in a single loop, so the same current flows through every component.">series circuit</dfn>, there is only one path for the current. This means the current is the same through every component. The total potential difference of the power supply is shared between all the components: the component with the greatest resistance receives the largest share of the voltage. If one component in a series circuit breaks, the circuit is incomplete and all components stop working.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The total (equivalent) resistance in a series circuit is simply the sum of all the individual resistances:
$$R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \ldots$$
Adding more resistors in series increases total resistance and reduces current.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Parallel Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected across the same two points, providing multiple paths for current to flow; each branch has the same potential difference across it.">parallel circuit</dfn>, there are multiple branches. Each branch has the same potential difference across it (equal to the supply voltage). Current splits between the branches: more current flows through branches with lower resistance. If one branch breaks, current still flows through the other branches.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Adding more resistors in parallel gives more paths for the current, so total resistance <em>decreases</em>. This is often misunderstood &mdash; students sometimes assume that more components always means more resistance. In parallel, the opposite is true: total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down what happens to current and potential difference in series vs parallel circuits.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Series: same current everywhere; voltage shared in proportion to resistance; total resistance = sum of all resistances. Parallel: same voltage across each branch; current splits (more through lower resistance); total resistance is less than the smallest branch resistance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">I-V Graphs and Non-Ohmic Components</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For an ohmic resistor (one whose resistance stays constant), the <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph of current against potential difference for a component; the gradient gives information about resistance.">I-V graph</dfn> is a straight line through the origin &mdash; doubling the voltage doubles the current. Some components are non-ohmic: their resistance changes with current or conditions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A filament lamp has an I-V graph that curves &mdash; as current increases, the filament gets hotter, its resistance increases, so the current does not increase as fast. A diode only allows current to flow in one direction: its I-V graph is nearly flat (almost zero current) for reverse voltage and rises steeply for forward voltage above a threshold. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A resistor whose resistance decreases as temperature increases; used in temperature-sensing circuits.">thermistor</dfn> has a resistance that decreases as temperature rises. A light-dependent resistor (<dfn class="term" data-def="A component whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases; used in light-sensing circuits.">LDR</dfn>) has a resistance that decreases as light intensity increases.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">A very common error is to say that &lsquo;current is used up&rsquo; as it passes through components in a series circuit. This is incorrect. Current (the flow of charge) is the same everywhere in a series loop. What changes around a series circuit is the potential difference (voltage) &mdash; each component causes a voltage drop proportional to its resistance. It is energy that is transferred to the components, not charge. Charge flows continuously in a loop, returning to the battery to be given more energy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">A helpful analogy is a water circuit: the pump (battery) pushes water (charge) around a loop through a series of turbines (resistors). The water speed (current) is the same everywhere in the loop; the water gives up pressure (potential difference) at each turbine; but the same water returns to the pump. In a parallel water circuit, the flow splits into multiple channels &mdash; each channel has the same pressure drop, but less water flows through each individual channel than in the equivalent series arrangement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Potential difference \(V = I \times R\) links voltage, current and resistance. In a series circuit, current is the same everywhere and voltage is shared; total resistance = sum of individual resistances.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">In a parallel circuit, voltage is the same across every branch; current splits between branches; adding branches reduces total resistance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">I-V graphs show whether a component is ohmic (straight line through origin) or non-ohmic (curved). Thermistors and LDRs have resistances that change with temperature and light intensity respectively.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">The most heavily examined mistake in circuits is claiming that current is smaller after passing through a component in a series circuit. Current does not get used up &mdash; it is the same everywhere in a series loop. Only potential difference changes across components. Make sure you also understand the contrast between series and parallel: in series, total resistance increases as you add components; in parallel, total resistance decreases. For I-V graph questions, describe the shape carefully: a straight line through the origin = ohmic; a curve = non-ohmic. State the reason for the curve (e.g. filament gets hotter so resistance increases). For calculation questions, always show your working and include units.</p>
