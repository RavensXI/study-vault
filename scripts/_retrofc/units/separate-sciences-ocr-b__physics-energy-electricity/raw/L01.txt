Energy stores, efficiency, power and how electricity is generated sustainably.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Stores and Transfers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Energy cannot be created or destroyed &mdash; it can only be transferred between different <dfn class="term" data-def="The ways in which energy can be held at any moment, including chemical, nuclear, kinetic, gravitational, elastic, thermal, electrostatic and electromagnetic stores.">energy stores</dfn>. Scientists recognise eight main stores: chemical, nuclear, kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal, electrostatic and electromagnetic. When energy is transferred from one store to another, it is moved by processes called working (for example, by an electric current or a force) or heating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Electricity is a particularly convenient way to transfer energy. Chemical energy stored in a fuel or battery is converted to electrical energy, which can then be transmitted over large distances and converted into other useful forms &mdash; thermal energy in a heater, kinetic energy in a motor, or light in a lamp.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Power and Energy Transfer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred, measured in watts (W); 1 watt equals 1 joule per second.">Power</dfn> is the rate at which energy is transferred. It is measured in watts (W), where 1 W = 1 joule per second. A 2 kW kettle transfers twice as much energy per second as a 1 kW toaster. The energy transferred by any electrical appliance depends on both its power rating and the time for which it is used:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$E = P \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">where \(E\) is energy in joules (or kilowatt-hours), \(P\) is power in watts (or kilowatts) and \(t\) is time in seconds (or hours).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the energy equation from memory, including the units for each quantity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Energy transferred = power × time. In SI units: joules = watts × seconds. In everyday units: kilowatt-hours (kWh) = kilowatts × hours. A device with a power rating of 2 kW running for 3 hours transfers 6 kWh of energy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Efficiency and Dissipation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">No energy transfer is perfectly efficient. Some energy is always <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy that spreads out to the surroundings as thermal energy, making it less concentrated and less useful; often described as &apos;wasted&apos; energy.">dissipated</dfn> &mdash; it spreads into the surroundings as thermal energy and becomes increasingly difficult to make use of. A light bulb converts electrical energy into useful light and wasted thermal energy; a car engine converts chemical energy into useful kinetic energy and wasted thermal energy.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of useful energy output to total energy input, expressed as a fraction or percentage; efficiency = useful energy transferred ÷ total energy transferred.">Efficiency</dfn> is the fraction of total energy input that is converted into a useful output:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy transferred (J)}}{\text{total energy transferred (J)}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">An efficiency of 0.75 means 75% of the input energy becomes useful energy; the remaining 25% is dissipated. Ways to increase efficiency include lubrication (reducing friction in motors) and thermal insulation (slowing heat loss from buildings).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Renewable and Non-Renewable Energy Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The main <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy resources that can be replenished naturally within a human timescale, such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biofuel.">renewable</dfn> energy sources include wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biofuels. They produce little or no carbon dioxide in use and will not run out. <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy resources that cannot be replenished within a human timescale and will eventually run out, such as coal, oil and natural gas.">Non-renewable</dfn> sources &mdash; fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) and nuclear fuel &mdash; provide reliable on-demand power but fossil fuels release carbon dioxide when burned, contributing to global warming, and nuclear fuel produces radioactive waste.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three renewable and two non-renewable energy sources from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Renewable sources (wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal, biofuel) do not run out and produce little CO<sub>2</sub> in use. Non-renewable sources (coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear) provide reliable generation but fossil fuels produce CO<sub>2</sub> and nuclear fuel creates radioactive waste.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n22">In most power stations, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that converts mechanical kinetic energy into electrical energy by rotating a coil of wire in a magnetic field.">generator</dfn> produces electricity by spinning a magnet near a coil of wire. The fuel source (fossil fuel, nuclear, or a renewable source) is used to drive the turbine that turns the generator. The UK mains supply is alternating current (a.c.) at 50 Hz and approximately 230 volts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Electricity is distributed across the country through the National Grid at very high voltages. High voltage reduces the current for a given power, which reduces energy losses in the cables (because power dissipated in a wire depends on the square of the current). Transformers step up the voltage for transmission and step it back down for domestic use. Most home appliances are connected by a three-core cable containing live, neutral and earth wires.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Decisions About Energy Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Choices about energy sources involve weighing up scientific evidence, environmental impact, reliability, cost and social factors. Different people in different contexts may come to different conclusions about which sources to prioritise &mdash; a country with strong winds may favour offshore wind differently from one with high solar irradiance. These decisions are shaped by risk-benefit analysis and are ultimately a matter of policy, informed by but not determined solely by science.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Building Insulation and Reducing Energy Loss</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">One of the most cost-effective ways to reduce energy consumption is to reduce the energy lost from buildings. Heat loss through walls, roofs and windows is determined by the thickness and <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how readily a material conducts heat; materials with high thermal conductivity transfer heat quickly.">thermal conductivity</dfn> of the materials used. A thicker wall slows heat transfer; a wall made of a material with low thermal conductivity (such as foam insulation) slows it further. Double-glazing traps a layer of air or gas between two panes of glass: air is a poor thermal conductor, so heat transfer across the window is significantly reduced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Sankey diagrams are a useful tool for visualising energy transfers. In a Sankey diagram, the width of the arrows is proportional to the amount of energy they represent. The main arrow shows the total energy input; branches show where useful energy goes and where energy is wasted (dissipated). Reading a Sankey diagram allows you to estimate efficiency quickly: a diagram where the useful output arrow is wide relative to the wasted energy arrows indicates a more efficient device.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Energy is stored in eight forms and transferred by working or heating; power (watts) is the rate of energy transfer, calculated using \(E = P \times t\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input; no transfer is 100% efficient &mdash; some energy is always dissipated as thermal energy. Efficiency can be improved through lubrication and insulation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Renewable sources (wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal, biofuel) do not run out and produce little CO<sub>2</sub>; non-renewable sources (fossil fuels, nuclear) are reliable but have significant environmental and waste concerns. Energy decisions balance these factors alongside cost and social context.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">The efficiency equation is often misapplied: always divide useful energy by total input, not the other way round. If you are asked to compare energy sources, use precise scientific language: &lsquo;renewable sources cannot run out within a human timescale&rsquo; is more precise than &lsquo;they last forever&rsquo;. For 6-mark evaluate questions comparing energy sources, always consider both advantages and disadvantages and reach a justified conclusion rather than just listing points. When calculating energy using \(E = P \times t\), check your units: if power is in kW and time is in hours, your answer is in kWh, not joules &mdash; state the unit clearly.</p>
