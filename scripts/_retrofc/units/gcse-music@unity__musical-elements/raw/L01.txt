<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Melody?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sequence of single notes that form a recognisable musical line or tune.">Melody</dfn> is one of the most important musical elements you will need to identify and describe in the Eduqas listening exam. It refers to the main tune or vocal line of a piece of music — the part you would hum or sing along to. To score well, you need precise vocabulary for describing how a melody moves, what shapes it creates, and how individual notes within it are played or sung.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">How Melodies Move: Conjunct and Disjunct Motion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Melodies move in two basic ways. <dfn class="term" data-def="Melodic movement by step, where notes move to the next note up or down in the scale.">Conjunct</dfn> motion means the melody moves by step — each note is next to the previous one in the scale. This creates a smooth, flowing sound. <dfn class="term" data-def="Melodic movement by leap, where notes jump over one or more scale degrees.">Disjunct</dfn> motion means the melody moves by leap — notes jump over one or more scale degrees. This creates a more angular, dramatic sound.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most melodies use a mixture of both. For example, in Toto’s <em>Africa</em> (a set work for AoS4), the verse melody is described as “mostly conjunct” because it moves mainly in step, with a syncopated rhythm and a syllabic vocal style. However, in Verse 2 (bar 33), the singer leaps up to higher pitches on the words “deep in,” creating a moment of disjunct motion for expressive effect.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the formula or equation from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Conjunct = stepwise (smooth). Disjunct = leaps (angular). In the exam, always state which type of movement you hear and try to give a bar number or lyric reference as evidence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Melodic Shape and Contour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">As well as describing how a melody moves note-to-note, you should describe its overall shape. A melody might be <strong>ascending</strong> (going up), <strong>descending</strong> (going down), or <strong>arch-shaped</strong> (rising then falling). Some melodies use a <strong>narrow pitch range</strong>, staying within a few notes, while others have a <strong>wide pitch range</strong>, spanning many notes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In <em>Africa</em>, the chorus melody uses only two pitches — the tonic and the leading note — giving it a very narrow pitch range. By contrast, the overall pitch range of the vocal line across the whole song is just under two octaves on the printed score (and even wider on the recording due to vocal improvisations towards the end).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Melodic Decoration: Ornaments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Composers and performers often add decorative notes to a melody. These are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Extra notes added to a melody for decoration; they are not essential to the main tune.">ornaments</dfn>. The most common ornaments you need to recognise are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Trill</strong> — a rapid alternation between two adjacent notes. <strong>Mordent</strong> — a quick alternation with the note above or below. <dfn class="term" data-def="Additional notes added as decoration that are not essential to the melody or harmony.">Grace notes</dfn> — small extra notes played quickly before the main note. <strong>Turn</strong> — a group of notes that “turn” around the main note (above, main, below, main).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In <em>Africa</em>, grace notes and syncopation appear in the instrumental section (bars 75–77), giving the melody a more “pop” style feel.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Grace notes are not essential to the melody or the harmony — they are decorative additions. In the exam, if you hear quick, small notes before a main note, identify them as grace notes or ornaments.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Other Useful Melody Terms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Here are further terms you should use when describing melody in the exam:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Syllabic</strong> — one note per syllable of text. <dfn class="term" data-def="A passage in vocal music where a single syllable is sung across several different notes.">Melisma</dfn> — one syllable sung across several notes (also called melismatic). <strong>Riff</strong> — a short repeated melodic phrase, common in pop and rock music. <strong>Ostinato</strong> — a repeated melodic or rhythmic pattern. <strong>Sequence</strong> — a melodic phrase repeated at a higher or lower pitch. <strong>Improvisation</strong> — making up a melody on the spot.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">In the verse of <em>Africa</em>, the vocal melody is mostly syllabic. However, at the end of Chorus 3, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated phrase in popular music and jazz, typically used as an introduction or refrain.">riff</dfn> is heard and the melody ends with a melisma. Solo vocal improvisation also appears during the repeated Phrase 3 of Chorus 3.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">What Is Articulation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="The way in which individual notes are played or sung, such as smoothly (legato) or detached (staccato).">Articulation</dfn> describes <em>how</em> notes are played or sung. Two performers can play the same notes but make them sound completely different depending on articulation. You must be able to identify and name these techniques in the listening exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Key Articulation Markings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">These are the main articulation terms the Eduqas spec expects you to know:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><dfn class="term" data-def="A smooth, connected style of playing or singing where notes flow into each other without gaps.">Legato</dfn> — smooth and connected. Notes flow into one another with no gaps. Often shown by a curved line (slur) above or below the notes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><dfn class="term" data-def="A short, detached style of playing where each note is separated by a brief silence.">Staccato</dfn> — short and detached. Each note is separated by a tiny silence. Shown by a dot above or below the note head.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Accent</strong> — a note played louder or with more force than the notes around it. Shown by a > symbol. <strong>Tenuto</strong> — a note held for its full length (or slightly longer), given weight. Shown by a short horizontal line above or below the note.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Slur</strong> — a curved line connecting two or more different-pitched notes, indicating they should be played legato. <strong>Tie</strong> — a curved line connecting two notes of the <em>same</em> pitch, combining their durations. Don’t confuse the two! <strong>Pizzicato</strong> — plucking string instruments instead of bowing. <strong>Arco</strong> — returning to bowing after pizzicato. <strong>Tremolo</strong> — rapid repetition of a single note or rapid alternation between two notes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A slur connects notes of different pitches (play legato). A tie connects notes of the same pitch (hold the note longer). This is a common point of confusion in exams.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Putting It All Together: Describing Melody and Articulation in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34">When you hear a listening extract, work through a mental checklist: Is the melody conjunct or disjunct? What is the pitch range (narrow or wide)? Is it ascending, descending, or arch-shaped? Are there any ornaments such as trills or grace notes? Is the vocal line syllabic or melismatic? What articulation can you hear — legato, staccato, accents? Always try to link your observations to specific moments in the music, using bar numbers or section names where possible.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Conjunct</strong> motion (stepwise) sounds smooth; <strong>disjunct</strong> motion (leaps) sounds angular — most melodies use a mixture of both.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Ornaments such as grace notes, trills, and turns decorate a melody but are not essential to the main tune.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Articulation (legato, staccato, accents, tenuto) changes <em>how</em> notes are performed — always name the specific technique and reference where it occurs in the music.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">In the Eduqas listening exam, the command word “describe” means you need to say <em>what</em> is happening in the music and <em>where</em>. For melody questions, don’t just say “the melody goes up” — use precise terms like “ascending conjunct movement” or “a disjunct leap of a 5th.” For articulation, name the specific technique (e.g. staccato, legato) and say what effect it creates. Always reference bar numbers or section names to show exactly where the feature occurs.</p>
