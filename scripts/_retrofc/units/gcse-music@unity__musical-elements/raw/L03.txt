<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Texture in Music?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The way different musical lines or parts are layered together in a piece of music.">Texture</dfn> describes how the different layers of sound in a piece of music relate to each other. Think of it like fabric — some textures are thin with just one thread, while others are thick with many threads woven together. In the Eduqas listening exam, you need to identify textures by ear and use the correct terminology to describe them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Types of Texture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">There are four main texture types you must know for your exam. Each one describes a different relationship between the musical parts.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you name all 2 main textures from memory?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main textures are: monophonic (one line), homophonic (melody and accompaniment), polyphonic (two or more independent melodies at the same time), and unison (everyone playing or singing the same notes).</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Monophonic Texture</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A texture consisting of a single melodic line with no accompaniment.">Monophonic</dfn> texture is the simplest type. It means a single melodic line is heard on its own, with no harmony or accompaniment. A solo singer performing without any instruments is monophonic. If several people sing the same melody at the same pitch, this is called <dfn class="term" data-def="When two or more performers play or sing the same notes at the same pitch at the same time.">unison</dfn> — it is still considered monophonic because there is only one melodic line.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Homophonic Texture</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A texture where one main melody is supported by an accompaniment of chords or other parts.">Homophonic</dfn> texture is the most common texture in popular music. It features one main melody with an accompaniment underneath. For example, a singer performing with a piano playing chords is homophonic. In Toto’s “Africa,” the overall texture is described as homophonic — there is a lead vocal melody supported by synthesiser chords, bass guitar, and drum kit.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Polyphonic Texture</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A texture where two or more independent melodic lines are heard at the same time.">Polyphonic</dfn> texture (also called contrapuntal) occurs when two or more independent melodies are played or sung at the same time. Each line has its own rhythm and shape. A round like “Row, Row, Row Your Boat” is a simple example. In “Africa,” a moment of polyphonic texture occurs in Verse 2, where a <dfn class="term" data-def="An independent melody played above or alongside the main melody.">countermelody</dfn> (or descant) on a flute-like instrument is heard across the top of the texture alongside the lead vocal.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n9">In the exam, you may also need to describe how texture changes within a piece. Common descriptions include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Layering</strong> — new parts are gradually added to build up the texture (as in the 8-bar percussion intro to “Africa,” where rhythm parts are looped and layered).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Thinning</strong> — parts are removed to reduce the texture (as in the outro of “Africa,” where the texture is gradually reduced until only the rhythm track and the bass line of Riff A remain).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Thickening</strong> — more instruments or voices join, making the texture fuller. In “Africa,” the choruses are forte with a full band texture, thicker than the verses.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the Eduqas listening exam, you may be asked to identify a change in texture at a specific point in the music. Listen for when instruments enter or drop out, and whether the melody has accompaniment (homophonic) or another independent melody (polyphonic).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">What Is Structure in Music?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The way a piece of music is organised into sections, such as verses, choruses, or movements.">Structure</dfn> (also called form) refers to how a piece of music is organised into sections. Recognising structure means being able to hear when sections repeat, change, or return. The Eduqas spec requires you to identify and describe common structures in both popular music and classical music.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Common Structures in Popular Music</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Most popular songs follow a <dfn class="term" data-def="A song structure that alternates verses (with changing lyrics) and choruses (with a repeated hook or lyric).">verse–chorus</dfn> structure. The verse tells the story with different lyrics each time, while the chorus is the catchy, repeated section. Many songs add a <dfn class="term" data-def="A contrasting section in a song, usually appearing once, that provides variety before returning to the chorus.">bridge</dfn> (sometimes called a middle 8) for contrast before the final chorus.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">“Africa” by Toto uses a verse–chorus structure. The full layout is:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n19">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Intro</div>
    <h4>Bars 1–4 (repeated)</h4><p>8-bar unpitched percussion intro followed by Riff A played 4 times. Key of B major. Layered rhythm parts and ostinatos show African influence.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Verse 1</div>
    <h4>Bars 5–39 (B major)</h4><p>35 bars. Four phrases: three 9-bar phrases and one 8-bar phrase. The unusual 9-bar length occurs because Riff A begins as the vocal line ends in bar 8 each time.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Chorus 1</div>
    <h4>18 bars (A major)</h4><p>Key changes to A major. Four phrases with the chord loop F#m–D–A–E (vi–IV–I–V).</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Link 1</div>
    <h4>8 bars (B major)</h4><p>Returns to B major. Based on Riff A material, connecting chorus back to verse.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Verse 2</div>
    <h4>B major</h4><p>Shortened — only two 9-bar phrases plus the final 8-bar phrase. Features a countermelody on a flute-like instrument and more prominent backing vocals.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Chorus 2 & Link 2</div>
    <h4>A major / B major</h4><p>Chorus 2 repeats Chorus 1. Link 2 repeats Link 1.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Instrumental</div>
    <h4>Bars 66–82 (B major)</h4><p>Based on verse accompaniment but vocal melody replaced by instrumental melody based on Riff B. Even shorter than Verse 2.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Chorus 3</div>
    <h4>22 bars (A major)</h4><p>Longer than previous choruses. “I bless the rains down in Africa” is heard five times. Solo vocal improvisation is added. A new electric guitar riff appears.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Outro</div>
    <h4>B major</h4><p>Repeats intro material. Texture gradually reduces — fades out to just the rhythm track and bass line of Riff A.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Other Structures You Need to Know</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The Eduqas spec covers several structural forms beyond verse–chorus. You may hear these in any area of study.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Ternary form (ABA)</strong> — Three sections where the first section (A) returns after a contrasting middle section (B). The return of A gives the piece a sense of balance. Some composition briefs ask students to write in <dfn class="term" data-def="A three-part musical structure (ABA) where the opening section returns after a contrasting middle section.">ternary form</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Rondo form (ABACADA…)</strong> — A main theme (A) keeps returning between contrasting episodes (B, C, D, etc.). Each episode introduces new material, but the listener always hears the familiar A section come back.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Theme and variations</strong> — A theme is stated and then altered in a series of <dfn class="term" data-def="A modified version of a musical theme, changed by altering elements such as rhythm, melody, key, texture, or dynamics.">variations</dfn>. Each variation changes one or more elements — for example, the melody might be decorated, the rhythm changed, or the key shifted from major to minor.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>32-bar song form (AABA)</strong> — Common in older pop and jazz. Four 8-bar sections: the A section is heard three times with a contrasting B section (the bridge or middle 8) before the final return of A.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Through-composed</strong> — Music that is continuously different with no repeated sections. Each verse or section has new music. This is common in film music where the score follows the action on screen.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27">In addition to naming overall structures, you should be able to identify these structural devices:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Intro</strong> — the opening section before the main melody or vocal enters.</li>
      <li><strong>Outro / Coda</strong> — the closing section. In “Africa,” the outro uses a fade out.</li>
      <li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated musical phrase, typically in the bass or rhythm part, that forms a foundation for the music above it.">Ostinato</dfn></strong> — a short repeated pattern. Riff B in “Africa” uses an ostinato pattern of five notes based on the E major pentatonic scale.</li>
      <li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated melodic or rhythmic phrase, commonly used in popular music and jazz as an introduction or refrain.">Riff</dfn></strong> — a short repeated phrase, often played on guitar or keyboard. “Africa” features two main riffs (Riff A and Riff B) that recur throughout the song.</li>
      <li><strong>Link / Transition</strong> — a short passage connecting two main sections.</li>
      <li><strong>Repeat and fade</strong> — a section is repeated while the volume gradually decreases to silence, as heard in the outro of “Africa.”</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Texture and Structure in “Africa” — Putting It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">In the exam, you may need to describe how texture and structure work together. In “Africa,” texture changes help define the structure:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">The <strong>intro</strong> builds texture through layering — unpitched percussion enters first, then pitched instruments playing Riff A are added.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The <strong>verses</strong> are mostly homophonic with a lead vocal over chordal accompaniment. In Verse 2, the texture becomes more complex as a countermelody and backing vocals are added.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">The <strong>choruses</strong> are thicker and louder (forte) with the full band and backing vocals creating a fuller homophonic texture.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">The <strong>outro</strong> thins the texture, gradually stripping parts away until only the rhythm track and bass line remain, creating a fade out.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Verse 2 of “Africa” differs from Verse 1 because it adds a countermelody (descant) on a flute-like instrument and more prominent backing vocals harmonising on “Ooo.” This makes the texture more complex — a detail worth naming in your answers.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">The four main textures are monophonic, homophonic, polyphonic, and unison — learn to identify each by ear and use the correct term.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Common structures include verse–chorus, ternary (ABA), rondo (ABACA), theme and variations, 32-bar song form (AABA), and through-composed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">In “Africa” by Toto, the overall texture is homophonic and the structure is verse–chorus form — but texture changes (layering in the intro, countermelody in Verse 2, thinning in the outro) help define each structural section.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">When a question asks you to describe the texture at a specific point, don’t just say “thick” or “thin.” Use precise vocabulary: <em>homophonic</em>, <em>polyphonic</em>, <em>monophonic</em>, or <em>unison</em>. Then add detail about what creates that texture — for example, “homophonic texture with a lead vocal accompanied by synthesiser chords and bass guitar.” For structure questions, always name the specific section (verse, chorus, link) and give bar numbers if you have a score. If you’re asked about changes in texture, describe what happens <em>before</em> and <em>after</em> the change to show the contrast.</p>
