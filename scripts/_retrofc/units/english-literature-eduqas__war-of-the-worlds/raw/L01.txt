--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">H.G. Wells & The War of the Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Herbert George Wells (1866–1946), a pioneering science fiction author who used fantastical scenarios to critique real Victorian society.">H.G. Wells</dfn> published <em>The War of the Worlds</em> in 1898, at the height of the British Empire. Wells was not simply writing an adventure story — he used the Martian invasion as a vehicle to critique Victorian society’s arrogance, its faith in science and technology, and its brutal treatment of colonised peoples. The novel asks a devastating question: what would happen if Britain, the world’s greatest imperial power, suddenly became the victim of a superior force?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Wells came from a working-class background in Bromley, Kent. His father was an unsuccessful shopkeeper; his mother a domestic servant. He won a scholarship to the Normal School of Science in London, where he studied biology under Thomas Henry Huxley, a champion of <dfn class="term" data-def="Charles Darwin’s theory that species evolve through natural selection — the survival of organisms best adapted to their environment.">Darwinism</dfn>. This scientific education profoundly shaped the novel. The Martians are not supernatural — they are a more evolved species applying the same logic of survival that Darwin described. Wells forces his readers to see humanity not as the pinnacle of creation, but as one species among many, vulnerable to extinction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Wells was also a committed <dfn class="term" data-def="A political ideology advocating collective ownership and equality; Wells was a member of the Fabian Society and used his fiction to critique inequality.">socialist</dfn>, a member of the Fabian Society alongside George Bernard Shaw. He believed the British class system was unjust and that imperialism was morally indefensible. <em>The War of the Worlds</em> channels these beliefs into fiction: the invasion exposes the fragility of Victorian social structures and forces readers to question whether their comfortable world is as secure as they believe.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three aspects of Victorian society does Wells critique through the Martian invasion?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells critiques three aspects of Victorian society: (1) Imperial arrogance — Britain conquered other nations; now it is conquered. (2) Scientific overconfidence — Victorian technology is useless against the Martians. (3) Social Darwinism — the belief that “superior” races have the right to dominate “inferior” ones is turned against humanity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The British Empire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">By 1898, the British Empire controlled a quarter of the world’s surface and governed over 400 million people. Victorians believed their technological and military superiority gave them the right — even the duty — to colonise other peoples. This attitude was reinforced by <dfn class="term" data-def="The misapplication of Darwin’s evolutionary theory to human societies, used to justify imperialism and racial hierarchies.">Social Darwinism</dfn>, the idea that some races were naturally “superior” and destined to rule. Wells deliberately reverses this dynamic: the Martians treat humans exactly as the British treated colonised peoples — as inferior beings to be conquered, exploited, and consumed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The timing of the novel is significant. The 1890s were a period of <dfn class="term" data-def="French for ‘end of century’; a mood of anxiety, decadence, and cultural exhaustion felt across Europe in the 1890s.">fin de siècle</dfn> anxiety — a widespread sense that the century was ending and that change was coming. The Boer War (1899–1902), which began just a year after the novel’s publication, would expose the weaknesses of the British military and shake public confidence in the Empire. Wells anticipated this crisis of confidence.</p>

By applying it to humanity as a whole, Wells forces his readers to confront the horror of their own imperial logic.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Science & Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The late Victorian period was an era of extraordinary scientific progress. Railways, telegraphs, electric lighting, and new weapons gave Victorians confidence that technology could solve any problem and that human progress was unstoppable. Wells subverts this confidence completely. The British military’s best weapons — artillery, warships, even the <dfn class="term" data-def="HMS Thunder Child, a fictional ironclad warship that sacrifices itself fighting the Martians, representing the futility of Victorian military power.">Thunder Child</dfn> — are destroyed by the Martians’ Heat-Ray. Technology, which Victorians worshipped as proof of their superiority, proves utterly inadequate against a genuinely superior civilisation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Wells also draws on real astronomical discoveries. In the 1870s and 1880s, astronomers observed what appeared to be canals on the surface of Mars, sparking speculation about Martian civilisation. The astronomer Percival Lowell published hugely popular books arguing that intelligent beings had built an irrigation system on Mars. Wells takes this speculation and turns it into a nightmare: the Martians are not just intelligent — they are so far ahead of humans that our species is no more significant to them than insects are to us.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why Wells references the Tasmanian genocide in the opening chapter.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells references the Tasmanian genocide to draw a direct parallel between the Martian invasion and British imperialism. He forces readers to see the invasion from the perspective of colonised peoples — making the novel a sustained critique of empire, not just an adventure story. This reference appears in the very first chapter, establishing the political argument before the action begins.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Wells uses the Martian invasion to critique Victorian imperial arrogance, scientific overconfidence, and Social Darwinism. His socialist beliefs and working-class background inform the novel’s political argument.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The reference to the Tasmanian genocide makes the colonial parallel explicit from the first chapter — the Martians treat humans as the British treated colonised peoples.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Victorian military technology is shown to be utterly inadequate against a superior force, and fin de siècle anxiety pervades the novel’s atmosphere.</li></ul>
