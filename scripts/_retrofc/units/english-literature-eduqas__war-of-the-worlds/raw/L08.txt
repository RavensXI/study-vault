<h2 data-narration-id="n1">19th-Century Novel Exam Technique: The War of the Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">19th-century novel questions typically give you a printed extract from the text and ask you to write about its significance and its importance in the novel as a whole. Your answer is marked against the Assessment Objectives: AO1 (critical response with textual references), AO2 (analysis of language, form and structure), AO3 (understanding of context), and on some boards AO4 (accuracy of written expression). Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact mark allocation and which AOs are assessed on your paper.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this: summarise what each AO rewards in a 19th-century novel response.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO1: Critical response with textual references. AO2: Analysis of language, form, structure. AO3: Context. AO4: Accuracy of expression. The strongest paragraphs show evidence of more than one AO at a time.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">On any paper where context is assessed, your understanding of Victorian imperialism, Darwinism, class, and science is just as important as your analysis of Wells’s language techniques. Where written accuracy is assessed, careless spelling, punctuation, and grammar will cost you marks. Use literary terminology accurately: words like “semantic field,” “pathetic fallacy,” “motif,” and “narrative perspective” demonstrate sophisticated analysis and strengthen AO2 (and, where assessed, AO4).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Key Extracts to Prepare</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The richest passages for analysis: (1) The opening chapter with Tasmanian reference. (2) The Heat-Ray scene on Horsell Common. (3) The London exodus. (4) HMS Thunder Child’s destruction. (5) The narrator trapped with the curate. (6) The bacteria ending.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">For each passage, prepare at least three analytical points covering different AOs. For the opening chapter, you might analyse: (1) the Tasmanian reference and its colonial critique (AO3), (2) the calm, scientific register that contrasts with the horror to come (AO2), and (3) the retrospective framing that creates dramatic irony (AO2). For the bacteria ending: (1) the anti-climactic resolution rejecting heroic conventions (AO2), (2) “the humblest things” as an ironic commentary on human pride (AO2), and (3) the Darwinian logic that mirrors colonial disease history (AO3).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Time Management</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Work out in advance how long you can afford to spend on this question and split the time between reading and planning, writing, and proofreading. A useful rule of thumb is to plan 3–4 key points before you start writing, and to leave at least 5 minutes at the end to proofread. Each point should cover the extract AND the wider text.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A good plan identifies your key points and the quotations you will use for each. Write short bullet points, not full sentences. For example: “Point 1: colonial parallel — Tasmanian reference, ‘inferior races’, red weed as territorial claim. Link to wider: blood feeding, Thunder Child = futility. Context: British Empire, Social Darwinism.” This plan takes under a minute to write but ensures your response is structured and addresses several AOs.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Model Paragraph Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The strongest paragraphs integrate several AOs naturally. Begin with a clear analytical point about the extract (AO1). Analyse Wells’s specific technique — a word choice, structural feature, or narrative device (AO2). Link this to a contextual factor such as imperialism, Darwinism, or Victorian class (AO3). Ensure the whole paragraph is expressed in clear, grammatically accurate English using literary terminology (AO4). A useful sentence scaffold: “Wells uses [technique] to present [idea/character], reflecting [context], which forces the reader to [response].”</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Common Mistakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Avoid: (1) Retelling the plot instead of analysing Wells’s methods. (2) Only discussing the extract without connecting to the wider novel. (3) Listing context (“The British Empire existed”) without linking to purpose. (4) Ignoring accuracy — clear expression is always rewarded and, on some boards, explicitly marked. (5) Not using <dfn class="term" data-def="Specialist vocabulary for discussing literature: terms like semantic field, motif, pathetic fallacy, narrative perspective.">literary terminology</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Another common weakness is treating the extract in isolation. A strong response always connects outward to other parts of the text. If your extract is from Book 1, discuss how the events connect to Book 2. If it features the narrator, compare his perspective with the curate’s or the artilleryman’s. If it describes the Martians, connect to the opening chapter’s colonial framework and the bacteria ending. This demonstrates the sustained critical style that examiners reward at the highest bands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Name three key AO2 methods specific to The War of the Worlds.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three AO2 methods: (1) First-person limited narrator creating suspense and uncertainty. (2) Journalistic register for realism. (3) Scientific biological language for the Martians, removing emotion and reinforcing the Darwinian framework.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">AO4: Quality of Expression</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Where AO4 is assessed, it is often underestimated. Write in formal academic register. Avoid slang, contractions, and informal phrases like “this shows that.” Instead, use precise analytical vocabulary: “Wells employs,” “this reinforces,” “the reader is compelled to.” Proofread your final response — check for comma splices, run-on sentences, and common misspellings of literary terms. Strong written expression also means structuring your paragraphs logically, with each one making a distinct point rather than repeating earlier ideas.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">40 marks split equally across AO1, AO2, AO3, AO4. Always write about extract AND wider text.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Prepare six high-value passages; use the paragraph scaffold to integrate all AOs.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Avoid retelling, context-listing, and ignoring AO4. Use literary terminology and proofread.</li></ul>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Use the phrase “Wells uses [method] to [effect], reflecting [context]” as your go-to sentence scaffold. This naturally integrates AO1, AO2, and AO3 in every paragraph.</p>
