--- DESCRIPTION ---
How igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic processes link over geological time, and the rates at which they operate.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Connecting the Three Rock Types</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the earlier units you studied igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks as separate categories. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A continuous series of geological processes by which rocks are created, altered, and recycled over geological time.">rock cycle</dfn> shows how all three types are connected &mdash; each can be transformed into another given enough time and the right conditions of temperature, pressure, erosion, or burial. No rock is permanent; every rock is on a journey.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The driving forces of the rock cycle are two distinct sources of energy. Heat from the Earth&rsquo;s interior (geothermal energy) drives igneous and metamorphic processes: it melts rock to form magma, powers volcanic activity, and provides the high temperatures needed for metamorphism. Energy from the Sun drives the surface processes: it heats the atmosphere, drives weather and river systems, and therefore controls erosion, transport, and deposition of sediment. Understanding this energy framework prevents the common misconception that the rock cycle has a single driver or a single pathway.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the two energy sources that drive the rock cycle. For each, give one specific process it powers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The rock cycle has two energy sources: geothermal energy from the Earth&rsquo;s interior (driving igneous activity and metamorphism) and solar energy at the surface (driving weathering, erosion, and deposition).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Main Pathways</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">There is no single fixed pathway through the rock cycle &mdash; a rock can take many routes depending on what happens to it next. However, some common journeys are worth understanding. An igneous rock at the surface is immediately attacked by weathering and erosion. The fragments (clasts) are transported by rivers, wind, or ice, then deposited as sediment. With burial, compaction, and cementation, the sediment lithifies into <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock formed by the compaction and cementation of sediment particles, or by chemical precipitation from water.">sedimentary rock</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">If that sedimentary rock is buried more deeply and exposed to elevated temperatures and pressures &mdash; but not melted &mdash; it recrystallises in the solid state to form <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock formed by the recrystallisation of pre-existing rock under elevated temperature and pressure without melting.">metamorphic rock</dfn>. This distinction matters: metamorphism is solid-state change. If melting occurs, the process becomes igneous, not metamorphic. If the metamorphic rock is buried even further and melts completely, it forms <dfn class="term" data-def="Molten rock material beneath the Earth&rsquo;s surface, composed of silicate minerals, gases, and dissolved water.">magma</dfn>, which can cool underground as an intrusive igneous rock or erupt as lava to form an extrusive igneous rock &mdash; completing one loop of the cycle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Critically, shortcuts exist. A sedimentary rock at the surface can be eroded back to sediment without ever becoming metamorphic. A metamorphic rock exposed at the surface by uplift and erosion can be directly weathered. The cycle has many parallel loops running simultaneously, and they operate at vastly different rates.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Rates of geological processes &mdash; gradualism vs catastrophism</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Most rock-cycle processes operate on timescales of millions of years &mdash; this is the principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that geological change happens slowly and continuously through the same processes operating today, over vast timescales.">gradualism</dfn> (also called uniformitarianism). River erosion, sediment deposition, burial, and metamorphism all operate gradually. However, geology also recognises <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that major geological events can result from sudden, violent catastrophes such as meteorite impacts or massive volcanic eruptions.">catastrophism</dfn> &mdash; the view that some major events happen suddenly and dramatically.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The end-Cretaceous meteorite impact is the classic example. Around 66 million years ago, a bolide roughly 10 km across struck the Yucat&aacute;n Peninsula. The impact triggered wildfires, tsunamis, and a prolonged &ldquo;impact winter&rdquo; caused by dust and debris blocking sunlight. The result was mass extinction, including the non-avian dinosaurs. This single catastrophic event left a clear geological signature &mdash; an iridium-rich layer at the Cretaceous&ndash;Paleogene boundary that is found worldwide. Modern geology accepts that both gradualism and catastrophism are real: most processes are gradual, but rare catastrophic events can punctuate the record.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Rate Problem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Different parts of the rock cycle operate at very different speeds. River erosion can cut a gorge metres deep in a human lifetime. In contrast, the subduction of oceanic crust back into the mantle &mdash; completing the plate-tectonic loop of the rock cycle &mdash; takes tens of millions of years. Metamorphism in a subduction zone happens over millions of years as rocks are slowly dragged to depth. Understanding this rate difference is important for interpreting the rock record: a thin sedimentary layer might represent a very short, high-energy event (a storm deposit, for example), while a thick sequence of deep-sea mudstone represents millions of years of quiet, slow accumulation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: give one example of a fast geological process and one example of a slow process. Estimate a rough timescale for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Geological processes span an enormous range of rates &mdash; from volcanic eruptions lasting hours or days, to the subduction cycle operating over tens of millions of years. The rock cycle therefore operates simultaneously on human timescales (eruptions, floods) and deep-time timescales (mountain building, ocean floor renewal).</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: does the rock cycle have a fixed direction?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common mistake is to treat the rock cycle as a one-way conveyor belt: igneous &rarr; sedimentary &rarr; metamorphic &rarr; back to igneous. This is only one of many possible pathways. Any rock type can be eroded to sediment, any rock type can be metamorphosed, and any rock type can eventually melt to produce a new magma. The power of the rock cycle model is precisely that it is cyclic and multi-directional. When answering exam questions, avoid writing &ldquo;sedimentary rocks become metamorphic rocks&rdquo; without adding &ldquo;&hellip;if buried and subjected to elevated temperature and pressure&rdquo; &mdash; the condition matters.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Deep Time and the Rock Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The rock cycle can only be appreciated against the backdrop of geological time. The Earth is approximately 4.6 billion years old. The oldest known rocks on Earth (the Acasta Gneiss of Canada) are around 4 billion years old, yet even these have been metamorphosed and deformed many times since their formation. The constant recycling of crustal material means that almost no original Hadean crust survives. The rock cycle, driven by plate tectonics and solar energy, has been continuously reworking the Earth&rsquo;s surface for as long as the planet has existed.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The rock cycle is a continuous series of processes linking igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks; it is driven by geothermal energy (internal) and solar energy (surface) and has many parallel pathways, not one fixed route.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Gradualism (slow, continuous processes) explains most of the rock record; catastrophism (sudden events such as meteorite impacts) explains rare but dramatic punctuations in Earth history. Modern geology accepts both.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Geological processes operate at vastly different rates &mdash; from hours (volcanic eruptions) to tens of millions of years (subduction cycles) &mdash; and the rock cycle has been reworking Earth&rsquo;s crust continuously for around 4 billion years.</li>
</ul>
