--- DESCRIPTION ---
Choosing the right maps, graphs and statistics to reveal patterns in fieldwork data.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="lesson-notice" data-notice-title="Your school&rsquo;s fieldwork" hidden><p>This lesson covers the general fieldwork skills, terminology, and exam-paper expectations that apply to every student. The specific sites, data, and methods your class actually used will be different &mdash; for the case study you&rsquo;ll write about in the exam, refer to your teacher&rsquo;s notes and the data you collected on the day.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Numbers to Meaning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Once your data sheets come back from the field, the next stages of the enquiry cycle are <strong>processing and presenting</strong> the evidence and then <strong>analysing</strong> it. The goal is to turn a column of raw numbers into a clear pattern that the reader can see, then explain what the pattern means and how strong it really is.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eduqas examiners reward two skills here. First, you must pick the right kind of graph or map for the data you have. Second, you must use the right kind of statistic to summarise it. The wrong choices hide the pattern; the right ones reveal it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down which graph type you would use for: pebble sizes by site, pedestrian flow over a day, and ages of shoppers at a centre.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Match the graph to the data type. Continuous data over time = line graph. Categories = bar chart. Bivariate (two variables) = scatter graph. Spatial pattern = choropleth or proportional symbol map. Frequency distribution = histogram.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Choosing the Right Graph</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that uses bars of different heights to compare totals between categories such as land-use types or pebble shapes.">bar chart</dfn> compares totals between separate categories — for example, the number of shops in each retail zone of a high street. The bars do not touch because the categories are independent. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that joins data points with a line to show how a variable changes over continuous time, such as temperature or river discharge.">line graph</dfn> joins data points with a line because the variable changes continuously over time, such as a hydrograph showing river discharge during a storm.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that plots paired data on two axes to show whether two variables are related, with each point representing one observation.">scatter graph</dfn> shows the relationship between two variables — for example, distance downstream against river width. A clear upward or downward trend suggests a positive or negative correlation. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A bar graph showing how a continuous variable is distributed across equal class intervals, with bars touching because the data is continuous.">histogram</dfn> shows how often values fall into equal class intervals, such as how many pebbles fall into each 10 mm size band. The bars touch because the data is continuous.</p>

    <span>Spatial Maps: Choropleth, Proportional and Flow-line</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">A choropleth map shades areas by value — darker shades for higher values such as deprivation index by ward. They are good for showing patterns across regions but hide variation within a single shaded area. A proportional symbol map uses circles or squares scaled to the value, useful for migration totals or city populations. A flow-line map uses arrows whose width matches the size of the flow, useful for traffic counts or river discharge between gauging stations.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">For a high-street pedestrian-flow study, you might place proportional circles on a sketch map of the town — one circle per count point, scaled to the number of pedestrians per minute. The size differences between circles make the pattern obvious at a glance.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Statistics: Central Tendency and Spread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Three measures summarise the ‘middle’ of a data set. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The arithmetic average, calculated by adding all values and dividing by the number of values.">mean</dfn> is the average. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The middle value when the data is placed in order from smallest to largest.">median</dfn> is the middle value once the data is sorted. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The most common value or class in a data set.">mode</dfn> is the most frequent value or category. Each suits different situations: the mean is sensitive to extreme values (one boulder pulls a pebble-size mean upwards), so the median is often a fairer measure for skewed natural data.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why the median is sometimes a better summary than the mean.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eduqas requires you to know mean, median, mode, range, quartiles and inter-quartile range. The inter-quartile range (IQR) measures the spread of the middle 50% of values, ignoring extremes.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Spread tells you how varied the data is. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between the largest and smallest value in a data set.">range</dfn> is largest minus smallest, but a single odd reading can stretch it. The inter-quartile range (IQR) is the difference between the upper and lower quartile — the middle 50% — so it ignores outliers and gives a fairer picture. A small IQR means readings cluster tightly around the median; a large IQR means they vary widely.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Identifying Patterns and Trends</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Analysis means describing what the graph or map shows and asking why. Useful sentence frames are: ‘The data shows a clear positive correlation between X and Y.’ ‘The pattern is strongest at site 4 and weakens towards site 7.’ ‘Most readings cluster between 25 and 40 mm, with one anomaly at 95 mm.’ Always include numerical evidence from your own results — not just ‘there is a trend’.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A data point that does not fit the general pattern of the rest of the data set.">anomaly</dfn> is a value that does not fit the trend. Geographers do not ignore anomalies — they investigate them. A river-width reading much wider than expected might mean a meander or human modification. A pebble much larger than its neighbours might have been dropped from a cliff fall rather than transported by longshore drift.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Linking Patterns to Geographical Theory</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Strong analysis links the pattern back to the geographical theory you used to write the hypothesis. If pebble size decreases downstream, that fits the Bradshaw model: attrition smooths and breaks pebbles as they are transported, so they get smaller and rounder away from the source. If pedestrian flow drops with distance from the town centre, that fits the bid-rent model: peripheral shops attract fewer visitors than the central honeypot.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Where the data does not fit the theory, say so. The Eduqas spec stresses that ‘geography can be messy’ — conclusions that admit unexpected results, and try to explain why, score higher than ones that pretend the data was perfect.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Drawing Lines and Trends</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">On scatter graphs, sketch a line of best fit by eye so the points are roughly balanced above and below it. The slope shows the strength of the relationship. A line of best fit also lets you <dfn class="term" data-def="Estimating a value within the range of the data, such as predicting a value between two measured points.">interpolate</dfn> — reading off a value within the data — or extrapolate beyond the data. Extrapolation is risky because the trend may not continue.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Quick Self-Check</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Before you move on to conclusions, ask three questions. Did you choose the right type of graph for the data? Did you use the right summary statistic, and explain why? Did you describe the pattern with figures from your own results, including any anomalies? If yes to all three, your analysis is on track.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The graph or map you choose must match the data type: bar charts for categories, line graphs for continuous time, scatter graphs for two variables, choropleth or flow-line maps for spatial patterns.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Mean, median and mode summarise the centre of a data set; range and inter-quartile range summarise the spread. The IQR ignores extremes and is often the fairest measure for skewed fieldwork data.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Strong analysis describes the pattern with numerical evidence, identifies anomalies and links the result back to a geographical theory or process.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">In the fieldwork paper, command words like ‘describe the pattern’ and ‘explain the trend’ carry different expectations. ‘Describe’ wants the shape, direction and any anomalies, with figures from the graph. ‘Explain’ wants you to link the pattern to a geographical process or theory. Always quote the highest, lowest and most unusual values when describing — vague answers like ‘it goes up’ lose the data marks. SPaG marks reward correct use of terms like ‘correlation’, ‘anomaly’ and ‘inter-quartile range’.</p>
