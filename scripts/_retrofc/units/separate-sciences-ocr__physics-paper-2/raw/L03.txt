--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reflection, refraction at boundaries, and how converging and diverging lenses form images.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reflection at Plane Surfaces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a wave strikes a smooth, flat surface, it is reflected. The key rule is that the <strong>angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection</strong> — both angles are measured from the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to a surface at the point where a wave or ray meets it. Angles of incidence and reflection are always measured from the normal.">normal</dfn>, which is an imaginary line at right angles to the surface at the point of contact.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane. During reflection, the wave speed, frequency and wavelength all remain unchanged &mdash; only the direction of travel changes. A plane (flat) mirror forms a <strong>virtual image</strong>: the image appears to be behind the mirror, is the same size as the object, is the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front, and is laterally inverted (left and right swapped).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the law of reflection. Then draw a quick diagram showing an incident ray, normal and reflected ray with the angles labelled.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection (both from the normal). During reflection, wave speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged. A plane mirror produces a virtual image the same size as and the same distance from the mirror as the object.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Refraction at Boundaries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The change in direction (and speed) of a wave as it passes from one medium into another with a different optical density.">Refraction</dfn> occurs when a wave crosses the boundary between two media with different optical densities. The wave changes speed, and if it hits the boundary at an angle (not along the normal), it also changes direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The rules of refraction are:<br>• When a wave passes from a <strong>less dense medium into a denser medium</strong> (e.g. air into glass), it <strong>slows down</strong> and bends <strong>towards the normal</strong>.<br>• When a wave passes from a <strong>denser medium into a less dense medium</strong> (e.g. glass into air), it <strong>speeds up</strong> and bends <strong>away from the normal</strong>.<br>• The <strong>frequency</strong> stays the same at both sides of the boundary. The <strong>wavelength</strong> changes because \(\lambda = \frac{v}{f}\) and the speed changes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Different wavelengths of light refract by different amounts when entering glass. Violet light slows more than red light, causing the colours to spread. This is how a prism separates white light into its spectrum.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">1. Draw the boundary between the two media (e.g. air and glass) as a solid line.<br>2. Draw the <strong>normal</strong> at the point of incidence &mdash; a dashed line at 90&deg; to the boundary.<br>3. Draw the incident ray and label the angle of incidence (\(i\)) from the normal.<br>4. Draw the refracted ray on the other side of the boundary. If the ray enters glass from air, it bends <em>towards</em> the normal; the angle of refraction (\(r\)) is smaller than \(i\).<br>5. If the ray exits glass into air, it bends <em>away</em> from the normal; \(r > i\).<br>6. Label both media and indicate the direction of travel with arrows.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Lenses &mdash; Converging and Diverging</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Lenses work by refracting light at two curved surfaces. There are two types:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thicker in the middle than at the edges. Parallel rays passing through it converge (meet) at the focal point on the other side.">converging lens</dfn> (convex lens) is thicker in the middle. Parallel rays of light passing through it bend and converge at the <dfn class="term" data-def="The point at which parallel rays of light converge after passing through a converging lens, or appear to diverge from after passing through a diverging lens.">focal point</dfn> on the far side. The distance from the lens centre to the focal point is the <strong>focal length</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thinner in the middle than at the edges. Parallel rays passing through it diverge (spread apart), appearing to come from a focal point on the same side as the incoming light.">diverging lens</dfn> (concave lens) is thinner in the middle. Parallel rays passing through it spread apart (diverge) and appear to come from a focal point on the <em>same side as the incoming light</em>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state the two types of lens and explain what happens to parallel rays of light as they pass through each type.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Converging (convex) lens: thicker in the middle; parallel rays meet at the focal point. Diverging (concave) lens: thinner in the middle; parallel rays spread apart, appearing to come from a focal point on the same side as the light source.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Ray Diagrams for Converging Lenses &mdash; Three Object Positions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">To draw a converging-lens ray diagram, use <strong>three principal rays</strong> from the top of the object:<br>1. A ray <strong>parallel to the principal axis</strong> &rarr; refracts through the far focal point.<br>2. A ray through the <strong>optical centre</strong> of the lens &rarr; passes straight through without bending.<br>3. A ray through the <strong>near focal point</strong> &rarr; refracts to travel parallel to the principal axis.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The image depends on the object&rsquo;s distance from the lens (measured in multiples of the focal length \(f\)):</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Object beyond 2f</strong>: the image is <em>real, inverted and diminished</em> (smaller than the object). This is how a camera forms an image on its sensor.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Object at 2f</strong>: the image is <em>real, inverted and the same size</em> as the object, formed at 2f on the other side.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Object between f and 2f</strong>: the image is <em>real, inverted and magnified</em> (larger than the object). This is how a projector works.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Object closer than f</strong>: the refracted rays diverge and do not meet on the far side. When extended back, they appear to come from a point on the same side as the object &mdash; a <em>virtual, upright and magnified</em> image. This is how a magnifying glass works.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Real and Virtual Images</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">A <strong>real image</strong> is formed where light rays actually meet. It can be projected onto a screen. Images formed by converging lenses when the object is beyond the focal point are real.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">A <strong>virtual image</strong> is formed where rays appear to come from when extended backwards &mdash; no light actually passes through the image position. It cannot be projected onto a screen. The image in a plane mirror and the magnifying glass image are both virtual. Virtual images are always upright; real images are always inverted (for a single converging lens).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n24">The <strong>power of a lens</strong> is defined as \(P = \frac{1}{f}\), where \(f\) is the focal length in metres and power \(P\) is measured in dioptres (D). A short focal length means a high-power (strongly converging) lens. For a diverging lens, the focal length is negative, so the power is negative. Opticians prescribe spectacle lenses in dioptres: a \(+2\) D lens has a focal length of 0.5 m; a \(-4\) D lens has a focal length of &minus;0.25 m.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection (from the normal); speed, frequency and wavelength unchanged. Plane mirror image is virtual, same size and distance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Refraction: wave slows and bends towards the normal entering a denser medium; speeds up and bends away when leaving. Frequency constant; wavelength changes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Converging lenses form real, inverted images when the object is beyond the focal point; a virtual, magnified image when the object is within one focal length (magnifying glass effect). Diverging lenses always form virtual, upright, diminished images.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Ray diagram questions are often worth 3&ndash;4 marks. You must draw all three principal rays accurately and clearly mark the image where two or more rays meet (or appear to meet). Label the image as real/virtual, upright/inverted and magnified/diminished/same size &mdash; all three descriptors earn marks. For refraction, always draw the normal first as a dashed line perpendicular to the boundary, then state which way the ray bends and why. A common error is bending the ray the wrong way &mdash; remember: slowing down = bends towards the normal.</p>
