Renewable and non-renewable energy sources, efficiency calculations, and reducing energy waste.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Renewable and Non-Renewable Energy Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every power station or device that generates electricity must draw on an energy source. These sources split into two broad categories. <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that will run out because they are consumed far faster than they can be replaced, including fossil fuels and nuclear fuel.">Non-renewable</dfn> sources &mdash; coal, oil, natural gas and nuclear fuel &mdash; will eventually run out. Fossil fuels formed over millions of years from ancient organic matter; once burned they are gone. Nuclear fuel (uranium) is extracted from finite ore deposits. <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that are naturally replenished and will not run out, including solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal and biomass.">Renewable</dfn> sources are naturally replenished: solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal and biomass.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list at least five renewable energy sources and two non-renewable sources from memory, then classify nuclear as non-renewable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Non-renewable sources (fossil fuels and nuclear) will eventually run out. Renewable sources (solar, wind, hydro, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are replenished naturally. Nuclear does not produce CO<sup>2</sup> during generation but creates radioactive waste.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Comparing Energy Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Each source has distinct advantages and disadvantages that must be weighed against each other:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Fossil fuels</strong> release carbon dioxide when burned, contributing to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which greenhouse gases such as CO2 trap heat energy in the Earth&rsquo;s atmosphere, causing global temperatures to rise.">greenhouse effect</dfn> and climate change. They also release sulfur dioxide (which causes acid rain) and particulates. Despite this, fossil fuels remain significant because they are reliable, energy-dense and can generate electricity on demand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Nuclear power</strong> produces no CO<sup>2</sup> during generation and can run continuously as a baseload source. The drawback is radioactive waste, which remains hazardous for very long periods and requires secure storage. Nuclear stations also carry a small but serious risk of accidents.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Renewable sources</strong> produce little or no CO<sup>2</sup> during operation, making them essential for reducing carbon emissions. Their main challenges are <dfn class="term" data-def="The unpredictable variation in output from sources such as wind and solar, which cannot generate electricity on demand when conditions are unfavourable.">intermittency</dfn> (wind and solar depend on weather) and, for some, limited suitable locations (tidal, geothermal).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Solar:</strong> no pollution during operation, negligible running costs. Disadvantages: depends on sunlight, large area needed for solar farms, panels are costly to manufacture.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Wind:</strong> no fuel cost, no direct emissions. Disadvantages: intermittent, visual impact, noise, potential harm to wildlife.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Hydroelectric:</strong> reliable, can respond quickly to changes in demand. Disadvantages: damming rivers floods valleys, displaces communities and disrupts habitats.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Tidal:</strong> highly predictable because tides are regular. Disadvantages: expensive infrastructure, limited suitable estuaries, risk to marine ecosystems.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Geothermal:</strong> reliable, low emissions, compact land use. Disadvantages: only viable where hot rocks are near the surface, high initial drilling costs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Biomass:</strong> theoretically carbon-neutral (CO<sup>2</sup> absorbed during plant growth equals CO<sup>2</sup> released during burning). Disadvantages: still produces pollutants, and land used for fuel crops competes with food production.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Calculating Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">No energy transfer is perfectly efficient. Some energy is always wasted &mdash; usually as heat or sound. <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of the total energy input that is usefully transferred to the desired output, expressed as a decimal or percentage.">Efficiency</dfn> tells us how much of the energy input is usefully transferred. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy transfer (J)}}{\text{total input energy transfer (J)}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Efficiency has no units and will always be between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). To express as a percentage, multiply the decimal answer by 100. For example, if a motor receives 500 J of electrical energy and transfers 350 J as useful kinetic energy, its efficiency = \(\frac{350}{500} = 0.70\), or 70%. The remaining 130 J is wasted, mostly as heat in the motor windings.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Efficiency Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">A wind turbine receives 2400 J of energy from the wind in one second. It transfers 960 J as useful electrical energy. Calculate its efficiency and identify the wasted energy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Step 1: Write the equation. \(\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{total input}}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Step 2: Substitute values. \(\text{efficiency} = \frac{960}{2400} = 0.40\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Step 3: Convert to percentage: \(0.40 \times 100 = 40\%\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Step 4: Wasted energy = 2400 &minus; 960 = 1440 J, mostly lost as heat due to friction in the bearings and turbulence in the air flow.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: a device has 800 J input and 200 J useful output. Write down the efficiency as a decimal and as a percentage without looking at the formula.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Efficiency = useful output energy &divide; total input energy. The answer is a decimal between 0 and 1 (multiply by 100 for a percentage). Efficiency can never exceed 1 (100%) because energy cannot be created.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Reducing Wasted Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Increasing efficiency means reducing the energy lost to unwanted pathways. Common techniques include:</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Lubrication:</strong> oil or grease between moving parts reduces friction, so less energy is wasted as heat. <strong>Insulation:</strong> wrapping pipes, boilers and buildings reduces energy lost as heat to the surroundings. <strong>Streamlining:</strong> shaping vehicles and turbine blades to reduce air resistance reduces energy lost to turbulence. <strong>Better materials:</strong> using low-resistance conductors in electric motors reduces resistive heating losses.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that represents the flow of energy through a system, with arrow widths proportional to the amount of energy in each pathway.">Sankey diagrams</dfn> display energy flows visually. The width of each arrow is proportional to the energy it represents. The main arrow splits into a useful output arrow and one or more wasted energy arrows. A more efficient device has a thicker useful output arrow relative to the total input.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The National Grid and Energy Transmission</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
    <p data-narration-id="n29">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of cables and transformers that carries electricity from power stations to homes, schools and businesses across the country.">National Grid</dfn> transmits electricity at very high voltage and low current. This matters because energy lost in cables depends on the current squared multiplied by resistance (\(P_{\text{lost}} = I^2 R\)). Keeping current low dramatically reduces heating losses along thousands of kilometres of cable.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Step-up transformers at power stations raise voltage to hundreds of thousands of volts for efficient long-distance transmission. Step-down transformers near homes and businesses reduce voltage to safe levels for appliances. Transmission efficiency is therefore very high compared with lower-voltage alternatives.</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Non-renewable sources (fossil fuels, nuclear) will eventually run out; renewable sources (solar, wind, hydro, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are naturally replenished but many are intermittent or location-dependent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Efficiency = useful output energy &divide; total input energy (decimal 0&ndash;1, or multiply by 100 for percentage). A more efficient device wastes less energy to heat, sound or other unwanted pathways.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Wasted energy can be reduced by lubrication (less friction), insulation (less heat loss) and streamlining (less air resistance). Sankey diagrams represent energy flows with arrow widths proportional to energy.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">When answering &lsquo;Calculate the efficiency&rsquo; questions, always write the equation first, then substitute values. Give your answer as a decimal unless the question specifically asks for a percentage. A common error is to write efficiency as a fraction greater than 1 &mdash; if your answer exceeds 1, you have the input and output the wrong way round. For &lsquo;Explain why&rsquo; questions about renewable versus non-renewable sources, give a balanced point (advantage and a limitation) for at least one source.</p>
