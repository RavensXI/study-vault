--- DESCRIPTION ---
The main construction trades, what each tradesperson does, and the safety regulations they follow.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The World of Construction Trades</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Construction is one of the largest and most diverse industries in the UK. Rather than one single job, the sector is made up of many skilled <dfn class="term" data-def="Skilled workers who specialise in one area of construction, such as bricklaying, plastering or electrical installation.">trades</dfn> — each responsible for a different part of a building project. Whether constructing a new house or restoring a historic church, skilled tradespeople work from plans and specifications and follow safety regulations throughout. Understanding what each trade does is essential for anyone entering the industry.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Bricklaying</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A tradesperson who constructs walls and structures from bricks or blocks, using mortar and traditional bonding patterns.">bricklayer</dfn> builds walls and structures from bricks or blocks. They work from drawings and written specifications, spreading layers of mortar and carefully placing each brick or block in position. After laying each course, the bricklayer checks that the work is perfectly horizontal (level) and vertical (plumb) using a spirit level. Walls are laid in traditional bonding patterns — such as stretcher bond or English bond — which distribute the load and give the structure strength. Correct bonding also produces a neat, professional finish.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Stonemasonry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A tradesperson who dresses, carves and lays natural stone, including dry-stone walling, and who repairs traditional stone features on existing buildings.">stonemason</dfn> works with natural stone rather than manufactured bricks. They dress (shape) stone blocks using chisels and other specialist tools, and carve decorative features such as window surrounds, cornices and column capitals. Stonemasons also construct dry-stone walling — walls built without mortar that are common in rural areas — and repair and clean the stone mouldings and ornamental features found on older and historic buildings. This trade is closely linked to heritage conservation work.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Plastering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A plasterer applies wet finishes and protective coverings to both internal and external surfaces. On the outside of a building, the plasterer applies render to protect the masonry from weather. Inside, they skim walls and ceilings with a smooth coat of finishing plaster. Plasterers also carry out <strong>dry lining</strong> — fixing plasterboard sheets to internal stud walls as an alternative to wet plaster — which is a fast, common method in modern construction. Skilled plasterers can also replicate traditional ornamental plasterwork, using moulds and casts to produce decorative ceiling roses and cornices for heritage projects.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Remember that plastering covers both wet (render, skim coat) and dry (plasterboard) methods, as well as ornamental work on heritage buildings.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plasterers work on external walls (render), internal walls and ceilings (wet plaster skim), internal studs (dry lining with plasterboard), and heritage features (ornamental plasterwork using moulds and casts).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Carpentry and Joinery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Carpentry and joinery are related but distinct trades. A <strong>joiner</strong> works primarily in a workshop, cutting and joining pieces of timber to make components such as doors, window frames, staircases and fitted furniture. These items are then delivered to site. A <strong>carpenter</strong> works on site, installing the components the joiner has made, as well as structural timber elements including floor joists, floorboards and roof trusses. Carpenters also construct wall partitions, fit interior woodwork such as skirting boards and kitchen units, and can replicate traditional ornamental mouldings. The key distinction is simple: joiners make components in the workshop; carpenters fix them on site.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">On a typical housing project, a joiner in a timber yard would cut, assemble and finish a staircase off site, ensuring every tread and riser is precisely made to the architect's drawings. The carpenter would then take that finished staircase to the building and fix it securely in position, fitting handrails, balusters and nosings. The same split applies to kitchens: the joiner makes the carcasses and drawer boxes; the carpenter installs them level and plumb, trims worktops and hangs doors. In exam questions, always use the correct term — do not call a site-based tradesperson a "joiner" or a workshop-based one a "carpenter".</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Electrical Installation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">An electrician installs, inspects and tests electrical services and equipment throughout a building. This includes running cables, fitting consumer units (fuse boards), wiring sockets, switches, lighting circuits and specialist equipment such as security systems or fire alarms. Electricians must follow relevant safety regulations at all times — electrical faults are a leading cause of serious injury and fire on construction sites. All electrical installation work in the UK must comply with BS 7671 (the IET Wiring Regulations), and qualified electricians are responsible for testing and certifying that the work is safe before a building is occupied.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Plumbing Installation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A plumber installs the systems that supply and remove water in a building. This covers cold-water and hot-water supply, sanitation systems (toilets, basins and baths), boilers, and central heating pipework and radiators. Plumbers follow safety regulations relevant to their trade — for example, any work on gas appliances or gas pipework must be carried out by a person registered with <dfn class="term" data-def="The official UK gas safety register; only Gas Safe registered engineers are legally permitted to work on gas appliances and pipework.">Gas Safe</dfn>, the UK's official gas safety register. As well as pipework, plumbers also install traditional lead flashings and lead roof coverings on heritage buildings, making the skill set broader than most people realise.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Gas Safe registration is the key safety regulation for plumbers working with gas. An unregistered person must not carry out gas work — this is a legal requirement.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plumbers install cold water, hot water, sanitation, boilers and central heating. Any work on gas systems must be carried out by a Gas Safe registered engineer — this is a legal requirement. Plumbers also install lead flashings and roof coverings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Painting and Decorating</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A painter and decorator prepares and applies paint, wallpaper and other decorative finishes to interior surfaces. On the outside of a building, they apply paint and protective finishes to external surfaces such as rendered walls, timber windows, doors and metalwork. Preparation is a major part of the role — this includes sanding, filling, priming and masking before any finish is applied. Like all construction trades, painters and decorators must follow relevant safety regulations, particularly when working at height on ladders or scaffolding, or when using chemical strippers and solvent-based paints.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Flooring and Tiling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A floor layer (sometimes called a floor and wall tiler) carries out a range of finishing tasks. They prepare surfaces using levelling compounds to create a flat, even base before any finish is applied. Once the subfloor is ready, they lay carpet and vinyl floor finishes, cutting and fixing them neatly to the walls and around obstacles. They also install ceramic wall and floor tiles in kitchens, bathrooms and other wet areas, mixing and applying adhesive and grout to the correct depth. Getting the preparation right is crucial — uneven subfloors cause tiles to crack and carpet to ripple over time.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Every tradesperson on a construction project works from a set of architect's drawings and a written specification. The drawings show dimensions, layouts and details; the written spec document describes the materials, standards and methods to be used. Working from these documents ensures that every trade delivers work that fits with the other trades' work — for example, a plumber must position pipework precisely where the architect has planned so that the plasterer can board over it and the tiler can finish around it. Safety regulations, such as Building Regulations and manufacturer's guidance, are also followed throughout, protecting workers, future occupants and the general public. Without this shared framework, buildings would not be safe or fit for purpose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Construction is made up of specialist trades: bricklaying, stonemasonry, plastering, carpentry and joinery, electrical installation, plumbing, painting and decorating, and flooring and tiling.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">A joiner makes timber components in a workshop; a carpenter installs them on site — this distinction is frequently tested in exams.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">All trades work from plans and specifications and must follow safety regulations relevant to their work — for example, Gas Safe registration for plumbers working with gas, and BS 7671 compliance for electricians.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">The most common mistake in trade questions is confusing carpenters and joiners, or confusing the plumber's role with the electrician's. Learn the joiner/carpenter split precisely: joiners make components in a workshop; carpenters fix them on site. For gas work, always name Gas Safe as the required registration — writing "a qualified person" alone is not specific enough to earn the mark. In extended-answer questions about roles and responsibilities, link each trade's work back to following plans, specifications and safety regulations, as this shows understanding of how the industry is organised.</p>
