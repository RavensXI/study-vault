--- DESCRIPTION ---
Construction site hazards, risk assessments, and the legislation employers must follow to protect workers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Construction Sites Are Hazardous</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Construction is consistently ranked among the most dangerous industries in the UK. Workers, employers and members of the public can all be placed at risk on or near a building site, and understanding those risks is the first step towards controlling them. Every person on site — from the site manager to the newest apprentice — must understand the hazards they face and the rules that exist to protect them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Risks on Construction Sites</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Employees working on construction sites face four main categories of risk. First, <strong>heavy plant and construction vehicles</strong> — such as excavators, dumper trucks and mobile cranes — move around sites carrying heavy loads. A worker in a vehicle's blind spot faces a serious risk of being struck or crushed. Second, <strong>working at height</strong> is one of the leading causes of death in construction; falling from a scaffold, ladder or unguarded edge can result in fatal injuries. Third, <strong>manual handling</strong> — lifting, carrying, pushing or pulling heavy or awkward loads — causes a high proportion of musculoskeletal injuries in the industry. Fourth, <dfn class="term" data-def="Hazards caused by wet, uneven or obstructed walking surfaces that cause a person to lose their footing and fall.">slips, trips and falls</dfn> on level ground — caused by wet surfaces, trailing cables or uneven ground — are among the most frequent causes of workplace injury on site.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Employers</strong> have a legal responsibility for the safe operation of construction sites. They are accountable for the systems, equipment and procedures in place, and can be prosecuted or fined in the event of accidents or incidents. <strong>Members of the public</strong> can also be put at risk when close to an active construction site — from falling objects, site traffic emerging onto public roads, and hazardous materials such as cement dust or asbestos. Sites must be securely fenced and clearly signed to prevent unauthorised access by members of the public, including children.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Remember three groups at risk: employees (from plant, height, manual handling, slips/trips), employers (legal accountability for accidents), and the public (from falling objects, site traffic and harmful materials).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main risks to employees on construction sites are: heavy plant and construction vehicles, working at height, manual handling, and slips, trips and falls. Employers are legally responsible for site safety and can be prosecuted if accidents occur. The public must be protected from site traffic, falling objects and hazardous materials.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Following Correct Procedures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Simply knowing about hazards is not enough — everyone on site must follow the correct procedures. Procedures are the documented rules and steps that ensure contractors and employees work safely and prevent accidents. Examples include signing in and out of site, wearing the correct personal protective equipment in designated areas, following a specific method statement for a task, and attending site induction briefings. When procedures are ignored — for example, removing safety barriers around a floor opening to carry materials more easily — the risk of a serious accident increases significantly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Risk Assessments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal process of identifying hazards in a workplace, evaluating the likelihood and severity of harm, and deciding on control measures to reduce that risk.">risk assessment</dfn> is a formal examination of the hazards associated with a task or workplace, and the control measures needed to reduce risk to an acceptable level. There are two types relevant to construction. A <strong>general risk assessment</strong> covers the overall health and safety hazards present on the site — such as access routes, site housekeeping, fire escape routes and general site traffic. A <strong>specific risk assessment</strong> is written for a particular hazardous activity, such as working at height on a scaffold, carrying out manual handling of heavy blocks, or working in a noisy environment. Both types must identify the hazard, assess who might be harmed and how, and set out appropriate control measures.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A control measure is any action, barrier or system that reduces the risk of harm. Effective control measures are chosen by following the hierarchy of control — a priority order that tries to eliminate or reduce the hazard at source before relying on personal protective equipment (PPE) as a last resort. At the top of the hierarchy: eliminate the hazard if possible (e.g. design out the need to work at height by pre-assembling components at ground level). Next: substitute the hazard with something safer (e.g. use a less hazardous chemical). Then: engineering controls such as guarding or extraction systems; administrative controls such as safe systems of work and limited working hours; and finally PPE. Examiners expect students to understand that PPE is the last line of defence, not the first choice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Health and Safety at Work Act</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The primary piece of UK legislation governing workplace health and safety, placing duties on employers to protect the health, safety and welfare of their employees and others affected by their work.">Health and Safety at Work Act</dfn> (1974) is the cornerstone of workplace safety law in the UK. It places clear duties on employers to protect the health, safety and welfare of workers, visitors and members of the public. Under this Act, employers in construction are legally required to prepare <strong>risk assessments</strong>, decide what <strong>control measures</strong> are necessary, and prevent or control exposure to hazards. They must also ensure that the <strong>hierarchy of control</strong> is followed when selecting those measures, <strong>monitor</strong> the level of exposure to hazards over time, prepare <strong>accident procedures</strong> so that incidents are dealt with promptly and correctly, and provide adequate <strong>training and supervision</strong> to all employees so they can carry out their work safely.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Learn the six employer duties under the Health and Safety at Work Act: risk assessments, control measures, preventing/controlling exposure, hierarchy of control, monitoring, accident procedures, training and supervision.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under the Health and Safety at Work Act, employers must: prepare risk assessments; decide on control measures; prevent or control exposure to hazards; follow the hierarchy of control; monitor exposure levels; have accident procedures in place; and train and supervise employees.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">COSHH Regulations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Regulations requiring employers to assess and control workers' exposure to hazardous substances such as dust, chemicals, fumes and biological agents.">Control of Substances Hazardous to Health (COSHH) regulations</dfn> sit alongside the Health and Safety at Work Act and deal specifically with hazardous substances. Construction sites contain many substances that can harm health — including cement dust (which can cause dermatitis and respiratory disease), silica dust from cutting stone or concrete (which causes a serious lung disease called silicosis), solvent fumes from adhesives and paints, and asbestos fibres in older buildings. Under COSHH, employers must identify hazardous substances on site, assess the risk they pose, and put in place control measures to prevent or reduce workers' exposure. This might mean using safer substitute products, providing local exhaust ventilation, or supplying appropriate PPE such as dust masks or gloves.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why is silica dust particularly dangerous on construction sites?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Respirable crystalline silica (RCS) is released when cutting, grinding or drilling materials that contain silica — including concrete, stone, brick and mortar. The particles are extremely fine and invisible to the naked eye, meaning a worker can inhale a dangerous amount without realising it. Over time, silica dust causes silicosis — permanent and irreversible scarring of the lungs that reduces breathing capacity and can be fatal. It is also linked to lung cancer. Because the damage occurs slowly, workers may not notice symptoms for years. COSHH assessments for any cutting or grinding task must identify RCS as a hazard, and control measures typically include wet cutting (using water to suppress dust), on-tool extraction, and where necessary a suitable dust mask rated for RCS (a P3 or FFP3 standard). The insidious nature of silica dust makes COSHH compliance critically important in construction.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Construction sites carry significant risks for employees (heavy plant, working at height, manual handling, slips/trips/falls), employers (legal accountability) and the public (site traffic, falling objects, hazardous materials).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Risk assessments — both general (whole-site) and specific (task-level) — identify hazards and set out control measures to reduce risk, following the hierarchy of control.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Health and Safety at Work Act requires employers to assess risks, implement control measures, monitor hazards, prepare accident procedures, and train and supervise workers. COSHH regulations add specific duties around hazardous substances such as dust and fumes.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">Questions on legislation often ask you to name the Act or regulations and state what they require employers to do. Always name the Health and Safety at Work Act specifically — do not write "health and safety law" alone, as this is too vague to earn the mark. Similarly, name COSHH when the question involves hazardous substances such as dust, fumes or chemicals. For risk assessment questions, make sure you distinguish between general (whole-site) and specific (task-level) assessments, and always relate control measures back to the hierarchy — eliminating or reducing the hazard at source is always preferable to relying on PPE.</p>
