<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 1: The Sound of the Shell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with Ralph and Piggy emerging from the jungle after a plane crash. Ralph is described as “the boy with fair hair” — immediately associated with light and goodness. Piggy, overweight, asthmatic, and wearing thick spectacles, is the opposite: physically vulnerable but intellectually sharp. Their discovery of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A large spiral seashell that Ralph uses to call assemblies. It symbolises democracy, order and the rule of law.">conch</dfn> shell on the beach is the novel’s first pivotal moment. Ralph blows it, and the sound summons the other survivors from across the island.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The conch immediately becomes a symbol of <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government where power is held by the people, who express their will through free discussion and voting.">democracy</dfn> and civilised order. Whoever holds the conch has the right to speak without interruption. This echoes the British parliamentary tradition that Golding’s readers would recognise — the idea that civilised people settle disagreements through debate, not force. Ralph instinctively grasps this: “I’ll give the conch to the next person to speak. He can hold it when he’s speaking.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does the conch symbolise, and what happens to it in Chapter 11?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The conch shell is the novel’s most important symbol. It represents democratic governance — free speech, fair turns, and collective decision-making. As Jack’s power grows, the conch loses authority. When it shatters in Chapter 11, democracy dies with it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Election: Ralph vs Jack</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When the boys gather for their first assembly, Ralph is elected chief. Golding describes the vote: “the toy of voting” — a telling phrase suggesting that democracy, for these boys, is a game they are imitating from the adult world rather than something they truly understand. Ralph wins because of his physical presence (“there was a stillness about Ralph as he sat that marked him out”) and because he holds the conch. He looks like a leader.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Jack, arriving at the head of his <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of singers in a church or school, led by Jack Merridew. They later become his hunting tribe.">choir</dfn> — marching in military formation, wearing black cloaks — expects to be chosen. “I ought to be chief,” he says, “because I’m chapter chorister and head boy. I can sing C sharp.” His claim to leadership is based on institutional authority, not personal qualities. Golding signals immediately that Jack’s understanding of power is about rank and dominance, while Ralph’s is about consent and cooperation.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Ralph represents rational, democratic leadership. His priorities are sensible: build shelters, maintain a signal fire, establish rules. He understands that rescue depends on cooperation. But Ralph’s leadership has a critical flaw — he lacks the rhetorical skill to hold the boys’ attention. He often loses his train of thought mid-assembly: “Ralph was puzzled by the shutter that flickered in his brain.” He needs Piggy to think through problems and lacks the charisma to inspire the group emotionally. This reflects Golding’s pessimistic view that rational leadership is fragile — it cannot compete with the visceral appeal of Jack’s authoritarianism.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Piggy: The Voice of Reason</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Piggy is the novel’s representative of <dfn class="term" data-def="The philosophical belief that reason and logic, rather than emotion or tradition, should guide human decisions.">rationalism</dfn> and scientific thinking. He is the first to suggest using the conch to call the others, the first to take a census of survivors, and the first to recognise the danger of the boys’ behaviour. His glasses — used to light the signal fire — represent technology and intellectual progress.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Yet Piggy is marginalised from the start. The boys mock his weight, his asthma, and his lower-class accent. Ralph betrays his trust by revealing his nickname in the first assembly. Golding uses Piggy to show how societies undervalue intelligence and dismiss those who don’t fit the physical ideal of power. Piggy repeatedly appeals to “the rules” — “What are we? Humans? Or animals? Or savages?” — but nobody listens until it is too late.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write Piggy's key question from memory: 'What are we?...' — then explain how Golding answers it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Piggy’s question “What are we? Humans? Or animals? Or savages?” articulates the novel’s central dilemma. Golding’s answer, delivered through the plot, is devastating: without civilisation’s restraints, the boundary between human and savage collapses.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Chapters 2–3: The Signal Fire and the First Cracks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In the second assembly, Ralph establishes a priority: keep a signal fire burning on the mountain so passing ships can see the smoke. This is the rational, civilised response to their situation — rescue depends on being seen. The boys enthusiastically build a fire, but their excitement quickly becomes destructive. They set a huge section of the forest ablaze, and a “littlun” with a birthmark on his face disappears — presumably killed. Nobody notices. The first death on the island is caused not by malice but by carelessness and unchecked excitement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By Chapter 3, the tensions between Ralph and Jack are clear. Ralph tries to build shelters with Simon while the other boys play. Jack becomes obsessed with hunting, spending hours alone in the jungle pursuing pigs. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A long, heavy knife used for cutting through thick vegetation, used by Jack to hunt pigs on the island.">signal fire</dfn> versus hunting represents the novel’s core conflict: rescue (civilisation, cooperation, long-term thinking) versus hunting (savagery, dominance, immediate gratification).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Simon: The Outsider from the Start</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Simon is introduced as a quiet, fainting boy from Jack’s choir. From the beginning, he is different. While Ralph builds shelters and Jack hunts, Simon retreats into a secret clearing in the forest where he sits alone among the butterflies and candle-bud flowers. Golding presents Simon as a spiritual, almost mystical figure — separate from both Ralph’s rationalism and Jack’s savagery. He is the only character who instinctively helps others: he gives fruit to the littluns and helps Ralph build.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Simon’s solitary nature and his connection to the natural world foreshadow his role as the novel’s moral visionary. He will be the one to discover the truth about the beast, and the one whose death most clearly demonstrates the boys’ complete moral collapse.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Littluns and the First Mention of the Beast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The youngest boys — the “<dfn class="term" data-def="The youngest boys on the island, approximately aged six. Their fears about the beast reflect the primal terror that drives the group's descent into savagery.">littluns</dfn>” — are the first to speak of a “beastie” or “snake-thing” in the jungle. The older boys dismiss this as childish fear, but Golding plants the seed that will grow into the novel’s most powerful theme. Ralph insists “there isn’t a beast!” and Jack boasts that if there were, “we’d hunt it and kill it.” Neither response is adequate. The beast cannot be killed because, as Simon will later understand, it is not an external creature but a darkness within the boys themselves.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">The conch symbolises democratic order — whoever holds it may speak. Its power depends on collective agreement, which gradually erodes as Jack challenges Ralph’s authority.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Ralph represents rational, cooperative leadership; Jack represents authoritarian dominance. Their rivalry drives the novel’s plot and embodies the civilisation vs savagery theme.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Piggy’s intelligence is ignored because he lacks physical presence, showing how societies undervalue rational thinking in favour of charisma and strength.</li></ul>
