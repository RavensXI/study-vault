<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Context Matters for <em>Lord of the Flies</em></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the Edexcel exam, AO3 requires you to show understanding of the social, historical and cultural context of the novel. For <em>Lord of the Flies</em>, this means understanding the post-war world Golding was writing in, his personal experience of human cruelty, and his deliberate rejection of the optimistic adventure stories that came before. Context must be woven into your analysis — never bolted on as a separate paragraph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">William Golding published <em>Lord of the Flies</em> in 1954, just nine years after the end of World War II and at the height of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The political and military tension between the USA and USSR from approximately 1947 to 1991, which included the threat of nuclear war.">Cold War</dfn>. The novel opens with a group of British schoolboys stranded on a deserted island after a nuclear <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced removal of civilians from a dangerous area, particularly during wartime. British children were evacuated from cities during WWII.">evacuation</dfn>. The plane carrying them has been shot down. From the very first page, Golding places his story in a world shaped by war and the threat of total annihilation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what wartime experience did Golding have, and what conclusion did he draw about human nature?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>William Golding served in the Royal Navy during World War II, commanding a rocket-launching ship during the D-Day landings. He later said: “I began to see what people were capable of doing. Anyone who moved through those years without understanding that man produces evil as a bee produces honey, must have been blind or wrong in the head.”</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Cold War and Nuclear Anxiety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">By 1954, the world lived under the shadow of nuclear weapons. The USA had dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, and both the USA and Soviet Union were now testing hydrogen bombs thousands of times more powerful. The arms race created a climate of existential dread — people genuinely feared that civilisation could be destroyed overnight. School children practised “duck and cover” drills, and governments built fallout shelters.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Golding embeds this anxiety directly into the novel. The boys are being evacuated because of a nuclear war. Piggy refers to “the atom bomb” in Chapter 1. The “beast from air” that terrifies the boys in Chapter 6 is actually a dead parachutist from an aerial battle — the adult world’s war literally falls into the children’s paradise. The novel asks: if adults are destroying the world through warfare, what hope is there for the children they leave behind?</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The dead parachutist who lands on the mountain in Chapter 6 is one of Golding’s most powerful symbols. The boys mistake the decaying body for “the beast,” but the real beast is what the figure represents: the violence of the adult world. The parachutist is a casualty of the very war the boys were fleeing. His tangled parachute lines make him rise and fall with the wind, creating the illusion of a living creature — a grotesque puppet controlled by the same forces (fear, violence) that will soon control the boys.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Only Simon, the novel’s moral visionary, discovers the truth. When he climbs the mountain and frees the parachutist’s lines, he symbolically confronts the reality of human evil. But the other boys are too far gone to hear his message — they kill him before he can share it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Golding’s Response to <em>The Coral Island</em></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Golding wrote <em>Lord of the Flies</em> as a deliberate response to R.M. Ballantyne’s <em>The Coral Island</em> (1857), a Victorian adventure novel in which three British boys — Ralph, Jack and Peterkin — are shipwrecked on a tropical island. In Ballantyne’s story, the boys thrive: they build shelters, hunt, and remain thoroughly civilised. Evil comes only from outside — from <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that British civilisation and Christianity were morally superior, often used to justify colonial expansion.">savages</dfn> and pirates. The message is clear: British boys are inherently good.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Golding deliberately borrows the names Ralph and Jack to signal his counter-argument. In his version, British boys are <em>not</em> inherently good. Without the structures of civilisation — adults, rules, punishment — the boys descend into violence, cruelty and murder. Evil does not come from outside; it comes from within. Golding later said: “The theme is an attempt to trace the defects of society back to the defects of human nature.”</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what Victorian belief did Golding reject, and what does Lord of the Flies argue instead?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Golding rejected the Victorian belief that British civilisation made people inherently good. <em>Lord of the Flies</em> argues the opposite: civilisation is a thin veneer, and without its restraints, humans — even children — are capable of terrible savagery. This is Golding’s concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Christian belief that all humans are born sinful as a result of Adam and Eve's disobedience in the Garden of Eden.">original sin</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Original Sin and Human Nature</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Golding was deeply influenced by the Christian doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="A story or narrative that uses symbolic characters and events to convey a deeper moral or philosophical meaning.">original sin</dfn> — the belief that all humans are born with an innate capacity for evil, inherited from the Fall of Man in the Garden of Eden. This theological idea runs through every page of the novel. The island itself functions as a kind of Eden: a paradise with fruit, fresh water and no predators. But the boys, like Adam and Eve, corrupt it. The “scar” left by the crashed plane in Chapter 1 is the first mark of human destruction on the landscape.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Island as a Fallen Eden</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The island is described in Edenic terms at the start: “the long scar smashed into the jungle” (the crash) gives way to descriptions of “a great platform of pink granite,” lagoons, fruit trees and coral reefs. It appears to be paradise. But the “scar” is already there — a wound inflicted by human technology. As the novel progresses, the boys set fire to the island, kill its pigs, and turn it into a wasteland. By Chapter 12, the entire island is ablaze. The destruction of the island mirrors the boys’ own moral destruction — they have turned paradise into hell.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">This parallels the broader context: the adult world is destroying itself through nuclear war while the children destroy their island through savagery. Golding suggests that human nature is the constant — adults and children are equally capable of evil.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Golding’s Life and Influences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Golding was born in Cornwall in 1911 and taught at a boys’ school before the war. His experience of teaching boys gave him insight into how children behave when adult authority is relaxed. After the war, he returned to teaching and began writing <em>Lord of the Flies</em>, which was rejected by 21 publishers before Faber and Faber accepted it in 1954. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1983, partly for this novel’s unflinching exploration of human nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Golding was influenced by the horrors of WWII: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The systematic murder of six million Jews by the Nazi regime during World War II.">Holocaust</dfn>, the atomic bombings, and the revelation that ordinary people — not monsters — had committed these atrocities. The philosopher Hannah Arendt called this the “banality of evil” — the idea that evil acts are committed by ordinary individuals following orders. Golding dramatises this through his schoolboys: Roger, who tortures and kills, is not a demon. He is a child who has lost all restraint.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Golding wrote <em>Lord of the Flies</em> in 1954 as a direct response to the optimistic Victorian adventure novel <em>The Coral Island</em>, arguing that evil comes from within humans, not from outside.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">His wartime experience in the Royal Navy, witnessing D-Day and the aftermath of WWII, convinced him that all humans are capable of evil — the novel’s central argument.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Cold War nuclear anxiety, the doctrine of original sin, and the island-as-Eden symbolism all provide essential context for exam essays.</li></ul>
