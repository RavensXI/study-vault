--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 10: The Shell and the Glasses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The morning after Simon’s murder, Ralph and Piggy sit in stunned silence. Ralph whispers: “That was murder.” He confronts the reality of what they have done. Piggy, desperate to deny it, insists: “It was dark. There was that — that bloody dance. There was lightning and thunder and rain. We was scared!” His excuses are psychologically realistic — people do rationalise their worst acts — but Golding makes clear that both boys know the truth. The difference between Ralph and Jack is not that Ralph is incapable of violence (he participated in the killing) but that Ralph has a <dfn class="term" data-def="The inner sense of right and wrong that makes a person feel guilt after committing a harmful act.">conscience</dfn> that forces him to confront his own guilt.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Meanwhile, Jack has consolidated power at Castle Rock. He rules through fear and violence, punishing boys who displease him. The theft he orchestrates next is both practical and deeply symbolic: his hunters raid Ralph’s camp at night and steal Piggy’s glasses. They do not take the conch — it no longer matters to them. They take the glasses because they need fire, and fire now means power.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what do Piggy's glasses symbolise, and what does their theft mean for the balance of power on the island?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The theft of Piggy’s glasses is the theft of rational thought and technology. Fire — the most important resource on the island — is now entirely in Jack’s hands. Ralph is left with the conch, a symbol of democratic authority that nobody obeys any more. Power has shifted completely from reason to force.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Chapter 11: Castle Rock — The Death of Piggy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ralph, Piggy, and the twins Sam and Eric march to Castle Rock to demand the glasses back. Piggy carries the conch, still believing it has authority: “I’m going to him with this conch. I’m going to hold it out. Look, I’m goin’ to say, you’re stronger than I am and you haven’t got asthma. You can see, I’m goin’ to say, and with both eyes. But I don’t ask for my glasses back, not as a favour. I ask you because what’s right is right.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Piggy’s appeal to “what’s right” is the novel’s most powerful statement of civilised values. He appeals to justice, reason and fairness. But justice means nothing at Castle Rock. When Piggy stands on the ledge holding the conch and asks “Which is better — to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill?”, <dfn class="term" data-def="A boy who represents the most extreme form of cruelty in the novel, escalating from throwing stones to committing murder.">Roger</dfn> deliberately levers a boulder from the cliff above. The rock strikes Piggy, who falls forty feet onto the rocks below. “His head opened and stuff came out and turned red. Piggy’s arms and legs twitched a bit, like a pig’s after it has been killed.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Conch Shatters: Democracy Dies</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The same boulder that kills Piggy also shatters the conch: “the conch exploded into a thousand white fragments and ceased to exist.” Golding’s phrasing is deliberate — the conch does not merely break; it “ceased to exist.” Democracy, fairness, and the rule of law are not just damaged — they are annihilated. Piggy and the conch die together because they represent the same thing: the belief that human beings can govern themselves through reason and mutual agreement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Golding compares Piggy’s twitching body to “a pig’s after it has been killed” — reducing the most intelligent boy on the island to the level of the animals the hunters have been slaughtering. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison between two things using 'like' or 'as', drawing attention to a shared quality.">simile</dfn> completes Piggy’s dehumanisation. In a world ruled by savagery, intellect has no value, and a person can be reduced to meat.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Sam and Eric: The Pressure to Conform</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Twin boys who represent the ordinary person caught between good and evil. They are loyal to Ralph but eventually forced to join Jack’s tribe under threat of violence.">Sam and Eric</dfn> (Samneric) are captured and forced to join Jack’s tribe. They represent the ordinary person — neither exceptionally brave nor exceptionally cruel — who goes along with tyranny out of fear. Their capitulation is realistic and deeply sad. When Ralph later approaches them in secret, they warn him that Roger has “sharpened a stick at both ends,” but they cannot help him. The implication is chilling: the stick sharpened at both ends was used for the sow’s head — the “Lord of the Flies.” Jack and Roger intend to mount Ralph’s head as a trophy.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write Piggy's last words from memory, then explain what Roger's boulder represents as an answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Piggy’s last words frame the novel’s central question: “Which is better — to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill?” Roger’s boulder provides Golding’s devastating answer: in the absence of external authority, brute force always defeats reason. This reflects Golding’s post-war pessimism about human civilisation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Chapter 12: Cry of the Hunters — The Manhunt for Ralph</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The final chapter is a sustained nightmare. Ralph hides alone in the jungle while the entire tribe hunts him like an animal. Jack sets the island on fire to smoke him out, destroying the very environment that has sustained them. The fire is an act of complete, irrational destruction — it will burn their food, their shelters, everything. Jack no longer cares about survival; he cares only about total dominance. The boys have become, in Golding’s words, “savages.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Ralph runs through the burning jungle, “screaming, snarling, bloody.” Golding reduces the leader who once stood on the platform calling orderly assemblies to a terrified, hunted creature. The regression is complete: the boys are now indistinguishable from the animals they have been killing. Ralph bursts out of the trees onto the beach and collapses at the feet of a naval officer who has landed after spotting the smoke.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Ironic Rescue</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The naval officer’s arrival is deeply <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique where the intended meaning is the opposite of what is expressed, or where the outcome contradicts expectations.">ironic</dfn>. He expects to find a group of plucky British boys — a <em>Coral Island</em> scenario. Instead, he finds painted savages, a burning island, and children responsible for multiple murders. “I should have thought that a pack of British boys — you’re all British, aren’t you? — would have been able to put up a better show than that,” he says. His disappointment echoes the very Victorian assumptions Golding is dismantling.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The deeper irony is that the officer stands on a warship. He is part of the same adult war that caused the boys’ evacuation in the first place. He will “rescue” them from one conflict and return them to another. Golding’s final message is bleak: the adult world is no more civilised than the boys’ island. The officer’s trim uniform and polished buttons are just a more sophisticated version of Jack’s face paint — a mask of civilisation over the same human darkness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Ralph Weeps: The End of Innocence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The novel’s final image is devastating. Ralph weeps “for the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart, and the fall through the air of the true, wise friend called Piggy.” This sentence encapsulates the entire novel’s meaning. “The end of innocence” refers to the boys’ irreversible loss of childhood purity. “The darkness of man’s heart” is Golding’s thesis: evil is innate, not learned. “The fall through the air” connects Piggy’s death to the dead parachutist’s fall — both are casualties of human violence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">“His mind was crowded with memories” — Ralph now carries the weight of everything he has witnessed and done. He has lost his innocence not because he experienced violence but because he participated in it and recognises that he was capable of it. This is the novel’s most profound insight: self-knowledge is painful, and growing up means confronting the darkness within yourself.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Piggy’s death and the conch’s destruction are simultaneous — both reason and democracy are annihilated at Castle Rock. Roger’s deliberate act completes his arc from restrained stone-thrower to killer.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The naval officer’s rescue is deeply ironic: he stands on a warship, part of the same violence the boys have been recreating. The adult world is no better than the island.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Ralph weeps for “the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart” — a summary of Golding’s entire thesis that evil is innate and civilisation is fragile.</li></ul>
