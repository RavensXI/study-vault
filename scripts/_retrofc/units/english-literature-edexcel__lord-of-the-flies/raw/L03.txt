<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 4: Painted Faces and Long Hair</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter 4 marks a turning point in the novel. The title itself — “Painted Faces and Long Hair” — signals the boys’ physical transformation away from civilisation. Their hair has grown wild, their clothes are ragged, and crucially, Jack discovers the power of <dfn class="term" data-def="Coloured clay, charcoal and other natural substances applied to the face and body, used by Jack and his hunters to create a new, savage identity.">face paint</dfn>. He smears his face with coloured clay and charcoal and looks at his reflection in a pool of water. What he sees transforms him: “He looked in astonishment, no longer at himself but at an awesome stranger.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The mask of paint is one of Golding’s most powerful symbols. It <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing someone’s individual identity, often through uniforms, masks or rituals, making them feel anonymous and capable of acting without personal responsibility.">dehumanises</dfn> Jack — he is no longer himself but a “stranger.” Behind the mask, he is freed from the shame and guilt that civilisation imposes. The paint allows him to act without conscience. Golding writes that the mask “compelled them,” suggesting it has its own power — once the boys adopt these new identities, they are no longer in control of their own behaviour. This reflects Golding’s belief that civilised identity is a construct that can be stripped away.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what does Jack's face paint represent, and what does Golding suggest about removing individual identity?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jack’s face paint represents the destruction of civilised identity. Behind the mask, he becomes an “awesome stranger” — freed from shame, guilt and personal responsibility. Golding suggests that removing individual identity is the first step towards savagery.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Roger and the Stones: The Taboo of Civilisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">One of the most significant moments in Chapter 4 is easily overlooked. <dfn class="term" data-def="A boy who represents the most extreme form of human cruelty. He begins by throwing stones near Henry and ends the novel as a torturer and killer.">Roger</dfn> follows a littlun called Henry to the beach and begins throwing stones at him — but deliberately misses, aiming to land them in a circle around the child rather than hitting him. Golding explains why: “Roger’s arm was conditioned by a civilisation that knew nothing of him and was in ruins.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This is one of the novel’s most important lines. Roger <em>wants</em> to hurt Henry but is still held back by the invisible <dfn class="term" data-def="An unwritten social rule or prohibition that prevents certain behaviours, enforced by social pressure rather than formal law.">taboo</dfn> of the old world — the memory of parents, teachers, police, punishment. That civilisation is described as knowing “nothing of him” and being “in ruins” tells us that this restraint is fading. Roger’s arc from throwing stones <em>near</em> Henry to rolling a boulder onto Piggy in Chapter 11 traces the complete erosion of civilised restraint. He becomes the novel’s most chilling character because his cruelty is deliberate, escalating, and ultimately unchecked.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Roger’s Arc: From Restraint to Cruelty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Roger is Golding’s study of how ordinary people become capable of atrocity. In Chapter 4, he throws stones but misses deliberately. By Chapter 6, he is one of Jack’s most loyal followers. By Chapter 11, he deliberately levers a boulder onto Piggy, killing him. In Chapter 12, he “sharpened a stick at both ends” — implying he intends to put Ralph’s head on a stake like the pig’s. Each step is a further removal of civilised restraint.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Golding’s wartime experience is crucial context here. He witnessed ordinary men commit terrible acts when the structures of law and consequence were removed. Roger is not a born psychopath — he is a schoolboy whose capacity for cruelty is unleashed by the absence of adult authority. This is Golding’s most disturbing argument: that the potential for evil exists in everyone.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Missed Rescue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The novel’s most devastating early scene comes when a ship passes the island while the signal fire has gone out. Jack and his hunters, responsible for maintaining the fire, abandoned their duty to hunt a pig. They return triumphant, chanting “Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood” — covered in blood and intoxicated by their first kill. Ralph is furious: they have sacrificed their best chance of rescue for the thrill of hunting.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">This moment crystallises the novel’s central conflict. The signal fire represents civilisation, cooperation and hope; the hunt represents savagery, dominance and immediate gratification. Jack cannot understand Ralph’s anger because his values have already shifted. The kill was more important to him than rescue. Golding structures this scene as a point of no return — after this, the hunters’ loyalty to Jack deepens, and Ralph’s authority weakens irreversibly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write the hunters' chant from memory, then explain how it transforms hunting into ritual.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The hunters’ chant — “Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood” — transforms hunting from a practical activity into a savage <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of repeated actions performed in a ceremonial way, often with symbolic meaning. The boys' hunting rituals become increasingly violent and primal.">ritual</dfn>. The rhythmic, imperative verbs create an almost hypnotic quality. The chant bonds the hunters into a tribe and replaces rational thought with collective violence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Chapter 5: Beast from Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Ralph calls an assembly to confront the deteriorating situation. The shelters are falling apart, boys are defecating in the wrong places, and nobody maintains the fire. Ralph tries to reimpose order: “The rules are the only thing we’ve got!” But the assembly collapses when the littluns’ fear of the beast takes over the discussion. Jack dismisses Ralph’s rules — “Bollocks to the rules!” — and promises to hunt the beast down. Fear, not reason, is now driving the group.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">It is during this assembly that Simon tries to express a profound truth. He suggests that “maybe there is a beast… maybe it’s only us.” The other boys laugh at him. Simon’s insight — that the beast is not an external creature but the darkness within each boy — is the novel’s central revelation. But Golding shows that truth-tellers are ridiculed and eventually destroyed. Simon’s inability to articulate his idea clearly mirrors how difficult it is to confront uncomfortable truths about human nature.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">A dead parachutist lands on the mountain during the night. Sam and Eric, tending the signal fire, see the body moving in the wind and flee in terror, convinced they have seen the beast. The boys organise a search party. They explore <dfn class="term" data-def="A rocky outcrop at one end of the island that becomes Jack’s fortress. Its fortress-like qualities appeal to Jack’s desire for power and control.">Castle Rock</dfn> — a narrow, rocky outcrop at the end of the island. Jack is immediately drawn to it: “What a place for a fort!” He sees its military potential, not as shelter but as a seat of power.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Castle Rock contrasts sharply with the open beach where Ralph holds assemblies. The beach is democratic, exposed, communal. Castle Rock is defensible, exclusive, and threatening — a place suited to a dictatorship, not a democracy. Its later significance as Jack’s headquarters is foreshadowed here. Golding is mapping geography onto political systems: open spaces foster debate; fortresses foster tyranny.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Fear as a Tool of Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Chapters 4–6 show how fear becomes a political tool. Jack cannot defeat Ralph through argument, so he exploits the boys’ fear of the beast instead. By promising to protect them from the beast through hunting, Jack offers an emotional solution that Ralph’s rational approach cannot match. Jack declares: “The thing is — fear can’t hurt you any more than a dream.” Yet he simultaneously encourages fear to consolidate his own power. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A statement that contradicts itself, or a situation that seems absurd but reveals a deeper truth.">paradox</dfn> is central to Golding’s critique: authoritarian leaders need fear. They create it, sustain it, and offer themselves as the only solution.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Face paint symbolises the destruction of civilised identity — behind the mask, the boys become capable of violence they could not commit as individuals.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Roger’s stone-throwing scene shows civilisation’s restraints still operating but fading. His arm is “conditioned by a civilisation that was in ruins” — the taboo is temporary.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The missed rescue and the hunters’ chant crystallise the core conflict: rescue (civilisation) vs hunting (savagery). After this, Jack’s power grows and Ralph’s declines.</li></ul>
