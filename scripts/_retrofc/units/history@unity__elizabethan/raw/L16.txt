<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Your Site Question for 2027</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">AQA has named the Spanish Armada as the historic environment for your 2027 exam. That gives Paper 2 a guaranteed 16-mark question: a statement about the Armada, and the instruction to test it against a study of the site <em>and</em> your contextual knowledge. You already know the story of 1588 from Lesson 9. What this lesson adds is the site angle that actually earns marks: the Armada as a journey through four environments &mdash; the Channel, the Calais anchorage, the waters off Gravelines, and the route home &mdash; each of which took its own toll.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what four environments should structure a site answer on the Armada?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact &mdash; Four Environments</div>
  <p>Structure your site knowledge as four places: the <strong>Channel</strong> (a week of running battle), <strong>Calais</strong> (an exposed anchorage and fireships), <strong>Gravelines</strong> (gunnery and sandbanks), and the <strong>north-about route home</strong> (the storms that finished the fleet).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Environment One: The Channel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From the first sighting off Cornwall on 19 July 1588, warning beacons flashed the news along the coast, and the English fleet came out of Plymouth to begin a week-long running fight eastward. Two facts about this environment decided its character. First, the wind: the English worked themselves upwind of the Armada &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The upwind position in a sailing battle, allowing a fleet to decide when to engage and when to pull away.">weather gauge</dfn> &mdash; so every clash happened at a time and range of their choosing. Second, the shore: every port on that coast was English. Howard&rsquo;s ships topped up powder, shot and food as they fought past their own harbours, while Medina Sidonia&rsquo;s fleet had not one friendly quay between Spain and the Netherlands. The Armada&rsquo;s crescent formation held together superbly all week &mdash; but it was a fleet holding its breath, spending supplies it could not replace.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Environment Two: Calais &mdash; the Anchorage With No Harbour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The plan required the Armada to collect the Duke of Parma&rsquo;s invasion army from the Netherlands coast. But Spain controlled no deep-water port there: Parma&rsquo;s barges sat in shallow harbours behind sandbanks, blockaded by Dutch <dfn class="term" data-def="Small, shallow-draught Dutch warships that patrolled the coastal sandbanks off the Flemish ports.">flyboats</dfn>, and needed days more to embark. So the Armada anchored in the open roadstead off Calais &mdash; a stretch of exposed water, not a harbour &mdash; because the map offered nothing better. The English answer took one night. Eight fireships came down the wind into the crowded anchorage, and the Spanish captains, unable to fight fire at anchor in the dark, cut their cables and ran. The formation that a week of cannon fire had failed to dent was scattered by the choice of anchorage &mdash; and every ship that cut its cable sailed for the rest of the campaign without its best anchors.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Environment Three: Gravelines and the Banks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Off Gravelines on 29 July, the English closed to effective gunnery range for the first time, battering the scattered Spanish ships while refusing the boarding fight the Armada was built for. Then geography nearly ended the campaign outright: the wind pressed the damaged fleet towards the Zeeland sandbanks, shoal water with no room to manoeuvre, and only a late change of wind let the Armada escape into the North Sea. That same wind shut the door behind it. With the English fleet between the Spanish and the Channel, the only route home was the long way &mdash; north around Scotland, west around Ireland.</p>

It was brilliant propaganda, and about one-third true. The storms did destroy more ships than the navy did; but the fleet the storms caught had already been forced from its anchorage, stripped of its anchors, beaten at Gravelines and pushed onto the northern route by English action. In the site question, that layering &mdash; environment finishing what human choices began &mdash; is exactly the kind of judgement the top level rewards.</p></div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Environment Four: The Route Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The north-about voyage was the deadliest environment of all. Autumn gales met a fleet with battle-damaged hulls, sick and starving crews, and &mdash; because of Calais &mdash; too few anchors to ride out storms off a lee shore. Wrecks piled up on the coasts of Scotland and, above all, western Ireland. Perhaps half the ships that left Spain never returned, and thousands of men died of drowning, hunger and disease &mdash; several times more than died to English guns. When Elizabeth delivered her famous speech at Tilbury on 9 August, the Armada was already limping into the North Sea; the environment spent the following weeks turning an English escape into a Spanish catastrophe.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Building Your 16-Mark Answer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Whatever the statement &mdash; about luck, leadership, weather, or English skill &mdash; answer it through feature-and-consequence chains drawn from the four environments: no deep-water port, therefore an exposed anchorage; an exposed anchorage, therefore fireships; cut cables, therefore wrecks on the Irish coast; English home ports and the weather gauge, therefore a fleet that could always refuse the fight the Spanish wanted. Then weigh in the human factors from Lesson 9 &mdash; the gunnery, the planning flaws, Parma&rsquo;s blockaded barges &mdash; and reach a judgement. Site plus context, feature plus consequence: that is the whole recipe.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">The 2027 site question treats the Armada campaign as an environment: think in four places — Channel, Calais, Gravelines, and the route home.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The plan's fatal flaw was geographical: no deep-water port meant the Armada could only wait for Parma in an exposed roadstead, where fireships broke in one night what gunfire had not broken in a week.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Cut cables at Calais became wrecks in Ireland: the environments are linked, and showing those links is what strong site answers do.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The storms finished a defeat that human choices began — English gunnery, the weather gauge and home ports created the situation the 'Protestant wind' completed.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Anchor every paragraph of your site answer in a named place doing causal work &mdash; the Calais roadstead, the Zeeland banks, the north-about route. The examiner can only credit site knowledge that is specific. &lsquo;Bad weather helped defeat the Armada&rsquo; earns little; &lsquo;the fleet had abandoned its anchors escaping the fireships at Calais, so the September gales drove it ashore on the Irish coast&rsquo; is a site-based cause with a consequence &mdash; Level 4 material.</p>
