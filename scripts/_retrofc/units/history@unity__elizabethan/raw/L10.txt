--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why poverty increased under Elizabeth, attitudes towards the ‘deserving’ and ‘idle’ poor, and the development of the Elizabethan Poor Laws.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Problem of the Poor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">During Elizabeth’s reign (1558–1603), poverty grew sharply across England. The government had to find new ways to deal with it. This led to the Elizabethan Poor Laws — some of the first attempts to make poverty a matter of <strong>government responsibility</strong> rather than private charity.</p>
<figure class="diagram" data-narration-id="n3">
<img alt="Infographic showing the three categories of poor in Elizabethan England: the Helpless Poor (given charity), the Deserving Poor (given work materials), and the Idle Poor/Vagabonds (punished)" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/history/elizabethan/diagram_categories_poor.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Why Was Poverty Increasing?</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n23">
<li><strong>Growing population:</strong> England’s population rose from about 3 million in 1550 to over 4 million by 1600. More people meant more competition for jobs, food, and housing.</li>
<li><strong>Bad harvests:</strong> A series of poor harvests — especially 1594–1597 — caused food shortages and rising prices.</li>
<li><strong>Enclosure:</strong> Wealthy landowners enclosed common land to farm sheep for the wool trade. This forced many small farmers off the land.</li>
<li><strong>Dissolution of the monasteries:</strong> Henry VIII closed the monasteries in the 1530s. They had provided food, shelter, and medical care for the poor. Nothing replaced them.</li>
<li><strong>Inflation:</strong> Prices rose much faster than wages. Between 1500 and 1600, food prices rose by about 400%, while wages only doubled.</li>
<li><strong>Demobilised soldiers:</strong> Men returning from wars in the Netherlands and Ireland often had no jobs waiting for them.</li>
<li><strong>Decline of the cloth trade:</strong> The Antwerp cloth market collapsed in the 1550s, causing unemployment in cloth-making towns.</li>
<li><strong>Plague outbreaks:</strong> Epidemics hit in 1563, 1578, and 1582. People feared that beggars moving from town to town were spreading disease.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n5">Attitudes Towards the Poor</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li><strong>Sympathy for the helpless:</strong> Most people agreed the old, sick, and disabled deserved help. Churches and wealthy individuals gave charity.</li>
<li><strong>Fear of vagabonds:</strong> ‘Sturdy beggars’ were widely feared. People believed they spread plague, committed crimes, and might cause rebellion. Some vagabonds were ex-soldiers or hardened criminals — pickpockets even had their own slang language called <dfn class="term" data-def="A secret slang used by criminals and vagabonds in Elizabethan England.">‘canting’</dfn>.</li>
<li><strong>Media panic:</strong> Pamphlets by authors like <strong>Thomas Harman</strong> warned about the danger of vagabonds. These were cheap and widely read. They created panic, much like sensational news stories today.</li>
<li><strong>Changing views:</strong> Over time, people began to accept that most vagabonds were ordinary unemployed people looking for work. Towns like <strong>Norwich</strong>, <strong>York</strong>, and <strong>Ipswich</strong> experimented with local schemes — licensing beggars, training children in trades, and conducting surveys to separate the idle from the genuinely poor.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Punishments for the Idle Poor</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Those classed as ‘idle’ faced harsh punishments designed to deter begging:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n25">
<li><strong>Whipping</strong> — the most common punishment for vagabonds</li>
<li><strong>Branding</strong> — a hot iron mark on the skin as a permanent warning</li>
<li><strong>Boring</strong> — a hole burned through the ear</li>
<li><strong>Hanging</strong> — repeat offenders could face the death penalty</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n8">Government Action: The Poor Laws</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Attitudes to poverty changed over time. Under Henry VIII, the poor were mostly just punished. Gradually, the government realised that a national system was needed — partly inspired by successful local schemes in towns like Norwich.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>1563 — Statute of Artificers:</strong> JPs could raise money for poor relief. Refusing to pay could lead to prison.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>1572 — Vagabonds Act:</strong> A national poor rate (tax) was introduced. Vagabonds caught begging could be whipped and have a hole burned through their ear. Repeat offenders could be imprisoned or executed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>1576 — Poor Relief Act:</strong> Towns had to provide raw materials (like wool) so the able-bodied poor could work. ‘Houses of Correction’ were set up for those who refused.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>1601 — The Elizabethan Poor Law:</strong> The most important law. It brought together previous laws into one system. Each parish had to appoint an <strong>Overseer of the Poor</strong>, collect a compulsory poor rate (taxing the wealthy), provide relief for those who could not work, set up work for the able-bodied, and punish those who refused to work.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what year was the Poor Law, and what was taxed to pay for the poor's care?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The 1601 Poor Law</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The 1601 Poor Law established that the <strong>wealthy should be taxed</strong> to pay for the care of the poor. It set the principle of <strong>government responsibility</strong> for welfare, and it remained the basis of poor relief for over <strong>200 years</strong> — until the 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">How Effective Were the Poor Laws?</h3>
<h4>Strengths</h4>
<ul data-narration-id="n26">
<li>Created a <strong>national system</strong> of poor relief for the first time</li>
<li>Made poverty a <strong>government responsibility</strong>, not just private charity</li>
<li>Distinguished between different types of poor and treated them accordingly</li>
<li>The system lasted over 200 years, suggesting it worked reasonably well</li>
</ul>
<h4>Weaknesses</h4>
<ul data-narration-id="n27">
<li>Relief was often <strong>very small</strong> — barely enough to survive on</li>
<li>Conditions in Houses of Correction were <strong>deliberately harsh</strong></li>
<li>The laws <strong>did not fix root causes</strong> like enclosure, inflation, or unemployment</li>
<li>Enforcement varied hugely from parish to parish</li>
<li>Punishments for vagabonds were cruel and did little to solve the problem</li>
</ul>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Conclusion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Poverty grew sharply under Elizabeth, driven by population growth, inflation, enclosure, bad harvests, and the loss of monastic charity. The government’s response changed over time — from harsh punishment towards a national system of relief. The 1601 Poor Law was a landmark, establishing that the wealthy should be taxed to support the poor. It was not perfect, but it lasted over 200 years.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n28">
<li><strong>Poverty had many causes:</strong> Population growth, enclosure, inflation, bad harvests, plague, and the loss of monastic charity all played a part.</li>
<li><strong>Attitudes changed over time:</strong> The government moved from punishing beggars to providing a national system of relief — partly inspired by successful local schemes in Norwich, York, and Ipswich.</li>
<li><strong>The 1601 Poor Law was a landmark:</strong> It made the wealthy pay a compulsory poor rate and established government responsibility for welfare.</li>
</ul>
