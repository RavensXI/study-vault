--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to carry out titrations accurately, and how chemical and fuel cells generate electricity.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Titrations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique used to find the exact volume of one solution needed to react completely with a known volume of another solution.">titration</dfn> is a practical technique used to find the precise volume of an acid or alkali needed to neutralise a known volume of the other. It is a required practical for AQA Separate Chemistry and a very commonly tested topic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the apparatus that holds the solution being added, the flask type used, and what the indicator shows at neutralisation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a titration, the solution in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A piece of glass apparatus with a tap, used to add precise volumes of solution drop by drop during a titration.">burette</dfn> is added to the solution in the conical flask until the indicator shows the <dfn class="term" data-def="The point in a titration at which the acid and alkali have completely reacted with each other.">end point</dfn> — the exact point at which neutralisation is complete.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Titration Method</h2>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n5">Use a <dfn class="term" data-def="A piece of glass apparatus used to measure a precise, fixed volume of solution.">pipette</dfn> to measure an exact volume of alkali (e.g. 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide) into a conical flask.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6">Add a few drops of indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein or methyl orange).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">Fill a burette with the acid solution and record the starting volume.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">Slowly add acid from the burette to the flask, swirling continuously.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">Near the end point, add the acid drop by drop until the indicator permanently changes colour.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">Record the final burette reading. The volume of acid used = final reading − initial reading.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11">Repeat the titration until you have <strong>concordant results</strong> (two or more results within 0.10 cm³ of each other). Calculate a mean from the concordant results only, ignoring any <dfn class="term" data-def="A result that is significantly different from other results and is excluded when calculating the mean.">anomalous results</dfn>.</li>
</ol>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Once you have the titre (volume of acid used), you can calculate the unknown concentration:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Calculate moles of the known solution: \(\text{moles} = \text{concentration} \times \text{volume (dm}^3\text{)}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Use the balanced equation to find the mole ratio and calculate moles of the unknown.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Calculate the unknown concentration: \(\text{concentration} = \frac{\text{moles}}{\text{volume (dm}^3\text{)}}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Example:</strong> 25.0 cm³ of 0.10 mol/dm³ NaOH was neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of HCl.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Moles of NaOH = 0.10 × 0.025 = 0.0025 mol</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">From \(\text{NaOH} + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\), the ratio is 1:1, so moles of HCl = 0.0025 mol.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Concentration of HCl = 0.0025 ÷ 0.020 = 0.125 mol/dm³</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Chemical Cells (Batteries)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions between two different metals or electrolytes.">chemical cell</dfn> (battery) produces electricity from chemical reactions. When two different metals are placed in an <dfn class="term" data-def="A liquid or dissolved substance that conducts electricity because it contains free-moving ions.">electrolyte</dfn> (a conducting solution), a voltage is produced because the metals have different reactivities.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why zinc-copper cells produce higher voltage than iron-copper cells using the reactivity series.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The voltage produced by a chemical cell depends on the <strong>difference in reactivity</strong> between the two metals. The greater the difference in reactivity, the higher the voltage. For example, zinc and copper produce a higher voltage than iron and copper because zinc is more reactive than iron.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n23">In a chemical cell, the more reactive metal is <dfn class="term" data-def="The loss of electrons by an atom, ion, or molecule during a chemical reaction.">oxidised</dfn> (loses electrons) at the negative electrode, and the less reactive metal (or its ions) is <dfn class="term" data-def="The gain of electrons by an atom, ion, or molecule during a chemical reaction.">reduced</dfn> (gains electrons) at the positive electrode. Electrons flow through the external circuit from the more reactive to the less reactive metal.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Rechargeable and Non-Rechargeable Cells</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Non-rechargeable cells:</strong> The chemical reactions are irreversible. Once the reactants are used up, the cell can no longer produce electricity and must be disposed of.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Rechargeable cells:</strong> The chemical reactions are reversible. Passing an electrical current through the cell reverses the reactions, regenerating the original reactants. Examples include lithium-ion batteries used in phones and laptops.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Fuel Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An electrochemical cell that produces electricity by reacting a fuel (usually hydrogen) with oxygen, with water as the only waste product.">fuel cell</dfn> produces electricity from the reaction of a fuel (usually hydrogen) with oxygen. Unlike batteries, fuel cells do not run out — they produce electricity continuously as long as fuel and oxygen are supplied.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the overall equation, both electrode equations, and name the only waste product in hydrogen fuel cells.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a hydrogen fuel cell, the overall reaction is: \(2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\). The only waste product is water, making fuel cells a clean energy source. At the negative electrode: \(\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-\) (oxidation). At the positive electrode: \(\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\) (reduction).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n29">Advantages of hydrogen fuel cells: the only waste is water (no carbon dioxide), they are very efficient, and they can power vehicles. Disadvantages: hydrogen is difficult and expensive to store and transport, most hydrogen is currently produced from fossil fuels, and fuel cell technology is expensive.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Titrations use a burette and indicator to find the exact volume of acid/alkali needed for neutralisation. Concordant results are essential for accuracy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Chemical cells produce voltage from two different metals in an electrolyte — greater reactivity difference means higher voltage.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Hydrogen fuel cells react hydrogen with oxygen to produce electricity and water only, but hydrogen storage and production remain challenges.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">Titration questions often ask you to describe the method — learn the step-by-step procedure and mention concordant results. For calculations, always convert cm³ to dm³ (divide by 1000) before using the concentration formula. For fuel cells, remember the half-equations at each electrode and that the overall product is <strong>water only</strong>.</p>
