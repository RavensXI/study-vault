--- DESCRIPTION ---
Properties of transition metals and the unique characteristics of nanoparticles.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Transition Metals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A block of metallic elements found in the central section of the periodic table, between groups 2 and 3.">transition metals</dfn> are found in the central block of the periodic table, between groups 2 and 3. They include everyday metals like iron, copper, nickel, and gold. Compared with Group 1 metals (alkali metals), transition metals have very different physical and chemical properties.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name three physical properties where transition metals are superior to Group 1 metals and two chemical differences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Transition metals have <strong>higher melting points</strong>, are <strong>harder</strong> and <strong>stronger</strong>, and are much <strong>denser</strong> than Group 1 metals. They are also <strong>less reactive</strong> and do not react as vigorously with water or oxygen.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Key properties of transition metals that distinguish them from Group 1 metals:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>High melting points</strong> — iron melts at 1538 °C compared to sodium at just 98 °C.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>High density</strong> — this makes them useful as structural materials (e.g. iron in construction).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Good conductors</strong> of heat and electricity (e.g. copper is used in electrical wiring).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Form coloured compounds</strong> — many transition metal compounds have distinctive colours: copper sulfate is blue, iron(III) oxide is orange-brown, potassium permanganate is purple.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Act as <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up, often used in industrial processes.">catalysts</dfn></strong> — iron is the catalyst in the Haber process; manganese dioxide catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.</li>
</ul>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Group 1 metals (lithium, sodium, potassium) are soft, have low melting points, low density, and are very reactive — they react vigorously with water. Transition metals are the opposite: hard, high melting points, high density, and much less reactive. This is why transition metals are used for structural and electrical applications, while Group 1 metals are not.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Transition metals can also form ions with different charges (e.g. iron can form Fe²+ and Fe³+), which is why they form compounds with different colours.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Nanoparticles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Particles with dimensions between 1 and 100 nanometres, which have a very high surface area to volume ratio.">Nanoparticles</dfn> are extremely small particles with at least one dimension between 1 and 100 nanometres (nm). A nanometre is one billionth of a metre: \(1\text{ nm} = 1 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why nanoparticles have different colours, reactivity, and electrical conductivity compared to bulk materials.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles have a very high <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the total surface area of a material to its volume; nanoparticles have an extremely high ratio.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn> compared with bulk materials of the same substance. This gives them different properties, including different colours, higher reactivity, and different electrical conductivity.</p>
</div>



<p data-narration-id="n15">As particles get smaller, their surface area to volume ratio increases dramatically. To illustrate: a single 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm². If you cut it into 1 mm cubes, the total surface area increases to 60 cm² — ten times more surface for the same volume of material.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Uses of Nanoparticles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Because of their unique properties, nanoparticles have many applications:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Medicine:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="Silver particles at the nanoscale that have antimicrobial properties and are used in wound dressings and deodorants.">Silver nanoparticles</dfn> have antibacterial properties and are used in wound dressings and some deodorants.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Sunscreens:</strong> Titanium dioxide nanoparticles provide UV protection without the thick white appearance of traditional sunscreen.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Electronics:</strong> Nanoparticles are used in computer chips and sensors to create smaller, more efficient components.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Catalysis:</strong> The high surface area means nanoparticle catalysts are more effective, requiring less material.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Risks and Concerns About Nanoparticles</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Nanoparticles are a relatively new technology, and there are concerns about their safety:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n23">They are so small that they can enter cells and even cross the blood-brain barrier, with unknown long-term health effects.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n24">The long-term environmental impact of releasing nanoparticles into water and soil is not fully understood.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n25">Their high reactivity could cause unexpected chemical reactions inside the body.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">More research is needed before nanoparticles can be declared completely safe for widespread use.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the size ranges in nm for atoms, nanoparticles, fine particles (PM 2.5), and coarse particles (dust).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles can be compared in size to other structures: atoms are about 0.1 nm, nanoparticles are 1–100 nm, fine particles (PM 2.5) are 100–2500 nm, and coarse particles (dust) are 2500–10,000 nm.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Transition metals have high melting points, high density, form coloured compounds, and act as catalysts — unlike the soft, reactive Group 1 metals.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Nanoparticles (1–100 nm) have a very high surface area to volume ratio, giving them unique properties used in medicine, sunscreen, electronics, and catalysis.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Concerns about nanoparticles include unknown long-term health and environmental effects due to their ability to enter cells.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">When asked to compare transition metals with Group 1 metals, always give <strong>specific contrasting properties</strong> (e.g. “transition metals have high melting points, whereas Group 1 metals have low melting points”). For nanoparticle questions, always link their properties to their <strong>high surface area to volume ratio</strong>. If asked to evaluate their use, balance benefits (effectiveness, smaller quantities needed) against risks (unknown health/environmental effects).</p>
