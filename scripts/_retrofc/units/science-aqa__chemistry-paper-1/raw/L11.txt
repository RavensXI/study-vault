--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Percentage Yield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In any chemical reaction, the amount of product actually made is often less than the theoretical maximum. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of the theoretical yield that is actually obtained in a chemical reaction, expressed as a percentage.">percentage yield</dfn> compares what you actually get with what you theoretically could get:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$\text{Percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Reasons why the yield is less than 100%:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5">The reaction may be <strong>reversible</strong> — not all reactants convert to products because the reaction reaches equilibrium.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6">Product may be <strong>lost during transfer</strong> between containers, or during filtration, evaporation, or other separation steps.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Side reactions</strong> may produce unwanted by-products instead of the desired product.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall why high percentage yield matters in industrial chemistry and what yield is rarely achievable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A high percentage yield is desirable in industrial chemistry because it means less waste, lower costs, and more efficient use of raw materials. A yield of 100% is rarely achievable in practice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Atom Economy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of the total mass of reactants that forms useful products, used to measure how efficiently a reaction uses its atoms.">Atom economy</dfn> measures how efficiently a reaction uses its starting materials. It compares the mass of the desired product with the total mass of all products:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$\text{Atom economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product}}{\text{Total } M_r \text{ of all products}} \times 100$$</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Atom Economy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Consider the reaction: \(\text{CaCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">If the desired product is calcium oxide (CaO), \(M_r\) of CaO = 56, \(M_r\) of CO<sub>2</sub> = 44.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Total \(M_r\) of all products = 56 + 44 = 100.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">$$\text{Atom economy} = \frac{56}{100} \times 100 = 56\%$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">This means 44% of the mass of the reactants ends up as the unwanted by-product (carbon dioxide).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which reaction types have 100% atom economy and which have lower atom economy due to by-products.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>High atom economy is important for sustainability. Reactions with low atom economy produce more waste by-products. <strong>Addition reactions</strong> have 100% atom economy because all atoms end up in the single product. Substitution and elimination reactions have lower atom economy because they produce by-products.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Molar Concentrations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution, measured in mol/dm³.">concentration</dfn> of a solution can be expressed in mol/dm³ (moles per cubic decimetre). This tells you how many moles of solute are dissolved in each dm³ of solution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The key formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$\text{concentration (mol/dm}^3\text{)} = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume of solution (dm}^3\text{)}}$$</p>



<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Converting Between Units</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Remember that 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 1 litre. To convert cm³ to dm³, divide by 1000.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Example:</strong> 0.5 mol of NaCl is dissolved in 250 cm³ of water. Calculate the concentration.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.25 dm³</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">$$\text{Concentration} = \frac{0.5}{0.25} = 2.0 \text{ mol/dm}^3$$</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Gas Volumes and the Molar Volume</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">At room temperature and pressure (RTP: 20 °C, 1 atmosphere), one mole of any gas occupies the same volume: <strong>24 dm³</strong> (or 24,000 cm³). This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a given temperature and pressure; at RTP this is 24 dm³.">molar volume</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the equation to calculate gas volume at RTP and the equation to calculate moles from volume.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>To calculate the volume of a gas at RTP:
  $$\text{Volume (dm}^3\text{)} = \text{moles} \times 24$$
  To calculate moles from volume:
  $$\text{Moles} = \frac{\text{volume (dm}^3\text{)}}{24}$$</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Example:</strong> Calculate the volume of 0.3 mol of carbon dioxide at RTP.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">$$\text{Volume} = 0.3 \times 24 = 7.2 \text{ dm}^3$$</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100. It is always less than 100% due to reversible reactions, losses, and side reactions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Atom economy measures how much of the reactant mass ends up as useful product. Addition reactions have 100% atom economy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Concentration in mol/dm³ = moles ÷ volume. At RTP, 1 mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³.</li>
</ul>
