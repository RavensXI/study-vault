--- DESCRIPTION ---
Giant ionic lattices, simple molecules, giant covalent structures, and allotropes of carbon.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Properties of Ionic Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Ionic compounds form <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of ions held together by strong electrostatic forces in all directions.">giant ionic lattices</dfn> — a regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions. The strong electrostatic forces between the ions act in all directions, giving ionic compounds these properties:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n3"><strong>High melting and boiling points</strong> — a lot of energy is needed to overcome the strong ionic bonds.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved</strong> — the ions are free to move and carry charge. They do <strong>not</strong> conduct when solid because the ions are fixed in position.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why ionic compounds conduct when molten but not when solid, mentioning ion movement.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ionic compounds: high melting points (strong bonds in all directions), conduct when molten or dissolved (ions free to move), do not conduct when solid (ions fixed in lattice).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Properties of Simple Molecular Substances</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Small molecules formed by covalent bonds between non-metal atoms, e.g. H2O, CO2, CH4. They have weak intermolecular forces.">Simple molecules</dfn> (e.g. \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\), \(O_2\)) have strong covalent bonds within the molecule but weak <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between molecules. They are much weaker than covalent bonds and require little energy to overcome.">intermolecular forces</dfn> between molecules. This gives them:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Low melting and boiling points</strong> — little energy is needed to overcome the weak intermolecular forces (not the covalent bonds).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Do not conduct electricity</strong> — no free electrons or ions to carry charge.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n10">As molecules get larger, the intermolecular forces become stronger, so larger molecules tend to have higher boiling points.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Giant Covalent Structures</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Structures where many atoms are bonded to other atoms by covalent bonds in a huge network. They have very high melting points.">Giant covalent structures</dfn> have millions of atoms connected by covalent bonds in a continuous network. They have <strong>very high melting points</strong> because many strong covalent bonds must be broken. Examples include <strong>diamond</strong>, <strong>graphite</strong> and <strong>silicon dioxide</strong>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Diamond, Graphite and Graphene</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Diamond:</strong> each carbon atom bonds to four others in a rigid tetrahedral structure. Very hard, high melting point, does not conduct electricity (no free electrons or ions).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Graphite:</strong> each carbon bonds to three others, forming flat layers with weak forces between them. The fourth electron is <dfn class="term" data-def="In graphite, one electron per carbon atom is delocalised and free to move along the layers, allowing graphite to conduct electricity.">delocalised</dfn>, allowing graphite to conduct electricity. Layers can slide over each other, making it soft and slippery (useful as a lubricant).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Graphene:</strong> a single layer of graphite — one atom thick. It is very strong, light, and an excellent conductor of electricity and heat. <strong>Fullerenes</strong> (e.g. \(C_{60}\) buckminsterfullerene) are carbon molecules shaped like hollow spheres or tubes. Carbon <dfn class="term" data-def="Cylindrical fullerenes with excellent electrical conductivity and high strength. Used in electronics, drug delivery and composite materials.">nanotubes</dfn> are cylindrical fullerenes with remarkable strength and conductivity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and compare the bonding in diamond (4 bonds) versus graphite (3 bonds) and their electrical conductivity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Diamond: 4 bonds per carbon, very hard, doesn’t conduct. Graphite: 3 bonds per carbon + delocalised electrons, conducts electricity, layers slide. Graphene: single layer of graphite, strongest known material, conducts.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Very large molecules made by joining many small molecules (monomers) together in long chains.">Polymers</dfn> are very large molecules made up of many repeating units called monomers. They have relatively strong intermolecular forces between the long chains, so they tend to be solid at room temperature. Examples include poly(ethene) and poly(propene).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Ionic compounds: giant lattice, high melting point, conduct when molten/dissolved.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Simple molecules: weak intermolecular forces, low melting point, don’t conduct.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Giant covalent: very high melting point. Diamond (hard, insulator), graphite (conducts, layers slide), graphene (single layer, strongest material).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When explaining properties, always link structure to property. For example: “Graphite conducts electricity because each carbon atom only forms three covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that is free to move and carry charge.” Don’t just state the property — explain WHY using the bonding model.</p>
