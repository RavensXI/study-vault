--- DESCRIPTION ---
How elements are arranged in the periodic table and the properties of Groups 1, 7 and 0.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Development of the Periodic Table</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Before the discovery of protons and neutrons, scientists attempted to classify elements by arranging them in order of atomic weight. Dmitri <dfn class="term" data-def="The Russian chemist who arranged elements in order of atomic weight and left gaps for undiscovered elements, creating the first successful periodic table in 1869.">Mendeleev</dfn> (1869) arranged elements in order of atomic weight but left gaps for undiscovered elements and swapped some elements so that those with similar properties lined up in the same columns. When elements such as gallium and germanium were later discovered and matched his predictions, his table was widely accepted.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The modern periodic table arranges elements in order of <strong>atomic number</strong> (not atomic weight). Elements in the same <strong>group</strong> (column) have the same number of electrons in their outer shell, giving them similar chemical properties. Elements in the same <strong>period</strong> (row) have the same number of electron shells.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall why Group 1 reactivity increases down the group while Group 7 reactivity decreases down the group.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elements are arranged by atomic number. Groups (columns) share the same number of outer electrons and similar properties. Periods (rows) share the same number of electron shells. Metals are on the left; non-metals are on the right.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Group 1 — Alkali Metals</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 1 of the periodic table (lithium, sodium, potassium, etc.). They have one electron in their outer shell and are very reactive metals.">alkali metals</dfn> (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) all have <strong>one electron</strong> in their outer shell. They are soft, have low density and react vigorously with water, producing a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">$$2Na + 2H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH + H_2$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Going <strong>down</strong> Group 1, reactivity <strong>increases</strong> because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is more easily lost (less electrostatic attraction). The elements also have lower melting points and higher densities going down the group.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Group 7 — Halogens</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 7 of the periodic table (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine). They have seven outer electrons, are non-metals, and form diatomic molecules.">halogens</dfn> (F, Cl, Br, I, At) have <strong>seven electrons</strong> in their outer shell. They exist as diatomic molecules (\(Cl_2\), \(Br_2\), etc.) and react with metals to form ionic compounds called <dfn class="term" data-def="Compounds formed when a halogen reacts with a metal, e.g. sodium chloride (NaCl). They are ionic compounds.">halides</dfn> (e.g. NaCl).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">Going <strong>down</strong> Group 7, reactivity <strong>decreases</strong> because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, making it harder to gain an extra electron. A more reactive halogen can <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction where a more reactive halogen takes the place of a less reactive halogen in a compound.">displace</dfn> a less reactive halogen from its compound, e.g. chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name what groups and periods represent, where metals/non-metals are located, and what elements are arranged by.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Group 1: reactivity increases down the group (easier to lose outer electron). Group 7: reactivity decreases down the group (harder to gain an electron). These trends are opposite because metals lose electrons while non-metals gain them.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Unreactive elements in Group 0 of the periodic table (helium, neon, argon, etc.). They have full outer electron shells, making them very stable.">noble gases</dfn> (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) have <strong>full outer electron shells</strong>, making them very stable and unreactive. They are used where inert gases are needed: helium in balloons and airships, neon in advertising signs, argon in light bulbs and welding. Boiling point increases down the group.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Transition metals are found in the central block of the periodic table. They have typical metallic properties: high melting points, high density, good conductors. Unlike Group 1 metals, transition metals are less reactive, harder and denser. They form coloured compounds and can act as catalysts (e.g. iron in the Haber process).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The periodic table is arranged by atomic number; groups share outer electron count and similar properties.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Group 1 (alkali metals): reactivity increases down the group. Group 7 (halogens): reactivity decreases down the group.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Group 0 (noble gases) are unreactive because they have full outer electron shells.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>For Group 1: more shells = outer electron further from nucleus = weaker attraction = easier to lose = more reactive. For Group 7: more shells = outer shell further from nucleus = weaker attraction for incoming electron = harder to gain = less reactive.</p>
