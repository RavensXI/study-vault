<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Introduction to Energy Changes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All chemical reactions involve energy changes. When chemical bonds are broken and new bonds are formed, energy is either released to the surroundings or absorbed from the surroundings. Understanding these energy changes is crucial for predicting how reactions behave and their practical applications.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State what happens to energy when chemical bonds are broken versus when new bonds are formed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Energy is required to break chemical bonds and energy is released when new bonds are formed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Exothermic Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that releases energy to the surroundings, usually as heat.">exothermic reaction</dfn> is one where energy is released to the surroundings. In these reactions, more energy is released when new bonds form than is needed to break the original bonds. This results in a net release of energy, usually observed as an increase in temperature of the surroundings.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Examples of Exothermic Reactions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Common exothermic reactions include combustion reactions, such as burning methane: \(CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O\). Other examples include neutralisation reactions between acids and bases, and many oxidation reactions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Hand warmers used in winter often contain iron powder that undergoes an exothermic oxidation reaction with oxygen from the air, releasing heat energy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Endothermic Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that absorbs energy from the surroundings.">endothermic reaction</dfn> is one where energy is absorbed from the surroundings. In these reactions, more energy is needed to break the original bonds than is released when new bonds form. This results in a net absorption of energy, usually observed as a decrease in temperature of the surroundings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why the temperature of surroundings decreases in endothermic reactions and what happens to the energy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In endothermic reactions, the temperature of the surroundings decreases because energy is absorbed from them.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Examples of Endothermic Reactions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Thermal decomposition reactions are typically endothermic. For example, heating copper carbonate: \(CuCO_3 \rightarrow CuO + CO_2\). The reaction requires continuous heating to proceed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Sports injury packs often contain ammonium nitrate that dissolves endothermically in water, absorbing heat and cooling the injured area.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Energy Profiles and Activation Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Energy profile diagrams show how the energy of reactants and products changes during a chemical reaction. These diagrams help us understand the energy requirements and energy changes involved in chemical processes.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum energy required for a reaction to occur.">activation energy</dfn> is the minimum energy required for reactants to overcome the energy barrier and start reacting. Even exothermic reactions require this initial energy input to break the first bonds and get the reaction started.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the type of energy that all reactions need to begin, regardless of whether they are exothermic or endothermic.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All reactions, whether exothermic or endothermic, require activation energy to begin.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Bond Breaking and Bond Making</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">During chemical reactions, existing bonds in reactant molecules must be broken before new bonds can form in the product molecules. Bond breaking is always an endothermic process — it requires energy input. Bond making is always an exothermic process — it releases energy.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The overall energy change of a reaction depends on the balance between energy required for bond breaking and energy released during bond making. If more energy is released than absorbed, the reaction is exothermic. If more energy is absorbed than released, the reaction is endothermic.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Calculating Energy Changes</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The energy change of a reaction can be calculated using bond energies:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">\(\text{Energy change} = \text{Energy required to break bonds} - \text{Energy released making bonds}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">If the result is positive, the reaction is endothermic. If negative, the reaction is exothermic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Practical Applications</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Understanding energy changes in chemical reactions has many practical applications. Exothermic reactions are used in heating systems, self-heating food containers, and rocket fuels. Endothermic reactions are used in instant cold packs for sports injuries and in some industrial processes where cooling is required.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n25">In everyday life, we rely on the exothermic combustion of fuels for heating our homes and powering vehicles. The energy released during these reactions provides the thermal energy we need for warmth and the kinetic energy for movement.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Exothermic reactions release energy to the surroundings, causing temperature to increase</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Endothermic reactions absorb energy from the surroundings, causing temperature to decrease</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">All reactions require activation energy to overcome the initial energy barrier</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The overall energy change depends on the balance between bond breaking (endothermic) and bond making (exothermic)</li>
</ul>

For energy profile diagrams, check whether products are at a higher or lower energy level than reactants.</p>
