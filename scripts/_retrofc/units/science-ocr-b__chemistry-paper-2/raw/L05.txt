How we pick a material for the job, recycling, and weighing the full environmental cost.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Choosing the Right Material</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chemists routinely select materials by measuring and comparing their physical properties against a product specification. A product specification is a list of the requirements a material must meet for a particular use &mdash; for example, a bicycle frame needs to be strong in tension, lightweight, and resistant to corrosion. The key properties used to compare materials include: melting point, softening temperature (for polymers), electrical and thermal conductivity, strength (in tension and compression), stiffness, flexibility, brittleness, hardness, density and ease of reshaping.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The four main categories of engineering materials are: <dfn class="term" data-def="Non-metallic inorganic solids such as glass and clay, which are typically hard, brittle and have high melting points.">ceramics</dfn> (glass, clay), <dfn class="term" data-def="Long-chain molecules made of repeating monomer units, often flexible and low-melting.">polymers</dfn> (plastics), <dfn class="term" data-def="A material made by combining two or more materials to gain properties that neither has alone.">composites</dfn> (concrete, carbon fibre reinforced polymers), and metals. Composites allow the properties of several materials to be combined &mdash; for example, steel-reinforced concrete is strong in both tension (from the steel) and compression (from the concrete).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four properties used to compare materials when selecting one for a specific use. Give one material that performs well in each property you name.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Materials are selected by comparing measurable physical properties against a product specification. Key properties include strength (tension and compression), hardness, density, electrical conductivity, melting point and ease of reshaping. The right choice depends on which properties are most critical for the application.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">What Is a Life Cycle Assessment?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Choosing a material by properties alone is not enough &mdash; we must also consider the environmental cost of the whole product&rsquo;s life. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An analysis of the total environmental impact of a product across every stage of its existence, from raw material extraction to final disposal.">life cycle assessment</dfn> (LCA) evaluates the environmental impact of a product at every stage of its life. The four main stages are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>1. Extracting and processing raw materials</strong> &mdash; energy, land disruption, water use, and emissions from mining or harvesting. <strong>2. Manufacturing the product</strong> &mdash; energy and water used in factories, waste generated, and transport of components. <strong>3. Using the product</strong> &mdash; energy consumed during use, maintenance materials, and transport of the product throughout its working life. <strong>4. Disposal at end of life</strong> &mdash; whether the product goes to landfill, is incinerated (releasing gases), or is recycled or reused.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Plastic Bag vs Paper Bag LCA</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">A common LCA comparison is a single-use plastic bag against a paper bag. At first glance, a paper bag seems better because it is biodegradable. But an LCA reveals a more complex picture: making one paper bag uses more water and more energy than making one plastic bag, and produces more greenhouse gases during manufacture. The plastic bag, however, is non-biodegradable and accumulates in ecosystems if not collected. If the plastic bag is reused several times or recycled, its total environmental impact may be lower than multiple paper bags used once each. This shows why LCA judgements are not straightforward &mdash; the answer depends on usage pattern, disposal infrastructure and which impacts are given greatest weight. Different analysts can reach different conclusions, which means LCAs have an element of subjectivity and must be evaluated critically.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Recycling and Reuse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">At the end of a product&rsquo;s life, the preferred outcome (from lowest to highest environmental impact) is: <strong>reuse</strong> &rarr; <strong>recycling</strong> &rarr; <strong>incineration with energy recovery</strong> &rarr; <strong>landfill</strong>. Reusing a product requires no energy for reprocessing &mdash; it simply extends the life of existing materials. <dfn class="term" data-def="Processing a waste material so that it can be made into new products, avoiding the need to extract virgin raw materials.">Recycling</dfn> reduces the need to extract new raw materials and saves energy compared with making products from scratch. For example, recycling aluminium uses only a small fraction of the energy needed to extract it from its ore by electrolysis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A thermoplastic polymer used to make drinks bottles, which can be mechanically recycled back into fibres or new bottles.">PET (polyethylene terephthalate)</dfn> drinks bottle provides a well-studied example. Collected PET bottles can be shredded, melted and remoulded into new bottles or extruded into fibres for clothing and insulation. This recycling is economically viable because PET is abundant in the waste stream, has a reliable market, and the energy saving compared with making new PET from crude oil is substantial. However, viability depends on factors including: the finite nature of crude oil (making virgin PET increasingly expensive), collection infrastructure, sorting complexity, and consumer behaviour.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Limitations and Subjectivity of LCAs</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">LCAs are limited by incomplete data, difficulties in quantifying some impacts (such as noise, visual impact or biodiversity loss), and the choices analysts make about which factors to weight most heavily. Companies may selectively publish LCA data that supports favourable marketing claims &mdash; so when reading an LCA, consider who funded it and what data was included or excluded. Decisions about sustainability require weighing scientific evidence alongside values about which environmental impacts matter most. This is a core example of how science and society interact in making policy decisions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Write the four stages of an LCA from memory. Then name one environmental impact at each stage, without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An LCA covers four stages: raw material extraction, manufacturing, product use, and disposal. It assesses water use, energy use, emissions and waste at every stage. LCAs can be partly subjective &mdash; different analysts may reach different conclusions depending on what they measure and how they weight different impacts.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">Materials are selected by comparing physical properties (strength, density, conductivity, melting point) against a product specification; composites allow properties of several materials to be combined.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">A life cycle assessment evaluates environmental impact across all four stages of a product&rsquo;s life: extraction, manufacture, use and disposal. LCAs can be subjective and must be interpreted critically.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Reuse and recycling reduce environmental impact by conserving raw materials and saving energy; the viability of recycling depends on economic factors, collection infrastructure, and the finite nature of the resources replaced.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n14">LCA questions often give you data about two products and ask you to evaluate which has the lower environmental impact. Always refer to specific data from the question, not general knowledge. For extended-response questions, show that you understand LCAs are not simple &mdash; acknowledge that different stages produce different conclusions, and that some impacts are hard to quantify. The command word &lsquo;evaluate&rsquo; requires you to reach a justified conclusion, not just list points. For material selection questions, match properties to the specific requirements given &mdash; never just list all properties of a material without explaining why they are relevant.</p>
