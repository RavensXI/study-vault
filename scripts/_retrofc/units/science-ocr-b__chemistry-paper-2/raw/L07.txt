--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why nanoparticles behave differently, where they appear in everyday products, and the unknowns.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Makes a Nanoparticle?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A particle with dimensions in the range 1&ndash;100 nanometres (nm), where 1 nm = 10&minus;9 m.">nanoparticle</dfn> is a particle with at least one dimension between 1 and 100 nanometres. To put this in context, a single nanometre is one-billionth of a metre &mdash; a human hair is roughly 80,000 nm wide. Nanoparticles sit between individual atoms (which are typically 0.1&ndash;0.5 nm across) and bulk materials that we handle at everyday scales. This &ldquo;in-between&rdquo; scale is what makes them scientifically interesting and commercially valuable.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The key feature of nanoparticles is their extraordinary <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of a particle&rsquo;s exposed surface area to its total volume. Smaller particles have a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio.">surface-area-to-volume ratio</dfn>. When you divide a lump of material into many tiny nanoparticles, the total surface area increases enormously while the total mass stays the same. More surface area means more atoms are at the surface and available to interact with other substances &mdash; reactions and interactions that occur at surfaces happen far more readily than in the bulk of a material.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why dividing a substance into nanoparticles increases its surface-area-to-volume ratio and why this matters for chemical reactions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles have a very high surface-area-to-volume ratio compared with the same material in bulk form. This increases reactivity and changes other properties such as colour, strength, electrical conductivity and melting point.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Properties That Change at the Nanoscale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Many materials behave differently at the nanoscale from their bulk counterparts. Gold is a classic example: in bulk form it is shiny and yellow, but gold nanoparticles can appear red or purple depending on their size because their electrons interact with light differently. <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of carbon made of a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice. It is extremely strong and conducts electricity well.">Graphene</dfn> is another example &mdash; a single layer of carbon atoms only 0.3 nm thick, yet stronger than steel and an excellent electrical conductor. These emergent properties are not simply predictable from the bulk material&rsquo;s properties; they arise from quantum effects and the dominance of surface atoms.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Other properties that can change at the nanoscale include:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">&bull; <strong>Melting point</strong> &mdash; nanoparticles often melt at lower temperatures than the bulk material because surface atoms are less tightly bound.<br>&bull; <strong>Electrical and thermal conductivity</strong> &mdash; some materials become better conductors or better insulators at the nanoscale.<br>&bull; <strong>Colour</strong> &mdash; the wavelengths of light absorbed and reflected change with particle size.<br>&bull; <strong>Catalytic activity</strong> &mdash; increased surface area makes nanoparticles far more reactive as catalysts, allowing reactions to proceed faster at lower temperatures.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Uses of Nanoparticles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The unusual properties of nanoparticles make them useful across a wide range of applications. You need to be able to evaluate these uses alongside the associated risks &mdash; this is a classic <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of weighing the potential harms of an action or technology against its likely benefits, particularly when scientific evidence is incomplete.">risk&ndash;benefit analysis</dfn> that science cannot answer alone.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sunscreen: Titanium Dioxide Nanoparticles</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Traditional sunscreens used large particles of titanium dioxide or zinc oxide, which left a thick white residue on the skin. By reducing these particles to the nanoscale, manufacturers created sunscreens that are transparent while still absorbing and scattering ultraviolet radiation effectively. The high surface-area-to-volume ratio of nano-sized titanium dioxide means more UV radiation is absorbed per gram of material, allowing effective protection with a much thinner layer.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The <strong>benefit</strong> is clear: a wearable, cosmetically acceptable product that encourages people to apply sun protection and thereby reduces skin cancer risk. The <strong>uncertainty</strong> is whether nanoparticles can penetrate damaged or sunburnt skin and enter cells. If they did, the high reactivity that makes them useful could potentially be harmful inside living tissue. Research is ongoing, and regulatory bodies have reviewed the evidence several times; current scientific opinion is that the particles are unlikely to penetrate intact healthy skin in meaningful quantities, but the question is not fully settled.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Other established uses of nanoparticles include:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">&bull; <strong>Medicines and drug delivery:</strong> nanoparticles can carry drugs directly to target cells (such as cancer cells), reducing side effects on healthy tissue. Some <dfn class="term" data-def="Nanoparticles made of silver, which kill bacteria by releasing silver ions that disrupt bacterial cell membranes.">silver nanoparticles</dfn> are incorporated into wound dressings and medical equipment because silver ions are toxic to bacteria but generally safe for human cells at low concentrations.<br>&bull; <strong>Self-cleaning glass:</strong> titanium dioxide nanoparticles on window glass react with UV light to break down organic dirt, which is then washed away by rain. This property is called <strong>photocatalysis</strong>.<br>&bull; <strong>Electronics:</strong> computer chips and memory devices use nanotechnology to pack more transistors into smaller spaces, driving the continuous improvement in computing power.<br>&bull; <strong>Cosmetics:</strong> nanoparticles of various materials are used in moisturisers, foundations and anti-ageing creams to improve texture and penetration.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three everyday products that contain nanoparticles and state one specific property of nanoparticles that makes each application useful.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanoparticles are used in sunscreens (UV absorption), wound dressings (antibacterial silver nanoparticles), self-cleaning glass (photocatalysis), drug delivery (targeted medicine) and electronics (smaller transistors).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Risks and Scientific Uncertainty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Nanoparticles present genuine unknowns that make risk assessment difficult. Because they are small enough to cross cell membranes, enter the bloodstream through the lungs, and potentially accumulate in organs, they may cause harm in ways that larger particles of the same material would not. Research into their long-term effects on human health and the environment is still developing.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Uncertainty Does Not Mean Zero Risk &mdash; or Zero Benefit</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">When scientific data is limited, decision-makers face a dilemma. Banning a technology prematurely prevents potential benefits (better medicines, more effective sun protection) and may push users towards alternatives that have their own risks. Allowing a technology without adequate testing could expose large numbers of people to harm that only becomes apparent later. Reasonable people can disagree on the right course of action when evidence is uncertain &mdash; this is not a failure of science but a feature of how new technologies develop.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Scientists and regulators distinguish between <strong>hazard</strong> (the potential to cause harm) and <strong>risk</strong> (the probability of harm occurring in real-world conditions). A substance may be hazardous in laboratory conditions but pose low risk in normal use if exposure is minimal or the substance is effectively contained. Good risk-benefit reasoning always asks: compared with what? If the alternative to nano-titanium-dioxide sunscreen is no sun protection at all, the risk calculus changes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Environmental concerns are also relevant. Nanoparticles washed off skin or released from products can enter waterways. Silver nanoparticles, for instance, are toxic to aquatic organisms at the concentrations released by some products. Whether this constitutes a significant ecological risk depends on <dfn class="term" data-def="The concentration of a substance that is lethal to 50% of a test population under specified conditions, used to compare toxicity.">toxicity levels</dfn> and dilution in real water bodies &mdash; an ongoing area of research.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">From an exam perspective, the key skill is to evaluate a <strong>named scenario</strong> using evidence: identify the benefit clearly, identify the risk clearly, acknowledge what is unknown, and reach a justified conclusion about whether the use is appropriate. Questions in this area are almost always levels-marked and reward reasoning over lists of facts.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Nanoparticles (1&ndash;100 nm) have a very high surface-area-to-volume ratio compared with bulk materials, giving them unusual properties including altered colour, melting point, reactivity and conductivity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">They are used in sunscreens, drug delivery, wound dressings, self-cleaning glass and electronics &mdash; each application exploits their enhanced reactivity or unique physical properties.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Risks are real but incompletely understood; good scientific reasoning distinguishes hazard from risk, acknowledges uncertainty, and weighs benefits and risks rather than assuming either zero risk or zero benefit.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">Questions on nanoparticles are almost always framed around a specific scenario &mdash; a product, an experiment, a proposed new use. For 6-mark extended-response questions, you must evaluate both sides: name the benefit and explain the science behind it, name the risk and explain why it is uncertain, then reach a conclusion. Simply listing risks or benefits without evaluating them will not reach the highest marks.
