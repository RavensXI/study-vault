--- DESCRIPTION ---
How structure determines whether a material conducts, melts easily, or stretches.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why the Same Atoms Can Give Very Different Materials</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The atoms in a material are the same regardless of the product made from them, but the way those atoms are bonded and arranged gives rise to completely different properties. Chemists use knowledge of bonding and structure to predict how a new material will behave, or to understand how to modify an existing material to improve it. This lesson looks at how the type of bonding &mdash; ionic, covalent (simple or giant), polymer or metallic &mdash; explains measurable properties such as melting point, conductivity, hardness and flexibility.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Carbon is an exceptional example: it can form four covalent bonds and can bond with itself in different arrangements, producing a vast array of materials with radically different properties. Diamond, graphite and graphene are all pure carbon, yet they have completely different bulk properties because of their different structures.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">When explaining a property, always identify: (1) the type of structure, (2) the relevant force or bond, and (3) how much energy is needed to overcome it. The table below summarises the key patterns:</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Polymer Structure and Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Long-chain molecules made of repeating monomer units joined by strong covalent bonds.">Polymers</dfn> are made of very long chains of repeating units bonded together by strong covalent bonds along the chain. However, the forces <em>between</em> neighbouring chains are weaker &mdash; similar in nature to the intermolecular forces in simple molecular substances, but stronger because of the chains&rsquo; length. It is the balance between bond strength along the chain and the strength of these between-chain forces that determines a polymer&rsquo;s properties.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A key contrast is between <dfn class="term" data-def="A polymer that softens and can be remoulded when heated, because the chains are held together only by weak intermolecular forces.">thermosoftening polymers</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A polymer that does not soften on heating because the polymer chains are cross-linked by strong covalent bonds.">thermosetting polymers</dfn>. Thermosoftening polymers (such as polyethene) soften on heating because only weak forces hold the chains together; these can be overcome at moderate temperatures, allowing the chains to slide past each other. They can be reshaped repeatedly &mdash; making them recyclable. Thermosetting polymers (such as epoxy resin or melamine) have strong covalent cross-links between the chains; these cannot be broken by heating without destroying the polymer itself, so thermosetting plastics do not soften and cannot be recycled by melting.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain in two sentences why thermosetting polymers cannot be remoulded when heated, while thermosoftening polymers can. Use the word &lsquo;cross-links&rsquo;.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thermosoftening polymers: chains held by weak forces &rarr; soften on heating, can be reshaped and recycled. Thermosetting polymers: chains cross-linked by strong covalent bonds &rarr; do not soften on heating, cannot be recycled by melting.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Giant Covalent Structures: Diamond vs Graphite vs Graphene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">All three are <dfn class="term" data-def="Different structural forms of the same element with different physical properties.">allotropes</dfn> of carbon. In <strong>diamond</strong>, each carbon atom forms four covalent bonds to four neighbours in a rigid three-dimensional tetrahedral network. There are no free electrons and no layers that can slide, making diamond the hardest natural material and an electrical insulator.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In <strong>graphite</strong>, each carbon bonds to only three others, forming flat hexagonal layers. One electron per carbon atom is delocalised within each layer, giving graphite electrical conductivity along the layers. The layers are only weakly attracted to each other, so they can slide easily, making graphite soft and an effective lubricant. <strong>Graphene</strong> is a single layer of this hexagonal arrangement &mdash; just one atom thick. It is extraordinarily strong (stronger than steel of the same thickness), an excellent conductor, and has potential uses in ultra-thin electronics and as a strengthening additive in composites.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: in graphite, each carbon bonds to ___ others; one electron per carbon is ___. Why does this make graphite conduct electricity while diamond does not?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In graphite, each carbon forms three covalent bonds. One electron per carbon is delocalised and free to carry charge &mdash; so graphite conducts electricity. In diamond, all four bonds are used, leaving no free electrons &mdash; so diamond is an insulator, despite both being made entirely of carbon.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Tensile Strength and Flexibility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16b"><dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum force a material can withstand when stretched before breaking.">Tensile strength</dfn> measures how much force a material can resist when pulled. Different bond types give very different tensile strengths. Metals have high tensile strength because the electron sea holds the ion lattice strongly and the ions can slide without breaking the overall bond. Polymers can have high or low tensile strength depending on chain length and cross-linking: thermosetting polymers with covalent cross-links are rigid and strong; thermosoftening polymers without cross-links are more flexible and stretch more easily under tension. Giant covalent structures like graphene have exceptionally high tensile strength because every bond in the network would have to be broken to tear the material.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The type of bonding and structure determines all bulk properties: melting point, conductivity, hardness, flexibility and tensile strength can all be explained by identifying the relevant forces and how much energy is needed to overcome them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Thermosoftening polymers soften on heating because their chains are held by weak intermolecular forces; thermosetting polymers do not soften because their chains are covalently cross-linked.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Graphite and graphene conduct electricity because of delocalised electrons; diamond does not because all four of carbon&rsquo;s bonds are used &mdash; a dramatic contrast between allotropes of the same element.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When a question asks you to explain a property in terms of structure, always give the three-step answer: (1) name the structure type, (2) identify the relevant force, (3) state how much energy is needed to overcome it and link this to the property. A common error is confusing the bonds within polymer chains (strong covalent) with the forces between chains (weaker intermolecular forces) &mdash; it is the forces between chains that determine the softening temperature, not the bonds along the chain. For graphite questions, always specify that conductivity occurs <em>along</em> the layers, not across them.</p>
