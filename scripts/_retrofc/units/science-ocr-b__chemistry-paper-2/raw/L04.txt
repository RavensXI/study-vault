<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Crude Oil &mdash; A Vital Finite Resource</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A complex mixture of many different hydrocarbon molecules, formed from the remains of ancient organisms over millions of years.">Crude oil</dfn> is one of the most important natural resources on Earth. It is used both as a source of fuels and as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A raw material used by industry as the starting point for making other products.">feedstock</dfn> for making an enormous range of chemicals, including polymers, medicines, dyes and fertilisers. Almost every consumer product we use involves crude oil somewhere in its manufacture or transport. This makes modern life crucially dependent on it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Crude oil is <strong>finite</strong> &mdash; it took millions of years to form from the compressed remains of ancient marine organisms, so it is being consumed far faster than it can be replaced. Decisions about how we use crude oil must balance short-term economic benefits with the need to conserve this resource for future generations. This is an important example of weighing risk, benefit and long-term consequence in a real-world context.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three different uses of crude oil &mdash; aim for two types of use: fuel and feedstock for chemicals. Give a specific chemical example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Crude oil is a finite resource used as both a fuel (petrol, diesel, kerosene) and a feedstock for making polymers, medicines and other chemicals. Because it is non-renewable and our current use is unsustainable, decisions about crude oil use require careful risk&ndash;benefit thinking.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Hydrocarbons and the Alkane Homologous Series</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Crude oil is a complex mixture of <dfn class="term" data-def="Organic molecules containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms.">hydrocarbons</dfn>. The most common are <dfn class="term" data-def="A family of hydrocarbons with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, containing only single carbon&ndash;carbon bonds.">alkanes</dfn>, which form a <strong>homologous series</strong> &mdash; a family of related compounds with the same general formula (\(C_nH_{2n+2}\)) and similar chemical properties. Each member differs from the next by one \(CH_2\) unit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The first four alkanes are: methane (\(CH_4\)), ethane (\(C_2H_6\)), propane (\(C_3H_8\)) and butane (\(C_4H_{10}\)). As the chain length increases, so does the boiling point and viscosity, because longer molecules have stronger <dfn class="term" data-def="Relatively weak forces of attraction between neighbouring molecules.">intermolecular forces</dfn> between them. Shorter alkanes are more flammable and volatile &mdash; valuable properties for fuels.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Fractional Distillation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Crude oil is separated into useful fractions by <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of separating a mixture of liquids with different boiling points by heating and condensing at different levels in a fractionating column.">fractional distillation</dfn>. The oil is heated and the vapour rises up a tall fractionating column that is cooler at the top than at the bottom. As vapours rise, they cool and condense at different heights depending on their boiling points. Fractions with shorter molecule chains (lower boiling points) are collected near the top; fractions with longer chains (higher boiling points) condense lower down or remain as residue at the base.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Fractional distillation separates crude oil by boiling point. Shorter molecules have weaker intermolecular forces, lower boiling points and condense higher up the column. Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular forces and condense lower down.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Cracking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">After distillation, there is too much of the heavier, longer-chain fractions (such as fuel oil) and not enough of the lighter, shorter-chain fractions (such as petrol and diesel). <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of breaking down long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful ones using heat and a catalyst, or high-temperature steam.">Cracking</dfn> is used to convert long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more commercially valuable molecules. It also produces <dfn class="term" data-def="Hydrocarbons with one or more carbon&ndash;carbon double bonds, with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ.">alkenes</dfn>, which are unsaturated and highly reactive &mdash; making them valuable starting materials for the manufacture of polymers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">There are two main methods of cracking. In <strong>catalytic cracking</strong>, hydrocarbon vapour is passed over a hot catalyst (usually a zeolite, a porous aluminium silicate material). In <strong>steam cracking</strong>, hydrocarbon vapour is mixed with steam and heated to very high temperatures. Both methods break C&mdash;C bonds. A typical cracking reaction splits a long-chain alkane into a shorter alkane and an alkene:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">$$C_{10}H_{22} \rightarrow C_8H_{18} + C_2H_4$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Here, a ten-carbon alkane is cracked into octane (a useful petrol component) and ethene (an alkene used to make polyethene). The ethene can be identified by testing with bromine water &mdash; alkenes decolourise bromine water because they react across the double bond; alkanes do not.</p>

    <strong>Step 1:</strong> Count carbons: 10 in decane, 3 in propene, so \(10 - 3 = 7\) carbons left.<br/>
    <strong>Step 2:</strong> Count hydrogens: 22 in decane, 6 in propene, so \(22 - 6 = 16\) hydrogens left.<br/>
    <strong>Step 3:</strong> The other product has formula \(C_7H_{16}\), which is heptane (an alkane, \(C_nH_{2n+2}\) with \(n=7\): \(2 \times 7 + 2 = 16\) ✓).<br/>
    <strong>Balanced equation:</strong> \(C_{10}H_{22} \rightarrow C_3H_6 + C_7H_{16}\)</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Crude oil is a finite mixture of hydrocarbons (mostly alkanes) used as both fuels and a feedstock; our current rate of use is not sustainable and requires careful long-term thinking.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Fractional distillation separates crude oil by boiling point &mdash; shorter molecules have weaker intermolecular forces and condense higher up the fractionating column.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Cracking converts long-chain alkanes into shorter, more useful alkanes (fuels) and alkenes (for making polymers), helping match supply of fractions to demand.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For questions about fractional distillation, always link the fraction&rsquo;s position in the column to its chain length and then to its intermolecular forces &mdash; never just say &lsquo;it has a lower boiling point.&rsquo; For cracking questions, remember to identify the product as an alkene if the formula fits \(C_nH_{2n}\). Extended-response questions often ask you to discuss why crude oil use is not sustainable &mdash; use the phrase &lsquo;finite resource&rsquo; and connect it to the consequences for future generations and the need for alternatives.</p>
