<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Shakespeare & the History Play</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare wrote <em>Henry V</em> around 1599, at the height of his career and during a period of English military anxiety. The Earl of Essex was leading a campaign in Ireland, and Elizabethan audiences were hungry for stories of English military triumph. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A theatrical genre that dramatises real historical events, shaping them to serve political or moral purposes. Shakespeare wrote ten history plays covering the reigns of English kings from Richard II to Richard III.">history play</dfn> was a genre Shakespeare helped create, and <em>Henry V</em> is its culmination: a play that dramatises England’s greatest medieval victory at Agincourt (1415) while forcing the audience to question what that victory cost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The play draws heavily on Raphael <dfn class="term" data-def="An Elizabethan chronicler whose Chronicles of England, Scotland, and Ireland (1587) was Shakespeare’s primary historical source for his history plays.">Holinshed</dfn>’s <em>Chronicles</em> (1587), but Shakespeare reshapes the material for dramatic effect. In Holinshed, Henry’s claim to France is straightforward; Shakespeare adds the comically long Salic Law speech to make the Archbishop look self-serving. In Holinshed, the battle is a clear victory; Shakespeare adds the common soldiers’ doubts and Henry’s agonised soliloquy the night before. The play can be read as patriotic propaganda celebrating English glory, or as an ambiguous exploration of the human cost of war. For the Eduqas exam, this duality is essential.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: When was Henry V written, what was happening in England, and why does this matter?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>Henry V</em> was written around 1599, during the Earl of Essex’s Irish campaign. Elizabethan audiences saw parallels between Henry’s French war and England’s current military ventures. The Chorus explicitly references Essex in Act 5 (“the General of our gracious Empress”). Shakespeare flatters Elizabeth I’s regime while also questioning whether military glory justifies its cost.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Tudor Propaganda & the Myth of Agincourt</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of art, literature or media to promote a particular political viewpoint. Shakespeare’s history plays served as Tudor propaganda by celebrating the royal lineage and justifying the ruling dynasty.">propaganda</dfn> function of the play is clear: the Tudor monarchs traced their lineage back to Henry V’s Welsh connections, so celebrating Henry flattered Elizabeth I. Agincourt (25 October 1415) was already a legendary English victory — a small, exhausted army defeating a vastly larger French force. Shakespeare transforms this historical event into a myth of national identity, using Henry’s speeches to create an imagined community of “we few, we happy few, we band of brothers.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">But Shakespeare complicates the propaganda. The Chorus’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A short concluding section at the end of a play. The Epilogue of Henry V reminds the audience that Henry’s son lost everything his father won.">Epilogue</dfn> reminds the audience that Henry’s son, Henry VI, “lost France and made his England bleed, / Which oft our stage hath shown.” The triumph is temporary. The cycle of war continues. Shakespeare refuses to let patriotic celebration have the last word.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Henry V is a sequel. In <em>Henry IV Parts 1 and 2</em>, Prince Hal is a wild young man who drinks with Falstaff and his disreputable companions in the Boar’s Head Tavern. His transformation into the ideal king is one of Shakespeare’s great character arcs. But the cost of transformation is high: in <em>Henry IV Part 2</em>, Henry publicly rejects Falstaff (“I know thee not, old man”), and in <em>Henry V</em>, we learn that Falstaff has died, broken-hearted. The question of whether Henry’s kingship requires the sacrifice of his private humanity runs throughout the play. Is he a great king because he puts duty above personal feeling, or is he diminished by his coldness?</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ambiguity: Patriotism or Anti-War?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The play has been performed as both a patriotic celebration and a critique of war. Laurence Olivier’s 1944 film version was made as wartime propaganda, emphasising Henry’s heroism. Kenneth Branagh’s 1989 film version emphasised the mud, blood and moral ambiguity. Shakespeare gives evidence for both readings: the St Crispin’s Day speech is genuinely inspiring, but the threat to Harfleur (“naked infants spitted upon pikes”) is genuinely horrifying. For the Eduqas exam, the strongest responses hold both readings simultaneously, arguing that Shakespeare deliberately creates a play that can be read in contradictory ways.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name one piece of evidence for the patriotic reading and one for the anti-war reading.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The play supports two contradictory readings: patriotic celebration (the St Crispin’s Day speech, the Chorus’s enthusiasm, the victory at Agincourt) and anti-war critique (the Harfleur threat, the common soldiers’ doubts, the Epilogue’s reminder that everything Henry won was lost). Top-band Eduqas responses hold both readings together.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Shakespeare wrote <em>Henry V</em> in 1599 during the Essex campaign — the play flatters Elizabeth I by celebrating her ancestor’s military triumph.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">The play supports two contradictory readings: patriotic propaganda and anti-war critique. The strongest exam responses hold both simultaneously.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The Epilogue undercuts the triumph by reminding the audience that Henry’s son lost everything — Shakespeare refuses to end on simple celebration.</li></ul>
