<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Salic Law Speech: Justifying War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 1 opens with the Archbishop of Canterbury delivering a famously tedious speech about the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Frankish legal code that excluded inheritance through the female line. Canterbury uses it to argue that the French claim it bars Henry from the French throne is invalid.">Salic Law</dfn> to justify Henry’s claim to the French throne. The speech is so convoluted that audiences often cannot follow it — and Shakespeare may have intended this. Canterbury and the Bishop of Ely have a private conversation beforehand (Act 1, Scene 1) revealing that they support the war partly to protect Church lands from a parliamentary bill. The legal justification for war is tangled with self-interest. Shakespeare invites the audience to question whether the cause is just from the very beginning.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Henry appears to demand certainty: “May I with right and conscience make this claim?” He insists Canterbury take “heed how you awake our sleeping sword of war” and warns him that the deaths of soldiers will be on Canterbury’s conscience. Shakespeare presents Henry as a king who wants moral clarity — but he asks the question of a man who has a financial incentive to give the answer he wants. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Irony arising from the structure of a scene or situation, rather than from individual words. Henry asks for moral guidance from men who have political reasons to encourage war.">structural irony</dfn> is sharp: the audience can see what Henry apparently cannot (or will not).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does Canterbury support the war, and what does this reveal about the justification for Henry’s invasion?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Canterbury supports the war partly to protect Church lands from a parliamentary bill. His Salic Law speech is deliberately convoluted. Shakespeare creates structural irony: Henry asks for moral guidance from men with political motives to encourage war. The justice of Henry’s cause is questionable from Act 1.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Tennis Balls: Insult & Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The French <dfn class="term" data-def="The eldest son of the French king. In Henry V, the Dauphin sends tennis balls as an insult, implying Henry is still the frivolous youth of his wild past.">Dauphin</dfn> sends Henry a gift of tennis balls, mocking him as a frivolous young man better suited to games than politics. Henry’s response is one of the play’s most powerful speeches: “tell the pleasant Prince this mock of his / Hath turned his balls to gun-stones.” The pun on “balls” is deliberate: the Dauphin’s joke becomes a threat of artillery. Henry transforms a personal insult into a justification for war, promising that “many a thousand widows / Shall this his mock mock out of their dear husbands.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The speech reveals Henry’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The art of using language effectively and persuasively. Henry is the play’s supreme rhetorician, adapting his language to every audience and situation.">rhetorical</dfn> brilliance. He shifts the moral responsibility for the war onto the Dauphin: it is the insult, not Henry’s ambition, that causes the invasion. This is a political performance — Henry frames his decision to invade France as a response to provocation rather than an act of aggression. Shakespeare allows the audience to admire the rhetoric while questioning whether it is genuine or calculated.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">In Act 2, Scene 2, Henry exposes three traitors — Cambridge, Scroop and Grey — who have been bribed by the French to assassinate him. Henry traps them brilliantly: he asks their advice about pardoning a man who insulted him, and when they argue for severity, he turns their own argument against them. “The mercy that was quick in us but late, / By your own counsel is suppressed and killed.” The scene reveals Henry as a cold, calculating strategist who uses performance to control others. His betrayal by Scroop — a close personal friend — is the one moment where genuine emotion breaks through: “I will weep for thee; / For this revolt of thine, methinks, is like / Another fall of man.” The religious language (“fall of man”) elevates personal betrayal to cosmic significance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Death of Falstaff (Act 2, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Falstaff never appears in <em>Henry V</em>, but his death is reported by Mistress Quickly in one of Shakespeare’s most moving passages: “his nose was as sharp as a pen, and ’a babbled of green fields.” The audience knows from <em>Henry IV Part 2</em> that Henry rejected Falstaff publicly (“I know thee not, old man”), and Pistol says Falstaff’s heart was “fracted and corroborate” (broken and crushed) — a typically mangled Pistol malapropism in Act 2, Scene 1. Falstaff’s death is the cost of Henry’s transformation from wild prince to disciplined king. Shakespeare does not let the audience forget that Henry’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The qualities and skills required to be an effective ruler. Henry V presents kingship as requiring the sacrifice of personal feeling.">kingship</dfn> was purchased at the expense of human warmth.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how Falstaff’s death connects to the play’s question about what kingship costs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Falstaff dies off-stage, broken by Henry’s rejection. His death symbolises the sacrifice of personal warmth and private humanity that Henry’s kingship demands. Shakespeare shows that to become the ideal public king, Henry must kill the private man he once was.</p>
</div>

They provide a cynical, self-serving counterpoint to Henry’s heroic rhetoric.">Bardolph, Pistol and Nym</dfn> — go to war in France, but they are cowards and thieves, not heroes. They provide a comic counterpoint to Henry’s noble speeches: while Henry talks of honour, Pistol talks of profit. Bardolph is later hanged for stealing from a church — and Henry, who once drank with him, allows the execution without mercy: “We would have all such offenders so cut off.” Shakespeare uses the underworld characters to show that war is not all heroism — for ordinary soldiers, it is brutality, opportunism and death.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">The Salic Law speech is deliberately convoluted, and Canterbury’s motives are self-serving — the justice of Henry’s war is questionable from Act 1.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Henry transforms the Dauphin’s tennis balls insult into a justification for war, demonstrating his rhetorical brilliance while shifting moral responsibility.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Falstaff’s off-stage death symbolises the sacrifice of private humanity that kingship demands.</li></ul>

Henry frames the invasion as a response to the Dauphin’s insult, but Canterbury’s motives are self-serving and the Salic Law justification is deliberately obscure. Top-band responses acknowledge this ambiguity: Henry is both impressive and calculating.</p>
