--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Henry Woos Katherine (Act 5, Scene 2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the military triumph at Agincourt, Shakespeare shifts to a radically different register. Act 5, Scene 2 is an extended <dfn class="term" data-def="A scene in which one character attempts to win another’s love through persuasion. Henry’s wooing of Katherine is both charming and politically calculated.">wooing scene</dfn> in which Henry attempts to win Princess Katherine of France. He presents himself as a “plain soldier” who “cannot look greenly, nor gasp out my eloquence” — but this claim of simplicity is itself a rhetorical strategy. The audience has watched Henry deliver some of the most eloquent speeches in English literature; his pretence of plain-speaking is a deliberate performance designed to seem sincere.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The scene mixes English and French, creating comic confusion as Henry and Katherine struggle to communicate. Shakespeare uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="A barrier created by different languages. Henry and Katherine cannot fully understand each other, and the language barrier becomes a metaphor for the gap between political reality and personal connection.">language barrier</dfn> to generate humour, but it also carries serious implications. Katherine has very little genuine choice: the marriage is a political necessity sealed by the Treaty of Troyes. Her consent is a formality. When Henry says “you and I cannot be confined within the weak list of a country’s fashion,” he is both charming and presumptuous — he assumes his will overrides both custom and Katherine’s own preferences.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does Henry present himself to Katherine, and why is this a performance?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Henry claims to be a “plain soldier” who cannot speak eloquently — but the audience knows he is the play’s supreme rhetorician. His pretence of simplicity is itself a performance. The wooing scene shows Henry <dfn class="term" data-def="Adapting language and register to suit different audiences. Henry speaks differently to soldiers, enemies and Katherine.">code-switching</dfn> once more: he uses a different register for love than for war, but both are calculated to achieve his objective.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Treaty of Troyes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The marriage is sealed by the Treaty of Troyes, which makes Henry heir to the French throne. The French King agrees to all of Henry’s terms — not because of the wooing scene but because of the military defeat at Agincourt. Love and politics are inseparable: the marriage is both a personal union and a political <dfn class="term" data-def="The joining of two kingdoms or territories through marriage. Henry’s marriage to Katherine is designed to unite England and France under one crown.">dynastic alliance</dfn>. Katherine is exchanged between nations in much the same way that Hero is exchanged between men in <em>Much Ado</em> — her consent is assumed, not genuinely sought.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Shakespeare presents the peace as fragile. The French King blesses the marriage with hopes that “English may as French, French Englishmen, / Receive each other.” But the audience already knows — from the Henry VI plays and from the Epilogue about to come — that this peace will not last. Henry’s triumph is temporary. Everything he has won will be lost by his son.</p>

The glory of Agincourt is framed by futility. Shakespeare refuses to let the play end on simple celebration: triumph and loss are inseparable, and the cycle of war continues.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Katherine’s Agency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Katherine is one of the play’s most debated characters. Her English lesson scene (Act 3, Scene 4) is charming and comic — she practises English words with her gentlewoman Alice, and the scene ends with double entendres that would have delighted Shakespeare’s audience. But the comedy conceals a serious point: Katherine is preparing herself for a future she cannot control. She will be given to the English king regardless of her own wishes. In the wooing scene, she speaks very little and mostly in French; Henry dominates the conversation. Her final consent — “Dat is as it shall please de roi mon père” (it is as my father the king shall please) — defers to her father’s authority, not her own desire.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Katherine’s final line in the wooing scene reveal about women’s agency?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Katherine’s consent — “as it shall please de roi mon père” — defers to her father, not her own desire. Like Hero in <em>Much Ado</em>, Katherine is exchanged between men as a political commodity. Shakespeare presents the marriage as both romantic and transactional, refusing to simplify the relationship between love and power.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">The tonal shift from Agincourt’s carnage to the wooing scene’s comedy is deliberately jarring. Shakespeare moves from the death of the Boy and the order to kill prisoners straight into Henry’s playful courtship. This juxtaposition forces the audience to question the relationship between war and its supposed rewards: is the marriage a happy ending or a political transaction imposed on a defeated nation? The Epilogue then undercuts even this ambiguous happiness by reminding the audience of what comes next. Shakespeare structures Act 5 to deny the audience simple satisfaction.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Henry’s claim to be a “plain soldier” is a performance — the audience knows he is a supreme rhetorician code-switching to suit his audience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Katherine’s consent defers to her father (“as it shall please de roi mon père”), revealing that the marriage is a political transaction, not a free choice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The Epilogue devastates the triumph: Henry’s son lost France, the cycle of war continues, and Shakespeare refuses a simple happy ending.</li></ul>
