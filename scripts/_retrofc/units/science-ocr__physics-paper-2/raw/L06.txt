Renewable and non-renewable energy sources, efficiency and sustainability.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Resources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The main energy sources available on Earth fall into two categories:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">• <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that will run out because they are used faster than they can be replaced. Examples: fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) and nuclear fuel.">Non-renewable</dfn> sources — will eventually run out. These include <strong>fossil fuels</strong> (coal, oil, natural gas) and <strong>nuclear fuel</strong> (uranium, plutonium). Fossil fuels formed over millions of years from the remains of dead organisms. They are being consumed far faster than they can form.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that are replenished naturally and will not run out if managed sustainably. Examples: solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, geothermal, biomass.">Renewable</dfn> sources — are replenished naturally and will not run out. These include <strong>solar</strong>, <strong>wind</strong>, <strong>hydroelectric</strong>, <strong>tidal</strong>, <strong>geothermal</strong>, <strong>wave</strong> and <strong>biomass</strong> (P6.2a).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four non-renewable and six renewable energy sources from memory, then classify nuclear as non-renewable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Non-renewable sources (fossil fuels, nuclear) will run out. Renewable sources (solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, wave, biomass) are replenished naturally. Nuclear is non-renewable but does not produce CO\(_2\) during generation.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Environmental Impacts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Every energy resource has environmental consequences (P6.2b):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Fossil fuels</strong> release CO\(_2\) when burned, contributing to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The trapping of heat energy in the Earth's atmosphere by gases such as CO2 and methane, causing global temperatures to rise.">greenhouse effect</dfn> and climate change. They also release sulfur dioxide (causing acid rain) and particulates (causing respiratory problems). Oil spills damage marine ecosystems.<br><strong>Nuclear power</strong> produces no CO\(_2\) during generation and is very reliable, but creates <strong>radioactive waste</strong> that remains dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored securely. There is also risk of nuclear accidents (e.g. Chernobyl, Fukushima).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Renewable sources</strong> generally produce little or no CO\(_2\) during operation, making them crucial for tackling climate change. However, they often have significant <strong>land use</strong> requirements (solar farms, wind farms) and can affect local ecosystems and communities. The <strong>embodied energy</strong> in manufacturing solar panels and wind turbines must also be considered, though this is typically repaid within a few years of operation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The <strong>carbon footprint</strong> of an energy source considers the total greenhouse gas emissions across its entire lifecycle — from construction through operation to decommissioning. On this measure, wind, solar, nuclear and hydroelectric all have much lower footprints than fossil fuels. Natural gas has the lowest carbon footprint of the fossil fuels (about half that of coal per unit of energy), which is why it is sometimes called a ‘transition fuel’ as countries move towards renewables.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Energy <strong>demand</strong> varies throughout the day (peak demand in the evening) and throughout the year (higher in winter). <strong>Baseload</strong> power stations (nuclear, gas) run continuously to meet minimum demand, while renewable sources and gas turbines provide additional capacity during peaks. The growing use of <strong>battery storage</strong> and <strong>pumped hydro storage</strong> helps balance intermittent renewable supply with variable demand.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Renewable sources produce little or no CO\(_2\) during operation but many are intermittent or require specific locations. No energy source is perfect — each has trade-offs between cost, reliability and environmental impact.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The National Grid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of cables and transformers that distributes electricity from power stations to homes and businesses across the country.">National Grid</dfn> is a network of cables and transformers that distributes electricity from power stations to consumers across the country (P6.2c). Electricity is transmitted at <strong>high voltage</strong> and <strong>low current</strong> through overhead power lines. This is because energy loss due to heating in the cables is proportional to the current squared (\(P = I^2 R\)). Using high voltage reduces the current and therefore reduces energy wasted as heat in the cables.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step-up transformers</strong> at power stations increase the voltage (to 400,000 V) for efficient long-distance transmission. <strong>Step-down transformers</strong> near homes reduce the voltage (to 230 V) for safe domestic use.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n21">The UK is transitioning from fossil fuels to renewables to reduce carbon emissions and improve <strong>energy security</strong> (not relying on imported fuels). The energy mix — the combination of sources used — is changing: wind and solar capacity is increasing while coal use has virtually ended.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Challenges include the <strong>intermittency</strong> of wind and solar (requiring backup from gas or nuclear), the need for <strong>energy storage</strong> (e.g. batteries, pumped hydro storage) and the cost of upgrading infrastructure. Nuclear power is being considered as a reliable low-carbon baseload source to complement renewables.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The National Grid transmits electricity at high voltage (low current) to reduce energy losses (\(P = I^2 R\)). Step-up transformers increase voltage for transmission; step-down transformers reduce it for safe domestic use.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Non-renewable sources (fossil fuels, nuclear) will run out; renewables (solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, biomass) are replenished naturally.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Fossil fuels cause climate change (CO\(_2\)), acid rain (SO\(_2\)) and pollution. Nuclear is low-carbon but produces radioactive waste.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The National Grid transmits at high voltage/low current to minimise heating losses. Step-up and step-down transformers control voltage.</li>
</ul>
