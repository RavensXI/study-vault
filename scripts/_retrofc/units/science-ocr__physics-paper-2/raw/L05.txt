<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Conservation of Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another. The total energy of a closed system remains constant.">law of conservation of energy</dfn> states that energy cannot be created or destroyed — only transferred between stores. In any closed system, the total energy before a process equals the total energy after. Energy that seems to ‘disappear’ has been <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy that is spread out into less useful stores, usually thermal energy in the surroundings. Also called wasted energy.">dissipated</dfn> — spread out into less useful thermal energy in the surroundings (P6.1a).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Energy can be stored in different ways: <strong>kinetic</strong> (moving objects), <strong>gravitational potential</strong> (objects at height), <strong>elastic potential</strong> (stretched or compressed objects), <strong>chemical</strong> (food, fuels, batteries), <strong>thermal</strong> (hot objects), <strong>magnetic</strong>, <strong>electrostatic</strong> and <strong>nuclear</strong>. Energy transfers between these stores via heating, radiation, electrical current or mechanical work.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the eight energy stores from memory. Then explain why the total energy in a closed system never changes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It transfers between stores (kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, chemical, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic, nuclear). Dissipated energy is wasted as heat.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Work Done</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When a force moves an object through a distance, <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred when a force moves an object. Measured in joules (J). Calculated as force multiplied by distance in the direction of the force.">work is done</dfn>. Work done is a measure of energy transferred. The equation is (P6.1b):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$W = F \times d$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">where \(W\) = work done in joules (J), \(F\) = force in newtons (N), and \(d\) = distance moved in the direction of the force in metres (m). One joule of work is done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Example: a person pushes a box with a force of 50 N across 3 m. Work done = \(50 \times 3 = 150\) J. This 150 J of energy has been transferred from the person’s chemical energy store to the kinetic energy store of the box (and some to thermal energy through friction).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When work is done against gravity (e.g. lifting an object), energy is transferred to the object’s <strong>gravitational potential energy</strong> store. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$GPE = m \times g \times h$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">where \(m\) = mass in kg, \(g\) = gravitational field strength (9.8 N/kg on Earth) and \(h\) = height gained in metres. For example, lifting a 5 kg bag to a shelf 2 m high: \(GPE = 5 \times 9.8 \times 2 = 98\) J.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Similarly, a moving object has <strong>kinetic energy</strong> calculated by:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$KE = \frac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">where \(v\) = speed in m/s. Notice that kinetic energy depends on \(v^2\) — doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. This is why braking distance increases dramatically at higher speeds and why speed limits exist in residential areas.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred or work is done. Measured in watts (W), where 1 watt = 1 joule per second.">Power</dfn> measures how quickly energy is transferred (P6.1c). The equations are:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">$$P = \frac{E}{t} \quad \text{and} \quad P = \frac{W}{t}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">where \(P\) = power in watts (W), \(E\) = energy transferred in joules (J), \(W\) = work done in joules (J), and \(t\) = time in seconds (s). One watt equals one joule per second.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Example: a 60 W light bulb transfers 60 J of energy every second. If it is on for 5 minutes (300 s), total energy transferred = \(60 \times 300 = 18{,}000\) J = 18 kJ.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">A more powerful device transfers the same amount of energy in <strong>less time</strong>. This is why a 2000 W kettle boils water faster than a 1000 W kettle — it transfers energy at twice the rate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the equations for work done and power from memory. Then calculate: how much work is done lifting a 200 N weight through 4 m?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Work done: \(W = F \times d\) (joules). Power: \(P = E/t\) or \(P = W/t\) (watts). Power measures the rate of energy transfer — higher power means faster energy transfer.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">No energy transfer is 100% efficient. Some energy is always dissipated as waste heat. <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of input energy that is usefully transferred. Calculated as useful output energy divided by total input energy, expressed as a decimal or percentage.">Efficiency</dfn> measures how much of the input energy is usefully transferred (P6.1d):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy}}{\text{total input energy}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Efficiency can be expressed as a decimal (0 to 1) or as a percentage (multiply by 100). For example, a motor that receives 500 J of electrical energy and does 350 J of useful work has efficiency = \(\frac{350}{500} = 0.70\) = 70%. The remaining 150 J is dissipated as heat and sound.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Reducing Wasted Energy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Engineers design systems to minimise energy waste and improve efficiency:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">• <strong>Lubrication</strong> — reduces friction between moving parts, decreasing the thermal energy wasted.<br>• <strong>Streamlining</strong> — reduces air resistance on vehicles, so less energy is wasted overcoming drag.<br>• <strong>Thermal insulation</strong> — slows heat transfer from buildings (loft insulation, double glazing, cavity wall insulation), keeping homes warmer with less heating.<br>• <strong>LED lighting</strong> — converts a higher proportion of electrical energy into light compared to incandescent bulbs, which waste most energy as heat.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Improving efficiency is important for reducing fuel consumption, lowering energy bills and decreasing carbon emissions from fossil fuels.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29">In calculations, you may need to combine the work, power and efficiency equations. For example, if a crane with 40% efficiency lifts a 2000 N load through 15 m in 30 seconds, the useful work done is \(2000 \times 15 = 30{,}000\) J. Since only 40% of the input energy is useful: total input energy = \(\frac{30{,}000}{0.4} = 75{,}000\) J. The power of the crane = \(\frac{75{,}000}{30} = 2{,}500\) W = 2.5 kW.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Elastic potential energy</strong> is stored when a spring or elastic material is stretched or compressed. The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">$$EPE = \frac{1}{2} \times k \times e^2$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">where \(k\) = spring constant in N/m and \(e\) = extension in metres. Like kinetic energy, elastic potential energy depends on the square of the extension — doubling the stretch quadruples the energy stored. This equation applies only within the <strong>limit of proportionality</strong>, where force and extension are directly proportional (Hooke’s Law).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: a light bulb uses 100 J of electrical energy and produces 15 J of light. Calculate its efficiency as a decimal and a percentage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input. No device is 100% efficient. Wasted energy can be reduced through lubrication, streamlining, insulation and using more efficient technologies like LEDs.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Energy is conserved: it transfers between stores but is never created or destroyed. Dissipated energy is wasted as heat.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Work done = force × distance (\(W = Fd\)). Power = energy ÷ time (\(P = E/t\)). Both are measured in standard SI units.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input. Reduce waste through lubrication, insulation and efficient technologies.</li>
</ul>
