<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed, Distance and Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The OCR Gateway specification requires you to know typical speeds for everyday scenarios: walking (~1.5 m/s), running (~3 m/s), cycling (~6 m/s), cars in town (~13 m/s or 30 mph), motorway driving (~30 m/s or 70 mph), speed of sound in air (~340 m/s), and wind speeds which vary widely (P8.1a).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="How fast an object is moving, calculated as distance divided by time. Measured in metres per second (m/s).">Speed</dfn> is the distance travelled per unit time. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \quad \text{or} \quad v = \frac{s}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">where \(v\) = speed in m/s, \(s\) = distance in metres, \(t\) = time in seconds. Speed can also be measured in km/h for longer journeys.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall four typical speeds from memory (walking, running, cycling, car on motorway), giving values in m/s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Speed = distance ÷ time (\(v = s/t\)). Typical speeds: walking 1.5 m/s, running 3 m/s, cycling 6 m/s, car on motorway 30 m/s, sound in air 340 m/s.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Velocity and Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a given direction. Unlike speed, velocity is a vector quantity because it includes direction.">Velocity</dfn> is speed in a given direction. It is a <strong>vector</strong> quantity (has both magnitude and direction), while speed is a scalar (magnitude only). An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a changing velocity because its direction is constantly changing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity. Measured in metres per second squared (m/s²). Positive acceleration means speeding up; negative acceleration (deceleration) means slowing down.">Acceleration</dfn> is the rate of change of velocity (P8.1b):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">where \(a\) = acceleration in m/s\(^2\), \(v\) = final velocity, \(u\) = initial velocity, \(t\) = time. Positive acceleration means speeding up; negative acceleration (<strong>deceleration</strong>) means slowing down.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Example: a car accelerates from rest (\(u = 0\)) to 20 m/s in 8 seconds. Acceleration = \(\frac{20 - 0}{8} = 2.5\) m/s\(^2\). If the same car then brakes from 20 m/s to rest in 4 seconds, the deceleration = \(\frac{0 - 20}{4} = -5\) m/s\(^2\). The negative sign indicates slowing down.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">An object in <strong>free fall</strong> near the Earth’s surface accelerates at approximately \(9.8\) m/s\(^2\) (often rounded to \(10\) m/s\(^2\) for calculations). This is the acceleration due to gravity, denoted \(g\). In the absence of air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of their mass — a key insight from Galileo’s experiments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The relationship between force, mass and acceleration (Newton’s Second Law) can also be written as \(a = \frac{F}{m}\), showing that for a given force, a heavier object accelerates less. This explains why a fully loaded lorry takes much longer to stop than an empty car travelling at the same speed — both the greater mass and the greater kinetic energy (\(KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\)) increase the braking distance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Reaction Time and Stopping Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="The time between a stimulus (e.g. seeing a hazard) and the start of a response (e.g. pressing the brake). Typical human reaction time is 0.2-0.9 seconds.">Reaction time</dfn> is the delay between seeing a hazard and responding. Typical human reaction time is 0.2–0.9 seconds. Factors that increase reaction time include tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions and age.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total distance a vehicle travels from the moment a hazard is seen to when it stops completely. Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance.">Stopping distance</dfn> = thinking distance + braking distance (P8.1d). <strong>Thinking distance</strong> is the distance travelled during the driver’s reaction time. <strong>Braking distance</strong> is the distance travelled once the brakes are applied. Factors increasing braking distance include wet or icy roads, worn tyres, worn brakes and higher speed. At higher speeds, the braking force needed is greater, which means more energy must be transferred to heat in the brakes.</p>

    <span>Forces and Newton’s Laws</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Newton’s First Law:</strong> an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays at constant velocity, unless acted on by a resultant (unbalanced) force.<br><strong>Newton’s Second Law:</strong> \(F = m \times a\) — the resultant force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. A larger force produces a larger acceleration; a larger mass reduces the acceleration for the same force.<br><strong>Newton’s Third Law:</strong> for every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force. These act on different objects.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">When an object falls, it initially accelerates due to gravity. As speed increases, air resistance increases until it equals the gravitational force. At this point, the resultant force is zero and the object reaches <strong>terminal velocity</strong> — falling at a constant speed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: state Newton's three laws of motion from memory. Then explain what terminal velocity is and why a skydiver reaches it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Newton’s Second Law: \(F = ma\). At terminal velocity, air resistance equals weight and acceleration is zero.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Speed = distance/time. Velocity = speed + direction. Acceleration = change in velocity/time (\(a = (v-u)/t\)).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Distance-time graphs: gradient = speed. Velocity-time graphs: gradient = acceleration, area = distance travelled.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Newton’s Second Law: \(F = ma\).</li>
</ul>
