--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Electromagnetic Waves?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Transverse waves that transfer energy from a source to an absorber. They travel at the speed of light in a vacuum and do not need a medium.">Electromagnetic (EM) waves</dfn> are transverse waves that can travel through a vacuum (empty space) — they do not need a medium. This is what makes them fundamentally different from mechanical waves like sound. In a vacuum, all EM waves travel at the same speed — the speed of light:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">EM waves are produced by changes in atoms and their nuclei, or by oscillating charges (such as electrons moving back and forth in an aerial). They transfer energy from a source to an absorber. Because the wave equation \(v = f\lambda\) still applies, and the speed is fixed in a vacuum, a higher frequency always means a shorter wavelength — and vice versa.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Electromagnetic Spectrum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous range of electromagnetic waves ordered by wavelength and frequency, from radio waves (longest wavelength) to gamma rays (shortest wavelength).">electromagnetic spectrum</dfn> is a continuous range of waves, ordered by wavelength and frequency. From longest wavelength (lowest frequency) to shortest wavelength (highest frequency):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Radio waves → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">There are no sharp boundaries between the regions — the spectrum is continuous, and one type of wave blends gradually into the next. Our eyes can only detect the <strong>visible light</strong> portion, which ranges from red (longest visible wavelength, about 700 nm) to violet (shortest, about 400 nm). Everything else in the spectrum is invisible to us but can be detected with instruments.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Learn the spectrum order using a mnemonic: 'Raging Martians Invaded Venus Using X-ray Guns' (Radio, Microwave, Infrared, Visible, UV, X-ray, Gamma). Remember: all travel at the same speed in a vacuum.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All EM waves travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum. The spectrum ranges from radio (long wavelength, low frequency, low energy) to gamma (short wavelength, high frequency, high energy). Visible light is a small section in the middle.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">• <strong>Radio waves</strong> (wavelength up to km): TV and radio broadcasts, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi. Can induce oscillations in electrical circuits, which is how aerials receive signals.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">• <strong>Microwaves</strong> (wavelength cm): microwave ovens (water molecules absorb the energy), satellite communication, mobile phone networks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">• <strong>Infrared (IR)</strong> (wavelength μm): thermal imaging cameras, remote controls, heating (toasters, grills), fibre optic communications, night-vision goggles. All warm objects emit IR radiation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">• <strong>Visible light</strong> (400–700 nm): human vision, photography, fibre optic data transmission, laser surgery.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">• <strong>Ultraviolet (UV)</strong>: fluorescent lamps, security marking (invisible ink that glows under UV), sterilising water, detecting forged banknotes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">• <strong>X-rays</strong>: medical imaging of bones and teeth, airport security scanners, checking welds in pipelines.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">• <strong>Gamma rays</strong> (shortest wavelength): cancer treatment (radiotherapy — targeted beams kill cancer cells), sterilising medical equipment and food, detecting leaks in pipes (industrial tracers).</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Hazards of EM Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Higher frequency EM waves carry more energy per <dfn class="term" data-def="A packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy. Higher frequency waves have more energetic photons.">photon</dfn> and can therefore be more dangerous to living tissue. The key hazards are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">• <strong>Infrared</strong>: can cause skin burns if exposure is prolonged (e.g. sitting too close to a fire).<br>• <strong>Ultraviolet</strong>: causes sunburn, premature skin ageing, and increases the risk of skin cancer. Can also damage the eyes (leading to cataracts). Sunscreen and UV-blocking sunglasses reduce exposure.<br>• <strong>X-rays</strong>: can damage cells and DNA, potentially leading to cancer. Medical exposure is carefully controlled — lead aprons shield staff, exposure times are minimised, and the number of X-rays is limited.<br>• <strong>Gamma rays</strong>: the most penetrating and most energetic. Can cause cell mutation and cancer with prolonged exposure. In medical use, beams are tightly focused and the surrounding healthy tissue is shielded.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="The exam may ask about the balance of risks for using EM waves. For example, X-rays can cause cancer but are essential for diagnosing broken bones. You should be able to evaluate these trade-offs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UV, X-rays and gamma rays can damage human tissue. Higher frequency = higher energy = more hazardous. The benefits of their use in medicine must be weighed against the risks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Absorption, Transmission and Reflection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Different substances interact with EM waves in different ways, depending on the wavelength of the radiation and the nature of the material:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">• <dfn class="term" data-def="When a material takes in the energy carried by an electromagnetic wave.">Absorption</dfn>: the wave's energy is taken in by the material and usually converted to heat. For example, dark surfaces absorb visible light, and your skin absorbs UV.<br>• <strong>Transmission</strong>: the wave passes through the material without being absorbed. For example, glass transmits visible light, and X-rays pass through soft tissue.<br>• <strong>Reflection</strong>: the wave bounces off the surface. For example, mirrors reflect visible light, and metal surfaces reflect radio waves.<br>• <strong>Refraction</strong>: the wave changes speed (and may change direction) when entering a different material. This is how lenses focus light.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n24">A given material may absorb some wavelengths, transmit others, and reflect the rest. For example, glass transmits visible light but absorbs UV. The atmosphere transmits visible light and radio waves but absorbs most UV, X-rays and gamma rays — which is fortunate for life on Earth.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Radio waves have a unique property at GCSE level: they can be <strong>produced by oscillations in electrical circuits</strong>. When electrons oscillate (move back and forth) in a transmitter aerial at a certain frequency, they generate radio waves at that same frequency. Conversely, when radio waves reach a receiver aerial, they <strong>induce oscillations</strong> (an alternating current) in the aerial circuit. This is the basis of all radio communication — from AM/FM radio to Wi-Fi and mobile phones. The frequency of the oscillation must match the frequency of the wave for efficient transmission and reception.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Calculating EM Wave Properties</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Since all EM waves travel at \(c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\) in a vacuum, you can calculate the wavelength of any EM wave if you know its frequency, using \(\lambda = \frac{c}{f}\). For example, a radio station broadcasting at 100 MHz: \(\lambda = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{100 \times 10^6} = 3 \text{ m}\). An X-ray with frequency \(3 \times 10^{18}\) Hz: \(\lambda = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{3 \times 10^{18}} = 1 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}\) (about the size of an atom).</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">All EM waves are transverse, travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum, and do not need a medium. Spectrum: radio (longest \(\lambda\), lowest \(f\)) to gamma (shortest \(\lambda\), highest \(f\)).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Each region has specific uses. UV, X-rays and gamma rays can be hazardous to human tissue because higher frequency means more energy per photon.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Substances can absorb, transmit, reflect or refract EM waves depending on the wavelength and the material. Radio waves can be produced by and induce oscillations in electrical circuits.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">EM spectrum questions often ask you to compare wavelengths, frequencies and energies across the spectrum, or to give uses and hazards for specific regions. Be precise: say ‘X-rays are used for medical imaging to detect broken bones’ rather than just ‘hospitals’. For calculations, all EM waves travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum, so you can use \(v = f\lambda\) or \(\lambda = \frac{c}{f}\). Watch out for unit prefixes — MHz = \(\times 10^6\), nm = \(\times 10^{-9}\).</p>
