--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Atomic Structure and Isotopes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every atom has a nucleus made of <dfn class="term" data-def="Positively charged particles found in the nucleus of an atom.">protons</dfn> (positive charge) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Neutral particles found in the nucleus of an atom, with roughly the same mass as protons.">neutrons</dfn> (no charge). Electrons (negative charge) orbit the nucleus at different energy levels. The number of protons defines the element (its atomic number). In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, giving different mass numbers.">Isotopes</dfn> are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. We write isotopes using nuclear notation: \(^A_Z X\) where \(A\) is the mass number (protons + neutrons) and \(Z\) is the atomic number (protons). For example, carbon-12 (\(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\)) has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 (\(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\)) has 6 protons and 8 neutrons.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what defines an isotope? Write the nuclear notation for carbon-12 and carbon-14, identifying protons and neutrons in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes have unstable nuclei that emit radiation — this is radioactive decay. The process is random and spontaneous.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Types of Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Unstable nuclei can emit three main types of radiation (P7.1):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">• <dfn class="term" data-def="A particle consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons emitted from an unstable nucleus. Identical to a helium nucleus. Stopped by paper or a few cm of air.">Alpha (\(\alpha\)) particles</dfn> — 2 protons + 2 neutrons (a helium nucleus, \(^4_2\text{He}\)). Strongly ionising because of their large charge and mass. Weakly penetrating: stopped by paper, skin or a few centimetres of air.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="A high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus when a neutron converts to a proton. Stopped by a few mm of aluminium.">Beta (\(\beta\)) particles</dfn> — a high-speed electron emitted when a neutron converts into a proton. Moderately ionising and moderately penetrating. Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="High-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted from the nucleus. Very penetrating; only significantly reduced by thick lead or concrete.">Gamma (\(\gamma\)) rays</dfn> — electromagnetic radiation from the nucleus. Weakly ionising but highly penetrating. Only significantly reduced by thick lead or several metres of concrete.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Alpha decay:</strong> the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2. Example:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Beta decay:</strong> a neutron converts to a proton, emitting a high-speed electron. Mass number stays the same, atomic number increases by 1. Example:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Gamma emission:</strong> the nucleus releases excess energy as a gamma ray. No change to mass number or atomic number. Gamma is often emitted alongside alpha or beta decay.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">In all nuclear equations, the mass numbers and atomic numbers must balance on both sides.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and complete these from memory: (a) alpha decay of Ra-226, (b) beta decay of C-14. Check mass and atomic numbers balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alpha: mass number −4, atomic number −2. Beta: mass number unchanged, atomic number +1. Gamma: no change to either. Mass and atomic numbers must balance in decay equations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Ionisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing electrons from atoms, creating charged ions. Ionising radiation can damage living cells.">Ionisation</dfn> occurs when radiation knocks electrons from atoms, creating ions. Alpha particles are the most ionising (because of their large charge and mass), followed by beta, then gamma. Ionisation in living cells can damage <strong>DNA</strong>, potentially causing mutations or cancer. The greater the ionising power, the more damage to cells — but higher ionising power also means lower penetrating power, because the radiation transfers its energy quickly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The activity of a radioactive source is measured in <strong>becquerels</strong> (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. In the laboratory, a <strong>Geiger-Müller (GM) tube</strong> connected to a counter measures the <strong>count rate</strong> (counts per second or per minute), which is proportional to the activity of the source.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">When investigating radioactivity, you must account for <strong>background radiation</strong>. Take a background count (with no source present), then subtract it from all measurements. This corrected count rate represents radiation from the source alone. Radioactive decay is random, so repeating measurements and calculating averages improves reliability.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <strong>nuclear model of the atom</strong> developed over time. Thomson proposed the ‘plum pudding’ model (positive charge spread throughout with embedded electrons). Rutherford’s gold foil experiment (1909) disproved this: most alpha particles passed straight through the foil, but some were deflected and a few bounced back. This showed the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus. Bohr later refined the model with electrons in fixed energy levels (orbits) around the nucleus.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Half-Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay, or for the activity/count rate to halve.">Half-life</dfn> is the time taken for the number of radioactive nuclei (or the activity) of a sample to halve. Radioactive decay is <strong>random</strong> and <strong>unpredictable</strong> — we cannot predict when an individual nucleus will decay, but we can predict the average behaviour of a large number of nuclei (P7.1d).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">After one half-life, half the original nuclei remain. After two half-lives, a quarter remain. After three, an eighth. For example, if a sample has an activity of 800 Bq and its half-life is 3 hours, after 9 hours (3 half-lives): \(800 \rightarrow 400 \rightarrow 200 \rightarrow 100\) Bq.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n22h">The fraction of radioactive nuclei remaining after \(n\) half-lives can be calculated using the formula: $$\text{fraction remaining} = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$$ For example, after 5 half-lives the fraction remaining is \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^5 = \frac{1}{32}\). If the original activity was 6400 Bq, the activity after 5 half-lives would be \(6400 \times \frac{1}{32} = 200\) Bq.</p>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: a sample has activity 1200 Bq and half-life 4 hours. What is the activity after 12 hours? Show your working using repeated halving.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Half-life is the time for half the unstable nuclei to decay. Decay is random — you can’t predict individual atoms, only the average behaviour of many atoms.</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23h" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: use the formula (1/2)^n to calculate what fraction of a sample remains after 4 half-lives, then find the activity if the original was 4800 Bq.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>After \(n\) half-lives, the fraction remaining is \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\). This formula lets you calculate the remaining activity or mass without repeated halving.</p>
</div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="Low-level ionising radiation that is always present in the environment, from natural sources (rocks, cosmic rays, food) and artificial sources (medical scans, nuclear industry).">Background radiation</dfn> is the low-level radiation that exists all around us. Natural sources include <strong>radon gas</strong> from rocks (the largest source in the UK), <strong>cosmic rays</strong> from space, and radioactive isotopes in food and building materials. Artificial sources include medical X-rays and the nuclear industry, but these contribute only a small fraction of total exposure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">When measuring the count rate of a radioactive source in the lab, you must subtract the background count rate first to get the corrected count rate. This ensures your measurement reflects only the source, not the environment.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Isotopes have the same protons but different neutrons. Unstable isotopes decay, emitting alpha, beta or gamma radiation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Alpha: most ionising, least penetrating (stopped by paper). Beta: moderate. Gamma: least ionising, most penetrating (reduced by lead).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Half-life = time for activity to halve. Decay is random. After \(n\) half-lives, fraction remaining = \(\frac{1}{2^n}\). Always subtract background radiation.</li>
</ul>
