<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Technique: The Poetry Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your poetry paper will typically include a comparison question on your anthology poems, focused on a specific theme. A clear plan, efficient time management, and a strategy for hitting all three <dfn class="term" data-def="Assessment Objectives: the specific skills the examiner is marking. AO1 = response, AO2 = methods, AO3 = context.">Assessment Objectives</dfn> in every paragraph are essential. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for exact mark weightings and recommended timings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How should you split your time in the poetry comparison?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Split your time roughly into three: a short planning stage (choose poem, identify 3–4 comparison points, select quotations), the main writing stage (4–5 developed paragraphs using alternating structure), and a final checking stage (re-read for clarity, add any missing AO2 terminology or AO3 context). Never skip the planning stage — it saves time overall.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Planning Before You Write</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">When you see the question, follow this three-step process:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Identify the theme in the question (e.g. “How is love presented as enduring?”). Underline the key word.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Choose your comparison poem. Pick one that shares the theme but differs in attitude, method, or era. Write it down immediately — don’t change your mind mid-essay.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Jot down 3–4 comparison points. For each, note a quotation from both poems and the method you will analyse. This becomes your paragraph plan.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Question:</strong> “Compare how love is presented as powerful in Sonnet 116 and one other poem.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Choice:</strong> Valentine (Duffy) — also about love’s power, but with a darker edge.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Point 1:</strong> Love as permanent force. S116: “ever-fixèd mark” (metaphor). Val: “fierce kiss will stay” (sensory verb). S = positive; D = ambiguous.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Point 2:</strong> Love vs time. S116: “not Time’s fool” (personification). Val: “Lethal” (single word = finality). Context: Elizabethan permanence vs post-modern honesty.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Point 3:</strong> Form reflects attitude. S116: sonnet = order. Val: free verse = rejection of convention. AO2 heavy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Point 4 (if time):</strong> Imagery. S116: navigation/lighthouse. Val: onion. Both unconventional but in different ways.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Quotation Bank: Love & Relationships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">You should memorise 2–3 <dfn class="term" data-def="Short, memorable phrases from a text that can be embedded in analysis to support a point.">key quotations</dfn> per poem. Short quotations (3–5 words) are easier to remember and more effective than long ones — they can be embedded within your sentence rather than block-quoted.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: cover the quotation bank and write from memory two quotations per poem for at least four poems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Short, embedded quotations score higher than long block quotes. Instead of: “Shakespeare writes, ‘Let me not to the marriage of true minds admit impediments’” — write: “Shakespeare presents love as a ‘marriage of true minds,’ elevating intellectual and emotional connection above physical attraction.” The quotation becomes part of <em>your</em> sentence.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Spend 5 minutes planning: choose your poem, identify 3–4 comparison points with quotations, and stick to your plan.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Write 4–5 paragraphs using alternating comparison structure, hitting AO1, AO2, and AO3 in each.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">Memorise 2–3 short quotations per poem and embed them within your sentences for maximum impact.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">In the final 5 minutes, re-read your essay and check: have I compared in every paragraph? Have I used at least one quotation per poem per paragraph? Have I named at least one method per paragraph? Have I embedded context at least 3 times? If any of these are missing, add a sentence. These small additions can push you up a mark band.</p>
