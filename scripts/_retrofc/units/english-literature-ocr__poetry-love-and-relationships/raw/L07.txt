--- DESCRIPTION ---
Building a comparative essay: structuring paragraphs, linking poems, and balancing AOs.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Art of Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The OCR poetry comparison (Component 02, Section A) is worth 40 marks and requires you to compare a <strong>named poem</strong> with <strong>one you choose</strong> from the collection. The question will focus on a theme (e.g. “How is love presented as powerful?”), and you must discuss both poems in a sustained, integrated comparison. This lesson teaches you the practical skills of structuring a comparison essay.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What are the three AOs assessed in the poetry comparison, and how many marks does each carry?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 40-mark comparison assesses: <strong>AO1</strong> (up to 15 marks) — informed personal response with textual references; <strong>AO2</strong> (up to 15 marks) — analysis of writers’ methods (language, form, structure); <strong>AO3</strong> (up to 10 marks) — understanding of contexts. You must address all three in <em>every</em> paragraph, not in separate sections.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Choosing Your Comparison Poem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Your choice of comparison poem is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate decision that shapes your entire response and should be justified in your answer.">strategic decision</dfn>. The best comparisons involve poems that share a theme but differ in attitude, method, or context. For example, if the named poem is Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116 (idealised love), choosing Duffy’s “Valentine” (honest love) creates rich contrast across time, form, and attitude. Avoid choosing a poem too similar — you need both similarities <em>and</em> differences to sustain a comparison.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Unconventional love:</strong> She Walks in Beauty + i wanna be yours — both celebrate a beloved through unconventional means (night imagery vs domestic objects), but from very different eras and registers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Structuring Your Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The most effective structure is <dfn class="term" data-def="Discussing both poems together in each paragraph, moving back and forth between them.">alternating comparison</dfn> — discussing both poems in every paragraph rather than writing about one and then the other. Each paragraph should follow this pattern:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>1. Topic sentence</strong> stating the comparison point: “Both poets present love as enduring, but they differ in whether this is presented as positive or threatening.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>2. Poem A analysis</strong> with quotation, method, and effect: “Shakespeare uses the metaphor of ‘an ever-fixèd mark’ to present love as a permanent, protective force…”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>3. Comparative connective</strong> linking to Poem B: “In contrast, Duffy presents persistence more ambiguously…”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>4. Poem B analysis</strong> with quotation, method, and effect.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>5. Context link</strong> explaining <em>why</em> the poets differ: “This shift reflects the movement from Elizabethan idealism to post-modern honesty about relationships.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write out the five-step paragraph structure for a comparison paragraph from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every comparison paragraph should contain: a <strong>topic sentence</strong> stating the comparison point, <strong>analysis of Poem A</strong> (with quotation and method), a <strong>comparative connective</strong>, <strong>analysis of Poem B</strong> (with quotation and method), and an <strong>embedded context link</strong>. This structure ensures you hit AO1, AO2, and AO3 in every paragraph.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Comparative Connectives</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Essential Linking Phrases</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>For similarities:</strong> “Similarly,” “In the same way,” “Both poets suggest,” “This idea is echoed in,” “Likewise.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>For differences:</strong> “In contrast,” “However,” “Whereas [Poet A] presents love as…, [Poet B] suggests,” “Unlike,” “On the other hand.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>For nuance:</strong> “While both poets explore…, they differ in,” “Although there is a surface similarity, the underlying attitude is,” “This apparent similarity masks a fundamental difference in.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Choose a comparison poem that shares a theme but differs in attitude, method, or context to create the richest comparison.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Use alternating comparison structure: discuss both poems in every paragraph with a topic sentence, analysis of each poem, comparative connective, and context link.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Every paragraph should address AO1 (ideas), AO2 (methods), and AO3 (context) — not in separate sections but woven together.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">The biggest mistake in comparison essays is writing two separate analyses and hoping the examiner sees the connection. You <strong>must</strong> explicitly compare in every paragraph. Use comparative connectives to move between poems and always explain <em>why</em> they are similar or different. Aim for 4–5 developed paragraphs in 45–50 minutes.</p>
