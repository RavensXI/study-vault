--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Comparing Across Time Periods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The OCR poetry comparison requires you to discuss poems from different time periods — this is how you access the AO3 marks for context. The Love and Relationships collection spans from the Elizabethan era through the Romantic period to the present day. Understanding <em>why</em> poets from different eras present love differently is just as important as analysing <em>how</em> they do it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Context is not a separate paragraph tacked on at the end. The best responses <dfn class="term" data-def="To incorporate contextual knowledge naturally within your analysis rather than separating it into a standalone paragraph.">embed</dfn> context within their analysis: “Shakespeare uses the sonnet form, which <em>in his era</em> was the established vehicle for love poetry, to create authority for his argument…” This approach weaves AO3 into AO1 and AO2 seamlessly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What are the three time periods represented in the Love and Relationships collection, and what defines each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The collection spans three eras: <strong>Literary Heritage</strong> (pre-1789, including Shakespeare and Jonson — formal conventions, love as public declaration), <strong>Romantic</strong> (1789–1900, including Byron and the Brownings — elevation of personal feeling, subversion of form), and <strong>Contemporary</strong> (1900–present, including Duffy, Clarke, Day-Lewis — everyday language, honesty about love’s complexity).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Key Contextual Shifts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Attitudes to marriage:</strong> In Shakespeare’s era, marriage was primarily a social and financial arrangement. Romantic poets elevated love above social convention. Contemporary poets write after the feminist movement, divorce reform, and the legalisation of same-sex marriage — love is now understood as a personal choice, not a social contract.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Women’s voices:</strong> Early love poetry was overwhelmingly male. Barrett Browning and Walsh wrote within a tradition that restricted women’s expression. Duffy writes with the freedom of a post-feminist era — she can reject Valentine’s Day, present love as “Lethal,” and use an onion as a love token without apology.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Shakespeare (1609) vs Duffy (1993) on love’s permanence:</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116 declares that love “is an ever-fixèd mark / That looks on tempests and is never shaken.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison in which something is described as being something else.">metaphor</dfn> of a lighthouse reflects Elizabethan navigation — love as the one reliable guide in a dangerous world. The formal sonnet structure reinforces permanence: love is contained, ordered, unshakeable.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Duffy’s “Valentine,” written nearly 400 years later, presents love through an onion whose “fierce kiss will stay on your lips, / possessive and faithful as we are.” Love persists, but it also <em>clings</em> — the word “possessive” introduces a darker reading that Shakespeare never acknowledges. Duffy’s free verse reflects post-modern rejection of neat categories: love cannot be contained in 14 lines.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Context link:</strong> Shakespeare writes when love poetry followed strict conventions; Duffy writes when those conventions have been dismantled. Both argue love endures, but Shakespeare sees this as entirely positive while Duffy recognises that persistence can become possession — reflecting a modern awareness of love’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two possible meanings or interpretations.">ambiguity</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Embedding Context Effectively</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">There are three ways to embed context without creating a separate “context paragraph”:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>1. Within analysis:</strong> “Browning uses the dramatic monologue — a form he pioneered in the Victorian era — to expose how patriarchal attitudes could lead to violence.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>2. As explanation of method:</strong> “Walsh structures her poem as an argument because, in the 1870s, women had no legal right to refuse their husband’s demands — the poem gives her speaker the rhetorical power she lacks in law.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>3. As comparison bridge:</strong> “While Shakespeare could confidently declare love eternal in a culture that prized permanence, Duffy writes in an era of divorce and relationship breakdown, which may explain her more ambivalent tone.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write: Three different sentence starters that embed context within analysis rather than separating it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Never write a standalone “context paragraph.” Instead, embed context as <em>explanation</em> for the poet’s choices: “[Poet] uses [method] because, in [time period], [relevant social/literary context], which means [effect on meaning].” This earns AO3 marks while keeping your analysis flowing.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Common Contextual Errors to Avoid</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Avoid:</strong> Biography as context (“Byron was famous for his affairs”) unless it directly affects meaning. Avoid generic historical facts (“In Victorian times, life was hard”) that don’t connect to the specific poem. Avoid treating all poems from one era as identical (“All Romantic poets believed…”). The examiner wants <em>specific</em> context that illuminates <em>this poem’s</em> meaning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The Love and Relationships collection spans Literary Heritage, Romantic, and Contemporary periods — each era brings different attitudes to love, gender, and poetic form.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">AO3 context must be embedded within analysis, not separated into a standalone paragraph. Use it to explain <em>why</em> poets make specific choices.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Key contextual shifts include attitudes to marriage, women’s voices in poetry, and the move from formal conventions to free expression.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">The OCR mark scheme rewards context that is <strong>integrated into analysis</strong>, not bolted on. A useful test: if you can remove your context sentence and the paragraph still makes sense, the context is probably not embedded well enough. Aim for context to be the <em>reason</em> behind a method, not a separate observation.</p>
