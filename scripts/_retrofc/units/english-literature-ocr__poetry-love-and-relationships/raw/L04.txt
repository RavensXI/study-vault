<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Modern & Unconventional Love</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Contemporary poets in the OCR anthology approach love with a directness and honesty that earlier writers often avoided. Instead of grand declarations or perfect sonnets, modern love poems use everyday language, humour, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Ordinary spoken language, as opposed to poetic or literary language.">colloquial</dfn> diction to present love as something complicated, imperfect, and rooted in real life. These poems acknowledge that love involves compromise, vulnerability, and sometimes failure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Studying modern love poems alongside Romantic and Literary Heritage texts allows you to trace how attitudes to love have changed over centuries. Where Shakespeare declared love eternal and Byron saw it as sublime beauty, contemporary poets are more likely to present love through the lens of dirty dishes, arguments, and ordinary kindness.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three ways do contemporary love poems differ from Romantic-era love poetry?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Contemporary love poems differ from Romantic-era poetry in three key ways: they use everyday language instead of elevated diction, they acknowledge love’s imperfections rather than idealising it, and they often define love through small domestic details rather than grand metaphors of nature and the sublime.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Valentine — Carol Ann Duffy (1993)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Duffy’s poem rejects conventional Valentine’s Day gifts — “Not a red rose or a satin heart” — and offers an onion instead. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended comparison that runs through a whole poem, often using an unusual or surprising object as the basis for comparison.">extended metaphor</dfn> of the onion represents love honestly: it has layers, it makes you cry, and its scent clings to your fingers “like a lover’s kiss.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The poem’s tone shifts from tenderness to something more threatening. “Its fierce kiss will stay on your lips” suggests passion, but “Its platinum loops shrink to a wedding ring” introduces a darker note — the ring becomes a symbol of possession, and the onion’s “loops” could suggest being trapped. The final single-word stanza — “Lethal” — is deliberately ambiguous. Is love ultimately dangerous? Or is Duffy simply being honest about its intensity?</p>

Duffy rejects the <dfn class="term" data-def="An overused phrase or idea that has lost its original impact through repetition.">cliché</dfn> of roses and hearts because they are dishonest — they present love as something neat and pretty when it is actually messy, painful, and persistent. The use of “trying” is significant: even Duffy admits that capturing love truthfully is difficult. The first-person pronoun and present tense make this feel immediate and confessional.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">i wanna be yours — John Cooper Clarke (1982)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Clarke’s poem takes the opposite approach to Duffy: instead of rejecting clichés, he embraces them with playful exuberance. The speaker offers to be everything for their beloved — “I wanna be your vacuum cleaner, / Breathing in your dust” — using absurd, domestic <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison saying one thing is another, without using ‘like’ or ‘as’.">metaphors</dfn> that mix the mundane with the passionate. A vacuum cleaner, an electric heater, a raincoat — these are not conventionally romantic objects, but Clarke uses them to show love that is practical, devoted, and all-encompassing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The poem’s repetitive structure — each stanza begins with “I wanna be your” — creates an insistent, almost desperate rhythm. The closing lines “i don’t wanna be hers” and “i wanna be yours” strip love down to its simplest expression: exclusive devotion. The lack of punctuation and lowercase title give the poem an informal, spoken-word quality that suits its origins as <dfn class="term" data-def="Poetry performed aloud to an audience, often with a rhythmic or musical quality.">performance poetry</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list three domestic objects Clarke offers to be in 'i wanna be yours' and explain what they suggest about love.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Clarke’s domestic metaphors (vacuum cleaner, raincoat, electric heater) redefine love as service and devotion in everyday life. Unlike Romantic poets who use nature and the sublime, Clarke finds love in the mundane — suggesting that real love is about usefulness, warmth, and constant presence rather than grand gestures.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Comparing Modern Love Poems</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Duffy vs Clarke: Two Approaches to Honesty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Similarity:</strong> Both poets reject traditional love poetry conventions. Both use everyday objects as extended metaphors. Both present love as something real and grounded rather than abstract and idealised.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Difference:</strong> Duffy is serious and sometimes threatening (“Lethal”). Clarke is playful and exuberant. Duffy acknowledges love’s dark side (tears, possession, danger); Clarke presents love as pure, joyful devotion. Duffy’s free verse reflects the unconventional gift; Clarke’s repetitive structure mimics the insistence of desire.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Context:</strong> Both poets write after the feminist movement challenged traditional gender roles in relationships. Duffy, as a female poet, reclaims the power to define love on her own terms. Clarke, influenced by punk culture, deliberately undermines literary pretension with colloquial language.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Duffy’s “Valentine” uses the extended metaphor of an onion to present love honestly — layered, tear-inducing, clingy, and potentially “Lethal.”</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Clarke’s “i wanna be yours” uses absurd domestic metaphors and repetitive structure to express devoted, all-encompassing love in everyday language.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Both contemporary poems reject Romantic idealism in favour of honesty, making them ideal for cross-period comparison in the OCR exam.</li></ul>

Always explain <em>why</em> these shifts occurred (AO3) — changing gender roles, the influence of feminism, the decline of formal poetic conventions.</p>
