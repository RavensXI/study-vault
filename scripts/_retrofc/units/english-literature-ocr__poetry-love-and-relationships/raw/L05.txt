--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Form, Structure & Language Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the OCR poetry comparison, AO2 asks you to analyse the <strong>effects of the writer’s methods</strong>. This means going beyond <em>what</em> a poem says to explore <em>how</em> it says it. The poets in the Love and Relationships collection use form, structure, and language deliberately — their choices about rhyme, metre, line breaks, and imagery are all part of how they communicate meaning. This lesson gives you a toolkit for identifying and analysing these methods across the collection.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: What are the three main areas of 'methods' that AO2 requires you to analyse?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO2 assesses three areas of method: <strong>form</strong> (the overall shape and type of poem — sonnet, free verse, dramatic monologue), <strong>structure</strong> (how the poem is organised — stanzas, volta, repetition, beginning/ending), and <strong>language</strong> (specific word choices, imagery, sound devices). A top-band response weaves all three into a single analysis rather than treating them as separate checklists.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Form: The Shape of Love</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 14-line poem with a set rhyme scheme and metre, traditionally associated with love.">sonnet</dfn> is the most significant form in this collection. Shakespeare’s Sonnet 116 uses the form traditionally — 14 lines of iambic pentameter with a final couplet that clinches his argument. But other poets <strong>subvert</strong> the sonnet: Barrett Browning’s Sonnet 43 (“How Do I Love Thee?”) uses the Petrarchan structure to catalogue love in religious and spatial terms, yet the relentless listing creates a breathless intensity that strains against the form’s containment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Free verse poems like Duffy’s “Valentine” reject formal constraints entirely. The irregular line lengths and lack of rhyme mirror the poet’s rejection of conventional love poetry — the form <em>enacts</em> the theme. Similarly, Clarke’s “i wanna be yours” uses a repetitive, chant-like structure borrowed from <dfn class="term" data-def="Poetry performed aloud, often with a rhythmic, musical quality influenced by popular culture.">performance poetry</dfn> — the form comes from punk culture, not literary tradition.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Structure: How Poems Are Organised</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Key structural features across the collection include the <dfn class="term" data-def="A turn or shift in a poem’s argument, tone, or focus — often found in sonnets.">volta</dfn> (the “turn” in a sonnet where the argument shifts), stanza breaks that signal emotional shifts, and the positioning of key ideas at the beginning or end for impact. Walsh’s “A Woman to Her Lover” structures its argument through three stanzas of rejection followed by one of acceptance — building tension before resolution. Day-Lewis’s “Walking Away” moves from a specific memory to a universal truth, using the final line as its emotional climax.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Language: Sound, Imagery & Diction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Language analysis means examining specific word choices and their effects. Key techniques across the collection include: <strong>metaphor and simile</strong> (Shakespeare’s “star to every wandering bark,” Day-Lewis’s “satellite wrenched from its orbit”), <strong>sound devices</strong> (Byron’s flowing rhythm mimicking grace, Browning’s deceptively smooth rhythm concealing violence), and <strong>diction</strong> — the register of language chosen. Compare Shakespeare’s elevated, formal diction with Clarke’s deliberately colloquial register, and you see how the <em>level</em> of language shapes the poem’s tone and meaning.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write one example each of metaphor, sound device, and diction from three different poems in the collection.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The best AO2 analysis connects method to meaning. Never just name a technique — explain <em>why</em> the poet chose it. For example: “Duffy uses free verse to reflect her rejection of conventional love poetry — the irregular form mirrors the unconventional gift of an onion” links form directly to theme.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Form choices (sonnet, free verse, dramatic monologue) shape the reader’s experience and should be linked directly to the poem’s themes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Structure (volta, stanza breaks, beginning/ending) controls the poem’s emotional arc and should be analysed as deliberate choices.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Language analysis (metaphor, diction, sound) must always connect the technique to its effect on meaning — never just name-spot.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When writing about form, structure, and language, always use the formula: <strong>identify the method &rarr; quote the evidence &rarr; analyse the effect &rarr; link to meaning/context</strong>. Avoid simply listing techniques without explaining <em>why</em> the poet used them. The OCR examiner rewards responses that show how methods create meaning, not just that you can spot them.</p>
