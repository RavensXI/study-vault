--- DESCRIPTION ---
Properties of Groups 1, 7 and 0, electron arrangement and trends in reactivity.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Periodic Table and Groups</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The modern <dfn class="term" data-def="An arrangement of all known elements in order of atomic number, organised into groups (columns) and periods (rows).">periodic table</dfn> arranges elements in order of atomic number. Elements in the same <strong>group</strong> (column) have the same number of outer-shell electrons, which is why they have similar chemical properties. Elements in the same <strong>period</strong> (row) have the same number of electron shells. Mendeleev’s original table was based on atomic mass and left gaps for undiscovered elements. His predictions about these missing elements were later confirmed, validating the periodic arrangement. The table was subsequently refined using atomic number (not atomic mass) as the fundamental organising principle, which resolved anomalies where elements would have been placed in the wrong group if ordered by mass alone.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Group 1 — The Alkali Metals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Group 1 elements (lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium, francium) are called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The elements in Group 1 of the periodic table: soft, reactive metals that react vigorously with water.">alkali metals</dfn>. They all have <strong>one electron</strong> in their outer shell, which they lose easily to form +1 ions. They are soft metals that can be cut with a knife, have low melting points compared to most metals, and have low densities — lithium, sodium and potassium all float on water. They must be stored under oil to prevent them reacting with moisture and oxygen in the air.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Group 1 metals react with water to produce a metal hydroxide (an alkaline solution, hence “alkali metals”) and hydrogen gas. For example: \(2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g)\). Lithium fizzes gently, sodium melts into a ball and fizzes vigorously, and potassium reacts so vigorously it catches fire with a lilac flame. <strong>Reactivity increases down the group</strong> because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more <dfn class="term" data-def="The blocking effect of inner electron shells, which reduces the attraction between the nucleus and the outermost electron.">shielded</dfn> by inner electrons, making it easier to lose.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Group 1 trend: reactivity INCREASES going down. Group 7 trend: reactivity DECREASES going down. The reasons are opposite — G1 metals lose electrons more easily further down; G7 non-metals gain electrons less easily further down.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Group 1: reactivity <strong>increases</strong> down the group because the outer electron is further from the nucleus, more shielded, so easier to lose. Melting points <strong>decrease</strong> down the group.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Group 7 — The Halogens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Group 7 elements (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine, astatine) are called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactive non-metal elements in Group 7 of the periodic table, which have seven outer electrons.">halogens</dfn>. They have <strong>seven electrons</strong> in their outer shell and need to gain one more electron to achieve a full outer shell, forming −1 ions. They exist as diatomic molecules (\(F_2\), \(Cl_2\), \(Br_2\), \(I_2\)). Halogens react with metals to form ionic compounds called halide salts (e.g. sodium chloride, NaCl) and react with non-metals to form covalent compounds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Physical properties change down the group as the molecules get larger and intermolecular forces become stronger: fluorine is a pale yellow gas, chlorine is a green gas, bromine is a red-brown liquid, and iodine is a grey solid that sublimes to form a purple vapour. Melting and boiling points <strong>increase</strong> down the group. <strong>Reactivity decreases</strong> going down Group 7 because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so the attraction for an incoming electron is weaker and it is harder to gain that extra electron.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">A more reactive halogen can displace a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt. For example, chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution: \(Cl_2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + Br_2(aq)\). The solution changes from colourless to orange-brown as bromine is released. Similarly, chlorine displaces iodine from potassium iodide, turning the solution brown. However, bromine cannot displace chlorine from potassium chloride because bromine is less reactive than chlorine. These displacement reactions provide experimental evidence for the reactivity trend.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Group 0 — The Noble Gases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Group 0 elements (helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon) are the <dfn class="term" data-def="The very unreactive elements in Group 0, which have full outer electron shells.">noble gases</dfn>. They have <strong>full outer electron shells</strong>, which makes them very stable and unreactive. They exist as individual atoms (monatomic) rather than molecules because they have no tendency to share, gain or lose electrons. Boiling points increase down the group as the atoms become larger and intermolecular forces increase.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Noble gases are used where unreactive gases are needed: helium for balloons and airships (low density, non-flammable), neon in advertising signs (produces a distinctive red-orange glow when electricity passes through it), and argon in light bulbs and welding (prevents oxidation of the hot filament or metal).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Remember: noble gases are unreactive because they have FULL outer shells. They don't need to gain, lose or share electrons.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Noble gases are unreactive because they have complete outer electron shells. They do not need to form bonds. Boiling points increase down the group as atomic size and intermolecular forces increase.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">Going down any group, atoms have more electron shells. In Group 1, the single outer electron is progressively further from the positive nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons — so it is easier to remove (lose), making the element more reactive. In Group 7, the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so the attraction for an incoming electron is weaker — making it harder to gain an electron, so reactivity decreases. In Group 0, the full outer shell provides stability regardless of size, so all noble gases are unreactive. These trends can be explained entirely by the electronic structure of the atoms. Understanding this pattern means you can predict the properties of any element from its position in the periodic table, even if you have never studied it before.</p>
</div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Group 1 alkali metals have 1 outer electron, react with water, and become more reactive down the group (outer electron easier to lose).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Group 7 halogens have 7 outer electrons, are diatomic, and become less reactive down the group (harder to attract an extra electron).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Group 0 noble gases have full outer shells, are unreactive, and exist as monatomic gases.</li>
</ul>
