--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Rate of Reaction?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed in a chemical reaction.">rate of reaction</dfn> tells us how quickly a reaction happens — how fast the reactants are used up or how fast the products are formed. A fast reaction (like an explosion) is over in a fraction of a second. A slow reaction (like rusting or the weathering of stone) may take months or years. Being able to control the rate of a reaction is vital in industry — too fast can be dangerous, too slow is uneconomical.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">You can measure rate in several ways: the volume of gas produced per unit time, the mass of reactant lost per unit time (using a balance), or the time taken for a colour change or for a solution to become opaque (disappearing cross experiment). The choice of method depends on the particular reaction and what can be conveniently measured.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Collision Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that reactions can only occur when particles collide with sufficient energy and correct orientation.">Collision theory</dfn> states that for a reaction to occur, the reacting particles must: (1) <strong>collide</strong> with each other, (2) collide with sufficient energy (at least the <strong>activation energy</strong>), and (3) collide with the correct <strong>orientation</strong>. The rate of reaction depends on the <strong>frequency</strong> of successful collisions — collisions that have enough energy and the right orientation to result in a reaction. Not every collision leads to a reaction; most collisions are unsuccessful because the particles bounce off each other without reacting.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="For 3-mark explain questions on rate factors, always mention: (1) what changes about the particles, (2) how this affects collision frequency/energy, (3) how this affects the rate. This three-step structure scores full marks.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with enough energy (the activation energy) and the correct orientation. Increasing the frequency of successful collisions increases the rate of reaction.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Factors Affecting Rate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Four main factors affect the rate of a chemical reaction:</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<span>Temperature, Concentration, Pressure and Surface Area</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Temperature</strong> — increasing the temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so they move faster and collide more frequently. More importantly, a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or exceeding the activation energy, so the rate increases significantly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Concentration</strong> (or <strong>pressure</strong> for gases) — increasing the concentration means there are more particles in a given volume, so they collide more frequently. For gases, increasing the pressure pushes particles closer together, having the same effect.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Surface area</strong> — breaking a solid into smaller pieces increases the <dfn class="term" data-def="The total area of exposed surface per unit volume of a solid, which increases when the solid is broken into smaller pieces.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn>. More of the solid is exposed to the other reactant, so collisions happen more frequently and the rate increases. This is why powdered marble chips react faster with acid than large lumps, even though the total mass of marble is the same. In the extreme case, a fine powder of a combustible material in air can cause a dust explosion because the surface area is so enormous that the reaction rate is dangerously fast.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Interpreting Rate of Reaction Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Rate graphs typically plot volume of gas produced (or mass lost) on the y-axis against time on the x-axis. The <strong>gradient</strong> (steepness) of the line shows the rate — a steeper gradient means a faster reaction. The line levels off when one of the reactants is used up (the reaction is complete). The final amount of product is the same regardless of rate (unless the amount of reactant changes). Two curves on the same graph — one steep and one gradual — that level off at the same point indicate the same amount of reactant was used but one reaction was faster than the other.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">You can also use \(\frac{1}{t}\) as a measure of rate, where \(t\) is the time taken for a certain amount of reaction to occur. A shorter time means a faster rate, so \(\frac{1}{t}\) is proportional to rate.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Catalysts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up, by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy.">catalyst</dfn> is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being used up itself. It works by providing an <strong>alternative reaction pathway</strong> with a lower activation energy, so more particles have enough energy to react. On a reaction profile, the catalyst lowers the energy barrier (the peak). The catalyst itself is unchanged at the end of the reaction and can be reused. Each catalyst is specific to a particular reaction or type of reaction — an iron catalyst works for the Haber process but not for decomposing hydrogen peroxide (which uses manganese dioxide).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Catalysts are NOT used up. They lower the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway. They do NOT change the amount of product or the energy change — only how fast equilibrium is reached.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Catalysts speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy. They are not used up and are not changed permanently. Different reactions need different catalysts. <dfn class="term" data-def="Biological catalysts (proteins) that control reactions in living organisms.">Enzymes</dfn> are biological catalysts that control reactions in living organisms.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Gas syringe method</strong>: collect gas produced in a gas syringe and record the volume at regular time intervals. Works well for reactions producing \(CO_2\) or \(H_2\) (e.g. marble chips and acid, magnesium and acid). <strong>Mass loss method</strong>: place the reaction on a balance and record the mass at regular intervals as gas escapes. <strong>Disappearing cross</strong>: time how long it takes for a cross viewed through the solution to disappear as the solution becomes opaque (sodium thiosulfate and acid producing sulfur). In all methods, change only one variable at a time and keep everything else constant.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions. Factors: temperature (more energy), concentration/pressure (more particles), surface area (more exposure).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Rate graphs show volume or mass against time. Steeper gradient = faster rate. The line levels off when the reaction is complete.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Catalysts lower the activation energy by providing an alternative pathway. They are not used up. Enzymes are biological catalysts.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When interpreting rate graphs, describe the gradient (“steep at first, then levels off”) and explain what each part shows. For catalyst questions on reaction profiles, draw a dotted line showing the lower activation energy peak — the overall energy change stays the same. Always specify that a catalyst provides an <strong>alternative pathway with lower activation energy</strong> rather than just saying it “speeds up” the reaction.</p>
