Why Vikings raided, how the Danelaw was carved out, and how Alfred saved Wessex.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Britain Before the Longships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the late eighth century Britain was already a patchwork made by migration. The Anglo-Saxons &mdash; themselves descendants of fifth-century settlers from northern Germany and southern Denmark &mdash; had divided the land into rival kingdoms: Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia and Wessex. Christianity, brought by missionaries from Rome and Iona, had given these kingdoms a shared faith but not a shared crown. Coastal monasteries held silver chalices, illuminated gospels and grain stores, and they were defended by little more than prayer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In 793 a fleet of Norse <dfn class="term" data-def="Scandinavian raiders, traders and settlers from modern Denmark, Norway and Sweden, active c790-1100.">Vikings</dfn> sacked Lindisfarne, the great island monastery off Northumbria. The chronicler Alcuin wrote that &ldquo;never before has such terror appeared in Britain&rdquo;. The raid is often treated as a Year Zero, but it was the start of a long process: nearly three centuries of arrivals that turned raiders into settlers and reshaped who counted as English.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the year, the place and the chronicler associated with the first major Viking raid on Britain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Lindisfarne raid of 793 is the conventional starting point for Viking activity in Britain. Alcuin&rsquo;s shocked account survives because monastic writers were the chief literate witnesses &mdash; and the main victims.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Did the Vikings Come?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">No single cause explains the raids. Population pressure in Scandinavia made younger sons hungry for land; partible inheritance meant farms were carved up too small to support a family. Improvements in shipbuilding produced the shallow-draught <dfn class="term" data-def="A long, narrow Scandinavian warship with a single sail and rows of oars, capable of crossing open sea and rowing up shallow rivers.">longship</dfn>, which could cross open sea and then row up rivers like the Thames or the Liffey. Trade routes already linked Scandinavia to Frankia and the Baltic, so raiders knew where the rich monasteries were. And the political fragmentation of Britain &mdash; competing kings, no standing fleet &mdash; made it a soft target.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Religion mattered too. Norse warriors did not share Christian taboos against attacking churches, so undefended monasteries with portable wealth looked less like sacred ground and more like an open vault. The role of individuals also fed the wave: war-leaders such as Ivar the Boneless and Halfdan built reputations on plunder, and reputations attracted followers.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">For the first sixty years the Norse came in summer fleets and went home in autumn. From 851 they began wintering on English soil. In 865 the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="A coalition of Viking forces, mainly Danish, that invaded England in 865 with the aim of conquest rather than plunder.">Great Heathen Army</dfn> landed in East Anglia &mdash; not to plunder and leave, but to conquer. Within ten years the Norse had killed or driven out the kings of Northumbria, East Anglia and Mercia. Only Wessex still stood.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Alfred &amp; the Defence of Wessex</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In 871 the young king Alfred came to the throne of Wessex with the Great Heathen Army camped on his border. After a string of defeats he was driven into the marshes of Athelney in early 878, hiding from a winter raid led by Guthrum. Later that year he rallied the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and Hampshire and broke the Danes at the Battle of Edington. Guthrum was forced to accept baptism &mdash; with Alfred as his godfather &mdash; and to withdraw beyond a treaty line.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Alfred did not just win one battle; he changed how Wessex defended itself. He built a network of fortified towns called <dfn class="term" data-def="Fortified towns established by Alfred across Wessex so that no settlement was more than a day&rsquo;s march from a defensible refuge.">burhs</dfn>, redesigned the army so that half could fight while half worked the fields, and ordered the construction of larger ships. He also commissioned learning: scholars translated key Latin works into Old English, and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of annals begun in Wessex in the 890s recording English history year by year, copied and continued in different monasteries until 1154.">Anglo-Saxon Chronicle</dfn> began to record the year-by-year story of his kingdom. By his death in 899 he was the only Anglo-Saxon king ever called &lsquo;the Great&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Danelaw</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Edington did not expel the Norse. The peace negotiated with Guthrum, refined under Alfred&rsquo;s son Edward and his daughter Aethelflaed, formalised a division of the country. North and east of a line running roughly from London to Chester became the <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of England, north and east of a line from London to Chester, where Danish law and custom prevailed from the late ninth century.">Danelaw</dfn>: an area where Danish custom, language and units of land measurement (the wapentake, the carucate) prevailed. South and west remained Anglo-Saxon under West Saxon kingship. The boundary did not freeze identities; it created a permeable frontier across which traders, marriages and Christian missionaries moved freely.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Within two generations Norse settlers in the Danelaw were buying land, attending English law courts and christening their children with hybrid names. Place names tell the story: villages ending in &lsquo;-by&rsquo; (Whitby, Grimsby), &lsquo;-thorpe&rsquo; (Scunthorpe) or &lsquo;-toft&rsquo; mark Norse settlement, while words like &lsquo;sky&rsquo;, &lsquo;egg&rsquo;, &lsquo;law&rsquo; and &lsquo;them&rsquo; entered everyday English. The legal pronoun &lsquo;they&rsquo; is a small daily reminder that the country&rsquo;s grammar is itself the product of migration.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Guthrum&rsquo;s baptism in 878 began a religious shift that ran the wrong way round to most modern assumptions: the conquerors converted to the religion of the conquered. By the early tenth century Norse settlers were endowing churches, minting coins with crosses, and being buried in Christian cemeteries. Religion thus became a glue holding the two populations together rather than a wedge driving them apart &mdash; an important corrective to the idea that medieval English identity was ever &lsquo;pure&rsquo; Anglo-Saxon.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and list, from memory, three things Alfred did after Edington that strengthened Wessex against future raids.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Danelaw was carved out by treaty after Edington (878). It was a permeable frontier, not a wall &mdash; place-name evidence (Whitby, Grimsby, Scunthorpe) shows Norse settlement persisted long after the boundary itself faded.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Viking raids from 793 grew from summer plunder into the conquest attempted by the Great Heathen Army in 865, leaving only Wessex intact under Alfred.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Alfred&rsquo;s defence rested on burhs, a reorganised army, a stronger fleet and a programme of learning &mdash; the institutional roots of a single English kingdom under his successors.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The Danelaw was a permeable frontier rather than a barrier; Norse and Anglo-Saxon populations intermarried, traded and converted, and the legacy survives in modern place names and core English vocabulary.</li></ul>
