--- DESCRIPTION ---
From the Norman Conquest to King John: how cross-Channel kingship grew, then unravelled.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Conquest to Cross-Channel Realm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">William of Normandy&rsquo;s victory at Hastings on 14 October 1066 ended the Anglo-Saxon dynasty and bolted England onto a French-speaking duchy across the Channel. The Norman elite replaced almost the entire English aristocracy: by 1086, when the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1086 survey of English landholding ordered by William the Conqueror to record who owned what and how much it was worth.">Domesday Book</dfn> was compiled, fewer than ten of England&rsquo;s 200 leading landholders had English names. The new rulers spoke Norman French, ate French food, built stone castles and brought continental architectural styles that produced Durham Cathedral and the White Tower of London.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For the next 150 years &lsquo;England&rsquo; was rarely a free-standing kingdom. The same king ruled both England and Normandy, and most royal courts and battles were on the continental side of the Channel. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle continued to be written in English &mdash; an act of cultural resistance &mdash; until the last entry in 1154, by which point the king (Henry II) ruled an even bigger French-language empire.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: by what year had English-named landholders been almost entirely replaced, and which survey records the change?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By the time of Domesday Book in 1086, fewer than ten of England&rsquo;s top 200 landholders had English names. The Norman Conquest was a near-total replacement of the ruling class, not just a change of king.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Henry II &amp; the Angevin Empire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Henry II became king in 1154. He was not just king of England; he had inherited Normandy, Maine and Anjou from his parents and acquired Aquitaine through marriage to Eleanor in 1152. Together these territories &mdash; running from the Cheviots to the Pyrenees &mdash; made up the <dfn class="term" data-def="The collection of territories ruled by Henry II and his sons (1154-c1204), including England, Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine and Aquitaine, held in personal union rather than as a single state.">Angevin Empire</dfn>. It was, like Cnut&rsquo;s earlier North Sea Empire, a personal union: each region kept its own laws, language and customs, and the king moved constantly between them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1171 Henry took an army to Ireland and forced its high king and major lords to accept him as overlord. The expedition began the long English involvement in Ireland that became one of the most consequential strands of the whole spec &mdash; the Ulster Plantations of the seventeenth century, the Famine migration of the 1840s, and the partition of 1921 all flow from decisions taken in this period. The Irish church accepted reform along Roman lines, and Anglo-Norman barons began to settle the eastern Irish coast around the area later called the Pale.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Three motives intersected. First, Pope Adrian IV had earlier issued the bull Laudabiliter, encouraging an English king to reform the Irish church &mdash; religion provided cover. Second, Anglo-Norman barons such as Strongbow had already entered Ireland on private invitation; Henry wanted to bring them under royal control before they became independent rivals. Third, the personal union model made expansion easy: Ireland could be added to the king&rsquo;s portfolio without any change to England&rsquo;s laws or institutions.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Government on the Move</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Ruling an empire that stretched from northern England to the Spanish border meant Henry was almost always travelling. To govern in his absence he expanded the royal exchequer at Westminster, sent <dfn class="term" data-def="Royal judges sent out from Westminster on circuits to hear cases across the country, central to the development of English common law from the late twelfth century.">justices in eyre</dfn> on circuits across England, and pushed the use of jury trial. The result was a paradox &mdash; a king who spent most of his time in France strengthened the institutions of royal justice in England. The seeds of <dfn class="term" data-def="The body of English law developed from court decisions and royal justice rather than from written codes, beginning under Henry II.">common law</dfn> were planted while the throne itself was rarely on English soil.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Henry&rsquo;s court was multilingual: Norman French for the king and barons, Latin for clergy and government documents, English for the great majority of the population. This three-language stack persisted for two centuries and is the reason modern English vocabulary contains parallel words from each tier &mdash; pig (English) and pork (French), house (English) and mansion (French), kingly (English) and royal (French).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">King John &amp; the Loss of Normandy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Henry&rsquo;s sons could not hold the empire together. After Richard I&rsquo;s death in 1199, John inherited the throne. In 1204 the French king Philip Augustus invaded Normandy and overran it within months. Within five years John had also lost Anjou, Maine and most of Poitou, leaving only Gascony in the south-west and a thin band of England-Channel-Aquitaine connections. The Angevin Empire was effectively over.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The losses had two enormous consequences. First, the king now ruled an island kingdom (plus a small French foothold) for the first time since 1066 &mdash; English politics turned inward, and the Norman aristocracy had to choose between their English and Normandy estates, breaking the cross-Channel landholding that had defined the previous 150 years. Second, John&rsquo;s desperate attempts to fund a war of reconquest through heavy taxation produced a baronial revolt in 1215 and the great charter known as Magna Carta &mdash; a document born directly from the unravelling of the Angevin Empire.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">The loss of Normandy in 1204 mattered for identity, not just territory. While the king ruled lands on both sides of the Channel, &lsquo;English&rsquo; and &lsquo;French&rsquo; were not opposites. After 1204 the two crowns ruled separate kingdoms, and over the thirteenth century the English aristocracy began to identify with England rather than with a continental homeland. Lesson 4 will pick up this thread, showing how the Hundred Years&rsquo; War turned the rivalry into a forge for English identity.</p>

  <p>The loss of Normandy in 1204 ended the cross-Channel realm built by William and Henry II. England turned inward, the Anglo-Norman aristocracy had to choose sides, and John&rsquo;s tax demands triggered the 1215 baronial revolt that produced Magna Carta.</p>
</div>
