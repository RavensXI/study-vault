<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Windrush 1948: Symbol, Not Beginning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">On 22 June 1948 the troopship HMT <em>Empire Windrush</em> docked at Tilbury with 492 Caribbean migrants on board. The arrival is often called the start of multicultural Britain. It was nothing of the sort. People of African descent had lived in Britain since the Roman period; the 18th century saw a community of around 10,000-15,000 (Lesson 6); thousands of Caribbean and African seamen had served in the merchant marine and the World Wars. What made Windrush different was scale and timing. The 1948 British Nationality Act, passed three weeks before the ship docked, made every <dfn class="term" data-def="The voluntary association of mostly former British colonies, headed by the British monarch; provided the framework for Commonwealth migration to Britain after 1948.">Commonwealth</dfn> citizen a citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies. Britain needed labour for post-war reconstruction. The Caribbean had unemployment. The combination produced the largest peacetime migration in modern British history.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the date the Empire Windrush docked, the act passed three weeks before, and the rough number of Caribbean migrants on board.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>HMT Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with 492 Caribbean migrants. The 1948 British Nationality Act, passed earlier that year, made every Commonwealth citizen a citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies, opening the door to large-scale post-war migration.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Settling, Working, Resisting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Between 1948 and 1962 around 250,000 Caribbean migrants came to Britain, joined by smaller numbers of Indians, Pakistanis and West Africans. They worked in the National Health Service (founded the same week Windrush docked), London Transport, British Rail and the textile mills of Yorkshire and Lancashire. Recruitment was direct: London Transport had recruitment offices in Barbados from 1956; Enoch Powell, as Minister of Health, oversaw NHS recruitment from the Caribbean.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Reception was hostile. &lsquo;No Irish, No Blacks, No Dogs&rsquo; signs appeared in boarding-house windows. Estate agents refused service. The 1958 Notting Hill riots saw white &lsquo;Teddy Boy&rsquo; mobs attacking Caribbean residents over five nights; one of the largest gatherings reached around 700 people. The next year, the racist murder of Antiguan carpenter Kelso Cochrane went unsolved &mdash; a turning point in Caribbean political organisation in Britain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Claudia Jones and the Carnival</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Trinidadian-born activist who founded the West Indian Gazette (1958) and the indoor Caribbean Carnival (1959), the direct ancestor of the Notting Hill Carnival.">Claudia Jones</dfn> had been deported from the United States for Communist activities in 1955 and arrived in London via Trinidad. In response to the Notting Hill attacks she founded the <em>West Indian Gazette</em> (1958), Britain&rsquo;s first major Black newspaper. In January 1959 she organised an indoor Caribbean carnival at St Pancras Town Hall, broadcast by the BBC. After her death in 1964 the carnival moved outdoors to Notting Hill itself; today the <em>Notting Hill Carnival</em> is the largest street festival in Europe and the most visible legacy of post-Windrush migration. Jones&rsquo;s achievement is the textbook answer to a 16-mark essay question on the &lsquo;role of individuals&rsquo; in shaping post-war Britain &mdash; she did not wait for British institutions, she built her own.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Indian and Pakistani migration was smaller than Caribbean migration in the 1950s but grew rapidly in the 1960s. The 1962 Commonwealth Immigrants Act &mdash; the first restriction on Commonwealth migration &mdash; produced a beat-the-ban surge that brought hundreds of thousands of Indians and Pakistanis to Britain before the door closed. They settled in textile-mill Yorkshire and Lancashire, in the Black Country, and in east London. The 1971 Immigration Act tightened restrictions further. By 1971 around 1.4 million Commonwealth-born residents lived in Britain; their British-born children would shape every aspect of British culture from Punjabi pop music to British curry to British politics.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Idi Amin and the Ugandan Asians, 1972</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In August 1972 Ugandan dictator Idi Amin announced the expulsion of the country&rsquo;s Asian community &mdash; around 80,000 people, most of them descendants of the Indian indentured workers and traders covered in Lesson 11. Amin gave them 90 days to leave, accusing them of &lsquo;sabotaging the economy&rsquo;. Around 27,200 came to Britain &mdash; the largest single arrival of refugees since the Huguenots almost three centuries earlier. The Heath government accepted them despite domestic opposition; the Uganda Resettlement Board ran a network of reception camps. Within ten years many had become entrepreneurs and shopkeepers in towns such as Leicester &mdash; one of Britain&rsquo;s clearest examples of refugee migrants becoming an economic asset.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Commonwealth and the Legacy of Empire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">As the empire ended, the Commonwealth replaced it. Originally a club of white-settler dominions (1931), it was redefined in 1949 to allow republics &mdash; opening it to newly independent India and later the African states. By the 1970s the Commonwealth was a multi-racial institution headed by the Queen, with a Secretariat in London (founded 1965). It anchored British migration policy: Commonwealth citizens had been the basis of the 1948 Act, and successive immigration restrictions in 1962, 1968 and 1971 progressively narrowed Commonwealth citizenship rights without abolishing them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The August-September 1958 attacks in Notting Hill ran for five nights. White &lsquo;Teddy Boy&rsquo; mobs &mdash; one estimated at around 700 people &mdash; threw petrol bombs and chased Caribbean residents off the streets. The fascist Union Movement, led by Oswald Mosley, tried to capitalise on the unrest. The following May, Antiguan-born carpenter Kelso Cochrane was stabbed to death walking home from hospital after treating a broken thumb. The murder was never solved, but the funeral procession of 1,200 mourners showed a Caribbean community organising politically. The 1958 attacks and the Cochrane case shaped Black British activism for decades &mdash; from Claudia Jones&rsquo;s carnival to the 1981 Brixton Riots and the 1993 murder of Stephen Lawrence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n15">In 1982 Britain went to war over the Falkland Islands, a sparsely populated South Atlantic archipelago invaded by Argentina. Margaret Thatcher dispatched a naval task force; 255 British and 649 Argentinian servicemen died before Argentina&rsquo;s surrender on 14 June 1982. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1982 war between Britain and Argentina over the British-controlled Falkland Islands; the last expeditionary war of the post-imperial era and a defining moment in Thatcher&rsquo;s premiership.">Falklands War</dfn> was the last expeditionary war of the post-imperial era. It revived a kind of patriotic sentiment that the post-Suez generation had thought impossible &mdash; and it confirmed the principle that the remaining British Overseas Territories were defended by their inhabitants&rsquo; choice rather than imperial possession.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the year of the Notting Hill attacks, the year Claudia Jones founded the indoor Caribbean Carnival, and the year of Ugandan Asian arrival.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1958 Notting Hill attacks led directly to Claudia Jones&rsquo;s 1959 indoor Caribbean Carnival &mdash; ancestor of today&rsquo;s Notting Hill Carnival. In 1972 Britain accepted 27,200 Ugandan Asians expelled by Idi Amin, the largest refugee arrival since the Huguenots.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Windrush Scandal, 2018</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In 2018 a series of <em>Guardian</em> reports revealed that members of the Windrush generation &mdash; people who had arrived as British subjects, lived legally in Britain for decades and never been issued papers &mdash; had been wrongly detained, denied healthcare, refused work, and in some cases deported to countries they had not seen since childhood. The 2017 destruction of landing-card archives by the Home Office had left them unable to prove their status. At least 164 people were wrongly removed; thousands more were illegally detained or denied benefits. The 2020 Windrush Lessons Learned Review described the scandal as the result of &lsquo;institutional ignorance and thoughtlessness towards the issue of race&rsquo;. Compensation has been slow: by 2024 fewer than half of valid claims had been resolved. The Windrush story therefore did not end in 1948 &mdash; it is a live political issue 75 years later.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The 1948 British Nationality Act and the arrival of HMT Empire Windrush opened the door to around 250,000 Caribbean migrants by 1962, in the face of widespread hostility &mdash; the 1958 Notting Hill attacks, the 1959 Kelso Cochrane murder.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Claudia Jones&rsquo;s West Indian Gazette (1958) and indoor Caribbean Carnival (1959) became the foundation of post-war Black British political and cultural organisation; the 1972 Ugandan Asian resettlement showed Britain could welcome refugees from its own former empire.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Commonwealth and the 1982 Falklands War reshaped post-imperial British identity; the 2018 Windrush scandal showed how unfinished the legacy of empire remained 70 years after Tilbury.</li>
</ul>
