--- DESCRIPTION ---
Huguenot refugees, the Highland Clearances and the Ulster Plantations — three migration stories.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Three Movements, Three Motives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between roughly 1600 and 1850, three very different migrations reshaped Britain and Ireland: French Protestant <dfn class="term" data-def="French Protestants who fled persecution in France, especially after Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685.">Huguenot</dfn> refugees fleeing Catholic France, Lowland Scots and English settlers planted into Ulster, and Highland Scots driven off their land by their own clan chiefs. Each was driven by a different mix of <strong>religion</strong>, <strong>government policy</strong> and <strong>economic resources</strong>. Together they show that British migration history before Windrush is not a single story &mdash; people moved in, out and within Britain for very different reasons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the year Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes, and roughly how many Huguenots came to Britain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1685 Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes, which had protected French Protestants. Around 50,000 Huguenots fled to Britain in the years that followed, settling mainly in Spitalfields, London, Canterbury and Norwich.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Huguenots: Religion as Push, Skills as Pull</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Huguenots had been a tolerated Protestant minority in France since the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1598 royal decree by Henry IV of France granting Huguenots religious freedom and civil rights; revoked by Louis XIV in 1685.">Edict of Nantes</dfn> of 1598. When Louis XIV revoked it in 1685, Huguenots faced forced conversion, the destruction of their churches and the kidnapping of their children. Around 200,000 left France in total &mdash; roughly 50,000 came to Britain. Charles II and James II issued royal proclamations promising shelter, and Parliament backed this with a 1689 act of naturalisation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The reception was unusually warm because Huguenots brought useful skills. They settled in <em>Spitalfields</em> in east London and turned it into the centre of British silk weaving. Others built up Canterbury&rsquo;s and Norwich&rsquo;s textile trades. Goldsmiths, watchmakers, gunsmiths and bankers (the founders of the Bank of England in 1694 included several Huguenot families) added economic weight. The word &lsquo;refugee&rsquo; entered English from this moment &mdash; from French <em>r&eacute;fugi&eacute;</em>, applied first to the Huguenots.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">In 1290 Edward I had expelled the Jewish population of England; in 1597 Elizabeth I expelled the Hanseatic merchants from the London Steelyard. Yet Huguenots in 1685 were welcomed. The difference was a mix of religion and economics: Huguenots were Protestant, like most of the English political class, and were arriving in a kingdom that had just lived through anti-Catholic panics. Their skills also matched a national strategy &mdash; English textile manufacturers were trying to compete with French silk and Dutch fine cloth, and the Huguenots brought exactly the techniques needed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Ulster Plantation: Government Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A century earlier, in 1609, James I had launched the <dfn class="term" data-def="The organised seventeenth-century settlement of confiscated Irish land in the province of Ulster by Lowland Scots and English Protestants, beginning in 1609.">Ulster Plantation</dfn>: confiscated Catholic Irish land in six counties (Donegal, Tyrone, Tyrconnell, Armagh, Cavan and Fermanagh) was redistributed to around 100,000 Lowland Scots and English Protestant settlers. The aim was political, not economic &mdash; the Crown wanted a loyal Protestant population in Ireland to prevent rebellion and Spanish intervention. Settlers received land cheaply on condition that they bring tenants, build defended towns and exclude Catholic Irish from the best holdings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The plantation succeeded as a population transfer but at the cost of long-term sectarian division. Catholic Irish populations were pushed onto poorer land. The 1641 rising killed thousands of settlers; Cromwell&rsquo;s 1649-50 reconquest then dispossessed the Catholic Irish even further. By 1700 Ulster had a Protestant Scots-Irish majority that has continued to shape Northern Irish politics ever since.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Highland Clearances: Forced Out at Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the Scottish Highlands between roughly 1750 and 1860, clan chiefs replaced their tenants with sheep. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The eviction of Scottish Highland tenants between c1750 and 1860 to make way for large-scale sheep farming, often by their own clan chiefs.">Highland Clearances</dfn> were a private, profit-driven migration: the chiefs had become landlords in the British legal sense after the failure of the 1745 Jacobite rising, and Cheviot sheep produced higher rents than Gaelic-speaking smallholders. Tenants were given short notice, their cottages were sometimes burnt down behind them (the Sutherland clearances of 1814 are notorious), and they were pushed to coastal &lsquo;crofting&rsquo; townships, to Lowland cities, or onto emigrant ships to North America and Australia.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">In May 1814 Patrick Sellar, factor (estate manager) for the Countess of Sutherland, evicted around 1,500 tenants from Strathnaver. Houses were torn down and sometimes burnt while their occupants were still inside; one elderly woman, Margaret McKay, died days later from her injuries. Sellar was tried for culpable homicide in 1816 and acquitted. The case became a touchstone of clearance memory: those who survived it carried Gaelic-language ballads and stories with them to Cape Breton, North Carolina and the Glasgow tenements, keeping the trauma alive across the Atlantic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Three Migrations, Compared</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">For the 4-mark &lsquo;two ways similar or different&rsquo; question, these three movements give you ready-made comparisons. <strong>Religion</strong> drove Huguenot flight and shaped the Ulster plantation, but had no role in the clearances &mdash; they were economic. <strong>Government</strong> directed the Ulster plantation explicitly and welcomed the Huguenots through naturalisation, but stayed out of the clearances. <strong>Economic resources</strong> attracted Huguenots to Spitalfields silk and pushed Highlanders onto emigrant ships, while shaping landlord behaviour in both Ulster and the Highlands. The reception of incomers also differed: Huguenots were welcomed for their skills, planted Ulster Scots were imposed on a hostile native population, and Highland evictees were strangers in their own country.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down which factor (religion, government or economic resources) was strongest in each of the three migrations.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Huguenot migration was driven mainly by religion (push) and economic resources (pull). The Ulster Plantation was driven mainly by government policy. The Highland Clearances were driven mainly by economic resources &mdash; landlord profit from sheep farming.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">After Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, around 50,000 Huguenots came to Britain. Their silk weaving, banking and craft skills made Spitalfields, Canterbury and Norwich centres of new manufacturing.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Ulster Plantation of 1609 transferred around 100,000 Scottish and English Protestants onto confiscated Catholic Irish land &mdash; a government-engineered migration with long-term sectarian consequences.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The Highland Clearances (c1750-1860) cleared Gaelic tenants for Cheviot sheep, sending them to Lowland cities, North America and Australia &mdash; an economic migration imposed by their own clan chiefs.</li>
</ul>
