--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the World Wars, Suez 1956, and Indian and African independence ended Britain’s empire.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From World Power to Post-Imperial Power, 1914-1965</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1914 Britain ruled around a quarter of the world&rsquo;s population. By 1965 it ruled almost none of it. The First and Second World Wars, the rise of nationalist movements led by figures such as Mahatma Gandhi, Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta, and the diplomatic humiliation of the 1956 Suez Crisis combined to end the largest empire in modern history. For a 16-mark factor essay, this is the textbook test of how war, ideas (especially anti-colonial nationalism) and the role of individuals interacted with each other &mdash; rarely working alone.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the year of Indian independence and partition, the year Ghana became independent, and the year of the Suez Crisis.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>India and Pakistan became independent on 14-15 August 1947 (around 12 million people displaced and at least one million killed in partition violence). Ghana became the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence on 6 March 1957. The 1956 Suez Crisis exposed Britain as no longer able to act independently of the United States.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The First World War: Promises and Mobilisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The First World War mobilised the empire on a scale never seen before. Around 1.5 million Indian troops served, more than 600,000 in combat. About 200,000 from Africa fought, and West Indies Regiment troops served on the Western Front. They returned home expecting recognition and reform. Britain&rsquo;s 1917 Montagu Declaration promised Indians &lsquo;the gradual development of self-governing institutions&rsquo;. The 1919 Government of India Act delivered limited reform; the same year, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1919 massacre at Amritsar in which British troops under General Dyer killed at least 379 unarmed Indian civilians; a turning point in Indian nationalism.">Amritsar Massacre</dfn> killed at least 379 unarmed Indians at a Sikh festival, exposing the gap between promise and reality. Mahatma Gandhi&rsquo;s Indian National Congress mass campaigns &mdash; non-cooperation (1920-22), the Salt March (1930), Quit India (1942) &mdash; built on the resulting anger.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Second World War: The Decisive Blow</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Second World War broke imperial Britain economically, militarily and morally. Around 2.5 million Indians served, the largest volunteer army in history; West African and East African forces fought in Burma and Italy; West Indian aircrew flew with the RAF. The 1942 fall of Singapore to Japan &mdash; in which 80,000 British, Indian and Australian soldiers surrendered &mdash; permanently shattered the myth of European racial superiority in Asia. The 1943 Bengal famine killed at least three million Indians; Churchill&rsquo;s government diverted food to British troops. By 1945 Britain owed the United States the equivalent of around four years of national income, was rationing food at home, and had a Labour government committed to the welfare state. There was no money, no army and no will to maintain empire by force.</p>

Around 12 million people moved across the new borders; at least one million died in inter-communal violence. Gandhi opposed partition and was assassinated in January 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who saw him as too friendly to Muslims. Independence ended British rule over the &lsquo;jewel in the crown&rsquo; &mdash; the empire&rsquo;s biggest asset and the colony around which all imperial strategy had been built since the 1857 Rebellion (Lesson 9).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Suez 1956: The Public Humiliation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In July 1956 Egypt&rsquo;s president Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal &mdash; British- and French-owned since 1869, the lifeline to the remaining empire. Anthony Eden conspired with France and Israel to retake the canal: Israel invaded the Sinai on 29 October; Britain and France issued an ultimatum and bombed Egyptian airfields on 31 October. Within ten days, US President Eisenhower ordered Britain to withdraw. The pound came under such pressure that the Treasury could not defend it without American help &mdash; help Eisenhower withheld until British troops left. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1956 international crisis in which Britain, France and Israel attacked Egypt to retake the nationalised Suez Canal; US pressure forced their humiliating withdrawal, exposing Britain&rsquo;s post-imperial dependence on the USA.">Suez Crisis</dfn> ended Eden&rsquo;s career and made it explicit: Britain could no longer act as a great imperial power without American consent. Decolonisation accelerated.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Africa: The Wind of Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Convention People&rsquo;s Party leader who became the first Prime Minister (1957) and President of independent Ghana &mdash; the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence.">Kwame Nkrumah</dfn> founded the Convention People&rsquo;s Party in 1949 and used a programme of strikes and &lsquo;positive action&rsquo; to make the Gold Coast ungovernable. Independent Ghana followed in 1957. Harold Macmillan&rsquo;s 1960 &lsquo;Wind of Change&rsquo; speech in Cape Town acknowledged that African nationalism would not be stopped. Nigeria became independent in 1960; Sierra Leone and Tanganyika in 1961; Uganda in 1962; Kenya in 1963 under <dfn class="term" data-def="KANU leader who became the first Prime Minister (1963) and President of independent Kenya, after being detained for nine years during the Mau Mau Emergency.">Jomo Kenyatta</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Kenya&rsquo;s path was the most violent. The 1952-1960 <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1952-1960 Kikuyu uprising in colonial Kenya, brutally suppressed by British forces and accompanied by mass detention; central to the British government&rsquo;s 2013 apology and compensation settlement.">Mau Mau Emergency</dfn> saw a Kikuyu insurgency against settler land-grabs and forced labour. The British response was extraordinary in its violence: at least 1.5 million Kenyans were detained or held in fortified villages; around 11,000 Mau Mau fighters were killed (some estimates run higher). Caroline Elkins&rsquo;s 2005 book <em>Britain&rsquo;s Gulag</em> exposed routine torture and unlawful killings in the camps. In 2013 the British government paid &pound;19.9 million in compensation to 5,228 surviving detainees and apologised &mdash; an admission of state crime that the empire had denied for half a century.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Gandhi did not invent Indian nationalism &mdash; the Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 &mdash; but he transformed it. After his return from South Africa in 1915, he developed <em>satyagraha</em> (non-violent resistance) into a mass political technique. The 1930 Salt March, in which 78 followers walked 240 miles to make salt from seawater in defiance of the British monopoly, made the British Empire look ridiculous on world cinema screens. Gandhi&rsquo;s influence reached far beyond India: Martin Luther King studied his methods, and Nkrumah&rsquo;s &lsquo;positive action&rsquo; in the Gold Coast was modelled on Gandhi&rsquo;s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name two reasons (one war, one nationalist movement) the British Empire ended after 1945, and write a one-line judgement linking them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The end of empire was not a single event but a process from 1945 to 1965. Britain&rsquo;s post-1945 economic exhaustion, the Suez 1956 humiliation, and the leadership of Gandhi (India 1947), Nkrumah (Ghana 1957) and Kenyatta (Kenya 1963) combined to dismantle most of the empire in around 20 years.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The two World Wars mobilised, indebted and discredited the British Empire; by 1945 Britain had no money, no army and no will to maintain rule by force.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Indian and Pakistani independence in 1947 displaced 12 million people and killed at least one million; Gandhi&rsquo;s non-violent campaigns made British rule politically untenable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The 1956 Suez Crisis showed Britain could no longer act independently of the US; the &lsquo;Wind of Change&rsquo; speech (1960) accepted decolonisation; Ghana 1957 (Nkrumah) and Kenya 1963 (Kenyatta) led the African transition.</li>
</ul>
