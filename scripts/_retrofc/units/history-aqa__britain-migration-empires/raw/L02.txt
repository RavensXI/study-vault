--- DESCRIPTION ---
Cnut, Emma of Normandy and how England joined a North Sea Empire after 1016.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Second Wave of Vikings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Alfred&rsquo;s descendants reconquered the Danelaw in the early tenth century, and by 927 Athelstan ruled a single kingdom of England for the first time. But the Norse were not finished. From the 980s a second wave of raids battered the country under King Aethelred, &lsquo;the Unready&rsquo; (more accurately &lsquo;ill-counselled&rsquo;). Aethelred tried to buy peace by paying a vast tribute called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A tribute paid by English kings to Viking armies in exchange for peace, especially under Aethelred II between 991 and 1014.">Danegeld</dfn>, raised by a heavy land tax. The strategy failed: each payment encouraged a fresh fleet to come back for more.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In 1002 Aethelred ordered the St Brice&rsquo;s Day massacre of Danes living in his kingdom &mdash; an episode the spec&rsquo;s longer view treats as a warning about how host populations have responded to migrants. The killings provoked the Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard, whose sister was reportedly among the dead, to launch a full-scale invasion. By the time Sweyn died in 1014 his son Cnut was poised to claim the English throne, and after a short war Cnut became king of England in 1016.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which Anglo-Saxon king ordered the St Brice&rsquo;s Day massacre, in which year, and what consequence did it have?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aethelred&rsquo;s 1002 St Brice&rsquo;s Day massacre of Danes living in England helped trigger Sweyn Forkbeard&rsquo;s invasion. Within fourteen years Sweyn&rsquo;s son Cnut was crowned king of England.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Cnut&rsquo;s Rule</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Cnut ruled England from 1016 to 1035, and after 1018 also Denmark, and from 1028 also Norway. Together these territories made up what historians call the <dfn class="term" data-def="The political union under Cnut after 1018 of England, Denmark and (from 1028) Norway, governed through personal kingship rather than a single administration.">North Sea Empire</dfn>. It was not an empire in the modern sense; there were no shared institutions, only the same king. But it tied England into a Scandinavian world of trade, marriage and military networks for two decades.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Cnut governed England through Anglo-Saxon laws and English-speaking earls. He confirmed the laws of King Edgar, gave generous endowments to English monasteries, and went on pilgrimage to Rome in 1027. The famous story of Cnut commanding the tide to retreat is a later fiction, invented to show that he knew &mdash; against the courtiers&rsquo; flattery &mdash; that no king is above God. He cultivated, in other words, the public image of a Christian English king, not a foreign conqueror, while keeping Danish housecarls as his personal guard.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A political arrangement in which one ruler holds two or more separate kingdoms, each retaining its own laws and administration.">personal union</dfn> meant the kingdoms shared a king but kept their own laws, churches and government. England remained governed in English; Denmark in Danish. The arrangement was fragile because it depended on one life. When Cnut died in 1035 his sons Harold Harefoot and Harthacnut squabbled, and the empire fell apart within seven years.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Emma of Normandy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The figure who held this Scandinavian-English-Norman world together was a queen, not a king. Emma of Normandy was the daughter of Duke Richard I of Normandy. She married Aethelred in 1002 to seal an Anglo-Norman alliance against Viking raids. After Aethelred&rsquo;s death she married his Danish conqueror Cnut in 1017 to seal another alliance &mdash; this time between the new Danish king and the existing English political class.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Emma was the mother of two future English kings &mdash; Edward the Confessor (Aethelred&rsquo;s son) and Harthacnut (Cnut&rsquo;s son) &mdash; and the great-aunt of William the Conqueror. Her unique position as queen of two rival dynasties commissioned the <em>Encomium Emmae Reginae</em>, a piece of political writing designed to justify her family&rsquo;s claim to the throne. She is a rare medieval figure who chose to write &mdash; or commission &mdash; her own version of events.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The 1066 Succession Crisis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Cnut&rsquo;s sons died young. The throne returned in 1042 to Emma&rsquo;s English son Edward the Confessor, who had spent his exile at the Norman court. Edward died childless in January 1066, and the question of who should rule England produced four rival claimants: Harold Godwinson (the leading English earl), Edgar Aetheling (the last male of the West Saxon line), Harald Hardrada of Norway (claiming through Cnut&rsquo;s son Magnus), and William of Normandy (claiming through Edward&rsquo;s mother Emma and a disputed promise).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The succession crisis was, in part, the legacy of Cnut&rsquo;s North Sea Empire and Emma&rsquo;s two marriages: each of the four claimants traced his case through the Scandinavian-English-Norman links forged in this generation. The events of 1066 &mdash; the battles of Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings &mdash; closed one period of cross-Channel kingship and opened another.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">From the Lindisfarne raid in 793 to the death of Harthacnut in 1042, Britain was repeatedly remade by Norse arrivals. Cnut&rsquo;s reign is the high point: a Danish king ruling through Anglo-Saxon institutions, married to a Norman noblewoman, while his housecarls stood guard. The picture is messier than &lsquo;Vikings raided Britain&rsquo; &mdash; by the eleventh century the rulers of England were Vikings, and English government, language and Christianity carried on around them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write, from memory, the names of Emma of Normandy&rsquo;s two royal husbands and her two sons who became king of England.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Emma of Normandy linked three royal houses. Married first to Aethelred and then to Cnut, she was the mother of Edward the Confessor and Harthacnut and the great-aunt of William the Conqueror. The 1066 crisis grew directly out of these family ties.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Aethelred&rsquo;s Danegeld and the 1002 St Brice&rsquo;s Day massacre helped provoke a second Viking conquest, ending in Cnut&rsquo;s accession in 1016.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Cnut governed England through Anglo-Saxon laws and earls while holding personal union with Denmark and Norway, creating a short-lived North Sea Empire.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Emma of Normandy&rsquo;s two marriages tied England to both Scandinavia and Normandy and produced the rival claimants who fought it out in 1066.</li></ul>
