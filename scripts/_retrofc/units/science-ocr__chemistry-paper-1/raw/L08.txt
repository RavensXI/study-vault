--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Oxidation and Reduction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction where a substance gains oxygen or loses electrons.">Oxidation</dfn> is the gain of oxygen or the loss of electrons. <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction where a substance loses oxygen or gains electrons.">Reduction</dfn> is the loss of oxygen or the gain of electrons. These always happen together in what are called <strong>redox reactions</strong>. A useful mnemonic is <strong>OIL RIG</strong>: Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For example, when copper oxide is reduced by carbon: \(2CuO + C \rightarrow 2Cu + CO_2\). The copper oxide loses oxygen (it is <strong>reduced</strong>) and the carbon gains oxygen (it is <strong>oxidised</strong>). Carbon is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that reduces another substance by providing electrons or accepting oxygen.">reducing agent</dfn> (it causes the reduction of copper oxide) and copper oxide is the <strong>oxidising agent</strong> (it causes the oxidation of carbon). At Higher Tier, you also need to explain redox in terms of electrons: the substance that is oxidised <strong>loses</strong> electrons, and the substance that is reduced <strong>gains</strong> electrons. For example, in the formation of magnesium oxide: \(Mg \rightarrow Mg^{2+} + 2e^-\) (oxidation) and \(O + 2e^- \rightarrow O^{2-}\) (reduction).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss of electrons, Reduction Is Gain of electrons. In any redox reaction, identify which species is oxidised and which is reduced.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Oxidation = gain of oxygen OR loss of electrons. Reduction = loss of oxygen OR gain of electrons. In terms of electrons (Higher): the substance that is oxidised <strong>loses</strong> electrons; the substance that is reduced <strong>gains</strong> electrons.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Acids and Alkalis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Substances that produce hydrogen ions (H+) when dissolved in water, with a pH below 7.">Acids</dfn> produce hydrogen ions (\(H^+\)) when they dissolve in water. Solutions of <dfn class="term" data-def="Soluble bases that produce hydroxide ions (OH-) in water, with a pH above 7.">alkalis</dfn> contain hydroxide ions (\(OH^-\)). The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is, running from 0 (very strong acid) to 14 (very strong alkali). pH 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic, and above 7 is alkaline. Each step on the pH scale represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">You can measure pH using <strong>universal indicator</strong> (which changes colour across a range: red for strong acids, green for neutral, purple for strong alkalis) or a <strong>pH meter</strong> (which gives a precise numerical reading). Litmus paper gives a simple acid/alkali test but cannot distinguish between different strengths.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Dilute</strong> and <strong>concentrated</strong> refer to the amount of acid dissolved in a given volume of water. <strong>Strong</strong> and <strong>weak</strong> refer to the degree of ionisation. A strong acid (e.g. hydrochloric acid) fully ionises in water — every molecule produces \(H^+\) ions. A weak acid (e.g. ethanoic acid) only partially ionises — only some molecules produce \(H^+\) ions. When the hydrogen ion concentration increases by a factor of ten, the pH decreases by one.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Neutralisation and Making Salts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The reaction between an acid and a base (or alkali) to produce a salt and water.">Neutralisation</dfn> occurs when an acid reacts with a base or alkali to form a <strong>salt</strong> plus <strong>water</strong>. The ionic equation for neutralisation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Key reactions to know:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Acid + metal</strong> \(\rightarrow\) salt + hydrogen. For example: \(Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow MgCl_2(aq) + H_2(g)\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Acid + metal carbonate</strong> \(\rightarrow\) salt + water + carbon dioxide. For example: \(CaCO_3(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Acid + alkali</strong> \(\rightarrow\) salt + water. For example: \(NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) \rightarrow NaCl(aq) + H_2O(l)\).</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="To work out the name of a salt: the metal comes from the base/metal/carbonate, and the second part comes from the acid (hydrochloric = chloride, sulfuric = sulfate, nitric = nitrate).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The name of the salt depends on the acid used: <strong>hydrochloric acid</strong> \(\rightarrow\) chloride salts, <strong>sulfuric acid</strong> \(\rightarrow\) sulfate salts, <strong>nitric acid</strong> \(\rightarrow\) nitrate salts.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Electrolysis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="The decomposition of an ionic compound using an electric current, when the compound is molten or dissolved in water.">Electrolysis</dfn> is the process of using an electric current to decompose (break down) an ionic compound that is either molten or dissolved in water. The ionic compound must be in a liquid state (molten or dissolved in water) so that the ions are free to move and carry the electrical current. Solid ionic compounds cannot be electrolysed because their ions are held in fixed positions within the lattice.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How Electrolysis Works</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">The ionic compound is placed in a container with two electrodes connected to a power supply. Positive ions (<dfn class="term" data-def="Positively charged ions that move towards the cathode (negative electrode) during electrolysis.">cations</dfn>) move towards the <strong>cathode</strong> (negative electrode), where they gain electrons and are <strong>reduced</strong>. Negative ions (<dfn class="term" data-def="Negatively charged ions that move towards the anode (positive electrode) during electrolysis.">anions</dfn>) move towards the <strong>anode</strong> (positive electrode), where they lose electrons and are <strong>oxidised</strong>. Metals (or hydrogen) form at the cathode; non-metals form at the anode.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n20">For <strong>molten binary compounds</strong>, predicting products is straightforward. For example, molten sodium chloride produces sodium at the cathode and chlorine at the anode. For <strong>aqueous solutions</strong>, there are competing reactions because water also contains \(H^+\) and \(OH^-\) ions. At the cathode, hydrogen is produced instead of the metal if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen. At the anode, oxygen is usually produced (from \(OH^-\) ions) unless halide ions are present, in which case the halogen is produced. For example, electrolysing aqueous copper sulfate with inert (carbon) electrodes produces copper at the cathode (because copper is less reactive than hydrogen) and oxygen at the anode (because no halide ions are present). The electrodes used matter: <strong>inert electrodes</strong> (such as carbon or platinum) do not take part in the reaction, whereas <strong>non-inert electrodes</strong> (such as copper) can themselves dissolve or be deposited during electrolysis.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Half equations at electrodes: cathode = reduction (gain electrons), anode = oxidation (lose electrons). Remember PANIC: Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>At the cathode: \(\text{Na}^+ + e^- \rightarrow \text{Na}\) (reduction). At the anode: \(2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow Cl_2 + 2e^-\) (oxidation). Half equations show what happens at each electrode separately.</p>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Oxidation is gain of oxygen or loss of electrons (OIL RIG). Reduction is the reverse. These always occur together in redox reactions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Acids produce \(H^+\) ions; alkalis produce \(OH^-\) ions. Neutralisation: \(H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O\). Salt names depend on the acid used.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Electrolysis decomposes ionic compounds using electricity. Cations go to the cathode (reduced); anions go to the anode (oxidised).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">For electrolysis questions, always identify whether the compound is molten or aqueous — this changes the products. Write half equations for each electrode and state whether oxidation or reduction occurs. For acid reactions, be systematic: identify the acid, the base/metal/carbonate, and then predict the salt name. Show balanced equations with state symbols where possible.</p>
