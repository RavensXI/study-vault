--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Structure of the Atom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest part of an element that can exist, made up of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.">atom</dfn> is the smallest part of an element that can exist. Atoms are incredibly small — a typical atomic radius is about \(1 \times 10^{-10}\) m. Each atom has a tiny, dense <dfn class="term" data-def="The central part of an atom, containing protons and neutrons, with a positive overall charge.">nucleus</dfn> at its centre. The nucleus contains two types of subatomic particle: <strong>protons</strong> (positive charge) and <strong>neutrons</strong> (no charge). <dfn class="term" data-def="Negatively charged subatomic particles that orbit the nucleus in energy levels (shells).">Electrons</dfn> orbit the nucleus in energy levels (shells) and carry a negative charge.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Learn the table of subatomic particles: proton (+1, mass 1), neutron (0, mass 1), electron (-1, mass ~0). This appears in almost every atomic structure question.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Relative charges and approximate relative masses: <strong>Proton</strong> — charge +1, mass 1. <strong>Neutron</strong> — charge 0, mass 1. <strong>Electron</strong> — charge −1, mass approximately \(\frac{1}{1836}\) (negligible). The nuclear radius is much smaller than the radius of the atom — most of the atom is empty space.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Atomic Number and Mass Number</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, which defines the element.">atomic number</dfn> tells you the number of protons in the nucleus. It defines what element an atom is — all atoms of the same element have the same atomic number. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the atom has no overall charge. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.">mass number</dfn> is the total number of protons plus neutrons.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">To calculate the number of neutrons:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$\text{number of neutrons} = \text{mass number} - \text{atomic number}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">For example, sodium (Na) has atomic number 11 and mass number 23, so it has \(23 - 11 = 12\) neutrons, 11 protons and 11 electrons.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Isotopes and Ions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, giving them different mass numbers.">Isotopes</dfn> are atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but <strong>different numbers of neutrons</strong>. This means they have different mass numbers but identical chemical properties. For example, carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<p data-narration-id="n11">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A charged atom that has gained or lost electrons, giving it a positive or negative charge.">ion</dfn> is an atom that has gained or lost electrons. Losing electrons forms a <strong>positive ion</strong> (cation) because there are now more protons than electrons. Gaining electrons forms a <strong>negative ion</strong> (anion). The nucleus does not change when an ion forms — only the number of electrons changes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Development of the Atomic Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Our understanding of the atom has changed over time as new experimental evidence emerged.</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n15">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Early 1800s</div>
<h4>Dalton’s Model</h4><p>John Dalton proposed atoms were tiny, solid, indivisible spheres. He thought all atoms of the same element were identical.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1897</div>
<h4>Thomson’s <dfn class="term" data-def="An early atomic model where negative electrons are embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge.">Plum Pudding Model</dfn></h4><p>J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed a ball of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1909–1911</div>
<h4>Rutherford, Geiger & Marsden</h4><p>They fired alpha particles at thin gold foil. Most passed straight through (the atom is mostly empty space), but some deflected at large angles and a few bounced back. This disproved the plum pudding model. Rutherford proposed the <strong>nuclear model</strong>: a small, dense, positively charged nucleus with electrons orbiting around it.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1913</div>
<h4>Bohr’s Model</h4><p>Niels Bohr refined the model by placing electrons in <strong>fixed energy levels</strong> at specific distances from the nucleus, rather than orbiting randomly.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="For timeline questions, name the scientist AND their evidence. Just naming a model without explaining the experiment won't score full marks.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The alpha scattering experiment disproved the plum pudding model. Most alpha particles passed through (mostly empty space), some bounced back (small, dense, positive nucleus).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Electronic Configuration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Electrons occupy energy levels (shells) around the nucleus. The first shell holds a maximum of <strong>2</strong> electrons. The second and third shells each hold a maximum of <strong>8</strong>. Electrons fill the lowest available energy level first.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Working Out Electronic Configuration</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">Find the element’s atomic number (= number of electrons in a neutral atom). Fill the first shell with up to 2, the second with up to 8, and the third with up to 8. For example: oxygen (8 electrons) is 2, 6. Sodium (11 electrons) is 2, 8, 1. Calcium (20 electrons) is 2, 8, 8, 2. The number of outer-shell electrons matches the group number for Groups 1–7, explaining why elements in the same group react similarly.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The arrangement of electrons in the energy levels of an atom, written as numbers separated by commas.">electronic configuration</dfn> determines how an element reacts. Group 1 elements have one outer electron and lose it easily. Group 7 elements have seven outer electrons and tend to gain one. Group 0 elements have full outer shells and are very unreactive.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Atoms have a small, dense nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in energy levels. Typical atomic radius is \(1 \times 10^{-10}\) m.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Isotopes have the same protons but different neutrons. Ions form when atoms gain or lose electrons — the nucleus does not change.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The atomic model evolved through Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr, driven by new experimental evidence at each stage.</li>
</ul>
