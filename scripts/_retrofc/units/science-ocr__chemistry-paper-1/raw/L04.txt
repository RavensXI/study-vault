<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Atoms Bond</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Atoms are most stable when they have a full outer shell of electrons. Most atoms do not naturally have a full outer shell, so they bond with other atoms to achieve one. There are three main types of chemical bond: <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed when electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal.">ionic bonding</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A bond formed by the sharing of a pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms.">covalent bonding</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The strong electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.">metallic bonding</dfn>. All chemical bonds involve <strong>electrostatic forces</strong> — the attraction between positive and negative charges. The type of bond that forms depends on whether the atoms involved are metals or non-metals and how many electrons are in their outer shells.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Ionic Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Ionic bonding occurs between <strong>metals</strong> and <strong>non-metals</strong>. The metal atom <strong>transfers</strong> one or more electrons to the non-metal atom. The metal atom loses electrons and becomes a positively charged ion (cation). The non-metal atom gains electrons and becomes a negatively charged ion (anion). The oppositely charged ions are then held together by strong electrostatic forces of attraction — this is the ionic bond.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For example, in sodium chloride (NaCl): sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one electron to become \(\text{Na}^+\) (2, 8), and chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains one electron to become \(\text{Cl}^-\) (2, 8, 8). Both ions now have full outer shells, making them stable. In magnesium oxide (MgO), magnesium (2, 8, 2) loses two electrons to become \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\), and oxygen (2, 6) gains two electrons to become \(\text{O}^{2-}\). In an <dfn class="term" data-def="A compound made of positive and negative ions held together in a regular three-dimensional arrangement by strong electrostatic forces.">ionic compound</dfn>, billions of ions are arranged in a regular three-dimensional structure called a giant ionic lattice, where each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions in all directions.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="In dot-cross diagrams for ionic compounds, show the charge on each ion and use square brackets. Remember: the metal LOSES electrons, the non-metal GAINS them.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In ionic bonding, electrons are <strong>transferred</strong> from metal to non-metal. The resulting ions have opposite charges and attract each other. Ionic compounds form giant ionic lattices.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Covalent Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Covalent bonding occurs between <strong>non-metal</strong> atoms. Instead of transferring electrons, the atoms <strong>share</strong> one or more pairs of electrons. Each shared pair of electrons is one covalent bond. By sharing, both atoms achieve full outer shells. A single shared pair is a single bond, two shared pairs form a double bond (e.g. \(O_2\)), and three shared pairs form a triple bond (e.g. \(N_2\)).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">For example, in a molecule of water (\(H_2O\)), the oxygen atom shares one pair of electrons with each of two hydrogen atoms, forming two covalent bonds. In chlorine gas (\(Cl_2\)), two chlorine atoms each share one pair of electrons, forming a single covalent bond. Covalent compounds include small <strong>simple molecules</strong> (like \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\), \(CH_4\)) and enormous <strong>giant covalent structures</strong> (like diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing the arrangement of electrons in a bond, using dots for electrons from one atom and crosses for electrons from another.">Dot-and-cross diagrams</dfn> show how electrons are arranged in a bond. Use dots for the electrons from one atom and crosses for the electrons from the other. For ionic compounds, show the electron transfer and put each ion in square brackets with its charge. For covalent compounds, show the shared pairs in the overlap between the two atoms. These diagrams are a useful model but have limitations: they do not show the three-dimensional shape of molecules, the relative sizes of atoms, or the distances between atoms. Ball-and-stick models and space-filling models address some of these limitations but introduce others. No single model is perfect — each emphasises different aspects of bonding.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Metallic Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In metals, the atoms are arranged in a regular structure. The outer-shell electrons are <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrons that are free to move through a metallic structure, not attached to any particular atom.">delocalised</dfn> — they are free to move through the whole structure rather than being attached to individual atoms. This leaves behind a lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by a “sea” of delocalised electrons. The strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the negative delocalised electrons is what holds the metal together.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Types of Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Substances can have different types of structure, which affects their properties:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Giant vs Simple Structures</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Giant ionic lattices</strong> (e.g. NaCl) — regular arrangement of alternating positive and negative ions, held by strong ionic bonds in all directions. <strong>Simple molecular structures</strong> (e.g. \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\)) — small molecules with strong covalent bonds within molecules but weak intermolecular forces between molecules. <strong>Giant covalent structures</strong> (e.g. diamond, graphite) — huge networks of atoms all bonded by strong covalent bonds. <strong>Polymers</strong> — very long chain molecules made of repeating units joined by covalent bonds. <strong>Metallic structures</strong> — regular lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Link bonding type to structure type: metals = metallic, metal + non-metal = ionic (giant lattice), non-metal + non-metal = covalent (simple molecular or giant covalent).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Chemical bonding is always about <strong>electrostatic forces</strong>: ionic = attraction between opposite ions; covalent = shared electron pairs attracting both nuclei; metallic = positive ions attracted to delocalised electrons.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Periodic Table helps predict bonding type. Elements on the left (metals) tend to lose electrons. Elements on the right (non-metals) tend to gain or share electrons. An element’s position in the table — its group and period — tells you its electronic configuration, which determines how it bonds. Group 1 elements have one outer electron and form +1 ions; Group 7 elements have seven outer electrons and gain one to form −1 ions. Elements in Group 0 have full outer shells and do not readily bond.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Ionic bonding = electron transfer between metals and non-metals, forming a giant ionic lattice of oppositely charged ions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Covalent bonding = electron sharing between non-metals, forming simple molecules or giant covalent structures.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Metallic bonding = delocalised electrons shared across a lattice of positive metal ions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When drawing dot-and-cross diagrams, always show the outer shell electrons clearly. For ionic compounds, use square brackets and include the charge (e.g. [Na]\(^+\)). For covalent compounds, only show the outer shell and make sure each atom achieves a full outer shell through sharing. In “explain” questions about bonding, always refer to <strong>electrostatic forces</strong> and <strong>electron transfer or sharing</strong>.</p>
