--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Changes in Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every chemical reaction involves an energy change. Energy is either transferred to the surroundings or absorbed from them. The key principle is that energy is <strong>transferred</strong>, never created or destroyed. Two categories describe the direction of energy transfer. Understanding these categories — and the bond-level explanation for why energy is transferred — is fundamental to the whole of chemistry. Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between the system and the surroundings.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Remember: EXO = EXIT (energy exits to surroundings, temperature rises). ENDO = ENTER (energy enters from surroundings, temperature falls). Link the prefix to the direction.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Exothermic</strong> reactions transfer energy to the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings increases. <strong>Endothermic</strong> reactions take in energy from the surroundings — the temperature of the surroundings decreases.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, causing a temperature increase.">Exothermic reactions</dfn> include combustion (burning fuels), neutralisation (acid + alkali), and many oxidation reactions. Everyday examples include hand warmers (the crystallisation of a supersaturated sodium acetate solution) and self-heating food cans. <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, causing a temperature decrease.">Endothermic reactions</dfn> include thermal decomposition, the reaction of citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate, thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate, and photosynthesis. Sports cold packs are a practical example — the endothermic dissolution of ammonium nitrate in water absorbs heat, cooling the injured area.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Measuring Energy Changes (PAG C5)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">You can identify whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic by measuring the temperature change of the surroundings (usually a solution in a polystyrene cup). If the temperature rises, the reaction is exothermic. If the temperature falls, the reaction is endothermic. A polystyrene cup is used as an insulator to reduce heat loss to the surroundings, giving a more accurate temperature reading. You should record the starting temperature, the maximum (or minimum) temperature, and calculate the change. In a well-insulated system, a bigger temperature change indicates a more energetic reaction.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Reaction Profiles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing the relative energy levels of reactants and products, and the activation energy needed to start the reaction.">reaction profile</dfn> is a diagram that shows the energy levels of the reactants and products, and the energy change during a reaction. On the vertical axis is energy; on the horizontal axis is the progress of the reaction (sometimes labelled “reaction pathway”). The diagram does not show time — it simply shows the energy changes as reactants transform into products.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">For an <strong>exothermic</strong> reaction, the products are at a <strong>lower</strong> energy level than the reactants — the energy difference has been released to the surroundings. The profile shows a “hump” from reactants rising to a peak (the activation energy barrier) then falling to a lower product level. For an <strong>endothermic</strong> reaction, the products are at a <strong>higher</strong> energy level than the reactants — energy has been absorbed. The profile shows a hump rising to the peak then settling at a level higher than the starting point. In both cases, the hump represents the <strong>activation energy</strong> — the minimum energy needed for the reaction to start. You must label four things on any reaction profile: the energy level of reactants, the energy level of products, the activation energy (from reactants to the peak), and the overall energy change (from reactants to products).</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="When drawing reaction profiles in the exam, label: reactants, products, activation energy (the 'hump'), and the overall energy change (the difference between reactant and product levels). Use arrows.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum amount of energy that reacting particles must have in order to react.">Activation energy</dfn> is the minimum energy that colliding particles need for a reaction to occur. It is shown on a reaction profile as the difference between the reactant energy level and the top of the energy barrier (the peak).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Bond Energy Calculations (Higher)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">During a chemical reaction, bonds in the reactants must be <strong>broken</strong> (this requires energy — endothermic) and new bonds in the products are <strong>made</strong> (this releases energy — exothermic). The overall energy change depends on the balance between these two processes. This is a Higher Tier calculation that requires you to use given bond energy values:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How to Calculate Energy Changes Using Bond Energies</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n13">Step 1: Add up the total energy needed to <strong>break</strong> all the bonds in the reactants. Step 2: Add up the total energy <strong>released</strong> when all the bonds in the products are formed. Step 3: Calculate the overall energy change:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$\text{energy change} = \text{energy to break bonds} - \text{energy released making bonds}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">If the answer is <strong>positive</strong>, the reaction is endothermic (more energy needed to break bonds than is released making them). If the answer is <strong>negative</strong>, the reaction is exothermic (more energy released making bonds than needed to break them).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For example, for \(H_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2HCl\): breaking bonds = H–H (436 kJ) + Cl–Cl (242 kJ) = 678 kJ. Making bonds = 2 × H–Cl (431 kJ) = 862 kJ. Energy change = 678 − 862 = −184 kJ. Negative, so exothermic. More energy was released making the H–Cl bonds than was required to break the H–H and Cl–Cl bonds, so the surplus energy is transferred to the surroundings as heat. This is why the overall reaction is exothermic. Remember: you must always consider <strong>all</strong> bonds broken and <strong>all</strong> bonds made, not just some of them.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Higher tier: 'breaking bonds = endothermic, making bonds = exothermic'. A common error is getting this the wrong way round. Think of it like this: you need to PUT IN energy to pull bonds apart.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Breaking bonds <strong>requires</strong> energy (endothermic). Making bonds <strong>releases</strong> energy (exothermic). If more energy is released making bonds than absorbed breaking them, the reaction is exothermic overall.</p>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Exothermic reactions release energy (temperature rises); endothermic reactions absorb energy (temperature falls).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Reaction profiles show energy levels of reactants and products, the activation energy barrier, and whether the overall change is exothermic or endothermic.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Bond energy calculations: overall energy change = energy to break bonds &minus; energy released making bonds. Negative = exothermic, positive = endothermic.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">For reaction profile questions, always label the axes (energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis) and draw clear arrows showing the activation energy and the overall energy change. For bond energy calculations, set out your working clearly in two columns: “bonds broken” and “bonds made.” Show the subtraction step. A negative answer = exothermic; positive = endothermic.</p>
