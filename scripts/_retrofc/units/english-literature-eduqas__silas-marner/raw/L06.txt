<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Characters in Silas Marner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Eliot’s characters serve a dual purpose: they are realistic, psychologically complex individuals and they represent broader moral positions. For the Eduqas exam, you must analyse how Eliot <em>presents</em> each character (AO2) and what they reveal about Victorian society (AO3).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Silas Marner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Silas is the novel’s moral centre. His arc — from trusting believer to betrayed exile to gold-obsessed recluse to loving father — is one of the most complete <dfn class="term" data-def="The transformation or inner journey of a character over the course of a narrative.">character arcs</dfn> in Victorian fiction. Eliot presents him sympathetically throughout. Even at his most isolated, Silas is never cruel — his withdrawal is a response to injustice, not a character flaw. His capacity for love has been damaged, not destroyed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What four stages make up Silas’s character arc?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Silas’s four-stage arc: (1) Trusting believer at Lantern Yard, (2) Betrayed and exiled, retreating into gold-hoarding isolation, (3) Gold stolen, forcing him back into contact with community, (4) Eppie arrives, transforming him into a loving father and valued community member.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Godfrey Cass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Godfrey is Silas’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose actions and choices contrast with another’s, highlighting moral differences.">moral foil</dfn>. Where Silas is open and honest (even when it costs him), Godfrey is secretive and self-serving. His defining characteristic is moral cowardice — he repeatedly chooses the easy option over the right one. He marries Molly in secret, refuses to claim Eppie, and only confesses when forced. Eliot uses Godfrey to argue that wealth and status do not make someone a good person.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Eppie is more than a passive symbol. Her choice to stay with Silas is an active moral decision. She values love, loyalty, and the community she knows over wealth and status she has never experienced. Eliot gives Eppie agency: she is not claimed by either man but makes her own choice. In a Victorian context, this is remarkable — a young working-class woman asserting her right to determine her own future.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Dunstan & Nancy Cass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Godfrey’s younger brother; a dissolute, selfish man who steals Silas’s gold and dies in the stone-pit.">Dunstan Cass</dfn> functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A morality figure representing selfishness and greed, whose fate serves as a warning.">cautionary figure</dfn>. He embodies greed, recklessness, and the sense of entitlement that comes from inherited wealth. His death in the stone-pit is both literal and symbolic — he is buried alongside the gold he stole, consumed by the very thing he pursued. Eliot shows that the Cass family’s wealth cannot protect its members from the consequences of their own moral failures.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Nancy Lammeter is the novel’s moral compass within the gentry. She is principled, honest, and dutiful. When Godfrey finally confesses about Eppie, Nancy responds with calm dignity: she is hurt but not vindictive. Her line “There’s debts we can’t pay like money debts” is one of the novel’s most important statements. Nancy represents what the gentry <em>could</em> be if they lived according to genuine moral principles rather than social convention. She accepts responsibility; Godfrey runs from it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Dolly Winthrop: Natural Goodness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Dolly is the character who most clearly embodies Eliot’s moral vision. She is uneducated, working-class, and speaks in dialect, yet she possesses a wisdom that no formal education could provide. Her theology is simple: she believes in a God who is “bigger nor what we can know,” and she trusts that doing good is its own reward. Dolly does not theologise — she acts. She brings food to Silas when he is a stranger, helps him raise Eppie, and supports him without ever asking for anything in return.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Eliot contrasts Dolly’s natural goodness with the institutional religion of Lantern Yard. The Lantern Yard congregation claimed to know God’s will through the drawing of lots, but this “knowledge” produced a terrible injustice. Dolly makes no such claims. Her humility — “us as knows so little” — is presented as truer faith than the congregation’s certainty. Through Dolly, Eliot argues that genuine morality comes from empathy and practical kindness, not from religious authority.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Molly Farren & the Absent Mother</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Molly Farren, Godfrey’s secret first wife and Eppie’s biological mother, is a significant absence in the novel. She dies in the snow on New Year’s Eve while carrying Eppie towards the Red House to expose Godfrey’s secret. Eliot presents Molly sympathetically despite her opium addiction — she is a victim of Godfrey’s cowardice and the class system that allowed him to marry her in secret and then abandon her. Her death is both a tragedy and a narrative necessity: it frees Godfrey from exposure and allows Eppie to reach Silas’s cottage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three ways Dolly Winthrop contrasts with the Lantern Yard congregation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dolly contrasts with Lantern Yard: (1) She offers kindness without judgement vs their rigid condemnation. (2) Her faith is humble and practical vs their superstitious drawing of lots. (3) She builds community through action vs their exclusion of outsiders.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Eliot uses an <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator who knows the thoughts and feelings of all characters and can comment on events from outside the story.">omniscient narrator</dfn> who frequently steps back from the action to offer moral commentary. The narrator explains characters’ motivations and invites the reader to judge them. This narrative distance allows Eliot to be sympathetic to flawed characters like Godfrey while making clear that their choices have moral consequences. The narrator’s voice also creates a sense of moral authority — the novel is not neutral about its characters but presents a clear moral argument about what constitutes a good life.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Silas’s four-stage arc shows that love can redeem even the most damaged person; Godfrey’s moral cowardice contrasts with Silas’s honesty.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Eppie has genuine agency — she chooses her own future. Dolly Winthrop represents practical, non-judgemental goodness.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The omniscient narrator provides moral commentary, guiding the reader’s judgement while maintaining sympathy for flawed characters.</li></ul>
