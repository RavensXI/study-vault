George Eliot’s context: Victorian religion, class, industrialisation, and why she wrote Silas Marner.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Was George Eliot?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The pen name of Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), a Victorian novelist who used a male pseudonym to be taken seriously as a writer.">George Eliot</dfn> was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans. She adopted a male pseudonym because Victorian society did not take female writers seriously — women’s novels were often dismissed as lightweight romances. By writing as “George Eliot,” Evans ensured her work was judged on its merits. She published <em>Silas Marner</em> in 1861, and it quickly became one of her most popular novels.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eliot was one of the most intellectually formidable Victorians. She translated German philosophy, edited the radical <em>Westminster Review</em>, and lived openly with the married writer George Henry Lewes — a relationship that scandalised Victorian society. Her personal experience of being judged and excluded gave her deep empathy for outsiders, which is central to <em>Silas Marner</em>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why did Mary Ann Evans use a male pen name, and how does this connect to the novel’s themes?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>George Eliot was really Mary Ann Evans. She used a male pen name because Victorian publishers and critics took male authors more seriously. Her experience of prejudice and exclusion informs the novel’s exploration of how communities judge and reject outsiders.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Victorian Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><em>Silas Marner</em> is set in the early 1800s but was written in 1861, during a period of massive social change. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The period from the mid-1700s to mid-1800s when Britain shifted from an agricultural economy to an industrial one, transforming work, communities, and society.">Industrial Revolution</dfn> was transforming Britain — rural communities were declining as people moved to cities for factory work. Eliot was deeply concerned about the loss of close-knit village life. She set her novel in the rural village of Raveloe precisely to explore what holds communities together and what tears them apart.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Religion was another powerful force in Victorian society. The novel features two types of religious community: the strict, <dfn class="term" data-def="A Protestant Christian group that separated from the Church of England, often characterised by strict moral codes and close-knit congregations.">Dissenting</dfn> chapel at Lantern Yard (where Silas originally lives) and the more relaxed Church of England community at Raveloe. Eliot herself had rejected organised religion after studying German theology, but she retained deep respect for the moral values religion could foster.</p>

In <em>Silas Marner</em>, the Cass family represents the rural gentry — wealthy landowners who hold power over the village. Squire Cass is a careless patriarch whose poor parenting produces two very different sons: the reckless Dunsey and the morally weak Godfrey. Silas, as a handloom weaver, occupies the bottom of this hierarchy. Eliot uses these class contrasts to show that moral worth has nothing to do with social status.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Eliot’s Intellectual Background</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Eliot was one of the most intellectually formidable Victorians. She translated the German theologian David Friedrich Strauss’s <em>Life of Jesus</em>, which argued that the Gospels were myths rather than literal history. This led her to reject organised Christianity, though she never lost her moral seriousness. Her novels explore how people find meaning and belonging without institutional religion — a question central to Silas’s journey from the rigid Dissenting chapel of Lantern Yard to the more humane community of Raveloe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Eliot’s personal life was itself an act of defiance. She lived openly with the married writer George Henry Lewes for over twenty years — a relationship that scandalised Victorian society and caused her to be shunned by respectable families. This experience of being judged and excluded gave her deep empathy for outsiders, which is central to Silas Marner. Silas is rejected by his community for something he did not do; Eliot was rejected for choosing to live according to her own moral convictions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Rural Idyll & Nostalgia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Eliot set the novel in the early 1800s but wrote it in 1861, when rural communities were rapidly disappearing under the pressure of industrialisation. The village of Raveloe is described with warmth: orchards, country lanes, communal gatherings at the Rainbow inn. But Eliot does not simply romanticise village life. She shows Raveloe’s limitations too — its suspicion of strangers, its class hierarchy led by the careless Squire Cass, and its superstitious fear of anyone different. The novel celebrates community while acknowledging its capacity for exclusion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The novel’s subtitle, “The Weaver of Raveloe,” is significant. Weaving was a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small-scale industry carried out in people’s homes rather than in factories; handloom weaving was being destroyed by mechanised factory production during the Industrial Revolution.">cottage industry</dfn> being destroyed by factory mechanisation. Silas represents the dying world of pre-industrial craftsmanship. His loom connects him to a disappearing way of life, and his isolation in Raveloe is partly the isolation of a man whose trade has been overtaken by progress. Eliot uses Silas to mourn the loss of individual skill and to question whether industrial progress truly improves human happiness.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Eliot Wrote Silas Marner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Eliot described the novel as a story that “came to me first of all, quite suddenly, as a sort of legendary tale.” She was interested in exploring how a broken, isolated person could be redeemed through human love. The central question of the novel is whether money or love brings true happiness — a question that was acutely relevant in an era of rapid industrialisation and growing <dfn class="term" data-def="The pursuit of wealth and possessions as the primary goal in life, often at the expense of human relationships.">materialism</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three key contextual factors that shaped Silas Marner.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three key contexts for the Eduqas exam: (1) The Industrial Revolution and the decline of rural communities. (2) Victorian religion: Dissenting chapels vs Church of England. (3) The rigid Victorian class system and attitudes towards outsiders. All three are explored through Silas’s journey from isolation to belonging.</p>
</div>
