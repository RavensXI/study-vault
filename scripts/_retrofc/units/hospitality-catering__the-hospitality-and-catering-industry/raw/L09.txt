--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Causes of Food-Related Ill Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Food can make people ill in four main ways: through <strong>bacteria</strong> (food poisoning), <strong>allergies</strong> (immune system reactions to specific foods), <strong>intolerances</strong> (difficulty digesting certain substances), and <strong>chemicals</strong> (contamination from cleaning products or pesticides). This lesson focuses on the specific bacteria, allergens, and intolerances named in the WJEC specification, plus the food labelling laws that help protect consumers.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Food Poisoning Bacteria</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The specification requires you to know seven specific bacteria that cause <dfn class="term" data-def="An illness caused by eating food contaminated with harmful bacteria, resulting in symptoms such as vomiting, diarrhoea, and stomach cramps.">food poisoning</dfn>. Each bacterium is found in different foods and conditions:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Seven Food Poisoning Bacteria</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Bacillus cereus</strong> — commonly found in rice, pasta, and starchy foods. It produces toxins when cooked food is left at room temperature too long. Reheating does not destroy the toxin. <strong>Campylobacter</strong> — the most common cause of food poisoning in the UK. Found in raw or undercooked poultry, unpasteurised milk, and untreated water. <strong>Clostridium perfringens</strong> — found in soil and the intestines of animals. Grows in large batches of food (stews, gravies) that are cooked slowly and not cooled quickly enough. <strong>E. coli (Escherichia coli)</strong> — found in raw or undercooked beef, unpasteurised milk, and contaminated water. Can cause very serious illness, especially in young children and the elderly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Listeria</strong> — found in soft cheeses, pâté, and pre-packaged salads. Unusual because it can grow at refrigerator temperatures. Particularly dangerous for pregnant women, as it can cause miscarriage. <strong>Salmonella</strong> — found in raw poultry, eggs, and dairy products. Destroyed by thorough cooking but can survive in undercooked food. <strong>Staphylococcus aureus</strong> — carried on human skin, especially in the nose and throat. Transferred to food through poor hand hygiene, sneezing, or touching food with infected cuts. The toxin it produces is heat-resistant, so reheating does not make the food safe.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name all seven bacteria, then write down the most common food source for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The seven food poisoning bacteria are: <strong>bacillus cereus</strong> (rice/pasta), <strong>campylobacter</strong> (poultry), <strong>clostridium perfringens</strong> (stews/gravies), <strong>E. coli</strong> (raw beef), <strong>listeria</strong> (soft cheese/pâté), <strong>salmonella</strong> (poultry/eggs), and <strong>staphylococcus aureus</strong> (transferred from human skin).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The 14 Allergens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A food <dfn class="term" data-def="An immune system reaction to a specific food protein, which can range from mild (rash, itching) to life-threatening (anaphylaxis).">allergy</dfn> is an immune system reaction to a specific food protein. Reactions can range from mild (rash, itching) to life-threatening (<strong>anaphylaxis</strong>). UK food law requires all food businesses to declare the presence of 14 specific allergens in their products:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The 14 allergens are: <strong>cereals containing gluten</strong> (wheat, rye, barley, oats), <strong>crustaceans</strong> (prawns, crab, lobster), <strong>eggs</strong>, <strong>fish</strong>, <strong>peanuts</strong>, <strong>soybeans (soya)</strong>, <strong>milk (dairy)</strong>, <strong>nuts</strong> (almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecans, Brazil nuts, pistachios, macadamia nuts), <strong>celery</strong>, <strong>mustard</strong>, <strong>sesame seeds</strong>, <strong>sulphur dioxide and sulphites</strong> (above 10mg/kg), <strong>lupin</strong>, and <strong>molluscs</strong> (mussels, oysters, squid, snails).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list all 14 allergens. Try grouping them into categories to help you recall them all.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There are <strong>14 allergens</strong> that must be declared by law. They include cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, nuts, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, sulphur dioxide/sulphites, lupin, and molluscs.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Food Intolerances</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A food <dfn class="term" data-def="Difficulty digesting a particular food substance, causing symptoms such as bloating, stomach pain, and diarrhoea, but not involving the immune system.">intolerance</dfn> is different from an allergy. It does not involve the immune system and is not life-threatening, but it can cause significant discomfort. The specification identifies four key intolerances:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Gluten intolerance (coeliac disease)</strong> — the body cannot digest gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. It damages the lining of the small intestine, causing bloating, diarrhoea, and poor nutrient absorption. <strong>Lactose intolerance</strong> — the body lacks the enzyme needed to digest lactose (the sugar in milk). Symptoms include bloating, stomach cramps, and diarrhoea after consuming dairy products. <strong>Aspartame sensitivity</strong> — aspartame is an artificial sweetener found in diet drinks and sugar-free products. Some people experience headaches, dizziness, or digestive problems after consuming it. <strong>MSG (monosodium glutamate) sensitivity</strong> — MSG is a flavour enhancer used in many processed foods and Chinese cooking. Some people report headaches, sweating, and chest tightness after consuming it.</p>

<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">An <strong>allergy</strong> involves the immune system and can be life-threatening (anaphylaxis). Even a tiny amount of the allergen can trigger a reaction. An <strong>intolerance</strong> does not involve the immune system and is not life-threatening, but causes uncomfortable symptoms like bloating, stomach pain, and diarrhoea. People with intolerances can often tolerate small amounts of the food without a serious reaction. Both require hospitality businesses to provide clear information about ingredients.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Food Labelling Laws</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">UK food labelling laws require all pre-packaged food to clearly list ingredients, highlight the 14 allergens (usually in bold or a different colour), display a use-by or best-before date, and include nutritional information. Since October 2021, <strong>Natasha’s Law</strong> (named after Natasha Ednan-Laperouse, who died from an allergic reaction to a Pret A Manger baguette) requires food prepared and packed on the same premises where it is sold (such as sandwiches made in-store) to carry a full ingredients list with allergens emphasised. Restaurants and takeaways must also be able to provide allergen information when asked, either on the menu or through trained staff.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The specification also requires you to be aware of <strong>food safety legislation</strong> and <strong>food hygiene</strong> more broadly. Food safety legislation sets the legal framework that all food businesses must follow — covering everything from how food is stored and prepared to how staff handle ingredients. Good food hygiene means following practices that prevent contamination: washing hands before and after handling food, keeping raw and cooked food separate, cleaning surfaces regularly, and ensuring food is cooked to the correct temperature. Together, labelling laws, food safety legislation, and hygiene practices form a chain of protection that keeps customers safe from food-related ill health.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Seven named bacteria cause food poisoning: bacillus cereus, campylobacter, clostridium perfringens, E. coli, listeria, salmonella, and staphylococcus aureus — each linked to specific foods.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">There are 14 allergens that must be declared by law, and four named food intolerances (gluten, lactose, aspartame, MSG). Allergies involve the immune system and can be fatal; intolerances cause discomfort but are not life-threatening.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Food labelling laws (including Natasha’s Law) require clear allergen information on all food products, whether pre-packaged or prepared on-site.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When asked to identify food poisoning bacteria, always match the bacterium to its <strong>food source</strong> — for example, “campylobacter, commonly found in raw or undercooked poultry.” For allergen questions, remember the number <strong>14</strong> and group them logically (grains, seafood, dairy, nuts, others). The difference between allergy and intolerance is a common exam question — always mention that allergies involve the immune system and can cause anaphylaxis, while intolerances do not.</p>
