<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Hospitality & Catering?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The hospitality and catering industry is one of the largest sectors in the UK economy, employing around 2.9 million people and generating £130 billion in economic activity. It is the third-largest private sector employer — bigger than automotive, pharmaceuticals, and aerospace combined. To revise this topic effectively, you need to understand the difference between <dfn class="term" data-def="A hospitality or catering business that aims to make a profit from the services it provides.">commercial</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A hospitality or catering operation that does not aim to make a profit; it provides food or accommodation as part of another service.">non-commercial</dfn> provision, and the difference between residential and non-residential services.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Draw a 2x2 grid from memory: Commercial/Non-commercial across the top, Residential/Non-residential down the side. Fill in two examples per box without looking.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Hospitality and catering provision is split into four categories: <strong>commercial residential</strong>, <strong>commercial non-residential</strong>, <strong>non-commercial residential</strong>, and <strong>non-commercial non-residential</strong>. Commercial means the business aims to make a profit. Non-commercial means the service is provided without a profit motive.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Commercial Residential Provision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A hospitality business that provides overnight accommodation alongside other services such as meals.">Residential provision</dfn> means overnight accommodation is included. Commercial residential providers aim to make a profit from guests who stay overnight. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Hotels, motels and hostels</strong> — ranging from budget chains like Travelodge to luxury five-star hotels.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>B&Bs, guest houses and Airbnb</strong> — smaller-scale accommodation, often family-run or privately owned.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Holiday parks, lodges, pods and cabins</strong> — self-catering or serviced accommodation in leisure settings.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Campsites and caravan parks</strong> — outdoor accommodation with catering facilities on site.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Cruise ships</strong> — floating hotels with restaurants, bars, and entertainment.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The range of commercial residential providers is broad. At one end, budget hotels like Travelodge and Premier Inn offer standardised rooms at low prices, relying on high occupancy to generate profit. At the other end, luxury boutique hotels and five-star resorts offer personalised service, fine dining, and exclusive facilities — charging significantly more per night but serving fewer guests. Airbnb has disrupted the traditional market by allowing private homeowners to rent out spare rooms or entire properties, creating competition for established hotels and B&Bs.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Commercial Non-Residential Provision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Commercial non-residential providers sell food, drink, or entertainment without offering overnight stays. Their main goal is still profit. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Restaurants and bistros</strong>, <strong>cafés, tea rooms and coffee shops</strong></li>
<li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Fast food outlets</strong> and <strong>takeaways</strong></li>
<li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Public houses and bars</strong></li>
<li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Pop-up restaurants</strong> and <strong>mobile food vans / street food trucks</strong></li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Airlines and long-distance trains</strong> (in-flight or on-board catering)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Stadia, concert halls and tourist attractions</strong> (food concessions)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Vending machines</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Non-Commercial Residential Provision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Non-commercial residential providers offer accommodation and meals, but making a profit is not the primary aim. The food and shelter are provided as part of a broader service. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Hospitals, hospices and care homes</strong> — patients and residents need meals as part of their care.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Boarding schools, colleges and university residences</strong> — students live on site and are provided meals.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Prisons</strong> — inmates receive meals as part of their custodial care.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Armed forces</strong> — service personnel are housed and fed during training and deployment.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n27">In all non-commercial residential settings, the quality of catering matters significantly even though profit is not the goal. Hospitals must provide nutritious meals that support patient recovery, and they must cater for a wide range of dietary requirements including allergies, religious preferences, and medical conditions such as diabetes. Boarding schools must provide balanced meals for growing adolescents, often serving hundreds of students three times a day. The cost of this catering is covered by the institution’s overall budget, government funding, or fees charged for the wider service.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Non-Commercial Non-Residential Provision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">These providers offer food without overnight accommodation and without aiming to make a profit. The food is subsidised or provided free of charge. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>School, college and university canteens</strong></li>
<li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Canteens in workplaces</strong> (subsidised by the employer)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Meals on wheels</strong> (delivered to people in their homes)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Charity-run food providers</strong> (e.g. food banks, soup kitchens)</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Commercial</strong> providers charge customers for their services and aim to make a profit. They include hotels, restaurants, and takeaways. <strong>Non-commercial</strong> providers do not aim to make a profit. Their food or accommodation is part of a wider service — for example, a hospital feeds patients to support their recovery, not to earn money. Non-commercial catering is often subsidised by the government, a charity, or an employer.</p>
</div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n35">Standards and Ratings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n36">Hotels and guest houses are rated using <strong>star ratings</strong> from 1 to 5 stars. A one-star hotel offers basic accommodation, while a five-star hotel provides luxury facilities and exceptional service. These ratings help customers choose where to stay and set expectations for quality.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n37" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three restaurant rating systems. For each, state how many levels it has.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Restaurants can be rated by the <strong>AA Rosette Award</strong> (1–5 rosettes for food quality), the <strong>Michelin Star</strong> system (1–3 stars for exceptional dining), and the <strong>Good Food Guide</strong>. These ratings influence customer choice and can significantly boost a restaurant’s reputation and revenue.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Do Ratings Matter?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n38">Ratings give customers confidence in the quality of service they will receive. A higher rating often means a business can charge higher prices, attracting wealthier clientele. For the business, achieving a Michelin star or AA rosettes brings prestige, media attention, and increased bookings. However, maintaining high standards is expensive — staff must be highly trained, ingredients must be top quality, and facilities must be immaculate. Losing a star can damage a restaurant’s reputation significantly.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n41">Hospitality and catering provision is divided into four categories based on whether it is commercial or non-commercial, and residential or non-residential.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">Commercial providers aim to make a profit (hotels, restaurants, takeaways); non-commercial providers supply food or accommodation as part of another service (hospitals, schools, armed forces).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n43">Star ratings (hotels), AA Rosettes, Michelin Stars, and the Good Food Guide help customers judge quality and influence business success.</li></ul>
