--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Mao's 1949 victory and the Korean War widened the Cold War from Europe into Asia.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Year That Changed the Map</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">1949 was a hinge year for the Cold War in Asia. On 1 October the Chinese Communist Party, after twenty-two years of civil war and resistance to Japan, proclaimed the People&rsquo;s Republic of China in Beijing. The Nationalist forces of Chiang Kai-shek retreated to the island of Taiwan. A few weeks earlier, in late August 1949, the USSR had tested its first atomic bomb, ending the American monopoly. From a Western perspective the world had shifted twice in two months: the largest country in Asia had turned communist, and the USSR could now reach back at the USA in kind.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when did Mao proclaim the People&rsquo;s Republic of China, where did the Nationalists go, and which 1949 Soviet test ended the US atomic monopoly?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mao Tse-tung proclaimed the People&rsquo;s Republic of China on 1 October 1949. Chiang Kai-shek&rsquo;s defeated Nationalists retreated to Taiwan. The USSR&rsquo;s first atomic bomb test, in August 1949, ended the four-year US atomic monopoly and convinced Western leaders that a global communist bloc was real.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Sino-Soviet Treaty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In February 1950 Mao and Stalin signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1950 alliance between the People&rsquo;s Republic of China and the USSR, pledging mutual defence and providing Chinese access to Soviet loans, equipment and technical advisers.">Sino-Soviet Treaty</dfn> of Friendship, Alliance and Mutual Assistance. The treaty pledged each side to help the other in case of attack, and gave Mao $300 million in Soviet loans together with technical assistance to rebuild Chinese industry. The two leaders did not entirely trust each other &mdash; Stalin had been slow to support the Chinese Communists during the civil war, and Mao resented being treated as a junior partner &mdash; but the treaty turned a regional defeat for the Nationalists into a global problem for the West. To US planners, China and the USSR now looked like a single communist bloc stretching from East Berlin to the Yellow Sea.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The political consequences in Washington were immediate. The Truman administration, criticised at home for &lsquo;losing China&rsquo;, drew up the National Security Council document NSC-68 in April 1950. The paper called for a massive increase in US defence spending, the buildup of conventional and nuclear forces, and active opposition to communism wherever it appeared. NSC-68 was a planning document, not yet policy &mdash; but two months later a war in Korea would turn its recommendations into reality.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Stalin&rsquo;s support for Mao Tse-tung had been patchy through the long Chinese civil war. He worked with the Chinese Nationalists during the Second World War, gave Mao only limited Soviet supplies, and as late as 1945 told the Communist leadership it could not win. After 1945 the picture changed. Soviet forces handed captured Japanese weapons to Mao&rsquo;s armies in Manchuria, and after Mao&rsquo;s clear advance in 1948 Stalin pivoted to full backing. The lesson Western leaders drew was unhelpful for nuance: rather than seeing the Sino-Soviet relationship as a complicated bargain between two communist powers with different interests, Washington framed it as a single coordinated threat. That framing would shape US decisions in Korea, Indochina and beyond.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Korea: How the War Began</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Korea had been split at the 38th parallel in 1945 between Soviet occupation in the north and US occupation in the south. By 1948 each side had its own government &mdash; Kim Il-Sung&rsquo;s communist <dfn class="term" data-def="The communist northern half of Korea, established in 1948 with Soviet backing under Kim Il-Sung, formally the Democratic People&rsquo;s Republic of Korea.">North Korea</dfn> and Syngman Rhee&rsquo;s authoritarian South Korea &mdash; and both leaders wanted to reunify the peninsula on their own terms. On 25 June 1950, North Korean forces attacked across the 38th parallel in a coordinated invasion. Stalin had given Kim approval (without committing Soviet troops); Mao had quietly endorsed it. Within three days Seoul had fallen, and the South Korean army was in retreat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The UN Security Council met the same day. The Soviet representative was boycotting in protest at the UN&rsquo;s refusal to recognise Mao&rsquo;s government as China&rsquo;s legitimate seat &mdash; an absence that meant Moscow could not veto the Western response. The Security Council passed Resolution 82 condemning the invasion, then Resolution 83 calling for armed assistance to South Korea, and on 7 July Resolution 84 authorising a unified UN command under American leadership. General Douglas MacArthur, the US commander in occupied Japan, was given the role.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">From Pusan to the Yalu and Back</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By August 1950 the South Korean and US forces were holding only a small bridgehead around the southern port of Pusan. On 15 September MacArthur launched an audacious amphibious landing at Inchon, far behind enemy lines. The North Korean army collapsed; UN forces drove northward, taking the capital Pyongyang on 19 October. The original UN aim had been to restore the 38th parallel; now MacArthur kept advancing toward the Yalu River on the Chinese border. Mao, fearing a US presence on his frontier, sent perhaps 250,000 Chinese &lsquo;volunteers&rsquo; into the war in late October 1950. The UN forces were thrown back; Pyongyang and then Seoul fell to communist forces again early in 1951.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A Western counter-offensive recovered Seoul in March 1951, and by mid-1951 the front had stabilised close to the original 38th parallel. The fighting, however, did not stop until July 1953. Stalin&rsquo;s death in March 1953 unblocked stalled negotiations, and an armistice was finally signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953. The line between North and South Korea remained almost where it had been three years earlier &mdash; but at the cost of approximately three million Korean civilians, hundreds of thousands of Chinese troops, and around 36,500 Americans.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">The Korean War turned NSC-68 from a paper into US policy. Defence spending tripled. NATO acquired an integrated military command and West Germany began to be considered for membership. The US signed mutual defence treaties with Japan (1951), the Philippines (1951) and the ANZUS countries (1951), establishing a chain of <dfn class="term" data-def="The post-1945 American policy of preventing communism from spreading further, used here in the broad sense of a worldwide commitment shaped by the Korean War.">containment</dfn> in Asia parallel to the one in Europe. The conflict also confirmed in Western minds that the communist bloc was willing to use force to expand &mdash; a lesson that would shape decisions on Indochina (the next lesson), the arms race and Western alliances for the next decade.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>North Korea invaded the South on 25 June 1950. UN forces under MacArthur landed at Inchon on 15 September 1950. Chinese forces entered the war in late October 1950 and threw the UN back to the 38th parallel. The armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Mao Tse-tung&rsquo;s victory in October 1949 and the August 1949 Soviet atomic test convinced Western leaders that the Cold War was now global, not just European.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Korean War (1950&ndash;53) was the first hot war of the Cold War &mdash; triggered by the North Korean invasion of 25 June 1950, transformed by Chinese intervention in October 1950, and ended by the Panmunjom armistice on 27 July 1953.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The war turned NSC-68 from a planning paper into policy: US defence spending tripled, NATO acquired a real military structure and the USA built a chain of alliances around the Pacific to contain communism in Asia.</li></ul>
