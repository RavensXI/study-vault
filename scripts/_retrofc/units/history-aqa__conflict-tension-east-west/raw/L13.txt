--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Czechoslovakia in the Late 1960s</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the mid-1960s Czechoslovakia was the most industrially advanced state in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Eastern Bloc &mdash; the Soviet Union and the satellite communist states of central and eastern Europe under Soviet control after 1945.">Eastern Bloc</dfn> but its economy was stalling. Centralised planning starved the country&rsquo;s factories of investment, consumer goods were scarce, and intellectuals were increasingly impatient with censorship. Antonin Novotny &mdash; a hardline Stalinist installed in the 1950s &mdash; faced rising criticism within his own party. In January 1968 he was replaced as First Secretary of the Communist Party by <dfn class="term" data-def="Czechoslovak Communist leader who introduced &lsquo;socialism with a human face&rsquo; reforms in 1968; removed after the Soviet invasion that August.">Alexander Dubcek</dfn>, a Slovak reformer who promised &lsquo;socialism with a human face&rsquo;.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who became Czechoslovak leader in January 1968, what phrase summed up his reforms, and what does the phrase mean?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alexander Dubcek became Czechoslovak Communist Party leader in January 1968. His reform programme &mdash; &lsquo;socialism with a human face&rsquo; &mdash; aimed to keep one-party rule and Warsaw Pact membership but to end censorship, allow economic reform and rebuild trust between citizens and the state.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Prague Spring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A common misconception is that Dubcek wanted to leave the Eastern Bloc. He did not. The Action Programme published in April 1968 promised liberalisation within communism, not its abandonment. It abolished censorship and let independent newspapers flourish; it released political prisoners; it began to decentralise economic planning; it allowed open debate of past Stalinist abuses. For four months &mdash; the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of liberalising reforms in Czechoslovakia between January and August 1968, ended by the Soviet-led invasion.">Prague Spring</dfn> &mdash; Czechoslovakia experienced something unique inside the bloc: a public sphere where genuine debate took place. Radio Prague carried discussions that would have been unthinkable a year earlier. Czech and Slovak citizens cautiously believed that reform without rupture was possible.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Moscow was alarmed. Brezhnev feared a chain reaction: if the Czech experiment succeeded, reformers in Poland, East Germany and elsewhere would press for the same. Through the summer of 1968 a series of crisis meetings took place. At Cierna nad Tisou in late July, Dubcek reassured Brezhnev that one-party rule and the Warsaw Pact were not in question. The reassurance did not satisfy Moscow. East German leader Walter Ulbricht and Polish leader Wladyslaw Gomulka pressed Brezhnev to act.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Operation Danube, 20-21 August 1968</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the early hours of 21 August 1968 around 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops &mdash; from the USSR, Poland, Hungary, Bulgaria and East Germany &mdash; crossed into Czechoslovakia. Soviet aircraft seized Prague airport; tanks rolled into the city. Romania refused to send troops, and the GDR&rsquo;s contribution was symbolic to avoid awakening 1939 memories. Dubcek and the leadership were arrested overnight and flown to Moscow.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Resistance Without War</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">There was no general armed resistance &mdash; the Czechoslovak army was ordered to stay in barracks. Instead, Czechs and Slovaks resisted symbolically. Street signs were turned around to confuse incoming troops; underground radio stations broadcast for days; in January 1969 the student Jan Palach set himself on fire in Wenceslas Square in protest at the loss of public hope. Around 100 Czechoslovaks died during the invasion week. Dubcek was forced to sign the Moscow Protocols accepting Soviet demands and was demoted within a year. The hardline Gustav Husak replaced him as First Secretary in April 1969 and oversaw twenty years of &lsquo;normalisation&rsquo;.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Around 70,000 Czechoslovaks fled west during 1968-69. Western governments protested but did not act &mdash; the geographical and military logic that had immobilised them in Hungary applied again. NATO confirmed it would not intervene in the Soviet sphere.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Brezhnev Doctrine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In November 1968 Brezhnev set out the principle behind the invasion in a speech to the Polish United Workers&rsquo; Party Congress. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet doctrine declared by Brezhnev in November 1968: socialist states could not permit reforms that threatened socialism, and other socialist states had a duty to intervene.">Brezhnev Doctrine</dfn> declared that any threat to socialism in any socialist state was a threat to all, and that the Warsaw Pact had the right and duty to intervene. The doctrine formalised what had happened in Czechoslovakia and stood as a Soviet warning to any future reformer in the bloc. It would govern Soviet policy until Mikhail Gorbachev quietly abandoned it in 1988-89.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Effect on Cold War Relations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The invasion damaged the Soviet position in Western Europe. Communist parties in Italy and France condemned the action; many Western Marxists abandoned their loyalties; Yugoslavia and Romania pointedly stayed out of the operation, weakening Warsaw Pact unity. Yet paradoxically the invasion did not derail superpower diplomacy: by stabilising the bloc, the Brezhnev Doctrine arguably made the Soviet leadership feel secure enough to negotiate with the United States. Within four years Brezhnev would sign SALT 1 with President Nixon. The Prague Spring had ended, but the path to d&eacute;tente was not closed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">For the bloc itself the long-run damage was harder to measure but more serious. After 1968 the official ideology had to admit that &lsquo;socialism with a human face&rsquo; was not allowed even when the country involved remained loyal to the alliance. Generations of younger reformers concluded that change inside the system was impossible, and turned to either silent emigration, dissident protest, or simple cynicism. When the bloc finally collapsed in 1989, the speed of its fall surprised most Western observers but not those who remembered Prague: by then the regime had no defenders left who genuinely believed in its renewal.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: invasion date, country leading invasion, country refusing to send troops, principle declared in November 1968.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 20-21 August 1968 around 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops invaded Czechoslovakia. Romania refused to take part. In November 1968 Brezhnev declared the Brezhnev Doctrine, which became the Soviet rule book for Eastern Europe until 1989.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Alexander Dubcek became leader in January 1968 and launched &lsquo;socialism with a human face&rsquo; &mdash; reform within the bloc, not departure from it &mdash; through the Action Programme of April 1968.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">On 20-21 August 1968 around 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops crushed the Prague Spring; Czechoslovakia did not resist militarily, but symbolic resistance and the suicide of Jan Palach in 1969 captured the loss of hope.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">In November 1968 Brezhnev declared the Brezhnev Doctrine &mdash; socialist states could not be allowed to slide back &mdash; which governed Soviet policy until 1989, even as it did not prevent superpower d&eacute;tente from beginning the same year.</li></ul>
