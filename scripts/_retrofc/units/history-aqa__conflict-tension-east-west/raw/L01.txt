--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the wartime alliance fractured at Yalta and Potsdam, and the seeds of postwar division.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Alliance Under Strain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By February 1945 the defeat of Nazi Germany was only weeks away. The Big Three &mdash; Joseph Stalin of the USSR, Franklin D. Roosevelt of the USA and Winston Churchill of Britain &mdash; met at the Crimean resort of Yalta to plan the peace. They had fought as allies for almost four years, but their interests had never truly aligned. Stalin wanted permanent security on his western border after losing perhaps 27 million Soviet citizens. Roosevelt wanted the USSR inside a new United Nations and willing to help finish the war against Japan. Churchill wanted to limit Soviet influence in Europe before the Red Army turned its conquest of Eastern Europe into political control.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when did Yalta and Potsdam take place, and which leader represented each of the three Allies at each conference?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Yalta Conference ran from 4&ndash;11 February 1945 and the Potsdam Conference from 17 July to 2 August 1945. Roosevelt died in April 1945, so by Potsdam Truman had replaced him; Churchill lost the British general election mid-conference and was replaced by Clement Attlee.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">What Yalta Agreed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Yalta produced a surprising amount of agreement on paper. Germany would be split into four zones of occupation, run by Britain, France, the USA and the USSR, with Berlin &mdash; deep inside the Soviet zone &mdash; also divided four ways. Nazi war criminals would be put on trial. The USSR pledged to enter the war against Japan within three months of Germany&rsquo;s surrender, in return for territorial concessions in the Far East. The Big Three signed a <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1945 Yalta pledge that liberated peoples of Europe would be allowed to choose their own governments through free elections.">Declaration on Liberated Europe</dfn> promising free elections in the countries freed from Nazi rule.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The hardest issue was Poland. The USSR had already installed a friendly communist government, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The pro-Soviet Polish provisional government installed by Stalin in 1944, named after the city where it was first based.">Lublin Committee</dfn>, while Polish exiles in London claimed legitimacy. The compromise was to broaden the Lublin government with a few non-communists and call it provisional, with elections to follow. Poland&rsquo;s borders shifted west: it lost territory to the USSR but gained land from defeated Germany. Stalin viewed this as a permanent buffer; Churchill viewed it as a temporary arrangement that genuine elections would correct.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">From Yalta to Potsdam: Mood Shift</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Five months changed everything. Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945; his successor Harry S. Truman was less inclined to trust Stalin and more willing to confront him. Germany surrendered on 8 May. By the time the Big Three reconvened at Potsdam, just outside ruined Berlin, Soviet troops occupied most of Eastern Europe and showed no sign of leaving. On 16 July, the day before Potsdam opened, the USA secretly tested the world&rsquo;s first atomic bomb in the New Mexico desert. Truman mentioned the existence of a powerful new weapon to Stalin in passing &mdash; Stalin, alerted by Soviet spies, was less surprised than Truman expected.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Britain held a general election on 5 July 1945, but the count was delayed for three weeks to allow servicemen&rsquo;s votes to be returned. Churchill flew to Potsdam on 17 July believing he would still be Prime Minister when the conference closed. The result, declared on 26 July, was a Labour landslide. Churchill resigned the same day and Clement Attlee took his place at the negotiating table on 28 July. The change made little practical difference to British policy &mdash; Attlee had attended Yalta as deputy and broadly shared Churchill&rsquo;s suspicion of Soviet intentions &mdash; but it underlined Stalin&rsquo;s point that democracies were unstable allies.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">What Potsdam Decided &mdash; and What It Did Not</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Potsdam confirmed the four-zone occupation of Germany and Berlin. It set out the &lsquo;Four Ds&rsquo; for occupied Germany: <dfn class="term" data-def="The Potsdam policy of removing Nazis from public life, dismantling the Nazi Party and prosecuting war criminals.">denazification</dfn>, demilitarisation, democratisation and decentralisation. Reparations would be drawn from each occupier&rsquo;s zone, with the Soviets allowed to take additional industrial equipment from the Western zones in exchange for food shipments from their agricultural east. The German-Polish border moved to the Oder-Neisse line, even though final borders were supposed to wait for a peace treaty.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Beneath the agreements, the disputes were sharper. Truman pressed for free elections across Eastern Europe under the Yalta declaration; Stalin replied that Soviet security required friendly governments and that the West had not held elections in liberated Italy. The atom bomb sharpened the gap. On 26 July the USA, Britain and Nationalist China issued the Potsdam Declaration demanding Japanese surrender; nine days after the conference closed, Hiroshima was destroyed. The Soviet entry into the war against Japan, agreed at Yalta as essential, suddenly looked optional.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Stalin demanded $20 billion in reparations from Germany, half for the USSR. Truman refused a fixed figure, remembering how the harsh reparations after 1919 had wrecked the Weimar economy. The Potsdam compromise &mdash; each occupier strips their own zone &mdash; looked workable but planted a seed of division. The Soviets took factories, livestock and machinery from the east; the West rebuilt the industrial Ruhr. Within two years the two halves of Germany were on visibly different economic paths, and the cross-zone food-for-equipment exchange had collapsed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why Historians Disagree About Origins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">From the start, observers asked who turned the wartime alliance into <dfn class="term" data-def="The decades-long political and ideological rivalry between the USA and USSR after 1945, fought through proxy wars, propaganda and arms competition rather than direct combat.">Cold War</dfn> rivalry. Some argue Stalin&rsquo;s expansion in Eastern Europe was the cause; some argue Truman&rsquo;s atom-bomb diplomacy was; some argue neither side &lsquo;started&rsquo; it because the structures of the postwar world &mdash; two surviving great powers with opposed ideologies &mdash; made conflict almost certain. Examiners do not expect students to name these schools of thought, but the strongest answers recognise that Yalta and Potsdam can be read as the moment ideological differences crystallised into rival policies, rather than as a story with a single villain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three concrete things Potsdam decided about Germany and one issue where Truman and Stalin clearly disagreed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Potsdam confirmed the four-zone occupation, the &lsquo;Four Ds&rsquo; policy and zone-based reparations, but exposed deep splits over free elections in Eastern Europe and the meaning of the new American atom bomb. The conference is often described as the moment the alliance turned into a rivalry that had not yet been named.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Yalta in February 1945 produced public agreement on dividing Germany, free elections in Eastern Europe and Soviet entry into the war against Japan, but glossed over fundamental disagreements about Poland.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Roosevelt&rsquo;s death, the atom-bomb test and the visible Soviet hold on Eastern Europe meant Potsdam in July 1945 was a sharper, more suspicious meeting between leaders who no longer shared a common enemy.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The two conferences crystallised the structures of postwar Europe &mdash; the four zones of Germany, the Oder-Neisse border, the &lsquo;Four Ds&rsquo; &mdash; while exposing the ideological gap that would harden into the Cold War.</li></ul>
