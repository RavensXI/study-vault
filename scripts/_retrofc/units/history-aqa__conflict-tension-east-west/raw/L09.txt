<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Thaw That Raised Hopes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Joseph Stalin died in March 1953, the East European satellites felt the change quickly. Stalin&rsquo;s successor Nikita Khrushchev cautiously loosened control, and in February 1956 he delivered a closed &lsquo;Secret Speech&rsquo; to the 20th Party Congress denouncing Stalin&rsquo;s purges and personality cult. The text leaked within months. Across the Eastern Bloc, reformers and workers concluded that the rules had changed and that demands for greater independence might now be tolerated. This brief easing was known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The relaxation of Stalinist control across the Eastern Bloc after 1953, particularly under Khrushchev, which seemed to allow more open political life.">Thaw</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who delivered the Secret Speech, in what year, and why did it raise hopes in Eastern Europe?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Khrushchev&rsquo;s Secret Speech of February 1956 attacked Stalin&rsquo;s purges and personality cult. Once the speech leaked, reformers across Eastern Europe believed Soviet control might soften, encouraging demands for change in Poland and Hungary the same year.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Hungary Before the Uprising</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Hungary in the early 1950s was governed by Matyas Rakosi, a hardline Stalinist whose secret police imprisoned tens of thousands. The economy was geared to Soviet needs: cheap food and raw materials flowed east; Hungarian wages stagnated; consumer goods were scarce. The state-imposed presence of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1955 Soviet-led military alliance of Eastern European states; Hungary was a founding member.">Warsaw Pact</dfn> bound Hungary into Soviet defence planning, and Soviet troops were stationed on its soil. After the Secret Speech, Khrushchev quietly forced Rakosi out in July 1956, but his replacement, Erno Gero, was equally unpopular.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Encouragement came from next door. In June 1956, workers in Poznan, Poland, struck against living standards. By October the Polish reformer Wladyslaw Gomulka had taken power and reached a compromise with Moscow that allowed limited reforms within the bloc. Hungarian students and workers asked: if Poland can do this, why not Hungary?</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Days of Revolution, 23 October to 4 November 1956</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">On 23 October 1956 Hungarian students marched in Budapest to demand free elections, an end to censorship, the withdrawal of Soviet troops and the return of <dfn class="term" data-def="Hungarian reformist Communist; Prime Minister during the 1956 Uprising; executed in 1958 after his deposition.">Imre Nagy</dfn>, a moderate Communist sacked in 1955. The crowd toppled a giant statue of Stalin. Security forces opened fire. Within days the secret police were in retreat, Soviet troops in Budapest had pulled back, and Nagy returned as Prime Minister.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Nagy moved quickly to meet popular demands: he ended one-party rule, freed political prisoners and prepared multi-party elections. On 1 November he announced that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact and become a neutral state. This was the line Khrushchev could not cross. Allowing Hungary to leave the alliance would have set a precedent that could unravel Soviet control of Eastern Europe.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In the early hours of 4 November, around 200,000 Soviet troops and 2,500 tanks rolled back into Budapest. Hungarian fighters &mdash; many of them teenagers with petrol bombs &mdash; resisted street by street, but the outcome was never in doubt. Roughly 2,500 Hungarians and around 700 Soviet soldiers were killed in the fighting. The new puppet leader Janos Kadar took control. Imre Nagy took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy, was lured out under safe-conduct, then arrested, tried in secret and executed in June 1958.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">By the end of 1956 around 200,000 Hungarians had fled west. The Cold War lesson was harsh: Soviet tanks would still decide what was permissible inside the bloc, no matter what the Secret Speech had said.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why Did the West Not Intervene?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A common misconception is that the West actively encouraged the uprising and was caught off-guard when the Soviets struck back. In reality, US rhetoric of &lsquo;rolling back communism&rsquo; was loud, but action was never likely. Three factors pinned Western governments down. First, the simultaneous Suez Crisis: on 29 October Britain, France and Israel invaded Egypt, splitting the Western alliance and absorbing diplomatic attention. Second, fear of nuclear war: any military move into Hungary risked direct conflict with the Soviet Union. Third, geography: Hungary sat firmly inside the Soviet sphere agreed at Yalta. Radio Free Europe broadcasts spoke of liberation; UN debates condemned Moscow; but no troops moved.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The crackdown shocked Western Communists: tens of thousands of party members in France, Italy and Britain tore up their cards in protest. Within the bloc, the lesson cut both ways &mdash; reformers learned that limited change might be tolerated (as in Poland) but that leaving the Warsaw Pact would not. The Soviet Union held the bloc together but lost much of its remaining moral authority among Western intellectuals. The pattern of crisis-then-crackdown would be repeated twelve years later in Czechoslovakia. Inside Hungary, the Kadar regime balanced repression with cautious economic reform: in 1968 it introduced the &lsquo;New Economic Mechanism&rsquo; allowing limited market pricing. Many Hungarians later remembered Kadar&rsquo;s &lsquo;goulash communism&rsquo; as the most tolerable version of the bloc &mdash; but the foundations were laid in November 1956 by the tanks that arrived to enforce them. Western policy too was shaped: future US presidents would speak more cautiously about &lsquo;rolling back&rsquo; Soviet power, recognising that the rhetoric had outrun the willingness to act.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three reasons the West did not intervene in Hungary in November 1956 &mdash; one diplomatic, one military, one geographical.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Soviet invasion of 4 November 1956 used 200,000 troops and 2,500 tanks. Around 2,500 Hungarians died, 200,000 fled west, and Imre Nagy was executed in 1958. The West condemned the action but did not intervene, partly because the Suez Crisis had divided Britain, France and the United States.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Khrushchev&rsquo;s Secret Speech of February 1956 raised hopes of reform in Eastern Europe; Poland&rsquo;s compromise that summer encouraged Hungarian students and workers to test the limits.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Imre Nagy&rsquo;s reforms went beyond what Moscow would tolerate &mdash; ending one-party rule was risky, but announcing departure from the Warsaw Pact on 1 November forced Khrushchev&rsquo;s hand.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The West condemned but did not intervene because Suez had split the alliance, nuclear war was unthinkable, and Hungary lay inside the Soviet sphere agreed at Yalta &mdash; the Cold War lesson was that limits inside the bloc would be enforced by Soviet tanks.</li></ul>
