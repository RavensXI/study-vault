--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan turned containment into policy and split Europe in two.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Crisis That Pushed Truman to Speak</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By February 1947 the British government was bankrupt. Coal shortages, a brutal winter and the cost of empire meant Britain could no longer fund the Greek government in its civil war against communist guerrillas, or maintain the British military presence in Turkey. On 21 February the British ambassador delivered a blunt note in Washington: from 31 March, support for Greece and Turkey would end. Officials in the State Department recognised the moment immediately. If Greece fell to the communists, Turkey would be next; if Turkey fell, the Soviet navy would have access to the Mediterranean and oil-rich states beyond.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when did Truman announce his doctrine, which two countries triggered it, and what did Britain&rsquo;s February 1947 note say?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Truman announced his doctrine to Congress on 12 March 1947. It was triggered by Britain&rsquo;s February note that it could no longer support Greece and Turkey, and it framed US foreign policy around the choice between &lsquo;two ways of life&rsquo;: democracy and free institutions, or oppression and control.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Truman Doctrine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In his speech to Congress on 12 March 1947, Truman asked for $400 million of military and economic aid for Greece and Turkey. The numbers mattered, but the language mattered more. Truman declared: &lsquo;It must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.&rsquo; This was a permanent commitment, known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1947 US pledge to support free peoples resisting communist takeover, marking a permanent shift toward an active anti-Soviet foreign policy.">Truman Doctrine</dfn>, to oppose the spread of communism wherever it appeared. Inside the US government, this approach was already known by a name: <dfn class="term" data-def="The US policy of preventing the spread of communism beyond the territory it already held, by economic aid, alliances and, where necessary, force.">containment</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Containment did not aim to roll back Soviet power in countries already controlled by Moscow. The point was to draw a line. Where the line ran would be tested over the next four decades, but Greece and Turkey were the first two stones in the wall. By the end of 1949, with US aid and reorganised forces, Greek government troops had defeated the communist guerrillas. Turkey had stabilised. The doctrine had passed its first test.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">George Kennan&rsquo;s Long Telegram in February 1946, and his July 1947 article in Foreign Affairs signed &lsquo;X&rsquo;, supplied the intellectual case for containment. Kennan argued that the USSR was driven by an internal need for hostile encirclement and would back down only when met with &lsquo;long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment&rsquo;. Truman&rsquo;s speechwriters borrowed the framing but stripped out Kennan&rsquo;s reservations. Kennan himself later complained that his policy had been globalised into a crusade he had not intended &mdash; a useful warning that policies and the speeches that announce them are not always the same thing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">From Doctrine to Dollars: The Marshall Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Three months after the Truman speech, US Secretary of State George Marshall stood at Harvard on 5 June 1947 and offered something more ambitious. Europe&rsquo;s economies were broken. Hunger and unemployment, Marshall argued, were a breeding ground for political extremism. The USA would provide large-scale economic aid to any European country that asked &mdash; on condition that the recipients meet together to plan how to use it. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The US programme of economic aid to Western Europe between 1948 and 1952, formally the European Recovery Program.">Marshall Plan</dfn> ran from 1948 to 1952 and delivered roughly $13 billion to sixteen European countries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Marshall was careful at Harvard to extend the offer to the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe. Stalin understood the trap. Accepting Marshall aid would mean opening Soviet economies to American inspection, attaching Eastern Europe to a US-led programme and undermining the case for Soviet protection. He refused, and forbade Czechoslovakia and Poland &mdash; both of which had begun to negotiate &mdash; from accepting either. The plan therefore did exactly what its critics in Moscow expected: it tied the recipients to the West and divided Europe in two.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Why It Worked &mdash; and Why Stalin Saw a Threat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Marshall aid was political as well as economic. The money rebuilt Western European industry, restored trade between countries that had spent six years at war, and gave moderate parties in France and Italy something concrete to point to against rising communist parties. By 1952 industrial output in the recipient countries was about a third higher than before the war. The plan also paid for the Allied zones of Germany to recover, which Stalin read as the rebuilding of a future enemy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">From the Soviet view, the plan was &lsquo;dollar imperialism&rsquo;: the USA buying influence with money the USSR could not match. The Soviet response, Cominform, would follow in September 1947 (covered in the next lesson), but the deeper damage was that the door of cooperation closed. After Marshall, the wartime alliance was no longer just strained &mdash; it had been replaced by a system in which each side built parallel institutions for its own bloc.</p>

    <span>Two Ways of Life: The Wider Argument</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The phrase &lsquo;two ways of life&rsquo; became central to American Cold War thinking. Truman insisted that economic aid would not work without political confidence: hungry people in 1947 did not believe in democracy because it tasted like freedom; they believed in whichever system kept them fed. The Marshall Plan was therefore not separate from the Truman Doctrine but its economic engine. Together they framed the USA as the protector of a free Western system while making any country that accepted the dollars a partner in that system, not a neutral bystander.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: who proposed the Marshall Plan, where, when, how much it delivered, and why Stalin refused it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Marshall Plan was announced by George C. Marshall at Harvard on 5 June 1947 and ran from 1948 to 1952. It delivered around $13 billion to sixteen Western European countries. Stalin refused it for the USSR and forbade Eastern European states from accepting, deepening the split between the two halves of Europe.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 turned the containment of communism into permanent US policy, triggered by the British inability to keep funding Greece and Turkey.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Marshall Plan, announced at Harvard on 5 June 1947 and running until 1952, delivered around $13 billion to sixteen Western European countries and tied their recovery to the USA.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Stalin&rsquo;s refusal to allow Eastern European states to accept Marshall aid converted what could have been a Europe-wide programme into a wedge that split the continent into two economic blocs.</li></ul>
