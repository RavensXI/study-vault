--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Khrushchev sealed Berlin in 1961 and how Kennedy chose a wall over a war.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Hole in the Iron Curtain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Berlin was the awkward city of the Cold War. Although it lay 110 miles inside Soviet-controlled East Germany, the four-power agreement of 1945 had divided the city itself into American, British, French and Soviet sectors. The Western sectors merged into <dfn class="term" data-def="The three combined Western sectors of Berlin (American, British, French), surrounded by but not part of communist East Germany.">West Berlin</dfn>, an island of Western prosperity surrounded by communist East Germany. Travel between the two halves of the city was easy: there was no wall, only checkpoints and an underground that crossed the sector line. By 1961 this open border had become an emergency.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: roughly how many East Germans had fled west through Berlin between 1949 and 1961, and what type of worker did the regime worry most about losing?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Between 1949 and August 1961 around 2.6 million East Germans &mdash; about one in six of the population &mdash; fled west, most of them through Berlin. They included a disproportionate number of doctors, engineers, teachers and skilled workers. The flow was bleeding the East German economy of its most productive citizens.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Khrushchev&rsquo;s Berlin Ultimatum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For Khrushchev, Berlin was both an embarrassment and a leverage point. He needed to staunch the refugee crisis to keep East Germany viable, and he wanted to force a Western retreat to demonstrate Soviet strength. In November 1958 he had issued an <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal demand backed by a threat of action if it is not met within a stated deadline.">ultimatum</dfn> demanding that the Western powers leave Berlin within six months. The Western powers refused; the deadline passed quietly. Khrushchev tried again at the Vienna Summit in June 1961, telling Kennedy that East Germany would sign a separate peace treaty by December and that Western access rights would then be at the mercy of East Berlin. Kennedy returned to Washington shaken; he privately admitted he had been &lsquo;savaged&rsquo; by Khrushchev.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Through the summer of 1961 the refugee numbers rose sharply. In June, around 19,000 East Germans crossed; in July, 30,000; in the first eleven days of August, more than 16,000. East Germany&rsquo;s leader Walter Ulbricht pressed Khrushchev for a permanent solution. The Vienna pressure had not moved the Western powers; Khrushchev now needed a way out that did not start a war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Wall Goes Up, 13 August 1961</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the early hours of Sunday 13 August 1961 East German troops and police rolled out barbed wire and concrete posts along the entire 27-mile sector boundary in Berlin. Tram lines were torn up, underground stations sealed and street crossings blocked. The first version was wire and improvised barriers; over the following weeks it hardened into a permanent concrete <dfn class="term" data-def="The 27-mile barrier dividing East and West Berlin from August 1961, designed to prevent East Germans fleeing to the West.">Berlin Wall</dfn> with watchtowers, mined &lsquo;death strips&rsquo; and armed guards. By 1989 around 140 people had been killed trying to cross.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Kennedy&rsquo;s Response: Firmness Without War</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Kennedy was on holiday at Hyannis Port when the Wall went up. His first private response was relief: &lsquo;a wall is a hell of a lot better than a war&rsquo;. He had no good military options &mdash; tearing down the wire would have meant troops opening fire on East German conscripts. Instead he sent Vice-President Lyndon Johnson and the wartime Berlin commander General Lucius Clay to West Berlin on 19 August. He authorised a 1,500-strong US battle group to drive up the autobahn from West Germany as a public demonstration of access rights. He approved the deployment of US tanks at Checkpoint Charlie in October, which faced down Soviet tanks for sixteen hours before both sides withdrew.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In June 1963 Kennedy travelled to West Berlin and delivered the famous &lsquo;Ich bin ein Berliner&rsquo; speech, declaring that the Wall was &lsquo;an offence against humanity&rsquo; but framing the city as proof that the West would not retreat.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Why a Wall, Not a War?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A common student misconception is that the Wall was a Soviet defeat. In fact it was a managed solution that suited Khrushchev. It stopped the refugee haemorrhage without forcing a Western retreat from West Berlin. It put the moral cost &mdash; shooting people trying to flee &mdash; on the East German regime, while keeping Soviet troops in the background. Kennedy, for his part, accepted the Wall because the alternative was nuclear escalation over a city he was not willing to die for.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Long Consequences</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The Wall stabilised East Germany economically: the regime no longer lost its skilled workers, and East Berlin gradually became a viable, if dreary, capital. It also stabilised the Cold War: by removing Berlin as an immediate flashpoint, the Wall let both leaders turn to other issues. The very next year, however, both faced a far sharper crisis &mdash; the Cuban Missile Crisis &mdash; in part because Khrushchev had read Kennedy&rsquo;s caution over Berlin as weakness. The Wall stood until 9 November 1989, when East German guards finally opened the checkpoints under pressure from a peaceful crowd.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three reasons Khrushchev built the Wall in August 1961, in order of priority for him.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Berlin Wall was built on 13 August 1961 to stop the flight of skilled East Germans to the West. It also let Khrushchev claim a victory at home without forcing a Western retreat. Kennedy chose a tough but non-military response &mdash; tank standoffs, public visits and the 1963 &lsquo;Ich bin ein Berliner&rsquo; speech.</p>
</div>
