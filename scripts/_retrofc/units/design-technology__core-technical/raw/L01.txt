<h2 data-narration-id="n1">New & Emerging Technologies</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Design and technology is constantly evolving. New and emerging technologies change the way products are designed, manufactured and used. As a designer, you need to understand how these developments affect industry, enterprise, society and the environment — and how they influence the choices you make.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Impact on Industry</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of machines and computers to perform tasks with minimal human intervention.">Automation</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Programmable machines that can carry out complex tasks such as welding, painting or assembly in a factory.">robotics</dfn> have transformed the modern workplace. Factories now use robotic arms for repetitive tasks like welding car bodies or picking items in warehouses. This increases speed, consistency and reduces human error, but it also means fewer manual jobs on production lines.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">New technologies have changed the design of buildings and workplaces too. Open-plan offices with digital collaboration tools allow flexible working. Factories are designed around automated systems, with safety zones separating robots from human workers. Tools and equipment have also evolved — 3D printers, laser cutters and CNC routers are now common in both industry and school workshops.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Name three ways automation has changed industrial manufacturing.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Automation increases production speed, improves consistency and reduces labour costs, but it can lead to job losses in manufacturing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Enterprise & Innovation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Enterprise means developing a business idea and turning it into a product or service. New technologies have created new ways for designers to fund and sell their work. <dfn class="term" data-def="Raising money for a project by collecting small contributions from a large number of people, usually online.">Crowd funding</dfn> platforms like Kickstarter allow designers to raise money directly from potential customers before manufacturing begins. This reduces financial risk and tests whether there is a real market for the product.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Promoting and selling products through online channels such as websites, social media and email.">Virtual marketing and retail</dfn> allow businesses to reach global audiences through websites and social media without needing a physical shop. <dfn class="term" data-def="A business owned and run jointly by its members, who share the profits and decision-making equally.">Co-operatives</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of trade in which producers in developing countries are paid a fair price for their goods.">fair trade</dfn> models use technology to connect producers in developing countries directly with consumers, ensuring fairer pay.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sustainability & the Environment</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Every product uses resources. Designers must consider whether those resources are <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources that will eventually run out because they cannot be replaced at the rate they are used, such as oil, coal and metal ores.">finite</dfn> (like oil and metal ores) or <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources that can be replenished naturally and will not run out if managed properly, such as timber and cotton.">non-finite</dfn> (like timber and cotton). The disposal of waste is also a major concern — landfill, incineration and ocean pollution all damage the environment.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Sustainable design aims to reduce negative environmental impact. This includes using recycled materials, designing for disassembly so parts can be reused, and minimising packaging. The ecological and social footprint of a product covers everything from raw material extraction to disposal.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: What is the difference between finite and non-finite resources? Give one example of each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Finite resources (oil, metal ores) will run out. Non-finite resources (timber, cotton) can be replenished if managed responsibly. Designers must consider resource type when selecting materials.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Technology Push & Market Pull</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="When new inventions or discoveries drive the development of products that consumers did not know they needed.">Technology push</dfn> happens when a new invention drives product development — for example, the invention of graphene leading to new ultra-strong, lightweight products. <dfn class="term" data-def="When consumer demand or a gap in the market drives the development of new products.">Market pull</dfn> is the opposite: consumer demand drives innovation, such as the demand for eco-friendly packaging leading to biodegradable alternatives.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Society, Culture & Ethical Design</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Products must be designed to avoid negative impacts on people. This means considering the needs of disabled users, elderly people and different religious or cultural groups. Inclusive design ensures products work for as many people as possible.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">Designers also face ethical decisions. <dfn class="term" data-def="Deliberately designing a product with a limited lifespan so that consumers need to replace it, generating repeat sales.">Planned obsolescence</dfn> — deliberately designing products to break or become outdated — generates repeat sales but creates waste. Ethical designers consider maintenance, repairability and the full lifecycle of their products.</p>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">Modern manufacturing uses several key systems. <strong>CAD</strong> (computer aided design) allows products to be modelled digitally before making. <strong>CAM</strong> (computer aided manufacture) uses computers to control machinery like CNC routers and laser cutters. <strong>FMS</strong> (flexible manufacturing systems) allow production lines to switch between different products quickly. <strong>JIT</strong> (just in time) manufacturing orders materials only when needed, reducing storage costs. <strong>Lean manufacturing</strong> aims to eliminate all waste from the production process.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Automation, robotics, CAD and CAM have transformed how products are designed and manufactured in industry.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Enterprise technologies like crowd funding, virtual marketing and fair trade connect designers directly with consumers and producers worldwide.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Designers must consider sustainability (finite vs non-finite resources), ethics (planned obsolescence) and inclusive design (disability, culture, religion) in every project.</li>
</ul>
