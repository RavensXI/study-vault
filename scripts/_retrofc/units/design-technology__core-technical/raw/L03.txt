<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Modern & Smart Materials</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Material science is advancing rapidly. Designers now have access to materials that would have seemed impossible a few decades ago — from ultra-strong, atom-thin sheets of carbon to fabrics that conduct electricity. This lesson covers modern materials, smart materials, composites and technical textiles.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Modern Materials</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Modern materials are developed through new inventions or improvements to existing processes. They offer properties that traditional materials cannot match.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, making it incredibly strong, lightweight and an excellent conductor of heat and electricity.">Graphene</dfn> is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb pattern. It is around 200 times stronger than steel, extremely lightweight, flexible, and conducts both heat and electricity brilliantly. Potential applications include flexible screens, ultra-fast electronics and stronger composite materials.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Metallic materials with a cellular structure containing gas-filled pores, making them lightweight yet strong.">Metal foams</dfn> are metals (usually aluminium) with gas-filled pores throughout their structure. They are lightweight but strong, and absorb energy well — making them useful for crash protection in vehicles and lightweight structural panels.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A strong, lightweight, corrosion-resistant metal used in aerospace, medical implants and high-performance sports equipment.">Titanium</dfn> is a strong, lightweight metal that resists corrosion. It is widely used in aerospace, medical implants (hip joints, dental implants) and high-performance sports equipment like bicycle frames.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Name three modern materials and give one property of each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Key modern materials: graphene (ultra-strong, flexible, conductive), metal foams (lightweight, energy-absorbing), titanium (strong, lightweight, corrosion-resistant). All were developed through improved manufacturing processes.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Other modern materials include <dfn class="term" data-def="A protective layer applied to the surface of a metal to improve corrosion resistance, appearance or other properties.">coated metals</dfn> (metals with protective surface layers for corrosion resistance), <dfn class="term" data-def="Screens that use liquid crystals sandwiched between layers of glass to display images, found in TVs, monitors and phones.">liquid crystal displays (LCDs)</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Materials engineered at the nanoscale (billionths of a metre) to give enhanced properties such as water resistance or antimicrobial action.">nanomaterials</dfn> — materials engineered at the atomic scale to achieve enhanced properties like water resistance or self-cleaning surfaces.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Smart Materials</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Materials that have one or more properties that can change significantly in response to an external stimulus such as temperature, light, pressure or moisture.">Smart materials</dfn> react to changes in their environment. Unlike modern materials, which have fixed enhanced properties, smart materials change their behaviour in response to external stimuli.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Metal alloys that can be deformed but return to their original pre-set shape when heated above a certain temperature.">Shape memory alloys (SMAs)</dfn> can be bent or deformed but return to their original shape when heated. Nitinol (a nickel-titanium alloy) is the most common example, used in dental braces, spectacle frames and stents.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Pigments that change colour in response to temperature changes, used in thermometers, mugs and mood rings.">Thermochromic pigments</dfn> change colour when the temperature changes. They are used in colour-changing mugs, baby spoons (to warn if food is too hot) and forehead thermometer strips.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Pigments that change colour or darken in response to light intensity, used in reactive sunglasses lenses.">Photochromic pigments</dfn> change colour in response to light. The most common application is reactive sunglasses lenses that darken in bright sunlight and return to clear indoors.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: What are the three types of smart material? Name one use for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Smart materials respond to external stimuli: shape memory alloys react to heat, thermochromic pigments react to temperature, and photochromic pigments react to light. The key difference from modern materials is that smart materials change reversibly.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Composite Materials</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Materials made by combining two or more different materials to create a new material with enhanced properties that neither original material has alone.">Composite materials</dfn> combine two or more materials to create something with better properties than either original. <dfn class="term" data-def="A composite material made from a polymer matrix reinforced with glass fibres, offering a good strength-to-weight ratio.">Glass reinforced plastic (GRP)</dfn> combines glass fibres with a polyester resin matrix. It is lightweight, strong and corrosion-resistant — used for boat hulls, car body panels and storage tanks. <dfn class="term" data-def="A composite material made from a polymer matrix reinforced with carbon fibres, extremely strong and lightweight but expensive.">Carbon fibre reinforced plastic (CRP)</dfn> uses carbon fibres instead of glass, making it even stronger and lighter (but more expensive). CRP is used in Formula 1 cars, aircraft components and high-end bicycles.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Technical Textiles</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fabrics engineered for specific technical performance rather than appearance, including fire-resistant, conductive and bulletproof textiles.">Technical textiles</dfn> are fabrics engineered for performance rather than appearance. <strong>Conductive fabrics</strong> have metallic fibres woven in and are used in wearable electronics. <strong>Fire-resistant fabrics</strong> are treated to resist ignition and are used in firefighter clothing. <strong>Kevlar</strong> is an extremely strong synthetic fibre used in bulletproof vests, cut-resistant gloves and motorcycle protective gear.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Ultra-fine synthetic fibres that are lightweight, breathable and water-resistant, used in sportswear and outdoor clothing.">Microfibres</dfn> are ultra-fine synthetic fibres used in sportswear and cleaning cloths. <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of embedding tiny capsules of active substances (such as fragrance or moisture) into fabric fibres for gradual release.">Micro encapsulation</dfn> embeds tiny capsules of substances (like fragrance, insect repellent or moisturiser) into fabric, releasing them gradually with wear.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Modern materials (graphene, metal foams, titanium, coated metals, LCDs, nanomaterials) have enhanced fixed properties developed through new processes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Smart materials (shape memory alloys, thermochromic pigments, photochromic pigments) change reversibly in response to external stimuli like heat or light.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Composites (GRP, CRP) combine materials for improved properties; technical textiles (Kevlar, conductive fabrics, microfibres) are engineered for performance.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">The exam often asks you to distinguish between modern, smart and composite materials. Remember: modern materials have enhanced fixed properties (graphene, titanium), smart materials change reversibly in response to stimuli (SMAs, thermochromic), and composites combine two materials for improved performance (GRP, CRP). Always give a specific example and its application.</p>
