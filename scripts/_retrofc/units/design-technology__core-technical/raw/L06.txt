<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Materials & Their Working Properties</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Choosing the right material is one of the most important decisions a designer makes. This lesson covers the five main material categories — papers and boards, timbers, metals, polymers and textiles — along with the physical and working properties you need to know for your exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Papers & Boards</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Papers are thin, flexible sheet materials made from wood pulp. Boards are thicker and more rigid. Key types include:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Papers:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="Paper with a coating that prevents ink from spreading through to the other side, used for marker pen work.">bleed-proof paper</dfn> (for marker pens), cartridge paper (general drawing), grid paper (technical drawing), layout paper (lightweight tracing for design development) and tracing paper (transparent, for copying designs).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Boards:</strong> corrugated card (packaging — strong, lightweight, cushioning), <dfn class="term" data-def="A board made from two layers with different finishes, typically one white and one grey, used for food packaging.">duplex board</dfn> (food packaging), foil-lined board (insulating food containers), <dfn class="term" data-def="A rigid, lightweight board made from a foam centre sandwiched between two paper faces, used for mounting and model-making.">foam core board</dfn> (mounting and model-making), ink jet card (printing) and solid white board (premium packaging).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Natural & Manufactured Timbers</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Timber from broad-leaved, deciduous trees that is generally harder, denser and more durable than softwood, but slower growing and more expensive.">Hardwoods</dfn> come from broad-leaved, deciduous trees and are generally harder, denser and more expensive. Examples: ash (tough, flexible — tool handles), beech (hard-wearing — furniture), mahogany (attractive grain — furniture), oak (very durable — flooring, outdoor furniture) and balsa (extremely lightweight — model-making).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Timber from coniferous (evergreen, cone-bearing) trees that grows faster, is cheaper and softer than hardwood.">Softwoods</dfn> come from coniferous, evergreen trees and grow faster, making them cheaper. Examples: larch (durable outdoors — fencing), pine (versatile — construction, furniture) and spruce (lightweight — construction, musical instruments).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Board materials made from processed wood fibres, chips or veneers, offering consistent properties and available in large sheets.">Manufactured boards</dfn> are made from processed wood. MDF (medium density fibreboard — smooth, easy to shape, good for painting), plywood (layers of veneer glued at right angles — strong in all directions) and chipboard (compressed wood chips — cheap, used in flat-pack furniture).</p>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Name two hardwoods, two softwoods and two manufactured boards with one use for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Hardwoods (oak, mahogany, beech, ash, balsa) are from deciduous trees — harder, denser, more expensive. Softwoods (pine, larch, spruce) are from coniferous trees — faster growing, cheaper. Manufactured boards (MDF, plywood, chipboard) offer consistency in large sheets.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Metals & Alloys</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Metals that contain iron and are generally magnetic and prone to rusting, such as low carbon steel, cast iron and high carbon steel.">Ferrous metals</dfn> contain iron. Low carbon steel (mild steel) is cheap and easy to work — used for car bodies, nuts and bolts. Cast iron is very hard but brittle — used for manhole covers and engine blocks. High carbon (tool) steel is extremely hard — used for cutting tools and drill bits.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Metals that do not contain iron, are generally non-magnetic and resist corrosion better than ferrous metals.">Non-ferrous metals</dfn> do not contain iron. Aluminium is lightweight and corrosion-resistant — used for drinks cans and aircraft. Copper is an excellent electrical conductor — used for wiring and plumbing. Tin resists corrosion — used for coating steel cans. Zinc is used for galvanising steel to prevent rust.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Mixtures of two or more metals (or a metal and another element) designed to improve properties like strength, hardness or corrosion resistance.">Alloys</dfn> are mixtures of metals. Brass (copper + zinc) is corrosion-resistant and attractive — used for taps and musical instruments. Stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel) resists corrosion — used for cutlery and surgical instruments. High speed steel (HSS) stays hard at high temperatures — used for drill bits.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Polymers that soften when heated and can be reshaped repeatedly, making them recyclable.">Thermoforming polymers</dfn> (thermoplastics) soften when heated and can be reshaped repeatedly. Key examples: acrylic/PMMA (transparent — display cases, signs), HIPS (easy to vacuum form — packaging, yoghurt pots), HDPE (tough — bottles, pipes), polypropylene/PP (flexible hinge — food containers, folders), PVC (durable — window frames, pipes) and PET (clear, strong — drinks bottles).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Polymers that undergo a chemical change when heated and set permanently into shape; they cannot be reshaped or recycled.">Thermosetting polymers</dfn> set permanently when heated and cannot be reshaped. Key examples: epoxy resin/ER (strong adhesive — bonding composites), melamine formaldehyde/MF (heat-resistant — kitchen worktops), phenol formaldehyde/PF (electrical insulator — plugs, handles), polyester resin/PR (used as matrix in GRP/fibreglass) and urea formaldehyde/UF (hard — electrical fittings, adhesive).</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Textiles</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Natural fibres:</strong> cotton (soft, absorbent, breathable — t-shirts, towels), wool (warm, elastic — jumpers, blankets) and silk (smooth, lustrous — scarves, formal wear). <strong>Synthetic fibres:</strong> polyester (strong, crease-resistant — sportswear), polyamide/nylon (strong, elastic — tights, ropes) and elastane/lycra (very stretchy — sportswear, underwear).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Blended/mixed fibres</strong> (e.g. cotton/polyester) combine the best properties of both. <strong>Woven fabrics</strong> (plain weave — interlocking warp and weft threads) are strong and stable. <strong>Non-woven fabrics</strong> include bonded fabrics (fibres stuck together — disposable cloths) and felted fabrics (fibres matted by heat/moisture — hats, craft). <strong>Knitted fabrics</strong> are stretchy and comfortable — used for jumpers, socks and t-shirts.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Name one natural fibre, one synthetic fibre and one blended fibre, with a use for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Textiles include natural (cotton, wool, silk), synthetic (polyester, nylon, elastane) and blended/mixed fibres (cotton/polyester). Fabrics are made by weaving, knitting, bonding or felting.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Physical & Working Properties</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n23">You need to know two types of material properties:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Physical & Working Properties Explained</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Physical properties</strong> describe a material's characteristics in its natural state. <dfn class="term" data-def="How much moisture a material absorbs; important for textiles and papers.">Absorbency</dfn> — resistance to moisture (cotton absorbs, polyester resists). <dfn class="term" data-def="Mass per unit volume of a material; high density means heavy for its size.">Density</dfn> — mass per unit volume (lead is dense, balsa is not). <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a material to melt and be reshaped; important for casting and moulding processes.">Fusibility</dfn> — ability to melt and be reshaped (crucial for casting metals and moulding polymers). <strong>Electrical and thermal conductivity</strong> — how well a material conducts electricity or heat (copper conducts well, wood does not).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Working properties</strong> describe how a material behaves when force is applied. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to withstand force without breaking; can be tensile (pulling), compressive (pushing) or shear (sliding).">Strength</dfn> — resistance to breaking under force. <dfn class="term" data-def="Resistance to scratching, cutting or indentation on the surface.">Hardness</dfn> — resistance to scratching or denting. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to absorb impact energy without fracturing; a tough material bends before it breaks.">Toughness</dfn> — ability to absorb impact without fracturing. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to be hammered or pressed into shape without cracking, such as gold or copper.">Malleability</dfn> — ability to be hammered or pressed into shape. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to be stretched into a thin wire without breaking, such as copper.">Ductility</dfn> — ability to be drawn into wire. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to stretch and return to the original shape, such as rubber or elastane.">Elasticity</dfn> — ability to stretch and return to shape.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n28">The five material categories are papers/boards, timbers (hardwoods, softwoods, manufactured boards), metals (ferrous, non-ferrous, alloys), polymers (thermoforming, thermosetting) and textiles (natural, synthetic, blended).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Physical properties include absorbency, density, fusibility and conductivity; working properties include strength, hardness, toughness, malleability, ductility and elasticity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">Always link a material’s specific properties to the product context — this is how marks are awarded in the exam.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">Material questions in Section A are often “name” or “state” questions worth 1–2 marks. Learn the key examples for each category. For longer questions, link the material’s properties to why it is suitable for a given product — don’t just name the material, explain the property that makes it the right choice.</p>
