<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mechanical Devices</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Mechanical devices are used in products to create movement, change the direction of force or multiply force. Understanding how these mechanisms work is essential for designing products that move. This lesson covers the four types of movement, levers, linkages and rotary systems.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Types of Movement</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4">There are four types of movement (motion) that mechanical devices produce:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Linear motion</strong> — movement in a straight line in one direction (e.g. a drawer sliding open). <strong>Rotary motion</strong> — movement in a circle around a central point (e.g. a wheel turning). <strong>Reciprocating motion</strong> — backwards-and-forwards movement in a straight line (e.g. a sewing machine needle). <strong>Oscillating motion</strong> — swinging movement back and forth along an arc (e.g. a pendulum in a clock).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Name the four types of movement and give one example of each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four types of motion are linear (straight line), rotary (circular), reciprocating (back-and-forth in a line) and oscillating (swinging arc). Many mechanisms convert one type of motion into another.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Levers</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rigid bar that pivots around a fixed point (fulcrum) to multiply force or increase the distance of movement.">lever</dfn> is a rigid bar that rotates around a fixed point called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The fixed pivot point around which a lever rotates.">fulcrum</dfn>. Levers multiply force (making it easier to lift heavy objects) or increase the distance or speed of movement. There are three classes:</p>



<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Three Classes of Lever</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>First-order lever</strong> — the fulcrum is between the effort and the load. Examples: seesaw, crowbar, scissors. These can multiply force or distance depending on where the fulcrum is placed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Second-order lever</strong> — the load is between the fulcrum and the effort. Examples: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener. These always multiply force (the effort moves further than the load).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Third-order lever</strong> — the effort is between the fulcrum and the load. Examples: tweezers, fishing rod, human forearm. These always multiply distance/speed (the load moves further than the effort), but require more force.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Linkages</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Systems of connected bars or levers that transmit motion and force, changing the direction or type of movement.">Linkages</dfn> are systems of bars connected by pivot points that transmit and change the direction of movement.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A linkage that changes the direction of movement through 90 degrees, often used in bicycle brake systems.">bell crank</dfn> changes the direction of movement by 90 degrees. When you pull a bicycle brake lever (input), the bell crank converts the pulling motion into a pushing motion that presses the brake pad against the wheel rim. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A linkage that reverses the direction of a push or pull force along a straight line.">push/pull linkage</dfn> (also called a reverse-motion linkage) reverses the direction of input motion — push in one end and the other end pulls out.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Rotary Systems</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Rotary systems transmit rotary motion and can change speed, direction or type of movement.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Cams, Gears & Pulleys</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A shaped disc (cam) mounted on a rotating shaft that converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion via a follower that rides on its edge.">Cams and followers</dfn> convert rotary motion into reciprocating motion. The cam is a shaped disc on a rotating shaft. As it turns, a follower rides along its edge, moving up and down. The shape of the cam determines the pattern of movement. Used in engines (opening valves) and toys.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Toothed wheels that mesh together to transmit rotary motion, changing speed, direction or torque between shafts.">Simple gear trains</dfn> use toothed wheels that mesh together. When gears of different sizes are used, the smaller gear (pinion) turns faster than the larger gear, but with less force (torque). The <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio between the number of teeth on the driven gear and the driver gear, which determines the speed and torque change.">gear ratio</dfn> determines the speed and torque relationship. Meshing gears also reverse the direction of rotation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Wheels connected by a belt to transmit rotary motion between shafts, often used when the shafts are far apart.">Pulleys and belts</dfn> transmit rotary motion between shafts using a belt wrapped around wheels. Like gears, different-sized pulleys change speed and torque. Unlike gears, pulleys connected by a flat belt rotate in the same direction. A crossed belt reverses the direction.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Draw a cam and follower and explain how it converts rotary motion to reciprocating motion.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Cams convert rotary to reciprocating motion. Gears transmit rotary motion and can change speed, torque and direction. Pulleys transmit rotary motion over distance using belts. All three are common in exam questions.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The four types of motion are linear, rotary, reciprocating and oscillating — many mechanisms convert between these.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Levers (first, second and third order) multiply force or distance; linkages (bell crank, push/pull) change the direction of movement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Rotary systems include cams and followers (rotary to reciprocating), gear trains (change speed, torque and direction) and pulleys and belts (transmit motion over distance).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Mechanical device questions often ask you to identify the type of motion or explain how a mechanism works. Use the correct technical vocabulary — “reciprocating” not “up and down”, “fulcrum” not “pivot point”. If a question involves gears, mention the gear ratio and explain how changing gear size affects speed and torque.</p>
