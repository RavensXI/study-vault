--- DESCRIPTION ---
Electronic systems: inputs, processes and outputs using sensors and microcontrollers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Systems Approach to Designing</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Many products use electronic systems to provide functionality. A security light that switches on when someone approaches, a washing machine that controls water temperature, or a music speaker that amplifies sound — all use a <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of connected components that work together to perform a function, typically following an input-process-output model.">systems approach</dfn>. Understanding how electronic systems work helps you design products that respond to their environment.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Input–Process–Output Model</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Every electronic system follows the same basic structure: <strong>input → process → output</strong>. The input component detects a change in the environment (light, temperature, pressure). The process component makes a decision about what to do with that information. The output component performs an action (sound, light, movement).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Draw a simple input-process-output diagram and label each stage with an example component.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>All electronic systems follow the input → process → output model. Inputs detect changes in the environment; the process makes decisions; outputs perform actions.</p>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Input Devices</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electronic components that detect changes in the environment, such as light levels, temperature, pressure or user actions, and convert them into electrical signals.">Input devices</dfn> (also called sensors) detect changes and convert them into electrical signals that the system can process.</p>

<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Input Devices</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sensor that detects the level of light in the environment, such as a light-dependent resistor (LDR), whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases.">Light sensors</dfn> (LDRs) detect light levels. Their resistance changes depending on brightness. Used in automatic streetlights that switch on when it gets dark.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sensor that detects temperature changes, such as a thermistor, whose resistance decreases as temperature increases.">Temperature sensors</dfn> (thermistors) detect temperature changes. Their resistance decreases as temperature rises. Used in central heating thermostats and car engine monitors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sensor that detects physical force or weight, converting pressure into an electrical signal.">Pressure sensors</dfn> detect physical force. Used in car seat sensors (to trigger seatbelt warnings) and digital scales.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Simple input devices that open or close a circuit, either manually (push switch) or automatically (tilt switch, reed switch).">Switches</dfn> are the simplest input devices. They include push switches (doorbells), toggle switches (light switches), tilt switches (detecting orientation) and reed switches (activated by magnets, used in security alarms).</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Process: Microcontrollers</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The process stage is handled by a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small programmable computer on a single chip that can be programmed to make decisions, count events, time actions and control outputs based on input data.">microcontroller</dfn> — a small, programmable computer on a single chip. Microcontrollers can be programmed to act as:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Counters</strong> — counting the number of times an event occurs (e.g. counting items on a production line). <strong>Timers</strong> — triggering actions after a set time (e.g. turning off a light after 30 seconds). <strong>Decision makers</strong> — using logic to choose between actions based on sensor data (e.g. if temperature is above 25°C, switch on the fan).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Name three roles a microcontroller can perform in an electronic system.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Microcontrollers can be programmed as counters (counting events), timers (triggering actions after a set time) and decision makers (choosing actions based on sensor data). They are the “brain” of an electronic system.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Output Devices</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Components that perform an action in response to processed signals, such as producing sound, light or movement.">Output devices</dfn> perform the action that the system is designed to produce.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Output Devices</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Buzzers</strong> produce a simple tone and are used in alarms, timers and doorbells. <strong>Speakers</strong> produce a wider range of sounds and are used in phones, radios and music systems. <strong>Lamps/LEDs</strong> produce light and are used for indicators, displays and illumination. <strong>Motors</strong> produce rotary movement and are used in fans, conveyor belts and electric vehicles.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">When designing an electronic system, you need to match the right input, process and output components. For example, a security light system uses a <strong>motion sensor</strong> (input) connected to a <strong>microcontroller</strong> (process) that switches on a <strong>lamp</strong> (output) when movement is detected, then uses a timer to switch it off after a set period.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">All electronic systems follow the input → process → output model, where sensors detect changes, microcontrollers make decisions, and outputs perform actions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Key input devices include light sensors (LDRs), temperature sensors (thermistors), pressure sensors and switches; microcontrollers can act as counters, timers and decision makers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Output devices include buzzers, speakers, lamps/LEDs and motors — the choice depends on the product’s function and the action required.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">Questions on systems often give you a scenario and ask you to identify the input, process and output. Always name the specific component (e.g. “thermistor” not just “sensor”) and explain what it does in that context. If the question asks about a microcontroller, state whether it is acting as a counter, timer or decision maker.</p>
