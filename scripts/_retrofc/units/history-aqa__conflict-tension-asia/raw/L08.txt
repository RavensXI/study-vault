--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the Domino Theory drew the US into Vietnam: advisers, aid, Strategic Hamlets and Diem's fall.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Geneva to Cold War Crisis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the French collapsed at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, the Geneva Agreements split Vietnam at the 17th parallel as a temporary line until reunification elections in 1956. Those elections never happened. Instead, the United States stepped into the gap left by France and propped up an anti-communist government in the South. American policy was driven by a single, powerful idea: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Cold War theory that if one country in a region fell to communism, neighbouring states would topple in sequence like a row of dominoes.">Domino Theory</dfn>. President Eisenhower used the phrase publicly in April 1954, warning that the loss of Indochina could trigger a chain reaction across South-East Asia, with Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Burma and even Indonesia falling next.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who first used the &lsquo;falling domino principle&rsquo; phrase, in which year, and which countries did he say might fall next?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In April 1954 Eisenhower publicly described the &lsquo;falling domino principle&rsquo; &mdash; the belief that if South Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of South-East Asia would follow. This idea, more than any single event, locked the United States into Vietnam for the next twenty years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Eisenhower and Ngo Dinh Diem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From 1955 the United States backed <dfn class="term" data-def="Catholic, anti-communist president of South Vietnam from 1955 to 1963; assassinated in a US-tolerated coup in November 1963.">Ngo Dinh Diem</dfn>, the Catholic, anti-communist leader of South Vietnam. Diem refused to hold the 1956 reunification elections promised at Geneva, fearing &mdash; correctly &mdash; that Ho Chi Minh would win them. Eisenhower&rsquo;s government accepted this. Washington poured economic aid into the South and sent the <dfn class="term" data-def="Military Assistance Advisory Group &mdash; the team of US military advisers training and equipping the South Vietnamese army from the mid-1950s.">MAAG</dfn> (Military Assistance Advisory Group) to train the new South Vietnamese army (the ARVN). Officially these were not combat troops, but advisers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Diem&rsquo;s regime alienated the rural majority almost from the start. He was a northern Catholic in a country that was mostly Buddhist; he placed his own family in positions of power; and he refused to deliver the land reform that southern peasants wanted. By 1960 communist insurgents in the South had organised themselves into the National Liberation Front &mdash; the Vietcong &mdash; with backing from Hanoi. The civil war that the Domino Theory was meant to prevent was already beginning.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Kennedy Inherits and Escalates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">When John F. Kennedy took office in January 1961 he inherited a deteriorating South. He believed in containment and was determined not to look weak after the failure of the Bay of Pigs and the building of the Berlin Wall. Rather than commit ground troops, Kennedy raised the number of US <dfn class="term" data-def="American military personnel sent to advise and train the South Vietnamese army; in practice they often accompanied combat operations.">military advisers</dfn> from around 800 to over 16,000 by late 1963. He also expanded helicopter support, introduced chemical defoliants and authorised covert operations.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The flagship counter-insurgency policy under Kennedy and Diem was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1962 counter-insurgency programme that moved South Vietnamese peasants into fortified villages, intended to separate them from the Vietcong.">Strategic Hamlets</dfn> programme, launched in 1962. Peasants were forcibly moved out of their ancestral villages into fortified compounds ringed with barbed wire, watchtowers and moats. The plan, modelled loosely on British tactics in the Malayan Emergency, was to physically separate the rural population from the Vietcong &mdash; cutting off the insurgents&rsquo; food, intelligence and recruits.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In practice the programme backfired. Peasants resented being uprooted from family land and ancestral graves. Many hamlets were poorly built and corrupt officials skimmed off the construction money. The Vietcong infiltrated some hamlets from within, and by 1963 large numbers had collapsed or fallen under insurgent control. Far from winning hearts and minds, Strategic Hamlets pushed many uncommitted peasants towards the Vietcong.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Buddhist Crisis and the Fall of Diem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In May 1963 South Vietnamese troops fired on Buddhists in Hue who were marking the Buddha&rsquo;s birthday with banned religious flags. Several were killed. The atrocity triggered nationwide protests. In June 1963 the monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death on a Saigon street; a press photograph of his self-immolation went around the world and turned American opinion sharply against Diem. Diem&rsquo;s sister-in-law, Madame Nhu, infamously dismissed the protests as &lsquo;barbecues&rsquo;, deepening the public-relations disaster.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">By autumn 1963 Kennedy&rsquo;s administration had concluded that Diem could not win the war. South Vietnamese generals signalled that they were planning a coup. Washington signalled, through ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge, that it would not stand in the way. On 1&ndash;2 November 1963 the generals seized power; Diem and his brother Nhu were murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier. Twenty days later, Kennedy himself was assassinated in Dallas.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Three factors locked the US deeper in. First, the Domino Theory framed every setback as a step towards regional collapse rather than a local problem. Second, Kennedy could not afford another perceived defeat after the Bay of Pigs and the Berlin Wall. Third, the advisers&rsquo; increasingly combat-like role made each escalation feel like a small adjustment rather than a strategic choice. By the end of 1963, with Diem dead and the South in political chaos, Kennedy&rsquo;s successor Lyndon Johnson would face a stark question: pull out, or push further in.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: how many US military advisers were in South Vietnam under Eisenhower at the end of 1960, and how many under Kennedy by late 1963?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under Eisenhower, US advisers in South Vietnam numbered around 800 by the end of 1960. Under Kennedy, that figure had risen to over 16,000 by late 1963 &mdash; a twentyfold increase. None were officially combat troops, but many accompanied ARVN operations.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The Domino Theory, articulated by Eisenhower in 1954, framed Vietnam as the test case for containment in Asia and locked the US into propping up Diem&rsquo;s South.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Kennedy massively expanded the adviser presence and the Strategic Hamlets counter-insurgency programme &mdash; both deepened US commitment without producing political stability.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Diem&rsquo;s assassination in November 1963 left South Vietnam in turmoil and presented Lyndon Johnson with a choice between withdrawal and full-scale war.</li></ul>
