Chinese intervention in October 1950, the long UN retreat and the sacking of MacArthur in 1951.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Enemy Crosses the Yalu</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In late October 1950, even as MacArthur was promising the troops would be home by Christmas, a new force was already in the Korean mountains. Tens of thousands of Chinese soldiers, calling themselves the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Chinese force sent into Korea from October 1950, officially &lsquo;volunteers&rsquo; rather than regulars to maintain the fiction that China and the United States were not at war.">People&rsquo;s Volunteer Army</dfn>, were crossing the Yalu River by night under careful blackout to avoid air detection. The label &lsquo;volunteers&rsquo; was a deliberate fiction; these were regular People&rsquo;s Liberation Army units. The fiction allowed Mao to argue that China itself was not at war with the United States &mdash; a useful cover that helped prevent escalation to nuclear weapons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when China entered the war, what its force was called, and how its tactics differed from US firepower.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Chinese People&rsquo;s Volunteer Army crossed the Yalu in October&ndash;November 1950 under the command of Marshal Peng Dehuai. By Christmas 1950 around 300,000 Chinese soldiers had entered the war, decisively changing its course.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Mao Decided to Intervene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Mao&rsquo;s decision was contested even within the Chinese leadership. Several Politburo members opposed war with the United States so soon after the civil war. Mao&rsquo;s case was strategic and ideological. Strategically, hostile forces on the Yalu posed a direct threat to Chinese industry in Manchuria and to the new revolution. Ideologically, allowing a fellow communist state to be destroyed would damage Beijing&rsquo;s prestige and weaken Sino-Soviet relations. Stalin promised air cover (and largely failed to deliver it on the front line) and Soviet equipment, which made the operation feasible. The decision to enter the war was finalised on 18 October 1950.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Chosin Reservoir and the Long Retreat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In late November 1950, Chinese forces launched massive surprise attacks on the divided UN columns. The Eighth Army was driven into headlong retreat in the west, abandoning equipment and suffering thousands of casualties in the long withdrawal south. In the east, around 30,000 US Marines and soldiers were surrounded by 120,000 Chinese troops at the <dfn class="term" data-def="The November&ndash;December 1950 battle in north-east Korea where US X Corps fought a 17-day breakout from Chinese encirclement in -30&deg;C conditions.">Chosin Reservoir</dfn>. In temperatures as low as -30&deg;C, with weapons freezing and rations frozen solid, the Marines fought a 17-day battle to break out southwards to the port of Hungnam &mdash; a fighting retreat now studied at military academies worldwide. UN forces were eventually evacuated from Hungnam by sea on Christmas Eve 1950, taking around 100,000 North Korean civilian refugees with them. By January 1951, Chinese forces had recaptured Pyongyang and Seoul fell for the second time on 4 January 1951.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Chinese had no air force at the front, no tanks, no heavy artillery to match American firepower. They compensated through tactics: night-time attacks to negate US air power; mass infantry assaults using infiltration to find UN flanks; bugles and horns instead of radios for communication; and operating without long supply lines by carrying their own rations. UN technical superiority did not translate into battlefield dominance. By the time Eighth Army stabilised the line south of Seoul under General Matthew Ridgway in February 1951, the war had become an attritional grind.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Truman vs MacArthur: A Limited or Total War?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">From the start, Truman&rsquo;s objective had been a <dfn class="term" data-def="A war fought with limits on weapons (no nuclear), geography (kept to Korea) and aims (no rollback into China) to prevent a wider Cold War conflict.">limited war</dfn> &mdash; restore South Korea, contain communism, do not provoke World War Three. MacArthur disagreed. He wanted to bomb China, blockade its coast, deploy the Nationalist Chinese army from Taiwan, and even threatened the use of atomic weapons. He believed limited war in Asia abandoned the wider fight against communism. The disagreement was therefore not just personal &mdash; it was a clash over what kind of Cold War the United States should fight.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Sacking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Through early 1951 MacArthur publicly criticised Truman&rsquo;s strategy, sent letters to Republican congressmen attacking the policy of limited war, and undermined attempts to negotiate. The breaking point came in March 1951, when MacArthur issued his own ultimatum to China &mdash; sabotaging a Truman peace initiative just as it was about to be released. On 11 April 1951, Truman fired him for insubordination. <strong>General Matthew Ridgway</strong> took over UN command. The principle that civilian government controls the military &mdash; central to American democracy &mdash; was upheld at huge political cost: Truman&rsquo;s approval rating dropped to 22 percent, and MacArthur returned to a hero&rsquo;s welcome.</p>

Subsequent Senate hearings (the &lsquo;MacArthur Hearings&rsquo;) saw senior officers including the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs General Omar Bradley testify against MacArthur&rsquo;s strategy, calling it &lsquo;the wrong war, at the wrong place, at the wrong time, and with the wrong enemy&rsquo;. The hearings broke MacArthur&rsquo;s political momentum. The principle that even popular generals must obey elected presidents was reaffirmed for the rest of the Cold War.</p>

</div>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 for insubordination after MacArthur sabotaged a peace initiative and publicly attacked the policy of limited war. Ridgway took over and stabilised UN forces along a line near the 38th parallel.</p>
