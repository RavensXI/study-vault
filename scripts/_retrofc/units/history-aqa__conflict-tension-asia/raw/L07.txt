--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Diem refused 1956 elections, why peasants turned against him, and how the Vietcong rose.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Catholic Mandarin in a Buddhist Country</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><strong>Ngo Dinh Diem</strong> took power in South Vietnam in 1955 after winning a referendum almost certainly rigged in his favour &mdash; the official result claimed he had secured 98.2% of the vote in Saigon, a city of 450,000 voters where 605,000 ballots were counted. The Geneva Accords had promised reunification elections by July 1956. Diem and his American backers refused to hold them. They knew Ho Chi Minh would win. The decision not to hold elections is the political starting point of what AQA calls the &lsquo;civil war in South Vietnam&rsquo;.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who refused the 1956 elections, why, and what international agreement they were ignoring.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ngo Dinh Diem refused to hold the 1956 reunification elections promised at Geneva. He and his American backers knew Ho Chi Minh would win. This decision turned a temporary partition at the 17th parallel into a permanent communist&ndash;non-communist border.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Peasants Turned Against Diem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Diem&rsquo;s regime alienated rural South Vietnam in three reinforcing ways. First, his land policy reversed Vietminh land reforms in the south, returning land to absentee landlords and demanding back-rents stretching back to 1945. Second, he relied heavily on his own family: his brother <strong>Ngo Dinh Nhu</strong> ran the secret police; another brother was archbishop in Hue; his sister-in-law <strong>Madame Nhu</strong> dominated public life. The regime appointed Catholics &mdash; around 10% of the population &mdash; to most senior positions. Third, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1962&ndash;1963 South Vietnamese rural relocation programme that moved peasants into fortified villages to deny the Vietcong access; deeply unpopular and largely a failure.">Strategic Hamlets programme</dfn> from 1962 forcibly moved peasants into fortified villages to separate them from Vietcong influence. The relocation broke ancestral ties to village land and was enforced brutally; many peasants joined the Vietcong because of it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Buddhist Crisis of 1963</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The single most damaging episode for Diem&rsquo;s reputation was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1963 confrontation between South Vietnamese Buddhists and the Diem regime, sparked by a ban on flying Buddhist flags; it produced the famous self-immolation of Thich Quang Duc and helped trigger Diem&rsquo;s overthrow.">Buddhist crisis</dfn>. In May 1963 his government banned Buddhists from flying religious flags on Buddha&rsquo;s birthday in Hue, while allowing Catholic flags. Protests followed; troops fired on the crowd. On 11 June 1963 the elderly Buddhist monk <strong>Thich Quang Duc</strong> sat down in a Saigon street and burned himself to death in protest. Photographs of his self-immolation appeared on front pages worldwide. Madame Nhu publicly described the suicides as &lsquo;barbecues&rsquo;. Within weeks American confidence in Diem had collapsed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">South Vietnamese generals plotted Diem&rsquo;s removal with quiet US encouragement. The Kennedy administration signalled it would not block a coup. On 1&ndash;2 November 1963 the generals seized Saigon. Diem and his brother Nhu were captured trying to flee a Catholic church and were murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier. Three weeks later, on 22 November 1963, Kennedy himself was assassinated in Dallas. South Vietnam plunged into a year of political instability with seven different governments in 1964 alone &mdash; the perfect environment for the Vietcong to grow.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Vietcong: Aims, Support, Leadership</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In December 1960, communist activists in the south formed the <strong>National Liberation Front (NLF)</strong> &mdash; the political organisation Diem and the Americans dismissively called the <dfn class="term" data-def="&lsquo;Vietnamese communists&rsquo; &mdash; the South Vietnamese communist insurgency, formally the National Liberation Front (NLF), formed in 1960. Its armed wing was the People&rsquo;s Liberation Armed Forces.">Vietcong</dfn> (a contraction of &lsquo;Vietnamese communist&rsquo;). Its aims were three: overthrow Diem&rsquo;s regime, expel American influence, and reunify Vietnam under Hanoi&rsquo;s leadership. It drew support from former Vietminh fighters who had stayed in the south after 1954, peasants angered by land policy and the Strategic Hamlets, and intellectuals opposed to Diem. Hanoi sent advisers, weapons and Northern-trained cadres south through the <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of jungle paths and roads through Laos and Cambodia that allowed Hanoi to send troops, weapons and supplies to the Vietcong in South Vietnam, named after Ho Chi Minh.">Ho Chi Minh Trail</dfn>, the network of jungle paths and roads winding through Laos and Cambodia.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">How Guerrilla Tactics Worked</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Vietcong fought a different war from the conventional armies of Korea. <dfn class="term" data-def="Irregular warfare in which small mobile forces fight a stronger conventional army through ambush, sabotage, hit-and-run raids and integration with civilian populations.">Guerrilla warfare</dfn> traded conventional firepower for mobility, surprise and political integration. Vietcong fighters lived among villagers, worked in the rice paddies by day and ambushed government patrols by night. They operated without uniforms or fixed bases; suspected collaborators were assassinated, and a parallel Vietcong administration collected taxes and recruited soldiers in many rural districts. Tunnel networks &mdash; most famously at Cu Chi north-west of Saigon, where over 200km of tunnels were eventually excavated &mdash; let fighters disappear underground when conventional forces arrived. Punji-stake traps, booby-trapped paths and unexploded American ordnance were repurposed against the army that had dropped it.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Examiners flag this distinction often. The Vietminh was Ho Chi Minh&rsquo;s nationalist coalition founded in 1941; it fought the French to 1954 and after Geneva became the political backbone of communist North Vietnam. The Vietcong (NLF) was the southern communist insurgency formed in 1960, six years later, with overlapping but distinct membership and a different theatre of war. Hanoi controlled both, but treating them as one in a narrative account loses precision &mdash; and marks.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the chain: Diem refuses elections &rarr; rural alienation &rarr; Buddhist crisis &rarr; coup &rarr; political chaos &rarr; Vietcong growth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By the time Diem fell in November 1963, the Vietcong had grown into a serious insurgency. Strategic Hamlets had failed, peasants were alienated, and Hanoi was sending men and weapons south down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. The conditions for full-scale US escalation under Johnson were now in place.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Ngo Dinh Diem refused the 1956 reunification elections promised at Geneva, turning a temporary partition into a permanent communist&ndash;non-communist border.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">Diem alienated rural South Vietnam through anti-Buddhist policy, land restoration to landlords, family rule and the failed Strategic Hamlets programme.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Vietcong (NLF), formed in 1960 and supplied via the Ho Chi Minh Trail, used guerrilla tactics that grew from peasant grievances; Diem&rsquo;s 1963 fall left a chaos in which the insurgency thrived.</li></ul>
