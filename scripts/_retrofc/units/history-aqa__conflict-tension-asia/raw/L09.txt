--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New President, A Wider War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Lyndon B. Johnson became president on 22 November 1963, twenty days after the coup that killed Diem. Johnson&rsquo;s personal preference was domestic reform &mdash; civil rights, Medicare, his &lsquo;Great Society&rsquo; programme &mdash; but he believed losing South Vietnam would be politically fatal. He inherited Kennedy&rsquo;s 16,000 advisers, a Vietcong insurgency that controlled large areas of the countryside, and a series of unstable South Vietnamese governments. Within his first year, Johnson would secure the legal authority to fight an open-ended war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Gulf of Tonkin Incident, August 1964</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">On 2 August 1964 the US destroyer USS Maddox, conducting electronic intelligence in the Gulf of Tonkin off the North Vietnamese coast, was engaged by three North Vietnamese torpedo boats. The Maddox returned fire and damaged the boats. Two days later, on the night of 4 August, the Maddox and a second destroyer reported a second attack in stormy conditions &mdash; a contact later widely judged to have been false sonar readings, but reported as confirmed at the time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Johnson moved quickly. On 7 August Congress passed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The August 1964 congressional resolution authorising the president to take &lsquo;all necessary measures&rsquo; against North Vietnamese aggression; effectively a blank cheque for war.">Gulf of Tonkin Resolution</dfn> by 88 votes to 2 in the Senate and unanimously in the House. The resolution authorised the president to take &lsquo;all necessary measures&rsquo; to repel armed attack. There was no formal declaration of war; the resolution was the legal basis for everything that followed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: the date, the Senate vote and what powers the Tonkin Resolution gave the president.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 7 August 1964 the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution passed Congress by 88 to 2 in the Senate. It gave the president authority to take &lsquo;all necessary measures&rsquo; against North Vietnamese aggression &mdash; effectively a blank cheque for war without a formal declaration.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Operation Rolling Thunder</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In February 1965 the Vietcong attacked the US base at Pleiku, killing several Americans. Johnson responded by launching <dfn class="term" data-def="The sustained US bombing campaign against North Vietnam from March 1965 to October 1968.">Operation Rolling Thunder</dfn> on 2 March 1965 &mdash; a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam designed to break Hanoi&rsquo;s will and choke off support for the Vietcong. The operation ran for three and a half years. <dfn class="term" data-def="A long-range American jet bomber capable of carrying very large conventional bomb loads; central to the Vietnam air war.">B-52</dfn> heavy bombers, fighter-bombers and helicopters dropped roughly 864,000 tonnes of explosives on North Vietnam &mdash; more than the United States dropped in the entire Pacific theatre of the Second World War.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Strategic-bombing theory assumed that pain would force a rational government to negotiate. North Vietnam did not behave that way. Targeting was constrained &mdash; Johnson would not bomb Hanoi&rsquo;s civilian centre or the port of Haiphong for fear of triggering Soviet or Chinese intervention. Aid from China and the USSR replaced losses faster than the bombs destroyed them. The North dispersed its industry, dug shelter networks and used a workforce of an estimated 500,000 mainly women to repair roads and bridges almost overnight. The Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia could not be cut. By 1968 the Pentagon&rsquo;s own analysts judged the campaign a strategic failure.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Vietcong Tactics and the US Ground Build-Up</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Vietcong fought a guerrilla war that neutralised American technological superiority. They moved at night, mined trails, set bamboo punji-stick traps, dug tunnel networks like the famous Cu Chi tunnels under US bases, and melted into peasant villages by day. They used the <dfn class="term" data-def="A network of supply paths through Laos and Cambodia used by North Vietnam to move troops and equipment to the Vietcong in the South.">Ho Chi Minh Trail</dfn> to move men and supplies into the South.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In March 1965 the first official US combat troops &mdash; 3,500 marines &mdash; landed at Da Nang to protect the airbase there. This was the moment ground combat by Americans, rather than advisers, began openly. The build-up was rapid: 184,000 by the end of 1965, 385,000 by the end of 1966, over 485,000 by 1967, peaking at 540,000 in 1968. General <dfn class="term" data-def="Commander of US forces in Vietnam from 1964 to 1968; architect of search-and-destroy and the body-count metric.">Westmoreland</dfn> believed in &lsquo;attrition&rsquo; &mdash; using superior firepower to kill enough Vietcong that Hanoi could not replace them. The reality was the opposite. North Vietnam matched US escalation step for step.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Napalm, Agent Orange and Civilian Cost</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The air war was not only about strategic targets. <dfn class="term" data-def="A jellied petroleum incendiary used by the US air force in Vietnam; sticks to skin and burns at intense heat.">Napalm</dfn> was used to clear jungle and burn villages suspected of harbouring Vietcong. Agent Orange, a chemical defoliant, stripped forest cover from millions of hectares. Iconic press images &mdash; a naked nine-year-old, Phan Thi Kim Phuc, fleeing a napalm strike in 1972 &mdash; ran on the front pages of every major newspaper in the world. By 1968 American TV news showed body bags coming home and burning villages every night, undermining official optimism.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Did Johnson Escalate Despite Doubts?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Johnson was trapped between his domestic agenda and his fear of being the president who lost Vietnam. He believed pulling out would discredit US commitments worldwide; he also believed full-scale war would discredit him at home. His &lsquo;graduated escalation&rsquo; tried to do both &mdash; just enough force to win, not enough to provoke China. The result was a pattern of half-measures that gave Hanoi time to adapt and gave the Vietcong space to expand. By the end of 1967 Johnson had committed nearly half a million US troops without a credible path to victory.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: which US base was attacked in February 1965, when did Rolling Thunder begin, and where did the first US combat troops land?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Vietcong attack on Pleiku in February 1965 triggered Operation Rolling Thunder, which began on 2 March 1965 and ran until October 1968. Three weeks later, on 8 March 1965, 3,500 US marines landed at Da Nang &mdash; the first openly committed American ground combat troops.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The Gulf of Tonkin incident in August 1964 gave Johnson a congressional resolution that effectively authorised open-ended war without a formal declaration.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Operation Rolling Thunder dropped more bombs on North Vietnam than the US dropped in the entire Pacific theatre of WWII, but failed to break Hanoi&rsquo;s will or stop the Ho Chi Minh Trail.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">By 1968 over 540,000 US troops were deployed; Westmoreland&rsquo;s attrition strategy could not defeat a guerrilla enemy that adapted faster than American firepower could destroy it.</li></ul>
