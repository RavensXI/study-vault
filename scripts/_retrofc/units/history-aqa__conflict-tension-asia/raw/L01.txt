--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the 1945 division of Korea and rival regimes set the peninsula on course for war by 1950.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country Cut in Half by Outsiders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Korea had been a single country for over a thousand years. From 1910 it was a colony of Imperial Japan, and Korean <dfn class="term" data-def="The political belief that a people sharing language, culture and history should govern themselves in their own state.">nationalism</dfn> was forged in resistance to harsh Japanese rule. When Japan surrendered in August 1945, Koreans expected independence. They did not get it. In a hurried decision taken in Washington over a single night, two American officers sketched a line on a National Geographic map at the <dfn class="term" data-def="The line of latitude that became the temporary 1945 dividing line, then the front line, of Korea.">38th parallel</dfn>. Soviet forces accepted Japanese surrender north of it, US forces south of it. The split was meant to be temporary. It was not.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who divided Korea, on what line, and in what year?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Korea was divided at the 38th parallel in August 1945 as a temporary arrangement between the USSR (north) and USA (south) to accept the Japanese surrender. By 1948 two rival states had hardened around that line.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Two Rival Regimes by 1948</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Talks on reunifying Korea collapsed almost immediately. In the Soviet north, the USSR installed <strong>Kim Il Sung</strong>, a Korean communist who had fought the Japanese as a guerrilla in Manchuria. In September 1948 the Democratic People&rsquo;s Republic of Korea was proclaimed, with Kim as leader. In the American south, the United States backed <strong>Syngman Rhee</strong>, a fiercely anti-communist exile who had spent decades campaigning for independence from the United States. The Republic of Korea was declared in August 1948 with Rhee as its first president.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Both leaders rejected partition and openly demanded reunification &mdash; on their own terms. Rhee crushed left-wing uprisings in the south with brutal violence, including the suppression of the Jeju Island uprising of 1948&ndash;49 in which thousands of civilians died. Kim built up a Soviet-equipped army in the north and tightened a one-party communist regime. Border clashes along the 38th parallel were already common before 1950, with thousands killed in skirmishes that neither side called a war. Refugees moved in both directions: roughly two million Koreans fled south between 1945 and 1950, and northern propaganda portrayed Rhee&rsquo;s regime as an American puppet government doomed to collapse.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Cold War Frame: Containment and China</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By 1948 the <dfn class="term" data-def="The post-1945 rivalry between the capitalist USA and communist USSR, fought through proxies, propaganda and arms races rather than direct combat.">Cold War</dfn> had hardened in Europe. President Truman&rsquo;s 1947 doctrine pledged American support to any &lsquo;free people&rsquo; threatened by communism &mdash; the policy of <dfn class="term" data-def="The US Cold War strategy of preventing the spread of communism beyond where it already existed, rather than rolling it back.">containment</dfn>. The Marshall Plan rebuilt western Europe, NATO formed in 1949, and the Berlin blockade of 1948&ndash;49 was a direct stand-off. Then, in October 1949, came a shock that would reshape Asia: Mao Zedong&rsquo;s communists won the Chinese Civil War. The defeated Nationalists fled to Taiwan, and the United States refused to recognise the new People&rsquo;s Republic of China.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">In American politics the communist victory in China was framed as a catastrophic defeat. Republicans accused Truman of &lsquo;losing China&rsquo;. Senator Joseph McCarthy began his anti-communist crusade in February 1950. Any further retreat in Asia was now politically unthinkable in Washington. From the communist side, Mao&rsquo;s success made revolution in Asia look unstoppable: when Kim asked Stalin and Mao for permission to invade the south, both eventually agreed, believing the Americans would not fight for Korea.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In January 1950 US Secretary of State Dean Acheson made a speech excluding Korea from America&rsquo;s &lsquo;defence perimeter&rsquo; in Asia. Kim and Stalin read this as a green light. They were wrong &mdash; but the speech is one of the most-cited triggers of the war.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Two Leaders, One Goal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By spring 1950 the Korean peninsula contained two governments that each claimed to rule the whole country. <strong>Kim Il Sung</strong> had spent eighteen months building a 135,000-strong North Korean People&rsquo;s Army, equipped with Soviet T-34 tanks and combat-experienced soldiers who had served with Mao&rsquo;s forces in China. <strong>Syngman Rhee</strong>, by contrast, led an unstable southern republic plagued by political assassinations, peasant uprisings and economic collapse. The Americans had deliberately limited Rhee&rsquo;s army to a 100,000-strong force without tanks or heavy artillery, fearing he would attack first.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Was Korea a Civil War or a Cold War Conflict?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Both at once. Korean nationalists on both sides genuinely wanted reunification &mdash; the conflict had deep local roots that pre-dated the Cold War. But the Cold War context turned a Korean civil quarrel into a global confrontation. Without Soviet weapons there would have been no Northern army powerful enough to attack; without American containment thinking there would have been no UN intervention to defend the south. Causation runs both ways.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three causes of the Korean War &mdash; one Korean, one American and one communist-bloc.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By June 1950 the causes of war were stacked: Korean nationalist division, two rival regimes under Kim and Rhee, the 1949 communist victory in China, the Acheson speech excluding Korea from US defence, and Stalin&rsquo;s green light for invasion after eighteen months of lobbying. Kim invaded on 25 June 1950, expecting a swift victory before Washington could respond. He had misjudged Truman&rsquo;s political position after the loss of China.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Korea&rsquo;s 1945 division at the 38th parallel was meant to be temporary, but produced two rival regimes by 1948 &mdash; Kim Il Sung&rsquo;s communist north and Syngman Rhee&rsquo;s anti-communist south.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">The 1949 communist victory in China and the Acheson speech of January 1950 changed the strategic picture, encouraging Stalin and Mao to back a Northern invasion.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The war was simultaneously a Korean civil war and a Cold War proxy conflict; both layers of causation matter for full marks.</li></ul>
