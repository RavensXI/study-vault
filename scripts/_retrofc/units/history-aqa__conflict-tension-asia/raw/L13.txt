--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Road to Paris</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Formal peace talks had opened in Paris in May 1968 after Johnson&rsquo;s partial bombing halt, but they spent four years deadlocked over procedural questions including the shape of the table. Real movement only came once Nixon&rsquo;s Secretary of State Henry Kissinger and North Vietnamese negotiator Le Duc Tho began secret bilateral talks in 1970. By the autumn of 1972 a draft agreement was close. Hanoi&rsquo;s Easter Offensive earlier that year had been beaten back and the Linebacker bombings had increased pressure to settle. South Vietnam&rsquo;s President Thieu objected fiercely to terms that left North Vietnamese troops inside the South.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The December 1972 Christmas bombings &mdash; Linebacker II &mdash; were partly aimed at Hanoi and partly at Saigon: showing Hanoi the cost of breaking talks, and showing Thieu that Nixon would still use force on the South&rsquo;s behalf. By January 1973 the deal was done.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Paris Peace Accords, January 1973</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The agreement signed on 27 January 1973 between the US, North and South Vietnam and the Vietcong; provided for US troop withdrawal and a ceasefire but left North Vietnamese forces in the South.">Paris Peace Accords</dfn> were signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Vietcong. The terms required: a ceasefire in place; US withdrawal of all military forces within sixty days; return of US prisoners of war; a vague commitment to political settlement between North and South. Critically, North Vietnamese troops already inside the South were not required to leave. <dfn class="term" data-def="North Vietnamese chief negotiator at the Paris talks; signed the 1973 Accords with Kissinger and shared the Nobel Peace Prize, which he refused to accept.">Le Duc Tho</dfn> and Kissinger were jointly awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize; Le Duc Tho refused to accept his.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: the date of the Paris Accords, the four signatories, and the key concession to Hanoi about NVA troops in the South.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Paris Peace Accords, signed 27 January 1973 by the US, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Vietcong, required US military withdrawal within sixty days and a ceasefire in place. Crucially, the deal did not require the roughly 150,000 North Vietnamese troops inside South Vietnam to withdraw &mdash; effectively conceding the future of the South.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">US Withdrawal and the Collapse of Political Will</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By 29 March 1973 the last US combat troops had left Vietnam. Around 591 American POWs were returned in Operation Homecoming. Nixon and Kissinger had publicly committed to defend South Vietnam if the North broke the ceasefire &mdash; including with renewed bombing. That commitment depended on continuing Congressional support and presidential authority. Both collapsed within eighteen months.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Watergate consumed Nixon. In June 1973 Congress passed the Case-Church Amendment banning further military operations in Indochina without congressional approval. In November 1973 Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon&rsquo;s veto, limiting presidential war-making power. On 9 August 1974 Nixon resigned to avoid impeachment. By the time North Vietnam launched its final offensive in spring 1975, Gerald Ford had no political authority and no legal power to renew air support. Congress slashed military aid to Saigon from 2.27 billion in 1973 to 700 million in 1975.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Fall of Saigon, 30 April 1975</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By late April 1975 North Vietnamese armoured columns were on the outskirts of Saigon. President Thieu had resigned on 21 April, accusing the Americans of betrayal. On 29 April the United States launched <dfn class="term" data-def="The April 1975 helicopter evacuation of US personnel and Vietnamese allies from Saigon as North Vietnamese forces closed on the city.">Operation Frequent Wind</dfn>, the largest helicopter evacuation in history. Over 18 hours, 7,000 Americans and Vietnamese were lifted from rooftops across the city to the US Seventh Fleet offshore. Iconic photographs of evacuees clambering up a ladder to a helicopter on a CIA building roof became the lasting image of American defeat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">On the morning of 30 April 1975, North Vietnamese tank 843 crashed through the gates of the Independence Palace in Saigon. Two hours later, the new South Vietnamese government broadcast its unconditional surrender. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The North Vietnamese capture of the South Vietnamese capital on 30 April 1975, ending the Vietnam War.">fall of Saigon</dfn> ended the war that the Domino Theory had said could not be allowed to happen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: when did Saigon fall, what was the helicopter evacuation called, and roughly how many people did it lift out of the city?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. Operation Frequent Wind, the day before, lifted around 7,000 Americans and Vietnamese allies from rooftops across the city in 18 hours. North Vietnamese tank 843 crashed through the gates of the Independence Palace; the war the United States had fought for over a decade ended in unconditional surrender.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Price of Conflict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The human cost was staggering. Around 58,000 Americans had died, with another 153,000 wounded. An estimated 1.1 million North Vietnamese and Vietcong soldiers, 250,000 ARVN soldiers and 2 million Vietnamese civilians had been killed across all parts of the conflict. Roughly 5 million hectares of forest had been defoliated. Cambodia and Laos suffered tens of thousands of additional dead from US bombing and post-war upheaval &mdash; the Khmer Rouge took power in Cambodia in 1975 and killed perhaps 1.7 million people in their own country between 1975 and 1979.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Vietnam was reunified in July 1976 as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam. The new government sent perhaps a million former South Vietnamese soldiers and officials to &lsquo;re-education camps&rsquo;. Around 800,000 people fled by sea between 1975 and 1995, becoming known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Vietnamese refugees, mostly from the South, who fled by sea between 1975 and the mid-1990s; tens of thousands died in the attempt.">boat people</dfn>; tens of thousands died in the attempt. Economic collapse, exacerbated by a US trade embargo and the loss of southern entrepreneurial life, was severe. Vietnam fought a brief border war with China in 1979 and occupied Cambodia from 1978 to 1989. The country only recovered economically after the Doi Moi market reforms began in 1986.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The American Reckoning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">For the United States, the impact was equally lasting. The &lsquo;Vietnam Syndrome&rsquo; &mdash; reluctance to commit ground forces in foreign wars &mdash; shaped foreign policy for two decades. The Veterans&rsquo; Memorial in Washington DC, opened in 1982, listed every American name. The credibility of the executive branch never fully recovered: the War Powers Resolution remained law, congressional scrutiny of intelligence increased, and public scepticism about military adventurism shaped responses to every later crisis from Iran to Iraq.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">The Paris Peace Accords of 27 January 1973, negotiated by Kissinger and Le Duc Tho, ended US combat involvement but left North Vietnamese troops inside the South &mdash; effectively conceding the future of South Vietnam.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Watergate, Congressional aid cuts and the War Powers Resolution destroyed the political will to enforce the Accords; Saigon fell to North Vietnamese forces on 30 April 1975 after a 55-day campaign.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">The cost was 58,000 American dead, perhaps three million Vietnamese dead, the Khmer Rouge genocide in Cambodia and a generation-long &lsquo;Vietnam Syndrome&rsquo; that reshaped US foreign policy.</li></ul>
