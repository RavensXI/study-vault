--- DESCRIPTION ---
Two years of attrition near the 38th parallel, the 1953 armistice and its long Cold War shadow.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A War of Trenches and Hills</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the dramatic movements of 1950, the Korean War in 1951&ndash;1953 became a grinding contest along the 38th parallel. Once General Ridgway stabilised the line in February 1951 and pushed it back to the parallel by June, neither side could break through. UN technological superiority &mdash; airpower, artillery, naval support &mdash; could destroy any concentration of communist forces. But the rugged Korean terrain, dug-in Chinese positions and the Chinese willingness to absorb enormous casualties made a decisive UN advance impossible. The result was two years of <dfn class="term" data-def="Warfare in which both sides try to wear each other down through casualties and resource depletion rather than decisive movement.">attritional warfare</dfn> over fortified hills with names like Pork Chop Hill, Heartbreak Ridge and Triangle Hill.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the date peace talks opened, the location they ended at, and the date of the armistice.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Peace talks began in July 1951 at Kaesong, moved to <dfn class="term" data-def="The village near the 38th parallel where Korean War armistice talks were held from October 1951 and where the armistice was signed on 27 July 1953.">Panmunjom</dfn> in October 1951 and continued for two more years before an armistice was signed on 27 July 1953. No formal peace treaty has ever been signed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Talks Took Two Years</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Both sides agreed by mid-1951 that they could not win outright. The hard question was prisoners of war. The communists demanded the return of all prisoners under the Geneva Conventions. But around half of the 132,000 Chinese and North Korean prisoners held by the UN said they did not want to go back &mdash; many had been forcibly conscripted from Mao&rsquo;s defeated Nationalist armies. The US insisted on <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that prisoners of war should not be returned against their will to the country they had fought for.">voluntary repatriation</dfn>. Beijing refused. For two years, both sides fought on while diplomats argued about prisoners. Tens of thousands of soldiers died in these talking months for objectives measured in metres of hilltop.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">What Broke the Deadlock</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Three changes pushed both sides to settle in 1953. In January, Dwight D. Eisenhower took office as US president, having promised to end the war. Eisenhower hinted &mdash; carefully &mdash; that nuclear weapons might be used if talks failed; the threat may have been real or bluff. In March, Stalin died. The new Soviet leadership wanted to ease East-West tensions. In June, the prisoner deadlock was broken: prisoners would be handed to a neutral commission, with non-returners eventually granted asylum. Syngman Rhee tried to sabotage the deal &mdash; releasing 27,000 anti-communist prisoners unilaterally &mdash; but the US held the line.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953 by representatives of the UN Command, the Korean People&rsquo;s Army and the Chinese People&rsquo;s Volunteer Army. South Korea refused to sign. The agreement created a 4km-wide <dfn class="term" data-def="The 4km-wide buffer zone that has separated North and South Korea since the 27 July 1953 armistice.">Demilitarized Zone (DMZ)</dfn> running 250km across the peninsula, broadly along the 38th parallel but with adjustments for the actual front line. No peace treaty has ever been signed: technically, the Korean War has never ended.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Cost</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The war was catastrophic for Korea. Around 2&ndash;3 million Korean civilians died on both sides &mdash; many from US bombing of the north that left no major city standing. The US dropped more bomb tonnage on Korea than it had in the entire Pacific theatre of the Second World War. Around 215,000 South Korean and 400,000 North Korean soldiers were killed, alongside 36,000 Americans, 600,000 Chinese (including Mao&rsquo;s eldest son, killed in a US airstrike) and roughly 4,000 troops from other UN nations. Britain lost over 1,100 dead, including at the Battle of the Imjin River in April 1951 where the Gloucestershire Regiment&rsquo;s &lsquo;Glorious Glosters&rsquo; held off a much larger Chinese force at the cost of being almost wiped out. The economic cost to the United States exceeded $30 billion at the time &mdash; equivalent to several hundred billion in modern terms.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">From the US perspective, Korea was the textbook case of <dfn class="term" data-def="The argument that the Korean War vindicated the US strategy of preventing communism from spreading: South Korea remained outside the communist bloc.">containment achieved</dfn>. South Korea was preserved; communism was halted at the line where it had begun in 1950. From a Korean perspective, the war ended where it had started, with the country still divided, but with three million dead and most of the north destroyed by bombing &mdash; hardly a victory. Both views are correct in their own terms; understanding why these views differ is exactly the kind of comparative judgement examiners reward.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Long Shadows over Asia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The Korean War shaped the rest of the Cold War in Asia. The UN was confirmed as a vehicle for collective security, but in practice as an instrument of US policy. NATO solidified rapidly: West Germany was rearmed by 1955 because Korea showed that Soviet-backed conventional invasion was a real risk. US-China relations froze; America blocked PRC entry to the UN until 1971. Most importantly for AQA, the war made the Vietnam War almost inevitable. Eisenhower took office in January 1953 with a doctrine of containing communism in Asia &mdash; just as France was losing its war against the Vietminh in Indochina.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three long-term consequences of the Korean War for the Cold War in Asia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Korean War demonstrated that limited Cold War wars in Asia were possible without nuclear escalation, hardened US-China hostility for two decades, and committed the US to containing communism elsewhere in Asia &mdash; including, fatefully, in Vietnam.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">From mid-1951 the Korean War became attritional warfare along the 38th parallel, with neither side able to break through; peace talks dragged on for two years over the issue of prisoner repatriation.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">The 27 July 1953 armistice signed at Panmunjom created the Demilitarized Zone but no formal peace treaty &mdash; the war is technically still unfinished.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">For the US, Korea showed containment could work in Asia at acceptable cost; this confidence shaped the decision to commit to South Vietnam later in the decade.</li></ul>
