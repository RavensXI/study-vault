<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Prayer?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Communication with God; the Catechism defines it as the raising of one's mind and heart to God, or the requesting of good things from God.">Prayer</dfn> is at the heart of Catholic spiritual life. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC 2559) defines prayer as &ldquo;the raising of one&rsquo;s mind and heart to God or the requesting of good things from God.&rdquo; This definition captures two directions: the movement of adoration and attention towards God (&ldquo;raising the mind and heart&rdquo;), and the movement of petition (&ldquo;requesting good things&rdquo;). Catholics believe that prayer is not a human technique but a relationship &mdash; God always takes the first step, inviting the person to respond. The Catechism further describes prayer as a gift, a covenant and a communion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Catholic prayer takes many forms. <dfn class="term" data-def="Prayer using fixed texts handed down by the Church, such as the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary and the Act of Contrition; said in the same words each time.">Formulaic prayer</dfn> (also called set prayer or formal prayer) uses fixed texts &mdash; the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Rosary, the Divine Office. These prayers are inherited from scripture and tradition, expressing the faith of the whole Church. <dfn class="term" data-def="Spontaneous prayer in a person's own words, arising from their particular situation and emotions at the time; also called informal or extemporary prayer.">Extempore prayer</dfn> (also called spontaneous or informal prayer) is made up by the individual in their own words, expressing personal needs and emotions freely. Both forms are valued in Catholic tradition: set prayers provide the language of the whole Church and anchor the individual in its tradition; extempore prayer allows an honest, personal response to God from the specifics of one&rsquo;s own life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does the Catechism define prayer as, and what two forms of Catholic prayer can you name with a brief description of each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Catechism (CCC 2559) defines prayer as &ldquo;the raising of one&rsquo;s mind and heart to God.&rdquo; Formulaic prayer uses fixed texts inherited from the Church&rsquo;s tradition (e.g. the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer). Extempore prayer uses the believer&rsquo;s own words in the moment. Both are used in Catholic communities across Britain and throughout the world.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Lord&rsquo;s Prayer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The most important formulaic prayer in Christianity is the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer, also called the &ldquo;Our Father&rdquo; from its opening words. Jesus taught it to his disciples in response to their request, &ldquo;Lord, teach us to pray&rdquo; (Matthew 6:9&ndash;13; also Luke 11:1&ndash;4). In the version recorded in Matthew 6, the prayer opens with &ldquo;Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name&rdquo; &mdash; an address that simultaneously honours God as transcendent (&ldquo;in heaven&rdquo;) and intimate (&ldquo;Father&rdquo;). It then moves through petitions: the coming of God&rsquo;s kingdom, the gift of daily bread, the forgiveness of sins, and the request for protection from evil.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Catechism devotes a substantial section to explaining each petition of the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer (CCC 2759&ndash;2865), calling it &ldquo;the summary of the whole gospel&rdquo; (Tertullian) and &ldquo;the most perfect of prayers&rdquo; (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, 83:9). The Lord&rsquo;s Prayer is prayed at every Catholic Mass, at Morning and Evening Prayer, and at Catholic baptisms and funerals. In British Catholic communities, it is often said or sung together at the graveside or crematorium as part of the committal rite.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Intercessory Prayer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Prayer offered on behalf of others, asking God to act in their lives; a key form of Catholic prayer that includes prayer for the living and for the dead.">Intercessory prayer</dfn> is the act of praying on behalf of others &mdash; bringing their needs before God. Catholics regard intercessory prayer as an expression of charity: the spiritual equivalent of feeding the hungry or visiting the sick. The Catechism teaches that &ldquo;intercession is a prayer of petition which leads us to pray as Jesus did&rdquo; (CCC 2634). Jesus himself is described in the New Testament as interceding for his followers before God (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25). Catholics also believe that Mary and the saints intercede for the living, which is why prayers such as the Hail Mary include the words &ldquo;pray for us sinners, now and at the hour of our death.&rdquo;</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Prayer for the Dead and Masses Offered for the Deceased</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A distinctive feature of Catholic practice is prayer for those who have already died. This flows directly from the doctrine of purgatory: if the soul continues after death in a process of purification before entering heaven, then prayer by the living can assist that process. The Catechism (CCC 1030&ndash;1032) states that the Church &ldquo;commends almsgiving, indulgences and works of penance undertaken on behalf of the dead.&rdquo;</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The most powerful form of prayer for the dead in Catholic tradition is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Mass celebrated with the explicit intention of seeking God's mercy for the soul of a person who has died; the central act of Catholic intercession for the deceased.">Mass offered for the dead</dfn> (also called a Requiem Mass or Mass of intention). When a priest celebrates Mass and dedicates it to the repose of a specific soul, Catholic theology understands this as applying the merits of Christ&rsquo;s sacrifice (made present in the Eucharist) to the needs of that person. This is why Catholics commonly request Masses to be said for deceased relatives &mdash; on the anniversary of their death, on All Souls&rsquo; Day (2 November) and in November generally, the month the Church dedicates to prayer for the dead.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain in three sentences why Catholics pray for the dead and how a Mass offered for a deceased person is believed to help them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Catholics pray for the dead because the doctrine of purgatory teaches that souls may still need purification before entering heaven. Offering Mass for the dead applies the merits of Christ&rsquo;s sacrifice to the needs of that soul. In British Catholic communities, Masses are regularly offered for the deceased at death anniversaries and throughout November, All Souls&rsquo; month.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Catholic Prayer for the Dead in British Communities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In British Catholic parishes, prayer for the deceased takes several communal forms. Every Mass includes prayers for the dead in the Eucharistic Prayer. All Souls&rsquo; Day (2 November) involves special Masses and the reading of the names of parishioners who have died in the past year. Many parishes maintain a &ldquo;book of the dead&rdquo; &mdash; a book of remembrance placed near the altar throughout November into which families write the names of their deceased relatives, who are then remembered at Mass. Candlelit vigils for the dead, the visiting and tidying of family graves, and the lighting of candles before statues of Our Lady or the saints are all forms of popular piety that express Catholic belief in the communion of the living and the dead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The theological concept underlying all of these practices is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Catholic teaching that all those united to Christ — the living faithful, the souls in purgatory, and the saints in heaven — form one community in prayer and love.">Communion of Saints</dfn>. The Catechism (CCC 946&ndash;962) teaches that the Church on earth, the souls in purgatory and the saints in heaven are united in a single spiritual community. Prayer flows in both directions: the living pray for the souls in purgatory, and the saints in heaven intercede for the living. Death does not break the bonds of charity &mdash; it simply changes their form.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">A Humanist or secular perspective rejects the premise of prayer for the dead entirely: since consciousness ceases at death, there is no soul to assist. From this viewpoint, prayers for the dead are psychologically valuable for the bereaved &mdash; they provide comfort and a sense of continuing connection &mdash; but have no effect on the deceased. Humanist commemorations of the dead focus on memory and the impact of the person&rsquo;s life on the living, rather than petition to God. This contrasts sharply with the Catholic conviction that love and prayer genuinely transcend death, because of the Communion of Saints and the reality of eternal life.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Catechism (CCC 2559) defines prayer as &ldquo;the raising of one&rsquo;s mind and heart to God.&rdquo; Catholic prayer includes formulaic prayer (fixed texts, e.g. the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer from Matthew 6:9&ndash;13) and extempore prayer (the believer&rsquo;s own words). Both are practised in Catholic communities in Britain and worldwide.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Intercessory prayer &mdash; praying on behalf of others, including the dead &mdash; is a key Catholic practice. Prayer for the dead flows from the doctrine of purgatory: souls may still need purification before heaven, and the prayers of the living (including Masses offered for the deceased) can assist them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Communion of Saints teaches that the Church on earth, souls in purgatory and the saints in heaven form one community in prayer. Death does not sever the bonds of love &mdash; Catholics continue to pray for, and receive the intercession of, those who have died.</li>
</ul>
