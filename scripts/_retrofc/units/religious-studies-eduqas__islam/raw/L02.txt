<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five Pillars: The Structure of Muslim Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Five Pillars of Sunni Islam are the five core acts of worship and practice that every Muslim is obligated to perform. They are not merely external rituals but the framework through which submission (<em>islam</em>) to Allah is expressed in everyday life. Together they provide a Muslim with a structure that integrates worship, charity, fasting, and pilgrimage into the fabric of existence. The five pillars are: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Islamic declaration of faith, the first Pillar: 'There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah.'">Shahadah</dfn> (declaration of faith), <dfn class="term" data-def="The ritual prayer performed five times daily by Muslims, the second Pillar of Islam.">Salat</dfn> (prayer), <dfn class="term" data-def="The obligatory alms tax paid by Muslims, the third Pillar — typically 2.5% of savings held for one lunar year.">Zakat</dfn> (almsgiving), <dfn class="term" data-def="Fasting during the month of Ramadan, the fourth Pillar of Islam.">Sawm</dfn> (fasting), and <dfn class="term" data-def="The pilgrimage to Mecca, the fifth Pillar — obligatory for every Muslim who is physically and financially able.">Hajj</dfn> (pilgrimage to Mecca).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Shahadah</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Shahadah &mdash; &ldquo;There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah&rdquo; &mdash; is both the entry point to Islam and its constant foundation. Sincere utterance of the Shahadah constitutes conversion to Islam. The Adhan (call to prayer, which contains the Shahadah) is whispered into the ear of a newborn at birth. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Islamic birth ceremony, typically held on the seventh day after birth, including shaving the baby's head, giving a name, and slaughtering an animal for charity.">aqiqah</dfn> &mdash; a separate ceremony &mdash; is held on the seventh day after birth and includes naming the child. It is recited in the call to prayer five times a day. Many Muslims aspire for the Shahadah to be the final words spoken or heard at death. Reciting the Shahadah in the presence of witnesses marks the formal entry of a convert into the Muslim community (<em>ummah</em>).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state the Shahadah in English and give two occasions when it is recited during a Muslim's life.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Shahadah (&ldquo;There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is his messenger&rdquo;) is the first Pillar. It is recited via the Adhan at birth, in every call to prayer, upon conversion, and ideally at death, making it the bookends of Muslim life. The aqiqah is a separate naming ceremony on the seventh day.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Salat: Ritual Prayer Five Times Daily</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Salat is the second Pillar and the most visible daily expression of submission to Allah. Muslims are required to pray five times each day: at dawn (<em>Fajr</em>), midday (<em>Zuhr</em>), afternoon (<em>Asr</em>), sunset (<em>Maghrib</em>), and night (<em>Isha</em>). The Qur&rsquo;an commands prayer and links it directly to moral life: Qur&rsquo;an 29:45 teaches that prayer restrains one from shameful and unjust deeds. Each prayer involves a set sequence called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A unit of Islamic prayer consisting of a set sequence of standing, bowing, prostrating, and sitting positions.">rakat</dfn> (plural: <em>rakat</em>), with physical positions &mdash; standing, bowing (<em>ruku</em>&rsquo;), prostrating (<em>sujud</em>), and sitting &mdash; each with specific Arabic phrases. The different prayers require different numbers of rakat (Fajr has two, Zuhr has four, and so on).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Before praying, Muslims must perform <dfn class="term" data-def="The ritual washing performed before Islamic prayer, involving washing the hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head, and feet in a set order.">wudu</dfn> (ritual washing), which involves cleaning the hands, mouth, nostrils, face, arms up to the elbow, head, and feet in a prescribed order. Wudu purifies the person physically and mentally, preparing them to stand before Allah. If water is unavailable, a symbolic dry purification using sand or earth (<em>tayammum</em>) is permitted. The worshipper also sets an intention (<em>niyyah</em>) before beginning, ensuring the prayer is a deliberate, conscious act of worship rather than a mechanical routine.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Prayer can be performed anywhere clean (at home, at work, outdoors), with the worshipper facing the direction of Mecca (<em>qibla</em>). Prayer at the mosque, however, is encouraged, especially the Friday midday prayer (<dfn class="term" data-def="The Friday congregational prayer, held at midday in the mosque and obligatory for Muslim men.">Jummah</dfn>), which is a communal act of worship. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The call to prayer sounded five times a day from the mosque, summoning Muslims to Salat.">Adhan</dfn> (call to prayer) is sung from the mosque to signal each prayer time. In addition to Salat, Muslims may also engage in <dfn class="term" data-def="Personal, informal supplication or petition to Allah, outside the set ritual of Salat.">du’ah</dfn>, which is private, informal supplication &mdash; speaking to Allah in one&rsquo;s own words about personal needs and concerns.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Zakat is the third Pillar &mdash; the obligatory payment of a portion of savings to those in need. The standard rate is 2.5% of any savings held for one full lunar year above the minimum threshold (<em>nisab</em>). Zakat may be used for a set list of purposes specified in the Qur&rsquo;an, including support for the poor, freeing those in debt, and supporting travellers in need. It is not regarded as charity in the Western sense of voluntary generosity; it is a religious duty that purifies wealth. The Arabic word &ldquo;zakat&rdquo; means &ldquo;purification&rdquo; &mdash; giving away a portion of one&rsquo;s wealth purifies what remains.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">In addition to Zakat, Islam strongly encourages voluntary charity called <dfn class="term" data-def="Voluntary charitable giving by Muslims, over and above the obligatory Zakat.">saddaqah</dfn>. While Zakat is a precise calculation with a fixed rate, saddaqah can be any act of generosity &mdash; giving money, sharing food, or even a smile directed at another person. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) is reported in the Hadith to have said that every act of goodness is saddaqah.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sawm: Fasting in Ramadan</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Sawm is the obligatory fast during the month of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ninth month of the Islamic calendar, during which Muslims fast from dawn to sunset as the fourth Pillar of Islam.">Ramadan</dfn>, the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. Qur&rsquo;an 2:183 commands the fast: the verse addresses those who believe and commands them to fast as it was prescribed for those before them, so that they may become God-fearing. During Ramadan, adult Muslims abstain from all food, drink (including water), smoking, and sexual relations from the first light of dawn until sunset. The pre-dawn meal (<em>suhoor</em>) sustains them through the day; the fast-breaking meal (<em>iftar</em>) at sunset begins with dates and water, following the Prophet&rsquo;s example.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The purposes of Sawm are multiple: self-discipline, empathy with those who go hungry, gratitude for Allah&rsquo;s provision, and heightened spiritual focus. Ramadan is also the month in which the Qur&rsquo;an was first revealed, making it a time of intensified Qur&rsquo;an recitation and prayer. Those exempted from fasting include the sick, pregnant or breastfeeding women, travellers, the elderly, and children. Rules about what food is permissible (<dfn class="term" data-def="Permitted according to Islamic law, used especially of food and drink.">halal</dfn>) and forbidden (<dfn class="term" data-def="Forbidden according to Islamic law, used especially of food, drink, and conduct.">haram</dfn>) apply throughout the year; during Ramadan, Muslims pay even closer attention to these boundaries.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Hajj: Pilgrimage to Mecca</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hajj is the fifth Pillar &mdash; a pilgrimage to Mecca in Saudi Arabia that every Muslim who is physically able and financially capable must perform at least once in their lifetime. It takes place during the Islamic month of Dhul Hijjah and draws millions of pilgrims from around the world. Qur&rsquo;an 3:97 underlines the duty of pilgrimage for those who are able. The Hajj commemorates the trials of the Prophet Ibrahim (Abraham) and his family, and the events it re-enacts are rooted in the pre-Islamic history of Mecca.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">On arrival in Mecca, pilgrims enter a state of ritual purity (<em>ihram</em>), wearing two plain white sheets that remove all distinctions of wealth, nationality, and status. Key rituals of the Hajj include: circling the Ka&rsquo;bah seven times (<em>tawaf</em>); walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa; standing in prayer at the plain of Arafah; throwing stones at three pillars in Mina to symbolise rejecting the devil (<em>Shaytan</em>); and the sacrifice of an animal (<em>qurbani</em>) commemorating Ibrahim&rsquo;s willingness to sacrifice his son. The pilgrimage concludes with the celebration of <em>Id-ul-Adha</em>. Hajj powerfully demonstrates the equality of all Muslims before Allah and the unity of the global Muslim community (<em>ummah</em>).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the five Pillars in order and write one sentence explaining the significance of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Five Pillars are: Shahadah (declaration of faith), Salat (five daily prayers), Zakat (2.5% alms tax), Sawm (Ramadan fast, commanded in Qur&rsquo;an 2:183), and Hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca). Together they form the practical structure of Muslim life and submission to Allah.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Five Pillars &mdash; Shahadah, Salat, Zakat, Sawm, and Hajj &mdash; form the core obligations of Sunni Muslim life. Each integrates worship with ethics: Salat restrains sin (Qur&rsquo;an 29:45), Zakat purifies wealth, Sawm builds God-consciousness (Qur&rsquo;an 2:183), and Hajj unites the ummah.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Salat requires wudu (ritual washing) and niyyah (intention) before five daily prayers, each consisting of set rakat. The Friday Jummah prayer at the mosque is a communal obligation for men. Du&rsquo;ah is additional personal supplication.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Hajj to Mecca is obligatory for those able to undertake it. Pilgrims wear identical ihram garments, removing all distinctions of status, and re-enact the trials of Ibrahim, affirming the unity and equality of the global Muslim community.</li>
</ul>
