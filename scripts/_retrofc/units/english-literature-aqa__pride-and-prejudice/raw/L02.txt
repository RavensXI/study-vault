--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Meryton Assembly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Meryton assembly ball in Chapter 3 is where the central conflict begins. Bingley is immediately charming and popular: he dances with Jane twice, signalling his interest. Darcy, however, refuses to dance with anyone outside his own party and delivers the novel’s most famous insult about Elizabeth: “She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Darcy’s remark is overheard by Elizabeth and shapes her entire opinion of him. Austen uses this moment to establish the novel’s central theme: <dfn class="term" data-def="A preconceived opinion formed without adequate knowledge or examination; Elizabeth’s prejudice against Darcy and his pride are both forms of hasty judgement based on first impressions.">prejudice</dfn> based on first impressions. Elizabeth decides Darcy is arrogant and dismissive; Darcy decides Elizabeth is beneath his notice. Both are wrong, but both cling to their initial judgements for much of the novel. Austen shows how <dfn class="term" data-def="An exaggerated sense of one’s own worth or social status; Darcy’s pride makes him disdainful of those he considers socially inferior.">pride</dfn> and prejudice feed each other: Darcy’s pride causes Elizabeth’s prejudice, and her prejudice reinforces her resistance to seeing his true character.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is Darcy’s insult at the assembly and how does it drive the plot?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” is the novel’s inciting incident. It establishes Darcy as proud and dismissive, creates Elizabeth’s prejudice against him, and sets up the central conflict: both characters must overcome their flawed first impressions before they can recognise each other’s true worth. Austen uses the word “tolerable” — meaning merely adequate — to show Darcy judging Elizabeth by appearance alone.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Elizabeth’s Wit and Intelligence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Elizabeth is Austen’s most celebrated heroine. She is intelligent, witty, and independent-minded in a society that valued female <dfn class="term" data-def="Behaviours and manners considered proper and acceptable in society; Regency women were expected to be modest, agreeable, and accomplished in music, drawing, and needlework.">propriety</dfn> over intellectual independence. She refuses to be intimidated by Darcy’s wealth or status and matches him in conversation with sharp, ironic dialogue. When Darcy begins to show interest, Elizabeth remains sceptical: “I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This line reveals Elizabeth’s self-awareness and her blind spot simultaneously. She acknowledges pride as a human failing but claims her own pride was “mortified” (wounded) rather than unjustified. Austen’s genius is in creating a heroine who is perceptive about everyone except herself. Elizabeth’s wit, which she uses as a defence mechanism, also prevents her from seeing beyond first impressions. She judges Darcy too quickly (prejudice) just as Darcy judges her too quickly (pride).</p>

Jane is trusting to a fault — she always sees the best in people — while Bingley is agreeable and easily influenced. Their separation (engineered by Darcy and Bingley’s sisters) shows how external forces can destroy a genuine connection when one or both partners lack the strength to resist social pressure.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Title: Pride and Prejudice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Austen’s original title was <em>First Impressions</em>, which she later changed to <em>Pride and Prejudice</em>. The new title is more complex because it assigns faults to both protagonists. Darcy suffers from <strong>pride</strong> — an excessive sense of his own importance based on his wealth and status. Elizabeth suffers from <strong>prejudice</strong> — a fixed opinion formed too quickly, based on Darcy’s initial rudeness and Wickham’s charming lies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">But Austen complicates this neat division. Darcy is also prejudiced (against the Bennets’ lower social status) and Elizabeth is also proud (her quick judgements reflect confidence in her own perceptiveness). The novel argues that pride and prejudice are two sides of the same coin: both involve judging others without adequate understanding. The characters’ growth requires them to recognise their own failings before they can see each other clearly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down how both Darcy and Elizabeth display both pride AND prejudice.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Darcy has pride (social superiority) and prejudice (against the Bennets’ class). Elizabeth has prejudice (against Darcy based on first impressions) and pride (in her own judgement). Both must recognise their flaws: Darcy must learn humility; Elizabeth must learn to question her assumptions. The novel’s resolution requires mutual growth, not one-sided change.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">Despite his initial dismissal, Darcy becomes increasingly drawn to Elizabeth. He notices her “fine eyes” and her intelligence. At the Netherfield ball, he seeks her out for conversation and asks her to dance. Austen uses dramatic irony: the reader begins to see Darcy’s growing admiration while Elizabeth remains oblivious, interpreting his attention as condescension. This gap between what the reader sees and what Elizabeth believes creates suspense and comedy simultaneously.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Darcy’s insult — “tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me” — creates Elizabeth’s prejudice and drives the central conflict of the novel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Both characters display pride AND prejudice: Darcy is proud of his status and prejudiced against lower classes; Elizabeth is proud of her judgement and prejudiced against Darcy based on first impressions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Austen uses dramatic irony through free indirect discourse: the reader sees Darcy’s growing admiration before Elizabeth does, creating comedy and suspense.</li></ul>
