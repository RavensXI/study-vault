--- DESCRIPTION ---
Darcy’s second proposal, Lady Catherine’s confrontation, and the novel’s resolution.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Lady Catherine’s Confrontation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Lady Catherine de Bourgh arrives at Longbourn to demand that Elizabeth promise never to accept Darcy’s proposal. She is outraged: “Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?” The word “polluted” reveals her view of class as a form of purity — Elizabeth’s lower status would contaminate the Darcy bloodline. Lady Catherine represents the most extreme, rigid form of <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive pride in one’s social position based on birth rather than merit; Lady Catherine believes rank automatically confers superiority.">class snobbery</dfn> in the novel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Elizabeth’s response is magnificent: “He is a gentleman; I am a gentleman’s daughter; so far we are equal.” She refuses to be intimidated. She adds: “I am only resolved to act in that manner, which will, in my own opinion, constitute my happiness, without reference to you, or to any person so wholly unconnected with me.” This declaration of independence — the right to make her own choices based on her own judgement — is the novel’s most powerful assertion of individual agency over social pressure.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does Elizabeth say to Lady Catherine and why is it important?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth tells Lady Catherine: “He is a gentleman; I am a gentleman’s daughter; so far we are equal.” She defines equality through character and birth, not wealth or connections. This scene demonstrates Elizabeth’s moral courage and her refusal to accept a class hierarchy based on money. Ironically, Lady Catherine’s visit has the opposite effect to what she intended: when Darcy hears that Elizabeth refused to deny the possibility, he takes it as a sign of hope.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Darcy’s Second Proposal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Darcy’s second proposal (Chapter 58) is everything the first was not. He does not insult Elizabeth’s family or dwell on the social gap between them. Instead, he speaks with humility and genuine feeling. He tells Elizabeth that his affections have not changed since the first proposal but asks whether hers have. This approach is crucial: Darcy no longer assumes he deserves Elizabeth’s love. He asks for it, acknowledging that she has the right to refuse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The conversation is characterised by mutual honesty. Elizabeth admits: “My feelings are so different from what they were… that I receive your assurances with pleasure and gratitude.” The word “gratitude” recurs — Elizabeth is grateful not for Darcy’s wealth but for his moral transformation and his secret rescue of her family. Austen presents the ideal proposal as one between <dfn class="term" data-def="A relationship where both partners have equal respect, honesty, and self-knowledge; Elizabeth and Darcy achieve this only after both have overcome their initial flaws.">equals</dfn>: both have grown, both have acknowledged their faults, and both choose each other freely.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The first proposal is a catalogue of Darcy’s pride: “In vain have I struggled” (Darcy’s actual opening — note: “against my will...” is Elizabeth’s paraphrase in her rebuttal) He lists the social obstacles as if they are Elizabeth’s fault. The second proposal is its opposite: humble, respectful, and focused on Elizabeth’s feelings rather than his own superiority. Austen structures the two proposals as a before-and-after of Darcy’s character. The first shows what pride looks like; the second shows what happens when pride is replaced by genuine humility. This structural contrast is one of the novel’s most effective techniques.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Resolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The novel contains four marriages: Charlotte and Collins (earlier), then Lydia and Wickham, Jane and Bingley, and Elizabeth and Darcy (the final resolutions). Elizabeth and Darcy marry for love and mutual respect. Jane and Bingley marry for genuine affection (Darcy having restored their connection). Lydia and Wickham remain unhappily married — their passion faded quickly, and Wickham’s debts continue. Austen uses these contrasting outcomes to deliver her final judgement: marriages based on understanding last; marriages based on superficial attraction or financial desperation do not.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Mr Bennet gives his blessing with a telling comment: “I know your disposition, Lizzy. I know that you could be neither happy nor respectable, unless you truly esteemed your husband.” The word “esteemed” — meaning respected and valued — captures Austen’s ideal. Love alone is not enough (Lydia and Wickham); financial security alone is not enough (Charlotte and Collins). The best marriage requires <dfn class="term" data-def="Deep respect and admiration for another person’s character; Austen presents esteem as the foundation of lasting love.">esteem</dfn> — a deep respect for the other person’s character that can only come from genuine self-knowledge.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down what Mr Bennet says about Elizabeth’s marriage and why ‘esteem’ matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mr Bennet says Elizabeth could not be happy unless she “truly esteemed” her husband. “Esteem” is Austen’s key word for the ideal marriage: it means deep respect based on knowing someone’s true character. Elizabeth and Darcy achieve this because both have overcome their flaws through self-knowledge. The novel’s ending argues that the best love is earned through moral growth, not bestowed by first impressions.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Novel’s Moral Vision</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Austen’s moral vision is nuanced: she does not reject class or wealth but argues they must be combined with personal merit. Darcy’s wealth is not a problem — his snobbery was. Elizabeth’s lower status is not a problem — her prejudice was. The novel suggests that a just society would value character over birth while acknowledging that social structures cannot be ignored. Elizabeth does not pretend class does not exist; she insists that it should not determine a person’s worth. This balanced, pragmatic idealism is what makes Austen’s moral vision enduringly relevant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Elizabeth’s defiance of Lady Catherine — “so far we are equal” — asserts personal worth over inherited status and is the novel’s strongest feminist moment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Darcy’s second proposal is humble, respectful, and asks for consent — the structural opposite of his first, proving his moral growth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The novel’s ideal is “esteem” — deep respect based on true knowledge of character. Love without understanding (Lydia) and security without love (Charlotte) both fall short.</li></ul>
