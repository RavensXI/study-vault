--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jane Austen and Regency England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane Austen published <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> in 1813, during the <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of British history (approximately 1811–1820) when King George III was deemed unfit to rule and his son acted as Prince Regent; Austen’s novels capture the social customs and class dynamics of this era.">Regency period</dfn>. She was the daughter of a country clergyman and lived among the minor gentry — the same social class she writes about. Austen never married, though she received at least one proposal, and her novels draw on intimate knowledge of the social pressures, financial anxieties, and marriage market that governed women’s lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Austen wrote with sharp <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of humour, irony, or exaggeration to expose and criticise human failings; Austen’s satire targets snobbery, materialism, and the absurdities of the marriage market.">satire</dfn> and a deceptively light touch. Her famous opening line — “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife” — appears to state a fact but actually satirises the Regency obsession with marriage and money. The “truth” is not universal at all; it reflects the desperate perspective of families like the Bennets, who need to marry their daughters to wealthy men because women had almost no other means of financial security.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does the opening line really mean and why is it ironic?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The opening line is <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of irony where the literal meaning differs from the intended meaning; Austen’s opening appears factual but actually mocks the Regency marriage market.">verbal irony</dfn>: it presents a social obsession as a universal truth. The real “truth” is that a single woman without fortune must be in want of a husband. Austen inverts the perspective to satirise a society where women’s worth is measured by their ability to attract wealthy men. This ironic tone — sharp, witty, and morally serious beneath the humour — defines the entire novel.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Marriage and Money</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In Regency England, marriage was the only respectable way for a woman to secure financial stability. Women could not inherit property under <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal rule that restricted inheritance to male heirs; the Bennet estate is entailed to Mr Collins because Mr Bennet has no sons.">entailment</dfn> laws, could not enter most professions, and had limited access to education. The Bennet family’s estate is entailed to their nearest male relative, Mr Collins, meaning that when Mr Bennet dies, his wife and five daughters will lose their home. Mrs Bennet’s desperate efforts to marry off her daughters are not just comic — they reflect a genuine economic crisis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Austen presents four different marriages to explore what makes a good union. <strong>Mr and Mrs Bennet</strong>: he married for beauty, she for security; they are incompatible and unhappy. <strong>Charlotte and Mr Collins</strong>: she marries for financial stability without love. <strong>Lydia and Wickham</strong>: she marries for passion without sense; he marries for money. <strong>Elizabeth and Darcy</strong>: they marry for mutual respect, love, and moral equality. Austen uses these four models to argue that the best marriage combines genuine feeling with rational judgement.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Charlotte’s marriage to Mr Collins is one of the novel’s most morally complex moments. Charlotte is twenty-seven, plain, and poor — she has no realistic prospect of a better offer. She tells Elizabeth: “I am not romantic, you know; I never was. I ask only a comfortable home.” Austen does not condemn Charlotte; she shows the brutal reality that drove intelligent women to marry foolish men. Charlotte makes the best decision available to her in a system that offers women almost no agency. Elizabeth’s shock reveals her own privilege — she can afford to wait for love because she has not yet felt the full weight of financial insecurity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Class and Social Hierarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Regency society was rigidly stratified. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The upper class of landowners who did not need to work; Darcy’s family belongs to this class, with an income of £10,000 a year and the grand estate of Pemberley.">landed gentry</dfn> (like Darcy) sat at the top of the social hierarchy below the aristocracy. Below them were the minor gentry (like the Bennets), then professionals, merchants, and tradespeople. The Bingley sisters sneer at the Bennets because Mrs Bennet’s brother is “in trade” — his money comes from business rather than land, making the family socially inferior in their eyes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Lady Catherine de Bourgh represents the most extreme form of class snobbery. She tells Elizabeth: “Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?” — treating Elizabeth’s lower social status as a form of contamination. Austen uses Lady Catherine as a satirical target: her pride is absurd and her authority is based entirely on birth, not merit. Elizabeth’s defiance of Lady Catherine — “He is a gentleman; I am a gentleman’s daughter; so far we are equal” — is one of the novel’s most powerful assertions of personal worth over inherited status.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the four marriages in the novel and what each represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four marriages represent different approaches to union: the Bennets (incompatible), Charlotte and Collins (pragmatic), Lydia and Wickham (reckless), and Elizabeth and Darcy (ideal). Austen argues that the best marriage combines love with rational judgement, mutual respect, and moral equality. The novel is a critique of a system that forced women to choose between love and financial survival.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Austen pioneered <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique that blends the narrator’s voice with a character’s thoughts, creating an intimate but ironic perspective; Austen uses it to show Elizabeth’s biases while allowing the reader to see beyond them.">free indirect discourse</dfn> — a technique where the third-person narrator blends with a character’s thoughts and language without direct quotation. This allows Austen to present Elizabeth’s perspective sympathetically while also exposing her errors. When Elizabeth believes Darcy is arrogant, the narrator’s tone seems to agree — but Austen plants clues that Elizabeth is wrong. The reader is drawn into Elizabeth’s prejudice and then forced to recognise it, creating a powerful experience of self-correction that mirrors Elizabeth’s own journey.</p>

</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">The opening line is verbal irony: it satirises the Regency marriage market where women’s worth was measured by their ability to attract wealthy husbands.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Austen presents four marriages to argue that the best union combines love, rational judgement, and moral equality — critiquing a system that forced women to choose between love and survival.</li>
