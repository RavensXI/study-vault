--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Elizabeth Bennet: Wit, Pride, and Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth is one of the most celebrated heroines in English literature. She is intelligent, witty, independent, and morally courageous. She refuses Collins, defies Lady Catherine, and rejects Darcy’s first proposal because it insults her dignity. But Austen also shows her flaws: she judges too quickly (Darcy), trusts too easily (Wickham), and is proud of her own perceptiveness. Her journey is one of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of recognising one’s own flaws and biases; Elizabeth’s self-knowledge develops through Darcy’s letter, Pemberley, and the Lydia crisis.">self-knowledge</dfn>: she must learn that intelligence is not the same as wisdom, and that quick judgement can be as dangerous as no judgement at all.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Her key quotation of self-recognition — “How despicably I have acted! I, who have prided myself on my discernment!” — is the moment she sees her own prejudice clearly. The pun on “prided” shows her recognising that her prejudice was a form of pride. From this point, Elizabeth becomes more cautious in her judgements, more open to evidence, and more willing to admit error. Austen presents this as the mark of true intelligence: not never being wrong, but being willing to change when proven wrong.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are Elizabeth’s main strengths and flaws, and how does she change?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Strengths:</strong> wit, intelligence, moral courage, independence. <strong>Flaws:</strong> quick judgement (prejudice), overconfidence in her own perception (pride), susceptibility to charm (Wickham). <strong>Growth:</strong> Darcy’s letter forces her to recognise her errors; Pemberley shows her Darcy’s true character; the Lydia crisis proves Darcy’s love. She learns that true discernment requires humility.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mr Darcy: Pride, Honour, and Humility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Darcy is initially presented as proud, aloof, and dismissive. His “tolerable” insult at the assembly creates an impression of arrogance that persists for half the novel. But Austen gradually reveals that Darcy’s pride conceals genuine virtues: he is loyal (to Bingley and Georgiana), generous (paying Wickham’s debts), honest (his letter), and responsible (Pemberley’s management). His flaw is not that he values himself highly but that he expresses it in ways that wound others.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Darcy’s growth is demonstrated through action rather than words. He tells Elizabeth: “You taught me a lesson… By you, I was properly humbled.” But the real proof is his secret rescue of the Bennet family — he overcomes both his pride (helping people he once disdained) and his prejudice (accepting the Bennets’ lower connections). Austen presents Darcy as a man whose best qualities were always there but needed Elizabeth’s honesty to bring them to the surface.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mr Bennet married for beauty; Mrs Bennet married for security. Neither married for mutual respect. Mr Bennet is intelligent but passive — he retreats to his library rather than managing his family. His wit masks a deep irresponsibility: he fails to control Lydia, fails to plan for his daughters’ futures, and uses sarcasm as a substitute for parental authority. Mrs Bennet is loud, embarrassing, and obsessed with marrying her daughters to wealthy men. But Austen’s satire is compassionate: Mrs Bennet’s desperation is understandable given the entailment. Their marriage stands as a warning: without esteem, even a comfortable partnership becomes empty.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Wickham and Lady Catherine: Contrasting Villains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Wickham represents vice disguised as charm; Lady Catherine represents authority without merit. Wickham’s smooth manners conceal dishonesty, predatory behaviour, and selfishness. Lady Catherine’s imperious manner reflects a belief that birth alone confers superiority. Both are <dfn class="term" data-def="Characters who serve as negative examples, demonstrating what the protagonists must avoid; Wickham shows the danger of charm without substance, Lady Catherine shows the danger of pride without merit.">anti-models</dfn> for the protagonists: Wickham shows what Darcy’s pride is NOT (malicious), and Lady Catherine shows what Elizabeth’s spirit should not become (bullying).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Austen’s sharpest satire is reserved for Collins, who combines sycophancy with pomposity. He worships Lady Catherine, lectures the Bennets, and proposes to Elizabeth as if conferring a great honour. He is comic but also threatening — he will inherit the Bennet estate, giving him real power over a family that despises him. Austen uses Collins to show how the entailment system rewards the undeserving while punishing the vulnerable.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down one key quotation for Elizabeth, Darcy, and Mr Bennet.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Elizabeth:</strong> “How despicably I have acted! I, who have prided myself on my discernment!” (self-recognition). <strong>Darcy:</strong> “You taught me a lesson… By you, I was properly humbled” (growth through honest feedback). <strong>Mr Bennet:</strong> “I know that you could be neither happy nor respectable, unless you truly esteemed your husband” (the novel’s moral standard for marriage).</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">Austen structures characters in deliberate pairs: <strong>Elizabeth/Jane</strong> (wit vs kindness), <strong>Darcy/Bingley</strong> (pride vs agreeableness), <strong>Wickham/Darcy</strong> (charm without substance vs substance without charm), <strong>Elizabeth/Charlotte</strong> (romantic idealism vs pragmatic realism), <strong>Lady Catherine/Mrs Bennet</strong> (snobbery from above vs desperation from below). Each pairing illuminates a different aspect of the novel’s themes. The most important is Wickham/Darcy: one looks like a gentleman but is not; the other looks unfriendly but IS a gentleman. Austen’s central argument is that appearance and reality rarely align.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Elizabeth is perceptive but prejudiced; her growth requires learning that intelligence without humility leads to error. Darcy is honourable but proud; his growth requires learning to express his virtues without condescension.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The Bennet marriage warns against unions based on surface attraction. Wickham and Lady Catherine represent opposite forms of social failure: charm without substance and authority without merit.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Austen structures characters in pairs to expose the gap between appearance and reality: Wickham looks like a gentleman but isn’t; Darcy looks unfriendly but is the novel’s truest gentleman.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n14">Character questions often ask “how does Austen present...” a character. Always analyse methods, not just describe behaviour. For Elizabeth: free indirect discourse. For Darcy: the shift from dialogue to action. For Mr Bennet: ironic wit masking failure. For Wickham: charm as a weapon. For Lady Catherine: imperious commands satirised through exaggeration.</p>
