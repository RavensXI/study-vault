--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Roman Theatre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Romans inherited theatre from the Greeks, but adapted it to their own tastes and social context. Roman theatre buildings differed from Greek ones in important ways: Greek theatres were cut into hillsides to use natural slopes for seating, while Roman theatres were freestanding stone structures built on level ground, using arched substructure to support the tiers. The seating area (cavea) was a semicircle rather than the more-than-semicircle of Greek theatres, and the stage building (scaenae frons) was an elaborate multi-storey stone backdrop decorated with columns, niches and statues. The stage itself was lower and wider than its Greek predecessor. Roman theatres in the prescribed sources include the large theatre at Pompeii, which was built into the slope of a hill and could seat an estimated 5,000 spectators.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Large Theatre at Pompeii</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The large theatre at Pompeii is a prescribed material source. Built in the second century BC and remodelled in the Augustan period, it is one of the oldest surviving examples of a Roman theatre. Its form combines elements of both Greek and Roman design. The cavea is cut partly into the slope of a hill (mons) and partly supported by arched substructure, giving it a transitional character between the purely Greek hillside theatre and the purely Roman freestanding design. The stage and the scaenae frons behind it have been largely lost, but inscriptions on the theatre and tombs nearby &mdash; including the <dfn class="term" data-def="A funerary monument erected at Pompeii by the freedwoman Naevoleia Tyche in honour of her husband Gaius Munatius Faustus, decorated with images including a ship and a bisellium, a two-seater magistrate's chair.">Tomb of Naevoleia Tyche</dfn> &mdash; provide evidence for the people who funded and used theatrical performances. Naevoleia Tyche was a freedwoman who funded a public building and was commemorated with images of a ship and a bisellium, suggesting she or her husband had held civic office and was proud of it.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n5">Roman drama ranged from literary comedy to popular mime. <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of Roman stage comedy featuring stock characters such as the cunning slave, the boastful soldier and the miserly old man, drawing on Greek New Comedy.">Roman comedy</dfn> (comoedia) was heavily influenced by Greek New Comedy. It relied on stock characters: the cunning slave (servus callidus) who outwits his master; the boastful soldier (miles gloriosus); the miserly old man (senex); the young man in love. Performances used masks, colourful costumes and exaggerated gestures so that audience members far from the stage could follow the action.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Mime (mimus) and pantomime (pantomimus) were even more popular with Roman audiences than literary comedy. Mime was a short, often bawdy, improvised or scripted performance without masks, featuring female performers as well as male &mdash; a significant difference from Greek drama, where all roles were performed by men. Pantomime was a more refined form: a solo dancer performed narrative scenes from myth using music and gesture, while a chorus narrated. The famous Juvenal passage about pantomime performers mocking the gods (Satires 6) shows that such performances were controversial as well as popular.</p>

</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Without looking, describe the social position of Roman actors and actresses. Were they respected? Use a specific source to support your answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Roman actors (and especially actresses) were legally classified as <dfn class="term" data-def="People of low social and legal standing in Rome, including actors, gladiators and prostitutes, who were excluded from normal civic rights.">infames</dfn> &mdash; people of low social standing who had forfeited normal civic rights. Yet popular performers could be idolised by audiences, causing tension with the elite. Pliny (Letters 7.24) records with disapproval that a senator was seen applauding a pantomime performer.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Chariot Racing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">If the amphitheatre offered Romans violence and drama, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Roman racetrack for chariot racing, oval in shape with a central dividing barrier (spina), typically holding more spectators than an amphitheatre.">circus</dfn> offered pure speed and the passionate tribal loyalty of the factions. Chariot racing was the oldest and arguably most popular form of public entertainment in Rome. Races were held in the circus, an oval track centred on a long narrow barrier called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central dividing barrier running down the middle of the Roman circus, around which chariots raced, decorated with monuments and lap counters.">spina</dfn>. Chariots raced seven laps around the spina. The standard unit was one race of seven laps, and a typical day of racing might feature up to twenty-four races.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Circus Maximus, Rome</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The greatest of all Roman circuses was the Circus Maximus in Rome, a prescribed material source. Sitting in the valley between the Palatine and Aventine hills, the Circus Maximus was the largest public building in the Roman world. In its imperial form it measured around 600 metres in length and 200 metres in width, with seating capacity estimated by ancient sources at well over 100,000 spectators &mdash; modern historians suggest the figure may have reached 150,000 or more. The starting gates (carceres) occupied one curved end of the track, arranged in a staggered line to give each chariot an equal distance to the first bend. The spina in the centre was decorated with obelisks, lap-counting mechanisms (seven wooden eggs and seven bronze dolphins were moved to count the laps), and temples. The Circus Maximus was a venue for religious processions as well as races; the games often honoured specific gods, and the procession (pompa) before racing was itself a religious ceremony.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the four racing factions at the Circus Maximus and write one thing that explains the intensity of fan loyalty to them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Charioteers and their horses competed for four colour-coded teams called <dfn class="term" data-def="The four competing teams in Roman chariot racing, identified by their colours: Blues (Veneti), Greens (Prasini), Reds (Russati) and Whites (Albati).">factions</dfn>: the Blues (Veneti), Greens (Prasini), Reds (Russati) and Whites (Albati). Support for a faction was inherited and passionate. The charioteer Gaius Appuleius Diocles, recorded in inscriptions, competed in over 4,000 races and won more than 1,400 &mdash; one of the most celebrated sporting careers in the ancient world.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Juvenal (Satires 11) describes the noise and excitement of the Circus Maximus in vivid terms, but from the perspective of a man who refuses to attend, preferring to read in peace while the rest of Rome empties into the races. His tone is one of superiority and mild contempt for the masses who are so easily swept up in faction loyalty. He contrasts the modern enthusiasm for chariot racing with what he sees as the more sober, martial values of earlier Romans.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Pliny the Younger (Letters 9.6) expresses a similar disdain. He writes to a friend confessing that he cannot understand the appeal of watching horses race round a track, when the fans care only for the colour of the cloth (the faction colours), not for the skill of the individual rider. Both sources reveal how chariot racing created an almost tribal loyalty that cut across social class &mdash; and both reveal the ambivalence of educated Romans towards this most popular form of entertainment.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Roman theatre differed from Greek theatre in key structural ways (freestanding semicircular cavea, elaborate scaenae frons). The large theatre at Pompeii is the prescribed material source. Actors were legally classified as infames despite their popularity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Circus Maximus was the largest public building in Rome, capable of holding over 100,000 spectators. Races were seven laps around the spina; the four factions (Blues, Greens, Reds, Whites) inspired intense and tribal loyalty.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Literary sources &mdash; Juvenal (Satires 11) and Pliny the Younger (Letters 9.6) &mdash; reveal elite ambivalence toward chariot racing and mass entertainment; both writers express superiority while acknowledging racing&rsquo;s enormous popular appeal.</li>
</ul>
