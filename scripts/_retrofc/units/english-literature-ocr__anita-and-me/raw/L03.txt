--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Anita Rutter: The Girl Meena Wants to Be</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Anita Rutter is the most important figure in Meena’s childhood world. She is everything Meena is not: blonde, confident, fearless and quintessentially English. When Meena first sees her, she is mesmerised: “She was the most glamorous girl I had ever seen.” Syal presents Anita through Meena’s adoring, uncritical gaze, allowing the reader to see both the attraction and the warning signs that Meena herself misses.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall two quotations that show Meena’s admiration of Anita.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Anita represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to act as one wishes, free from constraint or consequence.">freedom</dfn> to Meena — freedom from parental expectations, from cultural obligations, from the pressure of being “good.” Anita swears, steals, defies adults and roams the village unsupervised. To a child constrained by two sets of cultural rules, this is intoxicating.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Yet Syal carefully reveals the reality behind Anita’s bravado. Anita’s mother Deirdre is neglectful and chaotic — she “always looked half-finished” and conducts affairs openly while her children run wild. Anita’s father Roberto has abandoned the family. Anita’s toughness is not freedom but a <dfn class="term" data-def="A behaviour pattern that protects a person from emotional pain or vulnerability.">defence mechanism</dfn> — the armour of a child who has learned she cannot rely on anyone.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Power Dynamic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The friendship between Meena and Anita is defined by an unequal power dynamic. Anita is the leader; Meena is the follower. Anita sets the rules, decides the games and controls who is “in” or “out.” Meena accepts this because Anita’s approval feels like acceptance into Englishness itself. When Anita says “You’re my best friend, aren’t ya, Meena?” it is both an offer and a command.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Anita’s Troubled Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Syal draws a deliberate contrast between the Kumar and Rutter households. Where Meena’s home is warm, structured and full of family, Anita’s home is chaotic and emotionally barren. Deirdre serves tinned food and ignores her children. There are no books, no routines, no stability. Anita’s younger sister Tracey is a silent, neglected shadow. This contrast is not simply about “good” vs “bad” parenting — Syal links Deirdre’s failures to the wider <dfn class="term" data-def="The decline of traditional industries and the social problems that followed, including poverty, family breakdown and loss of community.">post-industrial decline</dfn> that has hollowed out Tollington.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how Syal contrasts the Kumar and Rutter households and what this reveals.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Kumar household represents cultural richness, emotional warmth and stability. The Rutter household represents the emotional poverty and family breakdown caused by economic decline. Syal uses this contrast to challenge the assumption that the immigrant family is the “problem” — it is actually the strongest, most functional family in Tollington.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Anita’s Vulnerability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Beneath her tough exterior, Anita is deeply vulnerable. She has been abandoned by her father and effectively abandoned by her mother. She uses cruelty and control to feel powerful in a world where she has no real power. Her treatment of Tracey — ignoring and belittling her — mirrors how she herself has been treated. Syal asks the reader to see Anita not just as a bully but as a product of her environment, making her a more complex and sympathetic character than she first appears.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Anita’s racism develops gradually through the novel. Early on, she treats Meena’s race as largely irrelevant — or at least, as less important than having a loyal follower. But as Sam Lowbridge’s influence grows and the political climate shifts, Anita begins to absorb the racist attitudes around her. By the end, she has aligned herself with Sam, making her friendship with Meena impossible. Syal shows how racism is not innate but learned — absorbed from family, community and political discourse.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Anita represents freedom and Englishness to Meena, but her bravado masks deep vulnerability caused by parental neglect.</li><li data-narration-id="n17">The Kumar/Rutter household contrast challenges the assumption that the immigrant family is the “problem” — it is actually the strongest family in Tollington.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">Anita’s racism is learned, not innate, developing as she absorbs Sam Lowbridge’s influence and the political climate around her.</li></ul>

The shift in how Meena sees Anita is one of the novel’s most important structural developments.</p>
