--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Character Analysis: Who Matters and Why</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">OCR rewards responses that analyse characters as <dfn class="term" data-def="Fictional creations shaped by a writer’s deliberate choices of language, action and context.">constructs</dfn> — not real people but creations shaped by Syal’s deliberate choices. For every character, ask: what does this character represent? How does Syal present them? Why has she made these choices?</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Meena Kumar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Meena is the novel’s narrator and protagonist. She is funny, bold, morally complex and desperate to belong. Her journey from childhood innocence to mature self-awareness drives the plot. Key quotations: “I do not have many memories of Tollington before Anita Rutter” (unreliable memory); “I was the only dark child for miles around” (otherness); her final decision to leave Tollington (growth).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three key quotations associated with Meena and what each reveals.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena functions as both protagonist and narrator, meaning the reader sees everything filtered through her perspective. Her unreliability as a narrator — lying, embellishing, misunderstanding adult situations — is a deliberate structural choice by Syal, allowing dramatic irony and humour while gradually revealing uncomfortable truths about race and class.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Anita Rutter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Anita is Meena’s foil: blonde, English, tough, neglected. She represents both the attraction and the danger of assimilation. Key quotations: “She was the most glamorous girl I had ever seen” (Meena’s idealisation); “You’re my best friend, aren’t ya?” (control disguised as affection). Her arc moves from charismatic rebel to racist bully, mirroring the political trajectory of Tollington itself.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mama is warm, cultured and quietly strong. She maintains Punjabi traditions while adapting to English life. Her cooking is a symbol of cultural identity. Papa is dignified, overqualified for his factory job, and practices patient integration. Together they represent the “model minority” ideal — working hard, not complaining, fitting in — yet Syal shows the emotional cost of this performance. Papa’s controlled anger after the racist attack reveals the exhaustion beneath his calm exterior.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Nanima</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Nanima is the most powerful character despite appearing late in the novel and not speaking English. She communicates through storytelling, food, physical affection and sheer presence. Her Partition stories give Meena a sense of historical depth — the Kumars’ story did not begin in Tollington. Nanima represents the cultural roots that Meena has been trying to escape but ultimately needs.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what Nanima represents and how she changes Meena.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nanima represents cultural heritage, resilience and the power of storytelling. She reconnects Meena with her Punjabi identity by showing her that the Kumar family’s story of displacement and survival is far larger than Tollington. Her visit is the catalyst for Meena’s rejection of Anita and embrace of her dual identity.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sam Lowbridge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Sam is charming, dangerous and pitiable in equal measure. He channels economic frustration into racist violence. Key moment: his outburst at the Spring Fete. Syal presents him as a product of post-industrial decline — explanation, not excuse. His relationship with Anita pulls her towards racism and away from Meena.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Aunties and Minor Characters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The “Aunties” function as a Greek chorus — commenting on events, providing comic relief and representing the diasporic community’s collective wisdom. Mrs Worrall is Tollington’s most sympathetic white character, showing genuine friendship to Mama, though her kindness has limits. Mr Ormerod and Mr Christmas represent casual, unexamined racism. Tracey Rutter, Anita’s silent, neglected sister, is a mirror for what Anita herself has suffered.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">When writing about characters in the OCR exam, always treat them as constructs. Do not say “Anita is mean” — say “Syal presents Anita as a product of neglect, whose cruelty functions as a defence mechanism.” This shows AO2 awareness of the writer’s craft.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each of the six main characters, identify one key quotation and one thematic purpose.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every character in Anita and Me serves Syal’s thematic purpose. Meena = dual identity. Anita = the danger of assimilation. Mama/Papa = the cost of integration. Nanima = cultural roots. Sam = racism as product of decline. Always connect character analysis to Syal’s wider message about race, class and belonging in 1960s Britain.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Every character serves Syal’s thematic purpose: Meena (dual identity), Anita (danger of assimilation), Mama/Papa (cost of integration), Nanima (cultural roots), Sam (racism as product of decline).</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Always treat characters as constructs — use “Syal presents” not “[Character] is.”</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The Kumars are the novel’s strongest, most functional family — Syal deliberately inverts the assumption that immigrant families are “the problem.”</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">In the OCR exam, use the phrase “Syal presents [character] as...” rather than “[Character] is...” This signals AO2 awareness — you understand that characters are constructs, not real people. For part (a), compare how Syal and the unseen writer create character through similar or different methods. For part (b), track how a character develops across the whole novel.</p>
