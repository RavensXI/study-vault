--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Britain in the 1960s and 70s: A Nation in Transition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Anita and Me is set in the fictional village of Tollington, based on the real village of Essington in the <dfn class="term" data-def="An industrial region in the West Midlands of England, known for coal mining and manufacturing.">Black Country</dfn>, where Meera Syal grew up. The novel is set in the late 1960s and early 1970s — a period of enormous social change in Britain. To understand the novel, you need to understand the world Syal was writing about and the tensions that shaped it.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three key facts about post-war immigration to Britain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The British Nationality Act 1948 gave citizens of Commonwealth countries the right to live and work in Britain. The ship <em>HMT Empire Windrush</em> arrived at Tilbury Docks in June 1948, carrying 492 passengers from the Caribbean — the symbolic start of mass post-war immigration.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Immigration from the Indian subcontinent increased significantly during the 1950s and 1960s. Families like the Kumars came to fill labour shortages in factories, foundries and the NHS. They were invited by the British government, yet often faced hostility from local communities. This contradiction — being invited yet unwelcome — is central to Meena’s experience in Tollington.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Enoch Powell and “Rivers of Blood”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">On 20 April 1968, Conservative MP <dfn class="term" data-def="A British politician whose anti-immigration speech in 1968 became one of the most controversial speeches in modern British history.">Enoch Powell</dfn> delivered a speech in Birmingham warning that continued immigration would lead to violence. He quoted the Roman poet Virgil: “I seem to see the River Tiber foaming with much blood.” The speech was dubbed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The popular name for Enoch Powell’s 1968 anti-immigration speech warning of racial conflict.">Rivers of Blood speech</dfn>. Powell was sacked from the Shadow Cabinet, but polls showed 74% of the British public agreed with him.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Black Country: Coal, Community and Decline</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Tollington is a former mining village. By the late 1960s, the coal mines and factories that had sustained communities like this were closing. Syal captures a world where the old industrial working class is losing its identity. The motorway being built nearby (the M6) literally tears through the landscape, symbolising modernisation destroying traditional community. This economic decline fuels resentment — immigrants become a convenient <dfn class="term" data-def="A person or group unfairly blamed for others’ problems.">scapegoat</dfn> for problems caused by deindustrialisation.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how mining decline connects to racial tensions in the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The closure of mines and factories created economic hardship and a loss of identity for white working-class communities. Syal shows how this frustration was redirected towards immigrant families like the Kumars, rather than at the economic system that caused the decline.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Syal’s Autobiographical Roots</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Meera Syal was born in Wolverhampton in 1961 to Punjabi parents who had emigrated from Delhi. She grew up in Essington, a small mining village where her family were among the only South Asian residents. Like Meena, Syal experienced the tension of being caught between two cultures — speaking Punjabi at home and English at school, eating Indian food but craving chips, wanting to fit in while being visibly different.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">While Anita and Me draws heavily on Syal’s childhood, it is not autobiography. Syal has said Meena is a “heightened version” of herself — funnier, bolder, more rebellious. Anita is a composite character. However, the emotional truth — the loneliness of being the only Asian child, the desperate desire to belong, the richness of family gatherings — is deeply personal. Syal published the novel in 1996, when British Asian voices were largely absent from mainstream literature.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why Context Matters for OCR</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">OCR’s Component 01 Section A tests your ability to analyse the studied text and compare it with an <dfn class="term" data-def="A passage not seen before the exam, used for comparison with the studied text.">unseen extract</dfn> from a modern prose text on a related theme. Part (a) asks you to compare the two extracts (20 marks), and part (b) asks a broader question about your studied text (20 marks). For both parts, <dfn class="term" data-def="Assessment Objective 3: understanding the relationship between texts and their contexts.">AO3</dfn> (context) is assessed — so weaving in knowledge of 1960s Britain, immigration and Syal’s background is essential.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three AOs tested in OCR Component 01 Section A.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO1 (personal response with textual references), AO2 (analysis of language, form and structure) and AO3 (context) are all assessed. You must integrate all three — a response that analyses language brilliantly but ignores context will not reach the top band.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Anita and Me is set in late 1960s/early 1970s Britain, during mass immigration, industrial decline and rising racial tensions symbolised by Powell’s “Rivers of Blood” speech.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Syal draws on her own experience in a Black Country mining village, though the novel is fiction not autobiography.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">OCR assesses AO1, AO2 and AO3 — context must be integrated throughout your analysis.</li></ul>

<p data-narration-id="n17">In the OCR exam, part (a) requires direct comparison — use phrases like “Similarly”, “In contrast” and “While both writers…”. Do not write about each text separately. Part (b) is about your studied text only but still requires AO3 context. Embed references to 1960s Britain, Powell’s speech and immigration naturally within your analysis.</p>
