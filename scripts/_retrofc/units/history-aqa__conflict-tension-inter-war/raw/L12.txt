Henlein, the September 1938 crisis, the Munich Agreement and 'peace for our time'.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Sudeten Pretext</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Czechoslovakia was the strongest democracy in central Europe in 1938 &mdash; a well-armed state of about 15 million people with French and Soviet defensive treaties. Yet within months of Anschluss it had been carved up at a four-power conference where its own representatives were not present. The pretext was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The mountainous border region of Czechoslovakia, home to about three million ethnic Germans, that Hitler demanded in 1938.">Sudetenland</dfn>, a mountainous border region containing about three million ethnic Germans &mdash; and crucially, Czechoslovakia&rsquo;s defensive fortifications. Hitler used the principle of national self-determination, championed by Wilson at Versailles, as cover for what was in reality a strategic move against the Czechoslovak state.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three Chamberlain-Hitler meetings of September 1938, with the date of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Sudeten Crisis came to a head in three Chamberlain-Hitler meetings: Berchtesgaden (15 September 1938), Bad Godesberg (22 September), and the Munich Conference (29-30 September). The Sudetenland was transferred to Germany; Czechoslovakia was not at the table.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Henlein and the Sudeten German Party</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From early 1938 the Sudeten German Party, led by <dfn class="term" data-def="Leader of the Sudeten German Party from 1933, who agitated for autonomy and union with Germany under instructions from Berlin.">Konrad Henlein</dfn>, ramped up demands for autonomy. Henlein had been instructed by Hitler in March 1938 to make demands the Czech government could never accept &mdash; ensuring the agitation would continue regardless of any concession. The Czech President Edvard Bene&scaron; offered a series of concessions through the spring and summer; each time Henlein raised the bar. By August 1938 the situation was at a deliberate boiling point. In May 1938 the Czech government had ordered partial mobilisation in response to false rumours of German troop movements; the &lsquo;May Crisis&rsquo; showed the Czechs would resist, but also showed Hitler that mobilisation was politically expensive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">On 12 September 1938 Hitler delivered a speech at the Nuremberg Rally demanding self-determination for the Sudeten Germans &mdash; effectively a demand to be allowed to take the territory. Riots broke out across the Sudetenland. The Czech government declared martial law. War in central Europe looked imminent.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Three Meetings, Three Stages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Chamberlain, sixty-nine and never having flown before, took the dramatic step of flying to meet Hitler personally. The shuttle diplomacy across September 1938 came in three stages:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>15 September: Berchtesgaden.</strong> At Hitler&rsquo;s mountain retreat, Chamberlain accepted the principle that the Sudetenland should be transferred to Germany on grounds of self-determination. He flew home to consult Cabinet and Paris; Bene&scaron; was effectively informed by his allies that the territory was lost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>22 September: Bad Godesberg.</strong> Hitler now raised the demands. Transfer must happen immediately, by military occupation, with no plebiscites and no orderly handover. Chamberlain refused. The Czechs and the French began preparing for war; the Royal Navy was mobilised; gas masks were distributed to British schoolchildren.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>29-30 September: Munich.</strong> Mussolini, alarmed at the prospect of war, proposed a four-power conference. Britain, France, Germany and Italy met at Munich. Czechoslovakia was not invited; Bene&scaron; was kept in an antechamber and informed of the result. The agreement followed Hitler&rsquo;s Bad Godesberg demands almost in full. The Sudetenland was transferred to Germany over ten days from 1 October. Czechoslovakia lost its border fortifications, around a third of its industrial capacity, and most of its strategic depth. Slovakia and Ruthenia gained autonomy. The Polish government opportunistically seized the Teschen district; Hungary took further border areas.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The exclusion of Czechoslovakia from the conference deciding its fate is one of the most criticised features of Munich. Chamberlain and Daladier reasoned that the alternative was war, and that Czechoslovakia&rsquo;s consent was unobtainable but unnecessary if Britain and France would not fight to defend it. The result was an <dfn class="term" data-def="The act of leaving an ally without support in a moment of crisis &mdash; in 1938 Britain and France abandoned Czechoslovakia at the Munich Conference.">abandonment of an ally</dfn> that Czechoslovakia, France&rsquo;s formal treaty partner since 1924, never forgave. Bene&scaron; resigned and went into exile. The Czech army, which had been ready to fight, was not consulted.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">&lsquo;Peace for Our Time&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Returning from Munich on 30 September, Chamberlain produced a separate Anglo-German declaration that he and Hitler had signed that morning &mdash; the famous &lsquo;piece of paper&rsquo;. Standing at 10 Downing Street he announced &lsquo;peace for our time&rsquo;, drawing parallels with Disraeli&rsquo;s 1878 return from Berlin. Most British and French opinion welcomed the agreement: war had been avoided. There were dissenting voices &mdash; Churchill called it &lsquo;a defeat without a war&rsquo; &mdash; but for the moment the relief was overwhelming. Chamberlain genuinely believed he had bought lasting peace, not merely time. The events of March 1939 would show how badly that judgement was misplaced.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A common student error is to write that Munich gave Hitler Czechoslovakia. It did not. The Munich Agreement transferred the Sudetenland alone &mdash; the German-speaking border region. The rest of Czechoslovakia, now indefensible without its border fortifications, remained a separate state. It was Hitler&rsquo;s seizure of the rump Czech lands in March 1939, not Munich, that ended Czech independence and ended British appeasement. Examiners report this confusion as a recurring error in narrative accounts.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain in two sentences why Munich settled the Sudetenland but not Czechoslovakia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Munich transferred the Sudetenland only &mdash; the rest of Czechoslovakia remained a state, but without its mountain fortifications and a third of its industry. The famous &lsquo;piece of paper&rsquo; promised future Anglo-German consultation. Hitler&rsquo;s seizure of the rump Czech lands the following March, not Munich itself, ended British appeasement.</p>
</div>
