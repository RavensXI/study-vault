--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Mood in Europe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1924, the worst of the post-war crisis was passing. Germany&rsquo;s 1923 hyperinflation had been stabilised by Gustav Stresemann&rsquo;s government with the new Rentenmark; the Dawes Plan of 1924 restructured reparations and brought American loans; and France had withdrawn its troops from the Ruhr. The result was a brief period of European optimism, often called the &lsquo;Locarno spirit&rsquo;. The 1920s reached their high-water mark of internationalism not through the League itself but through diplomacy outside it &mdash; the Locarno treaties of 1925 and the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who were the three foreign ministers behind Locarno, what year was the pact signed, and what borders did it guarantee?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Locarno treaties were signed in October 1925 by foreign ministers Gustav Stresemann (Germany), Aristide Briand (France) and Austen Chamberlain (Britain). Germany, France and Belgium voluntarily guaranteed each other&rsquo;s western borders, with Britain and Italy as guarantors.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Gustav Stresemann and Reconciliation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Stresemann was the leading figure of Weimar foreign policy, serving as Chancellor briefly in 1923 and as Foreign Minister from 1923 until his death in 1929. His policy was <dfn class="term" data-def="Stresemann&rsquo;s Weimar foreign policy of cooperating with the western powers in order to revise the Treaty of Versailles peacefully and rebuild Germany&rsquo;s position.">reconciliation diplomacy</dfn> &mdash; cooperation with the western powers to revise the Treaty of Versailles peacefully. He calculated that confrontation would only deepen Germany&rsquo;s isolation, while patient negotiation could relax reparations, end the occupation of the Ruhr, and bring Germany back into the great-power club.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">It would be wrong, however, to read Stresemann as an idealistic peace-maker. His private letters show a clear-eyed strategist who wanted to recover German territory, restore German military power and undo Versailles &mdash; just by negotiation rather than force. The reconciliation was tactical. He pursued three concrete objectives: ending the French and Belgian occupation of the Ruhr, lifting the burden of reparations, and bringing Germany back into the great-power club through admission to the League of Nations. He used his fluent diplomatic style to build personal trust with Briand and Austen Chamberlain, while quietly continuing secret rearmament cooperation with the Soviet Union under the 1922 Treaty of Rapallo. None of this contradicted his public reconciliation policy &mdash; the two ran in parallel. Between 1924 and 1929 he successfully repositioned Germany from outlaw state to respected partner, achieving more by negotiation than the German army had achieved on the battlefield in the four years of war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Locarno Treaties, October 1925</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Negotiations began in early 1925 in the Swiss town of Locarno. The central agreement was a <dfn class="term" data-def="Promises by Germany, France and Belgium to recognise their existing western borders as final, with Britain and Italy guaranteeing the agreement.">western frontier guarantee</dfn>: Germany, France and Belgium voluntarily accepted their existing borders as final. Britain and Italy guaranteed to act if any of the three were attacked. Germany also signed arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia &mdash; but, crucially, did not guarantee its eastern frontiers as final.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">This omission was deliberate and would matter enormously. By accepting the western borders but leaving the eastern ones open, Germany signalled that the Polish Corridor, Danzig and the Sudetenland were still subject to revision. Locarno was widely celebrated &mdash; Stresemann, Briand and Austen Chamberlain shared the 1925 Nobel Peace Prize &mdash; but it built in the very ambiguity that Hitler would exploit.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Three immediate consequences flowed from Locarno. First, in September 1926 Germany joined the League of Nations as a permanent Council member &mdash; rehabilitation completed. Second, French troops withdrew from the Rhineland zones earlier than the treaty required, the last departing in 1930 (five years ahead of schedule). Third, the &lsquo;Locarno spirit&rsquo; encouraged broader optimism that brought the Young Plan (1929) further reducing reparations and the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) renouncing war. For four or five years, Europe felt as if it had turned a corner.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Kellogg-Briand Pact, 1928</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The pact was a French initiative that an American Secretary of State turned into something larger. Aristide Briand initially proposed a Franco-American agreement to outlaw war between the two countries &mdash; partly as insurance against German recovery. Frank B. Kellogg, the American Secretary of State, suggested expanding it to include all states. The result, signed in Paris on 27 August 1928 by 15 countries and eventually ratified by 62, was a public <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal public commitment by a state to give up an action or claim, often used in diplomacy as a moral but not legally enforceable pledge.">renunciation</dfn> of war as an instrument of national policy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In effect, signatories pledged to settle disputes peacefully and never start a war of aggression. The pact had no enforcement mechanism &mdash; no penalties, no court, no army. Critics dismissed it from the start as &lsquo;a kiss in the wind&rsquo;. But the symbolism mattered. For the first time in history, almost every major state had publicly committed to outlawing war, providing the legal foundation that would later allow the prosecution of aggressive war as a crime at the Nuremberg trials in 1945-46.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Beneath the celebrations, three serious limits should be noted. First, eastern Europe was not protected: Germany did not commit to its frontiers with Poland and Czechoslovakia. Second, the agreement depended entirely on the personalities of the three foreign ministers; when Stresemann died in October 1929 and economic crisis followed in 1930, the Locarno spirit dissolved within months. Third, the agreement was made outside the League of Nations rather than through it, which subtly weakened the League&rsquo;s status as the central body for European security &mdash; great powers preferred bilateral or multilateral deals to the unanimity-bound Council.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why Locarno did not protect eastern European borders, and which countries were left exposed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Locarno guaranteed only Germany&rsquo;s western borders. Its eastern frontiers with Poland and Czechoslovakia were left open to revision. This deliberate ambiguity allowed Hitler to use the Sudetenland and the Polish Corridor as flashpoints in 1938-39.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The Locarno treaties of October 1925 marked the high-water mark of inter-war reconciliation, with Germany voluntarily accepting its western borders.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Locarno led directly to Germany&rsquo;s 1926 entry into the League and to the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, signed by 62 states renouncing war.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Both agreements had structural flaws: Locarno did not protect eastern borders; Kellogg-Briand had no enforcement. When economic crisis struck in 1929-30, the Locarno spirit collapsed within months.</li></ul>
