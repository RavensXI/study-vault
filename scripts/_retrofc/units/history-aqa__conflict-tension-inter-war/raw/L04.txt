--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the League was formed, organised, and which great powers joined or stayed out.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Idea: Collective Security</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Wilson&rsquo;s Fourteen Points called for a &lsquo;general association of nations&rsquo;, he was proposing something the world had never tried. The idea, set out in the League&rsquo;s founding document &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The founding document of the League of Nations, made up of the first 26 articles of the Treaty of Versailles, setting out its rules and aims.">Covenant</dfn> &mdash; was <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that aggression against one state is treated as aggression against all, with the international community responding together.">collective security</dfn>: aggression against any member would be treated as aggression against all, deterring war by threatening combined response. The Covenant formed the first 26 articles of the Treaty of Versailles. It came into force on 10 January 1920, the day the treaty took effect, and the League&rsquo;s headquarters opened in Geneva.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when did the League of Nations open, where, and what was its key principle of action?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The League of Nations opened in Geneva on 10 January 1920. Its founding document was the Covenant. Its core principle was collective security &mdash; that an attack on one member would be met by all.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Bodies of the League</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The League had four main parts. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The League of Nations decision-making body composed of permanent great-power members and rotating non-permanent members.">Council</dfn> was a small executive body for handling disputes. It originally had four permanent members &mdash; Britain, France, Italy and Japan &mdash; and four (later more) non-permanent members elected by the Assembly. The Council met three times a year and in emergency sessions. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The general meeting of all League members, where each state had one vote regardless of size.">Assembly</dfn> was the parliament of the League, with all member states represented. Each state had one vote, regardless of size. The Assembly met annually and dealt with budgets, admitting new members and broad policy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A common student error is to confuse the two: think of the Council as the engine room and the Assembly as the debating chamber. Both, fatally, required <dfn class="term" data-def="A voting rule under which a decision can only be taken if every member agrees; one veto blocks action.">unanimity</dfn> for major decisions, meaning a single member could veto action. Behind these political bodies sat the Secretariat (the civil service, headed by a Secretary-General) and the Permanent Court of International Justice (the legal arm, based in The Hague).</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Beyond political dispute resolution, the League ran a network of specialist agencies that did real work for ordinary people. The International Labour Organization (ILO) lobbied for shorter hours, fairer pay and bans on child labour. The Health Committee tackled cholera, malaria and leprosy and laid foundations for what became the World Health Organization. The High Commission for Refugees, led from 1921 by Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen, helped resettle hundreds of thousands of people displaced by the war and the Russian Civil War. The League&rsquo;s Slavery Commission worked to end the slave trade in Africa, and Mandate Commissions monitored the territories taken from Germany and the Ottomans. These agencies were among the League&rsquo;s most lasting successes &mdash; they outlived the League itself, becoming the basis of much of the modern UN system.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Powers of the League</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">When a member broke the rules, the League had three escalating responses. First came moral condemnation &mdash; a public statement that the aggressor was in the wrong. Second, economic <dfn class="term" data-def="Restrictions on trade or financial dealings imposed on a state to pressure it into changing its behaviour.">sanctions</dfn> &mdash; cutting off trade or finance. Third, in theory, military force &mdash; member states would contribute troops to enforce League decisions. In practice, the third option was almost never used because the League had no army of its own and members had no obligation to send troops.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">This made the League essentially a discussion forum backed by economic threat. Where great powers were the aggressors and willing to bear sanctions, there was little the League could do.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Membership: A League of Missing Powers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The League launched with 42 members in 1920. By the late 1920s membership rose past 50, and by 1934 it reached 58. But the membership story is largely a story of missing or temporary great powers.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The United States, despite Wilson&rsquo;s leading role in designing the League, never joined &mdash; the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles in 1919-20. The Soviet Union was excluded as ideologically hostile after the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution; it would not join until 1934, only to be expelled in 1939 for invading Finland. Germany was excluded as a defeated outlaw and only joined in 1926, after the rehabilitation of Locarno and Stresemann&rsquo;s reconciliation diplomacy. In 1933, the year Hitler came to power, Germany resigned from the League; Japan resigned the same year over the Manchurian crisis. Italy left in 1937 after invading Abyssinia. By 1939 the League&rsquo;s great-power membership was effectively just Britain and France.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down which years the USA, USSR, Germany and Japan were each in or out of the League between 1919 and 1939.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The USA never joined the League. Germany joined 1926, left 1933. Japan founding member, left 1933. The USSR joined 1934, expelled 1939. By 1939 the only remaining great-power members were Britain and France.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Eric Drummond and the Civil Service</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The League&rsquo;s first Secretary-General, the British diplomat Eric Drummond, served from 1920 to 1933. He built the Secretariat into a genuinely international civil service, recruiting staff loyal to the League rather than their home states. This was a quiet but important innovation: it created the model for the UN civil service that followed in 1945. Drummond&rsquo;s low-key style worked well in the 1920s when the League was fighting for legitimacy, but the system would prove no match for great-power aggression in the 1930s.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The League opened in Geneva in January 1920 with the Covenant as its rulebook and collective security as its principle.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Its main bodies were the Council (small executive), Assembly (all members, one vote each), Secretariat (civil service) and Permanent Court &mdash; with specialist agencies on labour, health and refugees.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The League&rsquo;s critical weakness was missing or temporary great powers: the USA never joined, Germany only from 1926, the USSR only from 1934 &mdash; and by 1939 only Britain and France remained.</li></ul>
