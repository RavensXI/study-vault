How November 1918 ended the fighting and what Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George each wanted.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A War That Ended Without an Invasion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By autumn 1918 Germany was exhausted. The Allied naval blockade had pushed civilians to near-starvation, the army was in retreat after the failed Spring Offensive, and revolution was breaking out in Kiel and Munich. On 9 November Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated, and two days later, in a railway carriage at Compi&egrave;gne, German representatives signed an <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement to stop fighting; not a peace treaty but a halt to allow negotiations.">armistice</dfn> that took effect at 11am on 11 November 1918. Crucially, no Allied soldier had set foot on German soil. This shaped everything that followed: many Germans believed they had not really been beaten on the battlefield, while the French were determined that this time Germany must be made to pay.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who were the Big Three, what country did each lead, and what year did the Paris Peace Conference open?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919. The dominant figures &mdash; the Big Three &mdash; were Woodrow Wilson (USA), Georges Clemenceau (France) and David Lloyd George (Britain). Italy&rsquo;s Vittorio Orlando attended but had less influence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Woodrow Wilson and the Fourteen Points</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The American president had set out his peace aims a full ten months before the armistice. In a speech to Congress on 8 January 1918 he proposed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Wilson's January 1918 plan for peace, including open diplomacy, free trade, disarmament, self-determination and a League of Nations.">Fourteen Points</dfn>. The points called for open treaties (no more secret diplomacy), free trade, freedom of the seas, disarmament, the adjustment of colonial claims to consider local populations, and the principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="The right of national or ethnic groups to choose their own government and form their own state.">national self-determination</dfn> for the peoples of Europe. The fourteenth and most ambitious point called for a general association of nations &mdash; what would become the League of Nations &mdash; to guarantee the independence of every state.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Wilson&rsquo;s thinking was idealistic. The United States had joined the war late, in April 1917, and had suffered far fewer casualties than the European powers. Wilson believed a fair peace was the only route to a lasting peace; a vengeful settlement would only sow the seeds of another war. He arrived in Europe to enormous public welcomes, presenting himself as a statesman above the petty hatreds of the Old World.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Georges Clemenceau and French Security</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">France had been invaded by Germany twice in fifty years &mdash; in 1870 and again in 1914. The fighting on the Western Front had taken place largely on French soil. Around 1.4 million French soldiers were dead and northern France lay devastated, its mines flooded and its factories destroyed. Clemenceau, nicknamed &lsquo;the Tiger&rsquo;, had no interest in idealism. His priority was simple: France must never face another German invasion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">That meant making Germany pay for reconstruction through heavy <dfn class="term" data-def="Money or goods a defeated state must pay to victors as compensation for war damage.">reparations</dfn>, taking back Alsace-Lorraine (lost to Germany in 1871), demilitarising the Rhineland to create a buffer zone, and stripping Germany of its army, navy and air force. Some French generals, including Marshal Foch, wanted to push the German border back to the Rhine. Clemenceau pushed for the harshest terms the British and Americans would accept.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The British prime minister had just won the December 1918 &lsquo;Khaki Election&rsquo; on slogans like &lsquo;Hang the Kaiser&rsquo; and &lsquo;Make Germany Pay&rsquo;. British public opinion wanted vengeance. Privately, however, Lloyd George worried about the long term. He did not want Germany to be so weakened that the Soviet revolutionary movement spread westward into central Europe, and he recognised that a recovered Germany was an essential trading partner. He also wanted to protect Britain&rsquo;s naval supremacy and its empire &mdash; including taking former German colonies as <dfn class="term" data-def="Former enemy territories administered by Allied powers under League of Nations supervision after 1919.">mandates</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Lloyd George therefore played a middleman&rsquo;s role &mdash; tougher than Wilson on reparations, softer than Clemenceau on Germany&rsquo;s long-term position. His chief contribution was to push reparations to a punishing but ambiguous figure while keeping Germany intact as a state.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Conflicting Aims, Inevitable Compromise</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The three leaders met in Paris from January 1919, often in private &lsquo;Council of Four&rsquo; sessions with Orlando. Their disagreements were sharp. Wilson wanted lenient terms; Clemenceau wanted Germany dismembered; Lloyd George wanted a workable Germany that could pay reparations and trade. A common claim by some students is that Versailles was uniquely punitive, but it is worth remembering that the terms Germany itself had imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 stripped Russia of one third of its population and most of its industry &mdash; far harsher than anything Germany would receive.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Wilson got his League of Nations and a redrawn map based largely on self-determination &mdash; but only four of his Fourteen Points were fully accepted, and the US Senate would later refuse to ratify the treaty at all. Clemenceau secured Alsace-Lorraine, a demilitarised Rhineland and large reparations &mdash; but not the permanent French occupation of the Rhine he had wanted. Lloyd George took German colonies and weakened the German navy &mdash; but the harshness of the final terms troubled him; he reportedly remarked that they would have to fight Germany again within twenty years.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down each leader&rsquo;s top priority in one phrase: Wilson wanted ___, Clemenceau wanted ___, Lloyd George wanted ___.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Each peacemaker arrived with different priorities: Wilson wanted a fair peace and a League of Nations; Clemenceau wanted French security through a weakened Germany; Lloyd George wanted to punish Germany without crippling it as a future trading partner. The Treaty of Versailles was the compromise between these aims.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The November 1918 armistice ended the fighting without an Allied invasion of Germany &mdash; a fact that shaped both German resentment and Allied determination to dictate terms.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Big Three brought sharply different aims to Paris: Wilson&rsquo;s idealism, Clemenceau&rsquo;s demand for security, Lloyd George&rsquo;s search for a balanced peace.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Versailles would be a compromise &mdash; pleasing none of the peacemakers fully, and storing up problems that would shape the next twenty years.</li></ul>
