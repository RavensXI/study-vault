--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Treaty Built in Five Blocks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Treaty of Versailles was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles on 28 June 1919 &mdash; deliberately the same room in which the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871. The German delegation was not invited to negotiate. They were handed the terms and told to sign or face renewed war. Germans called it the <dfn class="term" data-def="A dictated peace; terms imposed without negotiation rather than agreed between equals.">Diktat</dfn>. The treaty had hundreds of clauses, but for revision it helps to think in five blocks: blame, money, military, territory and the League covenant.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the five blocks of Versailles using the acronym &lsquo;BMMTL&rsquo; &mdash; Blame, Money, Military, Territory, League.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Treaty of Versailles, signed 28 June 1919, was structured around five themes: war guilt (blame), reparations (money), military restrictions, territorial changes, and the League of Nations covenant. Germany was forced to accept all five without negotiation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Article 231: The War Guilt Clause</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to accept sole responsibility for causing the First World War.">The war guilt clause</dfn> was the legal foundation of the whole settlement. Article 231 declared that Germany and her allies were responsible for &lsquo;all the loss and damage&rsquo; suffered by the Allies. This was not just an insult: it was the legal basis on which reparations could be demanded. Without an admission of guilt, Germany could not be billed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For Germans this was the most hated clause. Most believed responsibility for the war was shared across Europe &mdash; that Germany had simply been defending itself after Russia mobilised. Branding their entire nation as the sole cause of years of slaughter felt like a public humiliation, and the war guilt clause poisoned political debate in Germany for the next twenty years.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Reparations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The exact figure was not fixed at Versailles itself. A Reparations Commission set the bill in 1921 at &pound;6.6 billion (132 billion gold marks), to be paid in cash, coal, steel, ships and machinery over decades. The aim was both to compensate France and Belgium for war damage and to keep Germany economically weak.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The economist John Maynard Keynes, a British Treasury official at Paris, resigned in protest and published <em>The Economic Consequences of the Peace</em> (1919), arguing reparations were so heavy they would wreck the German economy &mdash; and through trade, the European economy too. His warnings would seem prophetic when Germany&rsquo;s 1923 hyperinflation arrived.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The treaty stripped Germany of the means to threaten its neighbours. The army was capped at 100,000 men &mdash; volunteers only, no conscription. Germany was banned from possessing tanks, submarines, military aircraft or heavy artillery. The navy was reduced to six battleships. The Rhineland &mdash; the strip of German territory along the French border &mdash; was permanently <dfn class="term" data-def="The removal of military forces and weapons from a designated zone, which is then forbidden from hosting troops or fortifications.">demilitarised</dfn>, with no German troops or fortifications allowed within 50km of the Rhine.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">For comparison, France kept an army of around 700,000 men. The intent was clear: Germany was to be a military pygmy beside her neighbours. From a French security perspective, this was the central achievement of the treaty.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Territorial Changes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Germany lost about 13% of its European territory and 12% of its population. In the west, Alsace-Lorraine returned to France after 48 years of German rule. The small districts of Eupen and Malm&eacute;dy went to Belgium. North Schleswig was given to Denmark after a plebiscite. In the east, Germany lost Posen and West Prussia to recreated Poland, the latter forming the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="A strip of former German territory granted to Poland in 1919, giving Poland access to the Baltic Sea but separating East Prussia from the rest of Germany.">Polish Corridor</dfn>&rsquo;. The port of Danzig (today&rsquo;s Gdansk) became a Free City under League of Nations administration, giving Poland sea access without giving Poland the German-speaking city itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Saar coalfield was placed under League administration for fifteen years, with its coal output going to France as compensation for damaged French mines. All of Germany&rsquo;s overseas colonies &mdash; in Africa, the Pacific and China &mdash; were stripped away and redistributed to Britain, France, Belgium, Japan and South Africa as League of Nations <dfn class="term" data-def="Former enemy territories administered by Allied powers under League of Nations supervision after 1919.">mandates</dfn>. Union with Austria (Anschluss) was specifically forbidden.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The first 26 articles of the treaty established the League of Nations. Germany was excluded from membership in 1919, treated as an outlaw state until it had &lsquo;proved&rsquo; itself. Russia, ravaged by civil war and ideologically opposed, was also kept out. This meant the body intended to safeguard the new order began life without two of Europe&rsquo;s largest powers, and (after the US Senate&rsquo;s rejection in 1920) without the country whose president had designed it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Was Versailles Uniquely Punitive?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Many Germans called Versailles a &lsquo;<em>Schmachfrieden</em>&rsquo; &mdash; a peace of shame. To evaluate this fairly, compare it with the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk that Germany imposed on Russia in March 1918. Brest-Litovsk took a third of Russia&rsquo;s population, a third of its agricultural land, and most of its iron and coal &mdash; territorial losses far greater than anything Germany faced. The Versailles peacemakers knew this; it shaped their willingness to dismiss German complaints about &lsquo;harshness&rsquo;. Versailles was harsh, but the claim that it was uniquely punitive ignores how Germany itself had behaved when it had won.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three territorial losses, two military restrictions and the reparations figure set in 1921.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reparations were set at &pound;6.6 billion in 1921. The army was capped at 100,000 men. Germany lost Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor and all overseas colonies. The Rhineland was demilitarised. Germany was excluded from the League of Nations.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Versailles imposed five blocks of terms: war guilt (Article 231), reparations (&pound;6.6 billion in 1921), military restrictions (army capped at 100,000), territorial losses (13% of land), and the League covenant.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">The treaty was a Diktat &mdash; Germany was excluded from negotiations and forced to sign on threat of renewed war.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Although harsh, Versailles was less severe than the terms Germany itself had imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk a year earlier.</li></ul>
