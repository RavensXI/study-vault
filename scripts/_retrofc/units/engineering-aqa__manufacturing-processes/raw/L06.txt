--- DESCRIPTION ---
Painting, dip coating, electroplating, galvanising and polishing — protection, aesthetics and cost.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Surface Finish Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A surface finish does two things simultaneously: it protects the base material from <dfn class="term" data-def="The chemical or electrochemical degradation of a material through reaction with its environment — most commonly the oxidation of iron and steel to form rust in the presence of moisture and oxygen.">corrosion</dfn> and it gives the product its appearance. Both matter commercially: a rusted chassis is structurally weakened, and an ugly surface finish damages a brand. Surface finishing is the last manufacturing stage before a product leaves the factory, so faults here affect every unit that ships. Understanding which finish suits which material, environment and cost envelope is a key engineering decision that examiners test every year.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Surface Preparation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Every surface finishing process begins with preparation. Paint, dip coatings and electroplating all bond poorly to a surface contaminated with grease, rust, mill scale or dust. Standard preparation steps are: degrease (solvent or alkali wash), abrade (grit-blast or sandpaper to provide a mechanical key), rinse and dry. Skipping preparation is the most common cause of coating failure in industry. A well-prepared surface can make a cheap paint finish outperform an expensive electroplated coating applied to a dirty surface.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Painting and Spray Painting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Painting applies a liquid coating to the surface that dries or cures to form a continuous protective film. <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of applying paint by forcing it through a nozzle as a fine mist under compressed air, producing a uniform, thin, even coat.">Spray painting</dfn> uses compressed air to atomise paint into a fine mist that settles evenly across the surface. It is used on vehicle bodywork, metal furniture, machinery guarding and architectural steelwork. Multiple thin coats are better than one thick coat, which sags and cures unevenly. High-volume industrial lines use electrostatic spray guns that charge the paint droplets so they are attracted to the earthed metal workpiece, reducing overspray waste.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Advantages of painting: wide colour choice; low cost for low-volume work; can be applied on-site to large structures (e.g. the Forth Road Bridge). Limitations: can chip or scratch to expose bare metal, allowing corrosion to undercut the surrounding paint (the most common failure mode); requires ventilation and personal protective equipment (PPE) due to solvent fumes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the key limitation of paint as a corrosion-protection method that causes it to fail faster than galvanising?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Paint can chip or scratch to expose bare metal. Once bare metal is exposed, corrosion undercuts the surrounding coating from underneath, spreading the failure zone. Galvanising (zinc coating) sacrificially protects even when scratched because zinc is more electrochemically active than iron and corrodes preferentially.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Dip Coating</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A surface finishing process in which a clean metal component is heated and then submerged into a fluidised bed or tank of powdered thermoplastic, which melts and flows onto the surface to form a thick, tough coating.">Dip coating</dfn> (sometimes called plastisol dipping or powder dip coating) involves heating the cleaned metal component to around 200&thinsp;&deg;C and then dipping it into a fluidised bed of thermoplastic powder (typically PVC or polyethylene). The hot metal melts the powder particles that contact it, building up a thick, rubbery coating. The component is then cured in an oven. Dishwasher racks, tool handles, garden-chair frames and cycle-basket wire forms are typical applications: the thick thermoplastic coating gives grip, insulates electrically, resists knocks and provides complete waterproofing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Advantages over spray paint: the coating is much thicker (typically 1&ndash;3&thinsp;mm vs 50&ndash;150&thinsp;&mu;m for paint), more chemically resistant, and resistant to impact without chipping. Limitations: colour range is narrower than paint; the process requires heating the component, so large structures cannot be dip-coated easily; it is more expensive than painting per unit for large flat surfaces where thin coverage is acceptable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Electroplating</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A surface finishing process that uses electrolysis to deposit a thin layer of one metal (e.g. nickel, chromium, gold) onto the surface of a base metal component.">Electroplating</dfn> uses electricity to deposit a thin, uniform layer of a metal onto the workpiece surface. The component to be plated is made the cathode (negative electrode) in an electrolytic cell; the plating metal (e.g. chromium, nickel, gold, copper, zinc) forms the anode (positive electrode). Current flows through the electrolyte solution, dissolving metal from the anode and depositing it on the cathode surface. Chromium plating on car bumpers and taps gives a mirror finish and excellent corrosion resistance. Gold plating on electrical contacts reduces contact resistance and prevents oxidation. Zinc electroplating (electrogalvanising) protects steel fasteners.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Advantages: very thin (1&ndash;50&thinsp;&mu;m), dimensionally precise coating that adds almost no thickness to machined surfaces; excellent surface finish and appearance; can deposit expensive metals (gold, platinum) very economically because the layer is so thin. Limitations: the plating bath and anodes must be managed carefully; wastewater from plating is chemically hazardous and requires specialist disposal; not suitable for complex internal cavities where current density is uneven.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Galvanising</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Galvanising coats steel with a layer of zinc. The dominant method is <dfn class="term" data-def="A galvanising process in which clean steel is immersed in a bath of molten zinc at around 450 °C; the zinc reacts with the steel surface to form a series of zinc-iron alloy layers topped by a pure zinc outer layer.">hot-dip galvanising</dfn>: clean, fluxed steel is submerged in a bath of molten zinc at approximately 450&thinsp;&deg;C. The zinc reacts with the steel to form a series of intermetallic alloy layers, bonded much more strongly than a paint film. Street furniture, motorway crash barriers, agricultural machinery and structural steelwork are galvanised as standard.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Galvanising has one property that paint lacks: <strong>sacrificial (cathodic) protection</strong>. Zinc is more electrochemically active than iron; if the zinc coating is scratched through to bare steel, the zinc around the scratch corrodes preferentially, protecting the exposed iron. The zinc &ldquo;sacrifices itself&rdquo; to protect the steel. Paint has no such mechanism &mdash; a scratch allows moisture to reach the steel and corrosion spreads under the coating.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Explain from memory why galvanising provides sacrificial protection whereas paint cannot, using the word electrochemical.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Zinc is more electrochemically active than iron. When both are in contact with moisture, zinc corrodes preferentially (sacrificial protection), so even a scratch through the galvanised coating does not allow the underlying steel to rust. Paint has no electrochemical activity &mdash; a chip exposes bare steel directly to moisture.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Polishing and Anodising</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Polishing removes surface roughness mechanically &mdash; using abrasive compounds, buffing wheels or electropolishing &mdash; to produce a reflective, smooth surface. It is used on cutlery, surgical instruments, jewellery and any part where appearance or hygiene demands a smooth surface (rough surfaces trap bacteria). Polishing alone does not protect against corrosion but prepares the surface for electroplating or lacquering.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="An electrochemical process that thickens the natural oxide layer on aluminium, increasing corrosion resistance and providing a porous surface that can be dyed in a range of colours.">Anodising</dfn> is specific to aluminium. Aluminium naturally forms a thin oxide layer that protects it from further corrosion, but anodising deliberately thickens this oxide layer using electrolysis. The anodised surface is hard, scratch-resistant and porous: dye can be absorbed into the pores before sealing, giving a wide range of colours. Laptop casings, window frames, bicycle components and camera bodies are commonly anodised. Unlike paint or plating, the anodised layer is part of the aluminium itself and cannot peel.</p>

Steel &rarr; galvanising, electroplating, paint or dip coating all possible.) <strong>2. Always name the application, the mechanism of corrosion protection, the process steps and one limitation before concluding which is better for the specific scenario.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Painting (including spray paint) is low-cost and versatile but can chip and expose bare metal; galvanising provides sacrificial zinc protection even when scratched; dip coating gives a thick, tough thermoplastic coat with good grip and impact resistance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Electroplating deposits a thin, precise metal layer (chromium, nickel, gold) using electrolysis; anodising thickens the natural oxide on aluminium and can be dyed; both give excellent surface finish and corrosion protection.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Surface preparation (degreasing, abrading, rinsing) is essential before any finishing process &mdash; contamination causes adhesion failure regardless of the quality of the coating applied.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">Extended-response questions on surface finishing appear regularly and reward comparison, not description. If asked to evaluate dip coating vs painting, give: similarities (both protect against corrosion, both applied after surface preparation, both give aesthetic finish), then key differences (coating thickness, chip resistance, cost, suitability for different applications, on-site vs factory application). &ldquo;Spray paint alone&rdquo; is not a sufficient alternative when an exam question asks you to &ldquo;name a different method&rdquo; &mdash; you must name the specific method (electroplating, galvanising, dip coating, powder coating, anodising). One-word answers such as &ldquo;paint&rdquo; without qualification score less than &ldquo;hot-dip galvanising&rdquo; followed by a sentence of explanation.</p>
