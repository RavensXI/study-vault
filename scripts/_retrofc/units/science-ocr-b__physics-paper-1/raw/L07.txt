--- DESCRIPTION ---
How appliances transfer energy, what efficiency means, and how your electricity bill is calculated.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Electrical Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every electrical appliance transfers energy. The rate at which it does so is its <dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred, measured in watts (W). 1 watt = 1 joule transferred per second.">power</dfn>. A 100 W bulb transfers 100 joules of energy every second; a 3 kW kettle transfers 3,000 joules every second. The key equations for electrical power are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$P = V \times I$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$P = I^2 \times R$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Where \(P\) is power (W), \(V\) is potential difference (V), \(I\) is current (A), and \(R\) is resistance (\(\Omega\)). The second equation comes from substituting \(V = IR\) into the first. It is useful when you know current and resistance but not voltage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall both power equations, then write down which variables you need for each. Practise rearranging P = VI to find V or I, and P = I<sup>2</sup>R to find R or I.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Power: \(P = VI\) and \(P = I^2R\). Both give power in watts (W). Use the first when you know voltage and current; use the second when you know current and resistance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Energy Transferred</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The total energy transferred by an appliance depends on its power and the time it runs:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$E = P \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Where \(E\) is energy (J), \(P\) is power (W), and \(t\) is time (s). Energy can also be calculated using the charge that flows: \(E = Q \times V\), where \(Q\) is charge (C) and \(V\) is potential difference (V). This tells you how much energy is transferred to each coulomb of charge as it passes through the component.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For domestic electricity bills, energy is measured in <dfn class="term" data-def="A unit of energy used on electricity bills. 1 kWh is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance running for 1 hour. 1 kWh = 3,600,000 J.">kilowatt-hours (kWh)</dfn> rather than joules, because joules are impractically small for household use. To calculate energy in kWh: multiply power in kilowatts by time in hours. The cost is then: \(\text{cost} = \text{energy (kWh)} \times \text{price per kWh}\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">AC, DC and Mains Wiring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Current that flows in one direction only, produced by batteries and solar cells.">Direct current (DC)</dfn> flows in one direction only. <dfn class="term" data-def="Current that constantly reverses direction. The UK mains supply is AC at 230 V and 50 Hz.">Alternating current (AC)</dfn> constantly reverses direction. The UK mains supply is AC at approximately 230 V and a frequency of 50 Hz. AC is used for the mains because its voltage can be easily changed using transformers, which is essential for the National Grid.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A UK three-pin plug has three wires with specific colours and roles:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">&bull; <strong>Live (brown)</strong> &mdash; carries the AC supply at about 230 V. Always dangerous, even when a switch is open.<br>&bull; <strong>Neutral (blue)</strong> &mdash; completes the circuit; normally at 0 V.<br>&bull; <strong>Earth (green and yellow)</strong> &mdash; a safety wire connected to metal casings; provides a low-resistance path to earth if a fault occurs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A safety device in a plug or fuse box containing a thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current exceeds a safe level.">fuse</dfn> is always connected in the live wire. If current exceeds the fuse rating, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A safety switch that detects tiny imbalances between live and neutral currents and cuts the circuit within milliseconds if current leaks.">residual current device (RCD)</dfn> provides even faster protection by detecting tiny current imbalances.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Remember the wire colours: brown = live, blue = neutral, green and yellow = earth. The fuse is always in the live wire. Write these from memory, then explain why the earth wire is needed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK plug wires: live (brown) at 230 V, neutral (blue) at 0 V, earth (green &amp; yellow) for safety. The fuse is in the live wire and melts if current is too high. The earth wire prevents the casing becoming dangerously live.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of energy input that is usefully transferred by a device. Efficiency = useful power output &divide; total power input (expressed as a fraction or percentage).">Efficiency</dfn> measures how well a device converts input energy into useful output energy. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful power output}}{\text{total power input}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Efficiency can be expressed as a decimal or multiplied by 100 to give a percentage. An incandescent light bulb might be only 5% efficient &mdash; 95% of electrical energy is wasted as heat rather than light. An LED lamp doing the same lighting job might be around 60-80% efficient, which is why the switch to LED lighting has been a significant energy-saving measure. No device is 100% efficient; energy is always lost to the surroundings, usually as heat.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Answer:</strong> \(\text{efficiency} = \frac{600}{800} = 0.75\) (or 75%). The remaining 200 W (25%) is transferred to the surroundings as heat due to friction in the bearings and resistance in the motor windings. This energy is not useful &mdash; it dissipates into the environment and cannot be recovered.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Reducing Energy Use at Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Understanding power and efficiency helps households make informed decisions about energy use. The biggest energy consumers in a typical home are <strong>heating and hot water</strong> (including showers and washing machines), followed by cooking, then refrigeration and lighting. Identifying which appliances draw the most power &mdash; and for how long &mdash; is the first step in reducing energy bills and environmental impact.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">For any given task, a more efficient appliance transfers more of the input electrical energy to a useful output. Replacing a 60 W incandescent bulb with an 8 W LED that produces the same brightness reduces the power draw by over 85%. Over hundreds of hours of use, this saving accumulates significantly on an electricity bill. Energy ratings on appliances (from A to G in the UK, with A being most efficient) allow consumers to compare efficiency at the point of purchase. A highly efficient appliance costs more upfront but typically pays back the extra cost through lower running costs over its lifetime.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Electrical power (W) = VI = I<sup>2</sup>R. Energy transferred (J) = power &times; time. For bills, use kilowatts and hours: energy in kWh = kW &times; hours; cost = kWh &times; price per unit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">UK mains supply is AC at 230 V, 50 Hz. The live (brown) wire is dangerous; the fuse is always in the live wire; the earth (green &amp; yellow) wire prevents metal casings becoming live.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Efficiency = useful power output &divide; total power input. No device is 100% efficient &mdash; energy is always lost as heat. Higher efficiency means less energy wasted for the same useful output.</li>
</ul>
