--- DESCRIPTION ---
What current really is, what determines it, and why resistance matters in real circuits.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Charge and Current</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of flow of charge through a conductor, measured in amperes (A).">Current</dfn> is the rate at which electric charge flows through a conductor. In metal wires, the charge carriers are free electrons &mdash; electrons that are not bound to any particular atom and can drift through the material when a potential difference is applied. For current to flow, two conditions must be met: there must be a <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred per unit charge across a component, measured in volts (V). Also called voltage.">potential difference</dfn> driving the charge, and the circuit must form a complete closed loop with no gaps.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The equation linking charge, current and time is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$Q = I \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Where \(Q\) is charge (coulombs, C), \(I\) is current (amperes, A), and \(t\) is time (seconds, s). One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes a point every second. Current is measured using an <strong>ammeter</strong> connected in <strong>series</strong>. Potential difference is measured using a <strong>voltmeter</strong> connected in <strong>parallel</strong> across the component.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="A very common error is thinking current is &lsquo;used up&rsquo; by components. It is not &mdash; only energy is transferred. In a series circuit, current is the same everywhere. Cover this and explain why.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Current is measured in amperes (A), potential difference in volts (V). Current is NOT &lsquo;used up&rsquo; by components &mdash; charge flows continuously around a closed circuit. Energy is transferred from the charge carriers, not the current itself.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Resistance and Ohm&rsquo;s Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how much a component opposes the flow of current, measured in ohms (&Omega;).">Resistance</dfn> opposes the flow of current. The greater a component&rsquo;s resistance, the smaller the current that flows for a given potential difference. The relationship between these three quantities is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$V = I \times R$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Where \(V\) is potential difference (V), \(I\) is current (A), and \(R\) is resistance (\(\Omega\)). This can be rearranged: \(I = \frac{V}{R}\) or \(R = \frac{V}{I}\). <dfn class="term" data-def="The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature remains constant.">Ohm&rsquo;s Law</dfn> is a special case of this: it states that the current is directly proportional to the potential difference <em>only if the temperature does not change</em>. Components that obey this are called <strong>ohmic conductors</strong>.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Answer:</strong> \(R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{12}{0.4} = 30 \, \Omega\). Note: current in amps, voltage in volts, resistance in ohms. Always write the equation, substitute, then calculate.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">I&ndash;V Characteristics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph of current (I) against potential difference (V) for a component. Its shape shows whether the component obeys Ohm&rsquo;s law and how resistance changes.">I&ndash;V characteristic</dfn> graph shows how current varies with potential difference. You need to know the shapes for three key components:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">&bull; <strong>Ohmic resistor</strong>: a straight line through the origin. The gradient is constant, showing that resistance does not change. This confirms Ohm&rsquo;s law.<br>&bull; <strong>Filament lamp</strong>: a curve that starts steep and flattens at higher voltages. As more current flows, the filament heats up, increasing its resistance. The curve is symmetrical in both directions.<br>&bull; <strong>Diode</strong>: current only flows in the forward direction. In reverse, resistance is extremely high and effectively no current flows. There is a threshold voltage (around 0.6 V for silicon diodes) below which forward current is also negligible.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Sketch the I&ndash;V graphs for a resistor, a filament lamp and a diode from memory, then explain the shape of each in terms of how resistance changes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ohmic resistor: straight line (constant resistance). Filament lamp: curve (resistance increases with temperature). Diode: current only in one direction (very high reverse resistance).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Series and Parallel Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In a <strong>series circuit</strong>, all components are in a single loop. The same current flows through every component. Potential difference is shared &mdash; the voltages across components add up to the supply voltage. Total resistance is the sum of individual resistances: \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\). If any component breaks, the whole circuit stops.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">In a <strong>parallel circuit</strong>, components are on separate branches. Potential difference is the same across every branch (and equal to the supply voltage). Current splits between branches &mdash; more current takes the lower-resistance path. Adding resistors in parallel <em>decreases</em> total resistance because there are more paths for charge to flow. If one branch breaks, others continue to work. Household wiring is parallel so that each appliance receives the full 230 V and can be switched independently.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Some components have resistance that responds to environmental conditions:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">&bull; A <dfn class="term" data-def="A component whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases. Used in automatic lighting and burglar alarms.">light-dependent resistor (LDR)</dfn> has high resistance in the dark and low resistance in bright light. It is used in automatic streetlights and security systems.<br>&bull; An <strong>NTC thermistor</strong> (negative temperature coefficient) has high resistance when cold and low resistance when hot. It is used in temperature sensors, fire alarms and thermostat circuits.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Both devices are non-ohmic &mdash; their resistance changes with conditions, so the current through them is not simply proportional to voltage. They are often used in potential divider circuits to give a variable voltage output.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Series vs Parallel</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Series:</strong> Two resistors, 6 &Omega; and 4 &Omega;, connected in series to a 10 V supply. \(R_{\text{total}} = 6 + 4 = 10 \, \Omega\). \(I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{10}{10} = 1 \text{ A}\) (same through both).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Parallel (same resistors):</strong> Voltage across each branch = 10 V. Current through 6 &Omega; branch: \(I_1 = \frac{10}{6} \approx 1.67 \text{ A}\). Current through 4 &Omega; branch: \(I_2 = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5 \text{ A}\). Total current from supply = \(1.67 + 2.5 = 4.17 \text{ A}\) &mdash; more than in series, because parallel reduces total resistance.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Current (A) is the rate of flow of charge; potential difference (V) drives it; resistance (&Omega;) opposes it. Ohm&rsquo;s law (V = IR) links all three &mdash; but only for ohmic conductors at constant temperature.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">In series circuits, current is the same everywhere and voltages add up. In parallel circuits, voltage is the same across every branch and currents add up. Adding resistors in parallel reduces total resistance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">I&ndash;V graphs reveal how resistance changes: a straight line means constant resistance (ohmic), a curve means resistance increases with temperature (filament lamp), and a one-way graph means a diode.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">For &lsquo;Explain&rsquo; questions on I&ndash;V characteristics, always link the shape of the graph to what happens to resistance &mdash; don&rsquo;t just describe the shape. The most common error is confusing current and potential difference units: current is always measured in <strong>amperes (A)</strong> and potential difference in <strong>volts (V)</strong>. On a &lsquo;Suggest&rsquo; question about a novel circuit, identify whether components are in series or parallel and apply the relevant rules for current and voltage distribution.</p>
