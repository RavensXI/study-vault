--- DESCRIPTION ---
Transverse and longitudinal waves, the equation v = fλ, and the EM spectrum of uses and hazards.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Wave?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Waves transfer energy from one place to another <strong>without transferring matter</strong>. The particles of the medium vibrate about their rest positions but do not travel along with the wave &mdash; it is the disturbance that moves, not the material. This is why you can feel a sound wave in your chest even though no air travels from the speaker to you; instead, the air molecules pass the vibration along like a row of falling dominoes.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">All waves fall into one of two categories. In <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the vibrations are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer. Examples include light, water ripples and all electromagnetic waves.">transverse waves</dfn>, the vibrations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer &mdash; the wave forms crests and troughs. Examples include light, water surface ripples and all electromagnetic waves. In <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the vibrations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound waves are the main example at GCSE.">longitudinal waves</dfn>, the vibrations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Instead of crests and troughs, longitudinal waves have <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are pushed close together, creating a high-pressure zone.">compressions</dfn> (regions where particles are pressed together) and <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are spread further apart, creating a low-pressure zone.">rarefactions</dfn> (regions where particles spread apart). Sound is the main longitudinal wave at GCSE.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state whether each of the following is transverse or longitudinal: light, sound, water ripples, seismic P-wave. Check your answers against the rule about vibration direction.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Transverse waves: vibrations perpendicular to direction of travel &mdash; light, water ripples, all EM waves. Longitudinal waves: vibrations parallel to direction of travel &mdash; sound. Only longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Wave Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">All waves share four measurable properties:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">&bull; <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum displacement of a point on a wave from its undisturbed (rest) position; related to the energy carried by the wave.">Amplitude</dfn> &mdash; the maximum displacement from rest position, measured in metres. A larger amplitude means more energy. For sound, greater amplitude means a louder sound; for light, it means brighter light.<br>
&bull; <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance from one point on a wave to the equivalent point on the next wave (for example, crest to crest); symbol &lambda; (lambda), measured in metres.">Wavelength</dfn> (\(\lambda\)) &mdash; the distance from one crest (or compression) to the next. Measured in metres.<br>
&bull; <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of complete waves passing a fixed point per second; measured in hertz (Hz).">Frequency</dfn> (\(f\)) &mdash; the number of complete waves passing a fixed point per second, measured in hertz (Hz). For sound, higher frequency means higher pitch.<br>
&bull; <strong>Period</strong> (\(T\)) &mdash; the time for one complete wave to pass a point. Period and frequency are reciprocals: \(T = \frac{1}{f}\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Wave Speed Equation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The relationship between wave speed, frequency and wavelength is one of the most important equations in physics:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$v = f \times \lambda$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Where \(v\) is wave speed in m/s, \(f\) is frequency in Hz, and \(\lambda\) is wavelength in m. This equation applies to all waves &mdash; whether a ripple on a pond or a gamma ray from a radioactive source. You can rearrange it to find frequency (\(f = \frac{v}{\lambda}\)) or wavelength (\(\lambda = \frac{v}{f}\)).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Reflection, Refraction and Diffraction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Waves can interact with boundaries and obstacles in three important ways:</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n18">&bull; <strong>Reflection</strong> &mdash; a wave bounces off a surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection (both measured from the normal &mdash; an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface). Speed, frequency and wavelength are unchanged.<br>
&bull; <strong>Refraction</strong> &mdash; a wave changes speed when passing from one medium to another (e.g. light entering glass from air). If it hits the boundary at an angle, it also changes direction. A wave bends <em>towards</em> the normal when it slows down (entering a denser medium). Frequency stays the same; wavelength and speed change.<br>
&bull; <strong>Diffraction</strong> &mdash; a wave spreads out as it passes through a gap or around an obstacle. The spreading is greatest when the gap size is similar to the wavelength. This is why sound diffracts around a doorway (sound wavelengths are comparable to door sizes) but light does not (visible light wavelengths are millions of times shorter than a doorway).</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: state what changes and what stays the same for a wave during (a) reflection, (b) refraction. Write your answer before checking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reflection: speed, frequency and wavelength all unchanged &mdash; wave bounces off at the same angle it hit. Refraction: speed and wavelength change; frequency does NOT change. Diffraction: greatest spreading when gap width is similar to wavelength.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Electromagnetic Spectrum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">All electromagnetic waves are <strong>transverse</strong> waves that travel at the same speed in a vacuum: approximately \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s (the speed of light). They are arranged on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The complete family of electromagnetic waves arranged in order of frequency (or wavelength), from radio waves through to gamma rays.">electromagnetic spectrum</dfn> in order of increasing frequency (and decreasing wavelength):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Radio waves &rarr; microwaves &rarr; infrared &rarr; visible light &rarr; ultraviolet &rarr; X-rays &rarr; gamma rays</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">As frequency increases, wavelength decreases (since \(v = f\lambda\) and \(v\) is constant). Higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength) EM radiation generally carries more energy per photon and tends to be more ionising &mdash; which is why UV, X-rays and gamma rays are more hazardous to living tissue than radio waves or infrared.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Radio waves</strong>: broadcasting (TV, radio), satellite communication. Very low energy; not hazardous in normal use.<br>
<strong>Microwaves</strong>: mobile phone signals, satellite data, cooking (microwave ovens heat water molecules). May cause tissue heating at high intensities.<br>
<strong>Infrared</strong>: remote controls, thermal imaging, optical fibre communication, radiant heaters. Causes skin burns at high intensities.<br>
<strong>Visible light</strong>: human vision, photography, optical fibre communication. Safe at normal intensities.<br>
<strong>Ultraviolet (UV)</strong>: causes skin tanning and can cause skin cancer and eye damage; used in fluorescent lights and sterilising water. Ionising at high frequency end.<br>
<strong>X-rays</strong>: medical imaging, airport security scanners. Ionising &mdash; can damage DNA; doses kept as low as reasonably practicable in medical use.<br>
<strong>Gamma rays</strong>: radiotherapy (killing cancer cells), sterilisation, medical tracers. Ionising &mdash; can cause cancer; penetrating power makes them both useful and hazardous.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Transverse waves (vibrations perpendicular to travel &mdash; light, all EM waves) differ from longitudinal waves (vibrations parallel to travel &mdash; sound). Wave speed is given by \(v = f\lambda\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Reflection: wave bounces, speed/frequency/wavelength unchanged. Refraction: wave changes speed and direction, frequency unchanged. Diffraction: wave spreads, greatest when gap &asymp; wavelength.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">All EM waves travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum. Higher frequency = shorter wavelength = more energy per photon. UV, X-rays and gamma rays are ionising; radio, microwave and infrared are not.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">For wave calculations, always state the equation you are using and substitute values with units before working out the answer. The most common error is confusing frequency and wavelength when substituting into \(v = f\lambda\). Check your answer makes sense: the frequency of visible light is around \(10^{14}\) Hz &mdash; if your answer for a visible light frequency is 400 Hz you know something has gone wrong. For the EM spectrum, you need to know the order, typical uses and the hazards linked to each region. &lsquo;Explain&rsquo; questions expect you to link a property (high energy, ionising, penetrating) to the observed effect (causes cancer, kills bacteria, passes through bone).</p>
