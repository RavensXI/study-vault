--- DESCRIPTION ---
Where our energy comes from, how it is used, and why demand keeps changing.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Does Our Energy Come From?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every time you switch on a light or charge a phone, you are drawing on an energy source somewhere in the national supply chain. The main energy sources available to us fall into two categories:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">&bull; <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that will eventually run out because they are used faster than they can be replaced; examples include fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear fuel.">Non-renewable</dfn> sources will eventually run out. They include <strong>fossil fuels</strong> (coal, oil and natural gas, formed over millions of years from dead organic material) and <strong>nuclear fuel</strong> (uranium and plutonium). Fossil fuels currently provide the majority of global energy, but their reserves are finite and their combustion releases carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change.
<br>&bull; <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that are naturally replenished and will not run out if used sustainably; examples include solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, geothermal and biomass.">Renewable</dfn> sources are naturally replenished. They include <strong>solar</strong>, <strong>wind</strong>, <strong>hydroelectric</strong>, <strong>tidal</strong>, <strong>geothermal</strong>, <strong>wave</strong> and <strong>biomass</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four non-renewable and five renewable energy sources from memory. Then classify nuclear power correctly &mdash; it is non-renewable, but does not emit CO&#8322; during generation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) and nuclear. Renewable: solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, geothermal, wave, biomass. Nuclear is non-renewable (uses uranium ore) but produces no direct CO&#8322; emissions during operation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Energy Demand and How It Changes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Energy demand is not constant. It varies over daily, seasonal and historical timescales:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">&bull; <strong>Daily variation:</strong> demand is highest in the morning (when people wake and use appliances) and in the early evening (cooking, lighting). It falls overnight. Power grid operators must match supply to demand in real time.<br>
&bull; <strong>Seasonal variation:</strong> demand is higher in winter because of heating and lighting needs.<br>
&bull; <strong>Long-term trends:</strong> energy demand has grown historically as populations increase and living standards rise. However, improvements in energy efficiency (better insulation, LED lighting, efficient appliances) are beginning to reduce the energy needed per unit of economic output in many countries.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum level of electricity supply maintained continuously on the national grid to meet baseline demand at all times.">baseload</dfn> is important: some power stations (typically nuclear and large gas plants) run continuously to provide a steady minimum supply. <dfn class="term" data-def="Additional generating capacity brought online to meet demand peaks above the baseload level, typically from gas turbines or renewable sources.">Peak demand</dfn> capacity is additional supply brought in when demand surges above this minimum.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Environmental Impacts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Every energy source has environmental trade-offs. The choice of energy mix involves balancing cost, reliability, environmental impact and carbon emissions:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing Renewables: Advantages and Disadvantages</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Solar:</strong> no CO&#8322; during operation, low running costs. Disadvantages: only generates in daylight and sunshine; large land area needed for solar farms; high upfront cost.<br>
<strong>Wind:</strong> no CO&#8322; during operation, abundant resource. Disadvantages: <dfn class="term" data-def="The variation in power output that occurs because some renewable sources (especially wind and solar) only generate when weather conditions allow.">intermittent</dfn> &mdash; only generates when wind blows; visual and noise impact; can harm birds.<br>
<strong>Hydroelectric:</strong> very reliable, can respond instantly to demand changes. Disadvantages: requires damming rivers, flooding valleys, displacing communities and disrupting ecosystems.<br>
<strong>Tidal:</strong> highly predictable (tides are regular). Disadvantages: very expensive to build; limited suitable locations; can affect marine ecosystems.<br>
<strong>Geothermal:</strong> reliable, low emissions, continuous output. Disadvantages: only available where hot rocks are near the surface (volcanic regions); high drilling costs.<br>
<strong>Biomass:</strong> considered broadly carbon-neutral if managed sustainably (CO&#8322; released equals CO&#8322; absorbed during crop growth). Disadvantages: requires large areas of land; produces air pollution when burned; carbon neutrality is disputed if land use change is factored in.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Fossil fuels release CO&#8322; and other pollutants when burned. Nuclear power produces no CO&#8322; during generation but creates <strong>radioactive waste</strong> that must be stored safely for very long periods. On a full lifecycle analysis (which includes the energy and emissions from manufacturing, construction and decommissioning), wind, solar and nuclear all have much lower carbon footprints than coal or gas.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and pick any two renewable sources. For each, write one specific advantage and one specific disadvantage. Do not use the same advantage for both.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>No energy source is without trade-offs. Renewables produce little CO&#8322; during operation but many are intermittent or require specific geography. Fossil fuels are reliable and energy-dense but emit greenhouse gases. Nuclear is reliable and low-carbon but produces long-lived radioactive waste.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Sankey Diagrams and Energy Transfer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A flow diagram where the width of each arrow is proportional to the amount of energy transferred in that pathway; used to show energy input, useful output and wasted energy.">Sankey diagram</dfn> shows how energy flows through a system. The total width of the arrow on the left (input energy) equals the total of all arrows on the right (useful output + wasted energy). This reflects the principle of conservation of energy &mdash; energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Reading and Drawing a Sankey Diagram</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">A coal power station takes in 100 J of chemical energy in fuel. Of this, 35 J is transferred as useful electrical energy and 65 J is wasted as heat (to the cooling towers and surroundings). On a Sankey diagram, the input arrow is 100 units wide. The useful output arrow pointing right is 35 units wide. The wasted heat arrow pointing down is 65 units wide. The total width of outputs (35 + 65) equals the input (100).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The <strong>efficiency</strong> of this transfer is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}} \times 100\%$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">$$= \frac{35}{100} \times 100\% = 35\%$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The remaining 65% is dissipated as thermal energy to the surroundings, where it is no longer in a useful form. This is not energy being &lsquo;lost&rsquo; &mdash; it still exists &mdash; but it has been transferred to a form that is too spread out to be useful.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">The National Grid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of high-voltage cables, transformers and switching stations that distributes electricity from power stations to homes and businesses across the country.">National Grid</dfn> is the network of high-voltage cables and transformers that distributes electricity across the country. Electricity is transmitted at high voltage to minimise energy losses during transmission. Because power loss in cables is proportional to the square of the current (\(P = I^2 R\)), reducing the current by using a higher voltage substantially reduces wasted heat in the wires.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Step-up transformers at power stations increase the voltage (to hundreds of thousands of volts) for efficient long-distance transmission. Step-down transformers near homes and businesses reduce the voltage to safe levels for use (230 V in UK homes). Balancing supply and demand on the grid in real time requires careful management as more intermittent renewable sources connect to the system.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Non-renewable sources (fossil fuels, nuclear) will run out; renewables (solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, wave, biomass) are replenished naturally. No source is without trade-offs between cost, reliability and environmental impact.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Energy demand varies daily (peak in morning and evening), seasonally (higher in winter) and historically. Baseload power runs continuously; peak capacity responds to surges in demand.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Sankey diagrams show energy flow (width proportional to energy); efficiency = useful output ÷ total input × 100%. The National Grid transmits at high voltage to reduce current and minimise wasted energy in cables.</li>
</ul>
