--- DESCRIPTION ---
How turbines and generators make electricity, and how the National Grid delivers it to your home.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Fuel to Electricity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When you boil your kettle, the electricity that heats the element began its journey kilometres away &mdash; perhaps in a wind farm on a hillside, or inside a large power station. Understanding how it got there involves two key ideas: the <dfn class="term" data-def="A machine that converts kinetic energy (rotational motion) into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.">generator</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The nationwide network of high-voltage cables, pylons, substations and transformers that distribute electricity from power stations to homes and businesses.">National Grid</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In most power stations, fuel (such as natural gas, coal, nuclear fuel, or the heat from geothermal sources) is used to produce <strong>steam</strong>. The steam pushes against the blades of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A device with angled blades that spins when steam or wind passes over them, converting thermal or kinetic energy into rotational kinetic energy.">turbine</dfn>, making it spin at high speed. The spinning turbine is connected to a generator, which converts the rotational energy into electrical energy. In a wind turbine, the wind itself turns the blades directly &mdash; no steam is needed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A generator works by <strong>electromagnetic induction</strong>: when a coil of wire rotates inside a magnetic field (or when a magnet rotates inside a coil), a changing magnetic field induces a <dfn class="term" data-def="The electrical energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts (V). Also called voltage.">potential difference</dfn> and drives a current. The output is <dfn class="term" data-def="Current that constantly changes direction, alternating back and forth. UK mains supply is AC at 50 Hz and about 230 V.">alternating current (AC)</dfn> because as the coil spins, the direction of the induced current reverses every half turn. The UK mains supply is AC at a frequency of 50 Hz and a voltage of about 230 V.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the energy transfer chain from fuel to mains electricity: chemical/nuclear/kinetic energy &rarr; thermal energy &rarr; kinetic energy (turbine) &rarr; electrical energy (generator).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In most power stations: fuel heats water &rarr; steam turns a turbine &rarr; turbine drives a generator &rarr; generator produces AC electricity. Wind turbines skip the steam stage. Generators work by electromagnetic induction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Transformers and Voltage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Once electricity leaves the generator, its voltage must be changed before it travels long distances. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that uses electromagnetic induction to change the voltage of an alternating current. Consists of a primary coil and a secondary coil wound around an iron core.">transformer</dfn> uses electromagnetic induction in an iron core to increase or decrease voltage. There are two types in the National Grid system:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">&bull; A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that increases voltage and decreases current, used at power stations before long-distance transmission.">step-up transformer</dfn> at the power station increases the voltage to very high levels (typically hundreds of thousands of volts) before electricity enters the transmission cables. At higher voltage, the current is lower.<br>&bull; A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that decreases voltage and increases current, used at substations to reduce voltage to safe levels for homes.">step-down transformer</dfn> at a local substation reduces the voltage back to 230 V for safe use in homes and small businesses.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Transformers only work with AC &mdash; they rely on a <em>changing</em> magnetic field in the iron core to induce a voltage in the secondary coil. DC produces a constant (non-changing) field and would not induce any current. This is one of the main reasons the National Grid uses AC rather than DC.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The turns ratio of a transformer determines how it changes voltage. For an ideal (100% efficient) transformer:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">$$\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Where \(V_p\) is the primary voltage, \(V_s\) is the secondary voltage, \(N_p\) is the number of turns on the primary coil, and \(N_s\) is the number of turns on the secondary coil. If the secondary coil has more turns than the primary, the voltage is stepped up; if it has fewer turns, the voltage is stepped down. Because power is conserved in an ideal transformer (\(V_p \times I_p = V_s \times I_s\)), a step-up in voltage is always accompanied by a step-down in current &mdash; and vice versa.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why Transmit at High Voltage?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The cables in the National Grid have resistance. Any current flowing through resistance causes energy to be wasted as heat: \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 \times R\). Because current is squared in this equation, even a small reduction in current makes a dramatic difference to energy losses.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By stepping voltage up before transmission, current is reduced proportionally (since power = voltage &times; current, and power must stay the same). Lower current means far less heat is produced in the cables. This is why transmission losses in the National Grid are small &mdash; the grid is a highly efficient system even though the cables themselves stretch for thousands of kilometres across the country.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: why does the National Grid transmit at high voltage? Link your answer to the equation P = I<sup>2</sup>R and explain what happens to current when voltage is stepped up.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Transmitting at high voltage reduces current. Since cable heat loss = \(I^2R\), low current means very little energy is wasted. Step-up transformers make the grid efficient; step-down transformers make it safe for homes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The National Grid in Practice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The National Grid is the system that connects electricity producers to consumers across the country. Electricity enters the grid at high voltage from a range of sources &mdash; large thermal power stations, offshore wind farms, solar farms, and others. It travels along <strong>overhead power lines</strong> (on tall pylons) or <strong>underground cables</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">At <strong>substations</strong>, step-down transformers reduce the voltage in stages. Industrial users may draw electricity at an intermediate voltage (e.g. 11,000 V), while homes and shops receive it at 230 V. The Grid is a national system, so electricity generated in one part of the country can be directed to areas of high demand elsewhere. This makes the supply more reliable and responsive to peaks in demand (such as during half-time of a major televised sporting event, when millions of people simultaneously use kettles).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Question:</strong> A power station outputs 500 kW. If transmitted at 250 V the current is 2000 A; if transmitted at 25,000 V the current is 20 A. The cable has a resistance of 4 &Omega;. Calculate the power lost as heat in each case.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>At 250 V:</strong> \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 \times R = 2000^2 \times 4 = 16{,}000{,}000 \text{ W} = 16 \text{ MW}\). This is more than the station&rsquo;s output &mdash; completely impractical.<br><strong>At 25,000 V:</strong> \(P_{\text{loss}} = 20^2 \times 4 = 1600 \text{ W} = 1.6 \text{ kW}\). Less than 0.4% of the power is wasted &mdash; far more efficient.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Most power stations convert fuel energy to steam, which drives a turbine, which drives a generator that produces AC electricity via electromagnetic induction. Wind turbines spin generators directly.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Step-up transformers increase voltage (and reduce current) for long-distance transmission; step-down transformers reduce voltage to safe domestic levels. Transformers only work with AC.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">High-voltage transmission keeps current low, minimising heat lost in cables (since \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R\)), making the National Grid efficient.</li>
</ul>
