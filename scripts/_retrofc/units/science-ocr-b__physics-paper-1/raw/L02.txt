--- DESCRIPTION ---
How EM radiation, Earth's energy balance and human emissions interact to warm the planet.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Lessons, Two Disciplines</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Climate change appears in two different parts of your course &mdash; once in chemistry (about the chemical composition of the atmosphere and the evidence for rising CO&#8322; from human activities) and once here in physics. The physics angle focuses on how radiation and energy balance explain why CO&#8322; traps heat. Understanding both together gives you the most complete picture and the tools to answer questions from either discipline.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Earth&rsquo;s Radiation Balance</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Earth receives energy from the Sun as shortwave radiation &mdash; predominantly visible light and some UV. It also loses energy by emitting its own longwave radiation (infrared). For the climate to be stable, the energy arriving must equal the energy leaving. This balance is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The equilibrium between the solar radiation absorbed by Earth and the infrared radiation emitted back to space; imbalances cause warming or cooling.">energy balance</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n5">Not all the solar radiation that arrives at Earth&rsquo;s surface is absorbed. Some is reflected back to space. The fraction of incoming radiation that is reflected is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface; high albedo surfaces (such as ice and snow) reflect more and absorb less.">albedo</dfn>. Surfaces with high albedo (ice, snow, clouds) reflect more solar energy and absorb less. Surfaces with low albedo (dark ocean, forests) absorb more. The average albedo of Earth is roughly 0.3, meaning about 30% of incoming solar energy is reflected without heating the planet.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why melting polar ice reduces Earth&rsquo;s albedo and what effect this has on warming. Use the terms albedo, absorption and energy balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>High albedo surfaces (ice, snow, clouds) reflect solar radiation; low albedo surfaces (ocean, forest) absorb it. As Arctic ice melts, it is replaced by darker ocean water &mdash; lower albedo means more absorption and more warming. This is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A process where the output of a system amplifies the original change, driving further change in the same direction; warming melts ice, which reduces albedo, which causes more warming.">positive feedback</dfn> loop.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Greenhouse Effect</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n8">Earth&rsquo;s surface warms because it absorbs solar radiation and re-emits this energy as <dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic radiation emitted by all objects above absolute zero, with wavelength depending on temperature; warm objects like Earth&rsquo;s surface emit mainly in the infrared range.">infrared radiation</dfn>. The atmosphere contains gases &mdash; principally water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane &mdash; that absorb this outgoing infrared and re-emit some of it back towards Earth&rsquo;s surface. These are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Atmospheric gases that absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth&rsquo;s surface and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards the surface, causing warming.">greenhouse gases</dfn>. Without them, Earth&rsquo;s average surface temperature would be far colder than it is &mdash; the greenhouse effect is natural and essential to life.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n9">The problem is not the greenhouse effect itself but its enhancement. As human activities release more CO&#8322; and methane into the atmosphere, more infrared is absorbed and returned to the surface. The energy balance is disrupted: more energy arrives at the surface than leaves to space, and the planet warms until a new equilibrium is reached at a higher temperature.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">A molecule absorbs radiation when the energy of the photon matches a vibrational mode of the molecule. CO&#8322; and H&#8322;O have molecular structures with vibrational modes (bending and stretching of bonds) that correspond to infrared wavelengths. Nitrogen (N&#8322;) and oxygen (O&#8322;), which make up most of the atmosphere, are symmetrical diatomic molecules with no such vibrational modes at infrared wavelengths &mdash; they are essentially transparent to outgoing infrared. This is why a small increase in CO&#8322; concentration has a disproportionately large effect on the greenhouse effect: the main atmospheric gases already let infrared through; adding CO&#8322; closes the gaps.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the greenhouse mechanism in three steps: what Earth emits, what greenhouse gases do with it, and what the net effect on surface temperature is.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Earth&rsquo;s surface emits infrared. Greenhouse gases (CO&#8322;, CH&#8322; = methane, water vapour) absorb this infrared and re-emit some back to the surface. More greenhouse gas &rarr; more infrared returned &rarr; higher equilibrium surface temperature.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Climate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Understanding why the atmosphere behaves differently towards incoming and outgoing radiation requires placing both on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The full range of electromagnetic radiation ordered by wavelength (or frequency), from long-wavelength radio waves through to short-wavelength gamma rays.">electromagnetic spectrum</dfn>. Incoming solar radiation peaks in the visible and near-infrared region (short wavelengths). Earth, at its surface temperature, emits radiation that peaks in the mid-infrared region (longer wavelengths). The atmosphere is largely transparent to the incoming shortwave radiation but absorbs strongly in specific infrared wavelength bands &mdash; precisely where greenhouse gases have their vibrational modes.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">UV radiation from the Sun is mostly absorbed by ozone in the stratosphere before reaching the surface. Visible light passes through the atmosphere largely unaffected. The key action is at the infrared end: shortwave in, longwave out, with the greenhouse gases acting as an asymmetric filter.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Models, Evidence and Uncertainty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Climate scientists use computer models to predict how rising greenhouse gas concentrations will affect global temperatures. These models are built on well-established physics (the radiation balance principles covered in this lesson) and tested against historical climate data. Where models from different research groups using different assumptions agree, scientists have greater confidence in the prediction. Where they disagree, this signals genuine uncertainty about feedbacks and timescales.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The key evidence that the current warming is human-caused rather than natural includes: the rate of warming is faster than any natural cycle detected in ice-core records; rising CO&#8322; concentrations in the atmosphere correlate with industrial activity; the isotopic signature of atmospheric CO&#8322; shows it comes from burning fossil fuels; and the stratosphere is cooling while the troposphere warms &mdash; the pattern expected from greenhouse gas forcing, not from increased solar output. This is an example of how multiple independent lines of evidence converge to support one explanation &mdash; a hallmark of strong scientific reasoning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Consequences and the Need for Action</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A disrupted energy balance has physical consequences that flow directly from the physics. Warmer oceans expand (thermal expansion) and melt land ice, raising sea levels. Warmer air holds more water vapour, intensifying rainfall events and droughts. The extra energy in the climate system drives more intense weather. These effects are not uniform globally, but the physics of the energy imbalance means they are unavoidable until the atmosphere returns to a new equilibrium.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Reducing greenhouse gas emissions directly addresses the physical cause: fewer greenhouse gas molecules in the atmosphere means less infrared is absorbed and returned to the surface, allowing the energy balance to stabilise at a lower equilibrium temperature. Switching from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources reduces the rate at which CO&#8322; is added to the atmosphere without removing CO&#8322; already there &mdash; hence why scientists also discuss carbon capture technologies that directly reduce atmospheric CO&#8322; concentrations.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Earth&rsquo;s energy balance: shortwave solar radiation in, longwave infrared radiation out. Albedo determines how much solar energy is reflected; high-albedo surfaces (ice, snow) reflect more and absorb less.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Greenhouse gases (CO&#8322;, CH&#8322;, water vapour) absorb outgoing infrared and re-emit some back to the surface; more greenhouse gas disrupts the energy balance and raises equilibrium surface temperature.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The atmosphere is largely transparent to incoming shortwave (visible) radiation but absorbs strongly at infrared wavelengths where greenhouse gases have vibrational modes &mdash; this asymmetry is the physical basis of the greenhouse effect.</li>
</ul>
