--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Permanent and Induced Magnets</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A magnet that produces its own magnetic field at all times, without needing an external current or field.">permanent magnet</dfn> produces a magnetic field continuously. It always has a north pole and a south pole, and the fundamental rule of magnetism applies: <strong>like poles repel; unlike poles attract</strong>. Common permanent magnet materials include iron, steel, nickel and cobalt &mdash; these are all <dfn class="term" data-def="Materials that are attracted to magnets and can be magnetised, such as iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.">magnetic materials</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A magnetic material that only becomes a magnet when placed inside an external magnetic field, losing its magnetism when removed.">induced magnet</dfn> is different &mdash; it only becomes magnetic when placed inside another magnet&rsquo;s field. The induced poles arrange themselves so that unlike poles face the permanent magnet, meaning an induced magnet is <em>always attracted</em> to the magnet that induces it. This explains why an ordinary iron nail is picked up by a permanent magnet even though the nail itself is not a magnet.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Remember: like poles repel, unlike poles attract. An induced magnet always attracts the permanent magnet near it &mdash; never repels. Cover this and explain why using the concept of induced poles.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. An induced magnet is temporary &mdash; it only has magnetism while inside another magnet&rsquo;s field &mdash; and always attracts the permanent magnet that induced it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Magnetic Fields</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The region around a magnet where a magnetic material or moving charge experiences a force.">magnetic field</dfn> is the region around a magnet where magnetic forces act. Fields are represented by field lines:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">&bull; Field lines run from <strong>north to south</strong> outside the magnet.<br>&bull; Where lines are <strong>closer together</strong>, the field is <strong>stronger</strong>.<br>&bull; The field is strongest at the <strong>poles</strong> and weakens with distance.<br>&bull; Field lines never cross.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">You can map magnetic fields using a <strong>plotting compass</strong>: place it near the magnet, mark where the needle points, move the compass to the new position, and repeat. Connecting the marks gives smooth field lines. Iron filings also show the pattern visually as they align along field lines. The Earth itself has a magnetic field, produced by convection currents in its liquid outer core &mdash; this is why a compass needle points roughly north.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">When current flows through a straight wire, it creates a circular magnetic field around the wire. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A coil of wire that produces a strong, uniform magnetic field inside it when current flows through it. Its field pattern resembles that of a bar magnet.">solenoid</dfn> is a coil of wire &mdash; the fields from each turn combine to produce a strong, uniform field inside the coil with clear north and south poles at each end.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Adding a <strong>soft iron core</strong> inside the solenoid creates an <dfn class="term" data-def="A solenoid with a soft iron core, producing a strong magnet that can be switched on and off with the current.">electromagnet</dfn>. The iron core becomes an induced magnet and dramatically strengthens the field. Unlike permanent magnets, electromagnets can be <strong>switched on and off</strong> and their strength adjusted by changing the current, the number of coil turns, or the core material. Uses include scrapyard cranes, electric bells, relays, loudspeakers and MRI scanners.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Motor Effect</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n12">When a current-carrying conductor is placed inside a magnetic field, it experiences a force. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field, caused by the interaction between the conductor&rsquo;s own field and the external field.">motor effect</dfn>. The force is greatest when the conductor is at <strong>right angles</strong> to the field and zero when it is parallel to the field.</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n13">The force can be increased by:</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n14">&bull; Increasing the <strong>current</strong> through the conductor<br>&bull; Increasing the <strong>magnetic flux density</strong> (using a stronger magnet)<br>&bull; Increasing the <strong>length</strong> of conductor within the field</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">These relationships are captured in the equation:</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$F = B \times I \times l$$</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(F\) is force (N), \(B\) is <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the strength of a magnetic field, measured in tesla (T).">magnetic flux density</dfn> (T), \(I\) is current (A), and \(l\) is the length of the conductor in the field (m).</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what three changes increase the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field? Then state the equation F = BIl with units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The motor effect equation: \(F = BIl\). Force is greatest when conductor and field are at right angles. Increasing current, field strength, or conductor length all increase the force.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Fleming&rsquo;s Left-Hand Rule</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="A rule for finding the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor: thumb = thrust (force direction), first finger = field direction (N to S), second finger = conventional current direction (+ to -).">Fleming&rsquo;s left-hand rule</dfn> tells you the direction of the force. Hold your left hand with the thumb, first finger and second finger all at right angles to each other:</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n21">&bull; <strong>Thumb</strong> (thu<strong>M</strong>b) = <strong>M</strong>otion / force direction<br>&bull; <strong>First finger</strong> (<strong>F</strong>irst) = <strong>F</strong>ield direction (north to south)<br>&bull; <strong>Second finger</strong> (se<strong>C</strong>ond) = <strong>C</strong>urrent direction (conventional, from positive to negative)</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n22">If any one of the three quantities reverses (e.g. the current direction reverses, or you flip the magnet so field reverses), the force direction also reverses.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">In a simple DC electric motor, a current-carrying coil sits between two permanent magnets. Using Fleming&rsquo;s left-hand rule, the two sides of the coil experience forces in <strong>opposite directions</strong> (because current flows in opposite directions on each side), creating a turning effect (torque) that spins the coil.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="higher-only">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Without further modification, the coil would stop after rotating halfway. A <strong>split-ring commutator</strong> reverses the current direction every half turn, ensuring the forces always produce rotation in the same direction &mdash; giving continuous spinning. Motor speed can be increased by using a stronger magnetic field, a larger current, or more coil turns.</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Real-World Applications of Electromagnetism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The motor effect is not just a classroom concept &mdash; it underpins a vast range of modern technology. <strong>Loudspeakers</strong> work by sending a varying electrical current through a coil (attached to a paper cone) that sits inside a permanent magnet. As the current changes direction and magnitude, the motor-effect force on the coil changes, pushing the cone in and out and creating sound waves.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)</strong> scanners used in hospitals contain powerful electromagnets that produce extremely strong, uniform magnetic fields. These align the magnetic properties of hydrogen atoms in the body, and by measuring how those atoms re-emit energy, detailed images of soft tissue can be produced without using ionising radiation. The strength of an MRI magnet is measured in tesla &mdash; clinical scanners typically use fields of 1.5 T to 3 T, far stronger than anything achievable with permanent magnets of practical size. This illustrates how electromagnetic principles developed in physics laboratories have direct, life-saving medical applications.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Induced magnets are temporary and always attract the magnet that induces them. Electromagnets can be switched on and off and have adjustable strength.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The motor effect: a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force (F = BIl). Force is greatest at right angles to the field.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Fleming&rsquo;s left-hand rule gives the force direction: Thumb = Motion, First finger = Field (N to S), Second finger = Current (+ to -). Electric motors use this effect to produce continuous rotation via a split-ring commutator.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">For questions on Fleming&rsquo;s left-hand rule, always identify all three quantities (force, field, current) before answering. A common mistake is using the right hand instead of the left &mdash; Fleming&rsquo;s <em>left</em>-hand rule is for the motor effect. For &lsquo;Suggest&rsquo; questions about increasing the force on a conductor, link each suggestion directly to the equation \(F = BIl\) &mdash; examiners reward causal language, not just lists.</p>
