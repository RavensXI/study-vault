--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five Major Themes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every exam question on <em>Great Expectations</em> will connect to one or more key themes. Prepare quotations for each so you can respond to any extract confidently. The five most commonly tested themes are: <strong>class and social ambition</strong>, <strong>crime and justice</strong>, <strong>love and loyalty</strong>, <strong>guilt and redemption</strong>, and <strong>appearance versus reality</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list the five themes and one quotation for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Class:</strong> "He calls the knaves, Jacks, this boy!" (Estella exposes class through speech). <strong>Crime &amp; Justice:</strong> "I've been done everything to, pretty well — except hanged" (Magwitch on class-based punishment). <strong>Love &amp; Loyalty:</strong> "Ever the best of friends, ain't us, Pip?" (Joe's unconditional love). <strong>Guilt &amp; Redemption:</strong> "If I could have kept him away by paying money" (Pip's shame about Joe). <strong>Appearance vs Reality:</strong> "I saw no shadow of another parting from her" (the ambiguous ending questions what is real).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">What the 19th-Century Novel Question Tests</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Your response is assessed on three strands: your critical response to the text with well-chosen references (AO1), your analysis of the writer's methods — language, structure and form (AO2), and your understanding of context (AO3). You will receive a printed extract (~400 words) and must write about both the extract and the novel as a whole. Check your exam board's specimen paper and mark scheme for the exact weightings and timings that apply to your exam.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each AO, note one thing your paragraph must do to hit it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>AO1 — Critical response:</strong> Build a clear argument and support it with well-chosen references from the extract AND the novel as a whole. <strong>AO2 — Writer's methods:</strong> Analyse Dickens's methods — language, structure, narrative voice, symbolism. Use terminology accurately. <strong>AO3 — Context:</strong> Show understanding of Victorian context and link it to Dickens's purpose. Embed context in your analysis; never bolt it on.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">How to Structure Your Essay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Introduction</strong> (1–2 sentences): state your argument clearly. <strong>Extract paragraphs</strong> (2–3): close analysis of language and structure in the given extract. <strong>Whole-novel paragraphs</strong> (2–3): develop your argument with references from elsewhere in the text. <strong>Conclusion</strong> (brief): summarise your argument and link back to Dickens's purpose.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Each analytical paragraph should follow <dfn class="term" data-def="Point, Evidence, Analyse Language, Link to Context, Evaluate — a paragraph structure for literature essays.">PEALCE</dfn>: Point → Evidence (quotation) → Analyse Language (what specific words/techniques do and how they affect the reader) → Link to Context (why Dickens chose this) → Evaluate (what it suggests about his purpose). The biggest differentiator is detailed analysis of methods, not just feature-spotting.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Example Paragraph: Analysing Joe's Visit</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Point:</strong> Dickens uses Pip's treatment of Joe to expose the moral corruption caused by class ambition. <strong>Evidence:</strong> Pip admits: "If I could have kept him away by paying money, I certainly would have paid money." <strong>Analysis:</strong> The <dfn class="term" data-def="A sentence structure that expresses a condition and its consequence; 'If I could have… I would have' shows a calculated willingness to pay.">conditional structure</dfn> ('If I could have… I certainly would have') shows this is not a passing thought but a considered willingness. The repetition of 'money' reduces a human relationship to a transaction, mirroring the class system's logic. <strong>Context:</strong> Dickens, who experienced class shame at the blacking factory, understood how social ambition could corrode genuine relationships. By making the reader feel Joe's hurt alongside Pip's embarrassment, Dickens forces us to judge a system that makes people ashamed of their own families. <strong>Evaluate:</strong> This sentence is the novel's most damning evidence that becoming a "gentleman" has made Pip a worse person — Dickens's central argument against the class system.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Common Exam Themes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Exam questions typically ask "How does Dickens present…" a theme or character, starting with the extract. Common themes: class and ambition, guilt and shame, crime and justice, love and loyalty, settings and symbolism, the gentleman ideal, redemption. For each, prepare 2–3 quotations from different parts of the novel and practise linking to Dickens's methods and Victorian context.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Prepare quotations for five themes: class/ambition, crime/justice, love/loyalty, guilt/redemption, and appearance/reality. Aim for 2–3 short quotations per theme from different parts of the novel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">The 30-mark question requires analysis of both extract and whole novel. Balance your time roughly equally. Always embed context — never bolt it on as a separate paragraph.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Follow PEALCE for every paragraph and focus on Dickens’s methods (AO2) — this is the biggest mark differentiator.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">The highest marks go to responses that analyse Dickens’s methods, not just his themes. Instead of “Dickens shows class is unfair,” write “Dickens’s conditional structure ‘If I could have… I certainly would have’ reveals Pip’s calculated willingness to buy Joe’s absence, exposing how class ambition reduces relationships to financial transactions.” Always explain the effect of specific words and techniques on the reader.</p>
