Victorian class, social mobility, and why Dickens wrote Great Expectations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dickens and the Victorian Class System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charles Dickens published <em>Great Expectations</em> as a weekly serial in his magazine <em>All the Year Round</em> between December 1860 and August 1861. Like much of Dickens’s work, the novel draws on his own painful experience of poverty and class humiliation. When Dickens was twelve, his father was imprisoned for debt and the young Charles was sent to work in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A factory where workers pasted labels onto pots of boot polish; Dickens worked in one as a child after his father’s imprisonment, an experience that shaped his lifelong sensitivity to class and poverty.">blacking factory</dfn>. This childhood shame never left him — even as a wealthy, celebrated author, he remained haunted by the fear of being judged for his humble origins.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Victorian Britain had a rigid <dfn class="term" data-def="A hierarchical system ranking people by birth, wealth, and occupation; in Victorian England, the upper classes had enormous power over the working classes, and crossing class boundaries was extremely difficult.">class system</dfn>. Your birth determined your place in society. At the top were the landed gentry and aristocracy; in the middle, the growing professional and merchant classes; at the bottom, the labouring poor. The novel asks a question that obsessed Victorian society: can a person rise above their birth, and if so, at what cost?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three biographical details about Dickens connect to Great Expectations?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dickens’s three key biographical links: (1) his father was imprisoned for debt, (2) he worked in a blacking factory as a child and felt deep shame about his class, and (3) he became wealthy but never lost his sensitivity to how the upper classes treated the poor. Pip’s shame about Joe and the forge mirrors Dickens’s own uncomfortable relationship with his origins.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Social Mobility and “Gentlemanly” Ideals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Victorian concept of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A man of good social position and refined manners; in Victorian society, being a ‘gentleman’ was defined by wealth, education, and behaviour, though Dickens questions whether money alone can create true gentility.">gentleman</dfn> is central to the novel. In theory, a gentleman was defined by moral character, education, and good manners. In practice, the title depended almost entirely on wealth and birth. Pip believes that becoming a gentleman will make him worthy of Estella’s love and superior to his humble origins. Dickens uses Pip’s journey to expose this belief as a dangerous illusion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The rapid development of industry in Britain from the late 18th century, which created a new class of wealthy industrialists and increased the gap between rich and poor.">Industrial Revolution</dfn> had created new opportunities for social mobility. Self-made men like Dickens himself could rise through talent and hard work. But the old aristocracy and landed gentry looked down on “new money” as vulgar. This tension between inherited status and earned wealth runs through the novel: Pip receives his fortune from a convict (the lowest class), not from Miss Havisham (the gentry), and his journey towards becoming a gentleman leads him to become snobbish, ungrateful, and morally corrupt.</p>

If a transported convict returned to England, they faced execution. Magwitch risks death by returning to see Pip, the “gentleman” he has secretly funded. Dickens uses the criminal justice system to expose class hypocrisy: Magwitch is treated as subhuman by the law, yet he shows more genuine love and generosity than the “respectable” Miss Havisham or Compeyson, the educated criminal who manipulated both Magwitch and Miss Havisham.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Childhood and Education</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Dickens was deeply concerned about the treatment of children in Victorian society. Child labour was rampant, education was limited for the poor, and orphans like Pip had few protections. Pip is raised “by hand” by his sister Mrs Joe, a phrase that suggests both physical discipline and the harsh conditions of his upbringing. The novel opens in a graveyard where Pip visits his parents’ and siblings’ headstones — five of his brothers and sisters died in infancy, reflecting the appallingly high child mortality rate of the period.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Education was seen as the pathway to self-improvement, but Dickens shows its limitations. Pip’s education at Matthew Pocket’s makes him more polished on the surface but does not make him a better person. Joe, who can barely read, is the novel’s moral heart. Biddy, who is self-educated, is kind and perceptive. Dickens argues that moral education — learning compassion, loyalty, and humility — matters far more than academic learning.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down how Dickens contrasts Joe’s moral worth with Pip’s ‘gentleman’ status.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dickens uses Joe Gargery to challenge the Victorian definition of a gentleman. Joe is uneducated, working-class, and unsophisticated, but he is also the most honest, loyal, and morally upright character in the novel. When Pip falls ill, Joe nurses him back to health and quietly pays his debts. Dickens’s message is clear: true gentility is defined by character, not by wealth, birth, or education.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Dickens’s Purpose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Dickens wrote <em>Great Expectations</em> to expose the hollowness of the Victorian class system and the moral dangers of social ambition. Through Pip, he shows how the pursuit of wealth and status can corrupt a person’s character, destroy genuine relationships, and lead to misery. The novel is also a story of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of earning forgiveness and moral restoration through suffering, self-awareness, and changed behaviour.">redemption</dfn> — Pip eventually recognises his faults, repairs his relationships, and learns that true worth comes from integrity and compassion, not from money or social position.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The serial format shaped the novel’s structure. Each weekly instalment ended on a cliffhanger or revelation, keeping readers hooked. Dickens revised the ending after feedback from a friend: the original ending was bleaker, with Pip and Estella parting permanently. The published ending is ambiguous — “I saw no shadow of another parting from her” — leaving readers to decide whether Pip and Estella finally find happiness together.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><em>Great Expectations</em> is a <em>Bildungsroman</em>: a coming-of-age novel that traces Pip’s moral and psychological development from childhood to adulthood. The first-person <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator looking back on events from their past, often with greater wisdom and self-awareness than they had at the time.">retrospective narration</dfn> is crucial: the adult Pip tells the story with hindsight, recognising and judging the mistakes of his younger self. This creates a double perspective — we experience events through young Pip’s eyes but hear them filtered through older Pip’s regret. Dickens uses this tension to invite the reader to both sympathise with and judge Pip’s choices.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Dickens drew on his own experience of poverty and class shame to write <em>Great Expectations</em>, using Pip’s journey to expose the moral emptiness of the Victorian class system.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The novel challenges the Victorian ideal of the “gentleman” by showing that wealth and education do not create moral worth — Joe, the uneducated blacksmith, is the truest gentleman in the book.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><em>Great Expectations</em> is a <em>Bildungsroman</em> told through retrospective narration, creating a double perspective that invites the reader to both sympathise with and judge Pip’s choices.</li></ul>
