--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Atlantic trade triangle and the entrenchment of plantation slavery on tobacco and rice estates.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Atlantic Trade Triangle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the early eighteenth century, the most profitable trade route in the British Empire ran in three legs around the Atlantic Ocean. From British ports such as Bristol and Liverpool, ships carried manufactured goods &mdash; cloth, iron bars, firearms, brandy and beads &mdash; to West Africa. There those goods were exchanged with African coastal rulers and merchants for captive Africans, sold into bondage by states that profited from the trade. The ships then crossed the ocean on the second leg, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The middle stage of the transatlantic slave trade, in which captive Africans were shipped across the Atlantic to the Americas.">Middle Passage</dfn>, carrying the captives to plantations in the Caribbean and on the American mainland. On the third leg, ships sailed back to Britain laden with the products that enslaved labour had grown: sugar, rum, tobacco and rice. Each leg fed the next; the system was, in the cold language of contemporary merchants, a single self-financing circuit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what travelled on each leg of the Atlantic trade triangle, and what was the middle leg called?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Atlantic trade triangle had three legs. Britain to Africa: manufactured goods. Africa to the Americas: enslaved people, on the Middle Passage. Americas to Britain: tobacco, sugar, rice and rum. The system tied British wealth to enslaved labour.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Middle Passage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Middle Passage was an act of mass cruelty. Enslaved Africans were chained below decks in ships designed to carry as many people as possible &mdash; often two adults per square metre &mdash; for crossings that could last six to ten weeks. Filth, disease and starvation were constant. Around one in seven captives died on the voyage; survivors landed in Caribbean or American ports broken in body and forced to begin a life of unfree labour. Around 12.5 million Africans were shipped across the Atlantic by European traders between 1500 and the early nineteenth century, of whom around 10.7 million survived to be sold. The British share of this trade rose sharply after the 1713 <em>asiento</em> &mdash; a Spanish contract Britain won at the Treaty of Utrecht &mdash; gave British traders the right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America. The slave trade was therefore not a peripheral activity but a central pillar of British Atlantic commerce.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">From Indentured Labour to Chattel Slavery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Enslaved labour was not the original solution to the colonial labour shortage. Through the seventeenth century, plantation owners in Virginia and Maryland had relied heavily on white indentured servants from England and Ireland, who served four to seven years in exchange for passage and a small parcel of land. As wages improved in Britain after 1660, fewer poor English people chose indenture. At the same time, after Bacon&rsquo;s Rebellion of 1676, Chesapeake elites became wary of arming a class of poor white labourers who might unite with enslaved Africans against them. Planters turned increasingly to the transatlantic slave trade for a workforce that, under colonial law, could be held for life and whose status was inherited by their children. By the early eighteenth century, this shift was complete. Slavery in the colonies became <dfn class="term" data-def="A system in which enslaved people are treated as personal property, owned for life, with their status passed to their children.">chattel slavery</dfn> &mdash; a form of bondage based on race, owned for life, and inherited.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the difference between indentured servitude and chattel slavery, and one reason planters made the shift.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Indentured servitude was time-limited, voluntary and ended in freedom. Chattel slavery was lifelong, racial and inherited. The shift in the late seventeenth century locked race into the colonial labour system.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Tobacco Plantations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the Chesapeake, enslaved Africans worked on <dfn class="term" data-def="Large agricultural estates that produced cash crops for export, worked by enslaved labourers under an overseer.">plantations</dfn> growing tobacco. Tobacco was a delicate, labour-hungry crop. The leaves had to be transplanted by hand, weeded, suckered, harvested, hung in barns to cure, and packed into hogsheads for shipment. The work ran almost the whole year. Plantation owners typically forced their enslaved workforce into a gang labour system, supervised by a paid overseer with the authority to punish. Living conditions were brutal: cabins for enslaved families were small, food rations basic, and the laws known as slave codes (extensively revised after the Stono Rebellion of 1739) gave owners almost unchecked power. By 1740, around 150,000 enslaved Africans worked the Chesapeake; by 1770, that figure had passed 250,000. Tobacco profits paid for the silver and china of Virginia&rsquo;s great houses; enslaved hands grew the crop that paid for both.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Rice Plantations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Further south, in coastal South Carolina and Georgia, the dominant crop was rice. Rice cultivation was even more punishing than tobacco. Enslaved Africans cleared swamp land, built earthen banks and floodgates, and worked knee-deep in water under a hot Atlantic sun. Many died of malaria and yellow fever. Carolina planters specifically sought enslaved people from rice-growing regions of West Africa, particularly Senegambia and present-day Sierra Leone, because of the agricultural skills they brought. By the 1740s, enslaved people made up the majority of the population of South Carolina &mdash; the only mainland colony where this was true. Plantation life on the Carolina coast operated under a <em>task</em> system rather than a gang system: an enslaved person was given a fixed daily quota and could (in theory) use the rest of the day for themselves, including time to grow food on small plots. The reality was still bondage; the slight difference in arrangement reflected the rhythm of rice agriculture, not any softening of slavery.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common misconception is to treat slavery as a Southern phenomenon. In fact enslaved people lived in every colony before the Revolution. New York City had one of the largest urban populations of enslaved Africans in the colonies; Philadelphia, Boston and Newport all had thriving slave markets. Newport in Rhode Island became one of the leading slave-trading ports of the British Empire. Northern abolition is a story of the 1780s and 1790s, after the Revolution &mdash; not a feature of the colonial period.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The transatlantic slave trade and the plantation system were not a backdrop to the colonial economy &mdash; they were the colonial economy. Tobacco and rice were the two most valuable mainland exports; both were produced by enslaved labour. Britain&rsquo;s wealth, the prosperity of colonial elites, the demand that drew Scots-Irish and German immigrants to the cheaper land of the backcountry, and the daily life of enslaved Africans were all knotted together. Any account of British America that puts slavery to one side has missed its centre.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Atlantic trade triangle linked Britain, West Africa and the Americas; the Middle Passage carried around 10.7 million enslaved Africans to the New World between 1500 and the early nineteenth century.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">By the early eighteenth century, colonial elites had shifted from indentured servitude to chattel slavery &mdash; bondage based on race, held for life, and inherited &mdash; to work tobacco and rice plantations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Slavery was a colonies-wide institution: tobacco in the Chesapeake, rice in the Carolinas, and enslaved domestic and dock labour in northern cities such as New York and Newport.</li>
</ul>
