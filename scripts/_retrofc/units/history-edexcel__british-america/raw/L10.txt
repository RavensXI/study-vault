--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reconciliation Was Still the Goal in 1774</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When delegates from twelve colonies (Georgia stayed away) gathered at Philadelphia in September 1774, almost none of them wanted independence. They wanted the Intolerable Acts repealed and the constitutional relationship of 1763 restored. The shift from that goal to the Declaration of Independence took less than two years and was anything but predictable. Battlefield events, the king&rsquo;s response and a single forty-page pamphlet all played a role. The lesson&rsquo;s central skill is causation: showing why a movement that began by petitioning the Crown in 1774 ended up rejecting the Crown altogether in July 1776.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who attended the First Continental Congress, when did it meet, and what was the Continental Association?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The First Continental Congress met at Philadelphia in September&ndash;October 1774. Twelve colonies sent delegates (Georgia did not). It produced a Declaration of Rights and Grievances and the Continental Association &mdash; a coordinated boycott of British imports enforced by local committees of inspection.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The First Continental Congress, 1774</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The first inter-colonial assembly of delegates from twelve colonies, meeting at Philadelphia in September&ndash;October 1774 to coordinate resistance to the Intolerable Acts.">First Continental Congress</dfn> sat at Carpenters&rsquo; Hall in Philadelphia from 5 September to 26 October 1774. Fifty-six delegates attended, including conservatives like John Dickinson and Joseph Galloway who hoped for reconciliation, and radicals like John Adams and Patrick Henry who already suspected war was coming. The Congress did three things. First, it issued a Declaration of Rights and Grievances objecting to the Intolerable Acts and to taxation without representation, but explicitly accepting Parliament&rsquo;s right to regulate trade. Second, it set up the Continental Association: a colony-wide non-importation, non-consumption and (from September 1775) non-exportation boycott of British trade, enforced by local committees of inspection. Third, it agreed to meet again in May 1775 if grievances were not resolved. The mood was firm but cautious. Galloway&rsquo;s plan for an American Parliament under the Crown was rejected by a single vote, but only just &mdash; reconciliation was still on the table.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Lexington, Concord and the Second Congress, 1775</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The next ten months hardened the conflict. On 19 April 1775 General Thomas Gage sent 700 redcoats from Boston to seize a colonial arms cache at Concord. Massachusetts militia met them on Lexington Green and at Concord Bridge. By the time the British column straggled back to Boston, 73 of its men were dead and around 95 were wounded. The Massachusetts militia laid siege to Boston the next day. When the <dfn class="term" data-def="The inter-colonial assembly that first met at Philadelphia in May 1775, raised the Continental Army and declared independence in July 1776.">Second Continental Congress</dfn> reconvened at Philadelphia on 10 May 1775 it was already a wartime government. It appointed George Washington commander-in-chief of a Continental Army on 15 June 1775. It also tried one last gesture of reconciliation: the Olive Branch Petition of July 1775, which addressed George III directly and asked him to call off his ministers. The king refused even to receive it and in August declared the colonies in rebellion. By December 1775 Parliament&rsquo;s Prohibitory Act stopped trade with the rebellious colonies and authorised the seizure of American ships &mdash; in effect putting the colonies outside the king&rsquo;s protection. Many colonists later said that this was the moment they accepted that independence was inevitable.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">A common student error is to write the Revolution as a foregone conclusion from the Stamp Act onwards. The First Continental Congress&rsquo;s Declaration of Rights and Grievances, the Olive Branch Petition of July 1775 and Galloway&rsquo;s near-successful plan for an American Parliament under the Crown all show that reconciliation was still the majority position deep into 1775. The shift to independence was contingent on three things that did not have to happen: George III&rsquo;s refusal to receive the Olive Branch Petition, the Prohibitory Act of December 1775, and the publication of Paine&rsquo;s &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo; in January 1776. Higher-band analytical-narrative answers explain how these contingent events converted opinion; lower-band answers treat the outcome as inevitable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Thomas Paine&rsquo;s &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo;, January 1776</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In January 1776 the English-born radical writer <dfn class="term" data-def="English-born radical writer whose pamphlet &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo; (January 1776) made the case for independence to a mass colonial readership.">Thomas Paine</dfn> published a forty-page pamphlet called &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo;. It sold around 120,000 copies in three months and perhaps 500,000 over the year &mdash; in a colonial population of roughly 2.5 million. Paine did three things no previous patriot writer had done. He attacked monarchy itself rather than the king&rsquo;s ministers, calling George III the &lsquo;Royal Brute of Britain&rsquo;. He framed independence as common sense rather than radical revolution &mdash; America was a continent, not a province. And he wrote in plain English aimed at farmers, sailors and tradesmen rather than lawyers and assemblymen. Paine made the case that no constitutional reform within the empire could solve the colonies&rsquo; grievances, because the problem was the system, not the personalities. Within months, colonial assemblies began authorising their delegates at the Second Congress to vote for independence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: when was &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo; published, who wrote it, and roughly how many copies sold in the first three months?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thomas Paine&rsquo;s &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo;, published January 1776, sold around 120,000 copies in three months in a population of 2.5 million. It attacked monarchy itself, not just the king&rsquo;s ministers, and reframed independence as common sense for a continent.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Jefferson and the Declaration of Independence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">On 7 June 1776 Richard Henry Lee of Virginia moved a resolution at the Second Congress &lsquo;That these United Colonies are, and of right ought to be, free and independent States.&rsquo; A drafting committee of five &mdash; John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, Robert Livingston and the thirty-three-year-old Virginia delegate <dfn class="term" data-def="Virginia delegate to the Continental Congress and principal drafter of the Declaration of Independence in July 1776.">Thomas Jefferson</dfn> &mdash; was appointed to write a justification. Jefferson did most of the writing. His draft argued that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed, that George III had broken that contract, and that &lsquo;all men are created equal&rsquo; with rights to &lsquo;life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness&rsquo;. On 2 July 1776 Lee&rsquo;s resolution passed; on 4 July 1776 the Congress approved Jefferson&rsquo;s amended Declaration of Independence. Eighteenth-century broadside printers had it in every colonial newspaper within weeks.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Jefferson&rsquo;s &lsquo;all men are created equal&rsquo; was selective, and contemporary readers knew it. Jefferson himself enslaved more than 600 people across his lifetime. His original draft included a clause condemning the king for the transatlantic slave trade, but the Congress struck it out at the insistence of Southern delegates &mdash; especially South Carolina and Georgia. The final Declaration is silent on slavery. It is also hostile to Indigenous peoples, accusing the king of having &lsquo;endeavoured to bring on the inhabitants of our frontiers, the merciless Indian Savages&rsquo;. Holding both readings together &mdash; the Declaration as a landmark statement of natural rights and the Declaration as a document that erased the rights of enslaved people and Indigenous nations &mdash; is what a top-band judgement looks like in the unit&rsquo;s final lesson.</p>
  </div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The First Continental Congress (Sept&ndash;Oct 1774) sought reconciliation through a Declaration of Rights and Grievances and a Continental Association boycott; the Second Congress (May 1775) became a wartime government, raising the Continental Army and trying the Olive Branch Petition before the king&rsquo;s rejection forced the issue.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Thomas Paine&rsquo;s &lsquo;Common Sense&rsquo; (January 1776) attacked monarchy itself, framed independence as common sense for a continent, and converted mass public opinion in three months.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776), drafted by Thomas Jefferson, made the case for independence in the language of natural rights &mdash; while remaining silent on slavery and hostile to Indigenous peoples.</li>
</ul>

Acknowledge that the Declaration was silent on slavery and hostile to Indigenous peoples &mdash; examiners reward this complication, not the celebratory reading.</p>
