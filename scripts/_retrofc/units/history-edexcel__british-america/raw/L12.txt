Slavery after independence, the cost to Indigenous peoples, and Loyalist exile to Canada.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Actually Won?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Declaration of Independence proclaimed that &lsquo;all men are created equal&rsquo;. The Peace of Paris ceded the United States territory the size of Western Europe. Yet for three groups the war was a defeat dressed as victory. Around half a million enslaved African Americans remained enslaved after independence, and the Constitution of 1787 entrenched that slavery in federal law. Indigenous nations, having mostly backed Britain to defend their lands, were treated by the new United States as a defeated people whose territory was forfeit. And around 60,000 Loyalists were forced into exile. Holding all three groups in view, alongside the celebrated story of patriot triumph, is what a balanced judgement on the unit looks like in the highest band.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three groups whose experience complicates the patriot victory narrative, with one detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The war&rsquo;s consequences cut three ways: enslaved African Americans (around half a million remained enslaved despite Northern abolition); Indigenous peoples (treated by the new republic as defeated and dispossessed); and around 60,000 Loyalists (driven into exile, mostly to Nova Scotia and the Niagara region).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Independence and Slavery: A Two-Speed Settlement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In the Northern states, the rhetoric of liberty did push abolition forward, but slowly. Vermont&rsquo;s 1777 constitution was the first in the world to ban adult slavery. Pennsylvania passed a Gradual Abolition Act in 1780; Massachusetts ended slavery through a series of court rulings in 1781&ndash;83 (notably the Quock Walker case); New York and New Jersey passed gradual emancipation laws in 1799 and 1804. Most freed children only on reaching adulthood, and many enslaved people were sold south before that deadline. In the Southern states the opposite happened. The cotton boom of the 1790s entrenched the plantation system. The 1787 Constitution&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="The compromise in the 1787 US Constitution that counted three out of every five enslaved people for the purpose of representation in the House of Representatives.">three-fifths clause</dfn> counted three out of every five enslaved people for the purpose of representation in Congress &mdash; giving slaveholding states more votes without granting any to the enslaved. The same Constitution forbade Congress from ending the transatlantic slave trade before 1808. The new United States legalised the regional split that the Civil War would eventually fight over.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Black Loyalists and the Promise of Freedom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Many enslaved people read the war differently from white patriots. In November 1775 Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, proclaimed freedom to any enslaved man who would join the British forces. Around 800 men responded. Sir Henry Clinton&rsquo;s Philipsburg Proclamation of 1779 widened the offer to any enslaved person of either sex who reached British lines. Tens of thousands ran. When the British evacuated New York in 1783 the navy carried away around 3,000 free Black Loyalists in what became known as the Book of Negroes &mdash; a meticulous register of their names and former enslavers. Most settled in <dfn class="term" data-def="British colony in eastern Canada that received around 30,000 white Loyalists and 3,000 free Black Loyalists after the war.">Nova Scotia</dfn>. They received less land than white Loyalists, faced segregation and a violent race riot at Shelburne in 1784. In 1792 around 1,200 dissatisfied Black Loyalists migrated under the leadership of Thomas Peters and the Sierra Leone Company to found the colony of Freetown in West Africa &mdash; the first organised return to Africa by people of African descent in the Atlantic world.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">A common error is to write enslaved people as objects of policy &mdash; freed by Vermont, kept in chains by Virginia &mdash; rather than as historical agents. Tens of thousands ran to British lines in response to Dunmore&rsquo;s proclamation, the largest single act of resistance to slavery in colonial America. The Black Loyalists who reached Nova Scotia, the founders of Freetown, the Quaker abolitionists who lobbied state legislatures, and the petitioners to the Massachusetts courts all show enslaved people and free Black Americans shaping their own settlement. Higher-band answers acknowledge this agency. Lower-band answers reduce slavery to a Southern institution that white politicians decided about.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Consequences for Indigenous Peoples</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Most Indigenous nations had judged that British policy &mdash; the 1763 Proclamation Line restricting white settlement &mdash; offered better protection of their lands than the patriot cause. The Iroquois Confederacy split, with the Mohawk under <dfn class="term" data-def="Mohawk leader who led pro-British Iroquois forces during the war and negotiated post-war refuge in Canada.">Joseph Brant</dfn> joining the British, while the Oneida and Tuscarora supported the Americans. Cherokee, Creek and Shawnee fought British alliances. The Peace of Paris of 1783 ignored them entirely: Britain ceded territory east of the Mississippi to the United States without consulting the nations who lived there. The new United States treated Indigenous peoples as a defeated people whose lands were forfeit. Treaties signed under duress at Fort Stanwix (1784), Fort McIntosh (1785) and Fort Finney (1786) extracted vast cessions. Pro-British Iroquois under Joseph Brant moved north of the Great Lakes into <dfn class="term" data-def="Region around the Niagara River where the British Crown granted around 950,000 acres to pro-British Mohawks and other Iroquois after the war.">the Niagara region</dfn> on land granted by the Crown &mdash; the Haldimand Tract of around 950,000 acres along the Grand River, where the Six Nations Reserve still exists today. The war that produced American independence was, for most Indigenous nations, a catastrophe.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: who was Joseph Brant, where did he lead his people after the war, and what was the Haldimand Tract?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) led pro-British Mohawks and other Iroquois after the war to the Haldimand Tract in the Niagara region &mdash; around 950,000 acres along the Grand River granted by the British Crown in 1784.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Loyalists: Persecution and Exile</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Around one in five colonists had remained loyal to the Crown, with stronger Loyalist communities in the middle colonies and the South. During the war Loyalists faced confiscation of property, tar-and-feathering, and trial by patriot Committees of Safety. After the war state legislatures passed Confiscation Acts that seized Loyalist estates. The Peace of Paris asked Congress to &lsquo;recommend&rsquo; restitution but did not require it; in practice almost no property was returned. About 60,000 to 80,000 <dfn class="term" data-def="Colonists who remained loyal to the British Crown during the war; around 60,000 to 80,000 went into exile, with most settling in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick and the Niagara region.">Loyalists</dfn> went into exile. Around 30,000 white Loyalists settled in Nova Scotia and the new Canadian colony of New Brunswick (carved out in 1784 to accommodate them). Others moved to Quebec, the Niagara peninsula in what became Upper Canada (1791), the British West Indies, or Britain itself. Black Loyalists made up around 3,000 of the Nova Scotia settlers. The British government paid around &pound;3 million in compensation through the Loyalist Claims Commission &mdash; substantial, though far less than was claimed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">Historians have not agreed on how to weigh the war&rsquo;s consequences. The traditional Whig reading, developed in the nineteenth century and revived by Bernard Bailyn, frames the Revolution as a triumph of natural rights ideology with consequences that took longer to work through but reached enslaved people and women in the long nineteenth century. Progressive historians like Charles Beard read the same documents as the work of a propertied elite securing their own economic interests. More recent &lsquo;history from below&rsquo; writing, including Gary Nash&rsquo;s &lsquo;The Unknown American Revolution&rsquo;, foregrounds working people, enslaved people and Indigenous nations &mdash; for whom independence delivered no rights at all in 1783. GCSE answers do not need to name historians, but a brief &lsquo;historians have disagreed&rsquo; framing in a judgement helps unlock the highest band on a balanced verdict.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Northern states moved towards abolition (Vermont 1777, Pennsylvania 1780, Massachusetts 1781&ndash;83) but Southern states entrenched slavery, and the 1787 Constitution&rsquo;s three-fifths clause and 1808 slave-trade clause built that division into federal law.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Most Indigenous nations had backed Britain to protect their lands; the Peace of Paris ignored them, and Joseph Brant led pro-British Mohawks to the Haldimand Tract along the Grand River in the Niagara region.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Around 60,000 to 80,000 Loyalists, including 3,000 Black Loyalists, were driven into exile, mostly to Nova Scotia and New Brunswick; around &pound;3 million was paid in compensation by the British government.</li>
</ul>
