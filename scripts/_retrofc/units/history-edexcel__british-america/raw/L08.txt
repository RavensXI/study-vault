<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Victory&rsquo;s Awkward Inheritance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the cannons of the Treaty of Paris fell silent in February 1763, Britain&rsquo;s ministers faced a strange problem: they had won too much. The empire in North America had nearly doubled, but so had the cost of governing it. The national debt stood at around &pound;130 million; interest payments alone consumed half the annual budget. Ministers concluded that the colonies, which had benefited from the war, must contribute to the cost. They also concluded that the new territories needed garrisoning to keep the peace with Indigenous nations. Both decisions &mdash; new taxes and a permanent army of 10,000 redcoats &mdash; would soon look like provocations to colonists who had seen them as friendly measures only months before.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: roughly how much was the British national debt in 1763, and what two major decisions did ministers make in response?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1763 Britain&rsquo;s national debt stood at around &pound;130 million. Ministers responded by stationing 10,000 troops in the colonies and beginning to tax colonial trade more vigorously &mdash; decisions that fed the post-war crisis.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Pontiac&rsquo;s Rebellion, 1763&ndash;64</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The first crisis came not from the colonists but from Indigenous nations. With the French gone, Indigenous peoples lost a counterweight against British settlement. The new commander, General Jeffery Amherst, made things worse by abandoning the French custom of giving annual diplomatic gifts and by allowing settlers and traders to push aggressively into former French forts. In May 1763, the Ottawa war leader <dfn class="term" data-def="Ottawa war leader of the pan-Indigenous resistance against British occupation in 1763&ndash;64.">Pontiac</dfn> began a coordinated assault on British posts across the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley. Within weeks, eight British forts had fallen and around 2,000 settlers had been killed or captured. Detroit and Fort Pitt held out under siege. The fighting included Britain&rsquo;s notorious attempt to spread smallpox among Indigenous warriors at Fort Pitt by handing over infected blankets &mdash; an act ordered by Amherst himself. The rebellion eventually exhausted itself by 1764, but it had already shaped British policy: the Proclamation Act of October 1763 banning settlement west of the Appalachians was issued partly in response.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">A common student error is to write about &lsquo;taxation without representation&rsquo; as though it were the only colonial grievance against Britain. The post-war crisis was actually a tangle. The Proclamation Act stopped settlers moving west into land they thought they had won; the standing army that enforced it was paid for, in part, by the Sugar Act tax that hit New England merchants; and the British determination to police the frontier irritated colonial assemblies who wanted to organise their own militias. Each grievance reinforced the others. The Sugar Act looked worse because settlers were already angry at the Proclamation Line; the Proclamation looked worse because new taxes were being levied to enforce it. Higher-band answers connect the two; weaker answers treat them as separate.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Paxton Boys, December 1763</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Indigenous resistance also exposed an ugly truth about colonial society. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Backcountry Pennsylvanians who massacred peaceful Conestoga Indigenous peoples in December 1763 and marched on Philadelphia in 1764.">Paxton Boys</dfn> were a group of Scots-Irish frontiersmen from Lancaster County in western Pennsylvania. Convinced that the colony&rsquo;s Quaker assembly had failed to protect them from Pontiac&rsquo;s warriors, they took matters into their own hands. In December 1763 they attacked the small, peaceful Conestoga community of around twenty people who had lived under colonial protection for generations. Six were killed in the first raid. The fourteen survivors were taken into protective custody in Lancaster jail, where the Paxton Boys broke in and murdered them too. In February 1764 several hundred armed Paxton men marched on Philadelphia to demand frontier protection and a louder voice in the assembly. Benjamin Franklin negotiated their dispersal at Germantown without further bloodshed. No one was ever prosecuted for the Conestoga killings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: who were the Paxton Boys, what did they do in December 1763, and who negotiated their dispersal in 1764?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Paxton Boys were Scots-Irish frontiersmen who in December 1763 massacred a peaceful Conestoga Indigenous community. They marched on Philadelphia in 1764 demanding frontier protection. Benjamin Franklin defused the standoff. No prosecutions followed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Sugar Act of 1764</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In April 1764 came the first of the post-war revenue measures: George Grenville&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1764 British law cutting the duty on foreign molasses to threepence per gallon but enforcing it strictly through vice-admiralty courts.">Sugar Act</dfn>. On the surface the Act looked like a concession. The duty on foreign molasses, set at sixpence per gallon under the unenforced Molasses Act of 1733, was halved to threepence. The change that mattered was enforcement. The Act tightened customs procedures, required ships to carry detailed paperwork, and moved smuggling cases out of colonial juries and into vice-admiralty courts presided over by a single Crown judge. New England merchants, used to evading the older sixpenny duty entirely, suddenly faced real prosecution at threepence. James Otis, a Boston lawyer, published a pamphlet objecting that &lsquo;taxation without representation is tyranny&rsquo;, an argument that would echo louder when the Stamp Act followed a year later. Boston town meeting condemned the Act in May 1764. The vocabulary of colonial resistance was beginning to take shape.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">A common error is to set up the post-1763 crisis as Britain oppressing innocent colonists. The Paxton Boys massacre tells a different story. Frontier colonists could be the aggressors, not just the victims of imperial policy. The peaceful Conestoga had lived under colonial protection for over a century before being murdered by their own neighbours; Indigenous peoples in Pontiac&rsquo;s Rebellion were defending lands being seized illegally by settlers crossing the Proclamation Line. Higher-band answers acknowledge that the post-war story has more than two parties: Britain, colonial settlers, and Indigenous nations all had competing interests, and the violence flowed in more than one direction. The unit&rsquo;s 2026 terminology change &mdash; using &lsquo;Indigenous peoples&rsquo; rather than older terms &mdash; is a teaching cue to keep this perspective visible.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">By the end of 1764 every element of the coming revolutionary crisis was on the table. Britain had decided to tax the colonies and to police the frontier; colonists had decided that both moves were illegitimate; and Indigenous peoples had shown that the new empire could not be governed without their cooperation. James Otis&rsquo;s pamphlet, the Boston town meeting&rsquo;s condemnation of the Sugar Act, and the willingness of frontier vigilantes to organise around grievances rather than wait for the assembly all pointed forward. The Stamp Act of 1765, which lit the next fire, did not come out of nowhere. It came out of an empire whose victors had not yet worked out how to live with their victory.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Britain emerged from the war in 1763 with a doubled empire and a doubled debt; new taxes and a standing army of 10,000 troops were the price of victory.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Pontiac&rsquo;s Rebellion (1763&ndash;64) and the Paxton Boys&rsquo; massacre of the Conestoga (December 1763) showed that the frontier was already in crisis &mdash; with violence flowing in more than one direction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Sugar Act (April 1764) tightened enforcement of trade duties, drew protests in Boston, and seeded the language of &lsquo;taxation without representation&rsquo; that the Stamp Act crisis would amplify.</li>
</ul>
