--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Tying the Colonies to Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Britain&rsquo;s North American colonies existed inside a tightly managed commercial system. From 1660 onwards, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of laws (from 1651) requiring colonial trade to flow through England in English ships and limiting which goods could be sold elsewhere.">Navigation Acts</dfn> required that the most valuable colonial goods &mdash; tobacco, sugar, indigo, rice &mdash; be shipped to England before they could be re-exported anywhere else, and that they travel in English-owned vessels. Behind these laws sat the doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="An economic theory that colonies exist to enrich the mother country by supplying raw materials and buying finished goods.">mercantilism</dfn>: the belief that colonies existed to enrich the mother country. The system gave Britain a guaranteed supply of raw materials, a captive market for its finished goods, and the customs revenue that paid for the Royal Navy. By 1713, this Atlantic economy had pulled the thirteen colonies firmly into Britain&rsquo;s commercial orbit &mdash; long before any political grievance forced them apart.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what did the Navigation Acts require, and why did Britain see them as essential?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Navigation Acts forced colonial trade through England in English ships. Tobacco, sugar and rice could only be shipped to British ports first. The system gave Britain wealth and a market &mdash; and gave colonial smugglers a reason to cheat.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Tobacco: The Chesapeake Staple</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">No commodity mattered more to the early eighteenth-century colonies than tobacco. Grown on the coastal plain of Virginia and Maryland &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Chesapeake Bay region, including the tidewater of Virginia and Maryland, where tobacco plantations dominated the economy.">Chesapeake</dfn> region &mdash; tobacco was a labour-hungry crop that exhausted the soil within a few years and required constant clearing of new ground. By the 1720s, Chesapeake planters were exporting around 30 million pounds of tobacco to Britain each year, and that figure roughly tripled by 1770. The crop reshaped colonial society. Wealthy planters built large estates run by overseers; smallholders cleared backcountry land to grow on a more modest scale; and increasing numbers of enslaved Africans were brought to the Chesapeake to do the back-breaking work of the tobacco fields. Tobacco profits paid for the silver, books, fine cloth and furniture that filled the houses of the Virginia gentry &mdash; goods that almost all came from Britain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Triangular Trade with the West Indies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The other arm of the Atlantic economy ran south to the British West Indies. Caribbean sugar islands such as Jamaica and Barbados produced sugar on a scale that dwarfed any single colonial crop, but they devoted almost all their land to cane and so depended on the mainland colonies for food and timber. New England sent salted fish, livestock and barrel staves; the Middle colonies sent flour and grain; the South sent rice and timber. In return, the West Indies sent sugar, molasses and rum. Much of the molasses went to New England distilleries, which turned it into rum that was then traded with West African slavers for captive Africans &mdash; the third leg of what historians call the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three-way Atlantic exchange of manufactured goods, enslaved Africans and colonial raw materials between Europe, Africa and the Americas.">triangular trade</dfn>. The economic ties between mainland and Caribbean were so important that colonial merchants regularly broke the Navigation Acts to buy cheaper French sugar and molasses, a practice the Sugar Act of 1764 was later designed to stop.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Golden Age of Piracy, 1715&ndash;25</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">After the Treaty of Utrecht ended the War of the Spanish Succession in 1713, thousands of demobilised sailors and privateers found themselves out of work. Many turned to <dfn class="term" data-def="Robbery or violence at sea, especially the unauthorised plundering of ships.">piracy</dfn>, preying on merchant vessels along the Atlantic seaboard and in the Caribbean. American waters became the centre of what is now called the Golden Age of piracy. Two figures stand out from the spec. &lsquo;Black Sam&rsquo; Bellamy, captain of the <em>Whydah</em>, captured more than 50 ships before his vessel was wrecked off Cape Cod in April 1717, killing him and most of his crew. Edward Teach, better known as <em>Blackbeard</em>, blockaded the port of Charleston in 1718 to demand medicine and ransom, then was killed in November of that year by a Royal Navy boarding party off the Carolina coast. Pirates targeted American waters because the colonial coastline was long, the Royal Navy was thinly stretched, and ports such as Charleston, New Providence (Nassau) and many small Carolina inlets offered shelter. Some merchants were happy to do business with pirates: stolen goods were cheap and avoided customs duties.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what did the Piracy Act 1717 do, and which governor led the suppression of piracy in American waters?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>King George I&rsquo;s Proclamation (1717) offered a pardon to pirates who surrendered. The Piracy Act (1717) made trying pirates faster by setting up vice-admiralty courts in the colonies. Governor Spotswood of Virginia organised the campaign that killed Blackbeard.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Suppressing Piracy: The Crown Strikes Back</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The British state could not let piracy flourish on the routes that carried tobacco and sugar. King George I&rsquo;s <em>Proclamation for Suppressing of Pyrates</em> (September 1717) offered a royal pardon to pirates who surrendered by a fixed date &mdash; an attempt to thin their ranks without firing a shot. The <em>Piracy Act</em> of the same year created vice-admiralty courts in the colonies so pirates could be tried locally rather than shipped back to London, dramatically speeding up convictions. Governor Alexander Spotswood of Virginia took the most direct action. Convinced that Blackbeard was harbouring a small fleet in the Carolina inlets, he commissioned a Royal Navy expedition under Lieutenant Robert Maynard. On 22 November 1718, Maynard&rsquo;s sloops fought Blackbeard at Ocracoke Inlet; Blackbeard died in the battle, his head was cut off and tied to the bowsprit, and the back of the Atlantic pirate threat was broken. By the mid-1720s the Golden Age was over: convicted pirates were being hanged in colonial ports, and the trade routes that knit the empire together were safer than they had been in a generation.</p>

The same logic returns sixty years later, when Britain tries to enforce the Stamp Act and the Tea Act on colonies whose smuggling networks were already well established.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By 1741, the Atlantic economy bound the colonies to Britain in a relationship that looked profitable on both sides. Tobacco built fortunes; West Indian trade fed New England; the Royal Navy made the sea lanes safe. But the same period also taught colonial merchants how to evade unwelcome regulation, normalised smuggling along the seaboard, and showed how dependent imperial control was on local cooperation. When taxation became the issue in the 1760s, the habits and networks built in this earlier period would prove very hard to break.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Navigation Acts and mercantilism tied the colonies to Britain economically: tobacco from the Chesapeake and triangular trade with the West Indies were the spine of the Atlantic economy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">After 1713 the Golden Age of piracy &mdash; figures such as &lsquo;Black Sam&rsquo; Bellamy and Blackbeard &mdash; threatened these trade routes by exploiting the long American coastline and weak naval coverage.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">King George I&rsquo;s Proclamation (1717), the Piracy Act (1717) and the work of Governor Spotswood &mdash; whose expedition killed Blackbeard in November 1718 &mdash; broke the back of the threat by the mid-1720s.</li>
</ul>
