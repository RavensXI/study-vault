King George’s War, the French and Indian War, Wolfe at Quebec and the 1763 Peace of Paris.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The European War on American Soil</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For most of the eighteenth century, when Britain and France went to war in Europe they fought it out in North America too. King George&rsquo;s War (1744&ndash;48) was the colonial face of the wider War of the Austrian Succession. New England militia, supported by the Royal Navy, captured the great French fortress of <dfn class="term" data-def="A French fortress on Cape Breton Island guarding the Gulf of St Lawrence; captured by New England forces in 1745.">Louisbourg</dfn> in 1745, only for London to hand it back at the peace in 1748. The decision infuriated New Englanders who had paid for the campaign with their own lives and money. The conflict left the colonial frontier unsettled, with French and British traders, settlers and Indigenous nations all jostling for control of the Ohio Valley.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which fortress did New England forces capture in 1745, and why was its return to France so resented?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>King George&rsquo;s War (1744&ndash;48) saw New England capture Louisbourg in 1745. Britain returned it to France in 1748, leaving colonists feeling their sacrifices had been wasted &mdash; an early sign that imperial and colonial interests did not always align.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Outbreak of the French and Indian War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The peace lasted less than a decade. In 1754 a young Virginia officer named George Washington led a small force into the Ohio Valley to block French expansion and clashed with French troops at the Forks of the Ohio. The skirmish escalated into the <dfn class="term" data-def="The North American war (1754&ndash;63) between Britain and France and their Indigenous allies; the wider Seven Years&rsquo; War in Europe began in 1756.">French and Indian War</dfn>, which became the global Seven Years&rsquo; War from 1756. Most Indigenous nations of the interior &mdash; including the Wabanaki, Wyandot, Lenape and Shawnee &mdash; sided with France. The reason was practical, not romantic: French traders dealt in furs and largely left Indigenous land alone, while British colonists wanted to clear the forests for farms. The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy, by contrast, mostly aligned with Britain. The early years of the war went badly for Britain. General Edward Braddock&rsquo;s expedition to the Ohio Valley was ambushed and destroyed in 1755.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">A common misconception is that Indigenous peoples were marginal to the war. In fact they were the deciding factor in many campaigns. From an Indigenous perspective the choice between Britain and France was a choice between two kinds of European. The French Empire in Canada was small, militarily reliant on Indigenous allies, and built around the fur trade &mdash; trade that needed forests intact. British settlers, by contrast, came in their thousands, cleared land for permanent farms and pushed the frontier west. Most western nations, faced with a choice between traders and settlers, chose traders. Where they fought alongside the French, as the Lenape did at the Battle of the Monongahela (1755), they did so to defend a way of life threatened far more by British settlement than by a French garrison.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Pitt&rsquo;s War and the Conquest of Canada</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The turn came when William Pitt the Elder took charge of British war strategy in 1757. Pitt poured money and troops into North America, paying colonial assemblies for their militia and shipping regulars across the Atlantic in unprecedented numbers. The Royal Navy cut off French reinforcements from Europe. Louisbourg was retaken in 1758. In 1759 came the climactic stroke: a young general, James Wolfe, led 4,500 men up sheer cliffs at night to the Plains of Abraham above Quebec. The morning battle was brief but decisive. The French commander Montcalm was mortally wounded; Wolfe died on the field; the city of Quebec surrendered. With Quebec gone, Montreal fell the following year, and French Canada was British. The Treaty of Paris in 1763 confirmed the gains: Britain took all French territory east of the Mississippi, plus French Canada, and Spanish Florida from France&rsquo;s ally Spain. Britain&rsquo;s North American empire had nearly doubled in size.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the date of Wolfe&rsquo;s victory at Quebec, the treaty that ended the war, and one piece of land Britain gained.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wolfe&rsquo;s victory at Quebec (1759) led to the conquest of Canada. The Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Britain all French territory east of the Mississippi, plus Canada and Spanish Florida. The empire was almost doubled.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Proclamation Act of 1763</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Victory came with a problem. Indigenous nations who had fought for France or who had remained neutral now faced a Britain whose settlers were already pouring westward into newly opened territory. Within months Pontiac&rsquo;s Rebellion broke out (covered in the next lesson) and British forts across the Great Lakes fell to Indigenous attack. To buy time and reduce the costs of frontier defence, the new British ministry in October 1763 issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="A royal proclamation of October 1763 forbidding colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains and reserving the land as Indigenous territory.">Proclamation Act</dfn>, drawing a line along the crest of the Appalachian Mountains and forbidding colonial settlement beyond it. The land west of the line was reserved for Indigenous nations and placed under the supervision of imperial Indian agents. To London the Proclamation looked like prudent administration; to thousands of colonial families &mdash; and to land speculators including George Washington and Benjamin Franklin &mdash; it looked like a betrayal of a war they had just helped Britain win. Settlers crossed the line anyway, and the Crown lacked the troops to stop them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">A common error is to assume that doubling the empire made Britain&rsquo;s position in America secure. The opposite is closer to the truth. Victory removed the French threat that had bound the colonies to Britain through fear; it added vast frontier responsibilities Britain could not afford to police; and it left a national debt of around &pound;130 million that London now wanted the colonies to help pay. Almost every measure that triggered colonial protest after 1763 &mdash; the Proclamation Act, the Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, the standing army of 10,000 troops &mdash; was a direct consequence of the war Britain had just won. The taxation crisis is born here, in the cost of victory, not in the malice of the king.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The French and Indian War is the pivot of the whole period. Before 1754 the colonies sheltered under a British umbrella against a French neighbour; after 1763 that neighbour was gone and the umbrella felt heavy. Britain had inherited a frontier it could not control, debts it expected the colonies to share, and Indigenous nations whose grievances it had to manage with too few troops. Every later flashpoint of the road to revolution &mdash; tax acts, customs reform, the Quebec Act, the standing army &mdash; is rooted in the strategic geography that Wolfe&rsquo;s victory at Quebec produced. Win the war, lose the empire: the irony is exact.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">King George&rsquo;s War (1744&ndash;48) ended unsatisfactorily for New England, leaving the frontier unsettled and colonial trust in imperial decisions weakened.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The French and Indian War (1754&ndash;63) involved most Indigenous nations of the interior alongside France; Wolfe&rsquo;s victory at Quebec in 1759 and the Treaty of Paris in 1763 doubled British territory in North America.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Victory bred new tensions: the Proclamation Act of 1763 angered land-hungry colonists, and the costs of the war set up the taxation crisis that followed.</li>
</ul>
