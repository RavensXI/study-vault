--- DESCRIPTION ---
Customs evasion, the fur trade and how lightly Britain really governed its colonies before 1740.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Light Touch from Westminster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the 1720s the thirteen colonies were technically governed under the <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of laws (1651 onwards) requiring colonial trade to be carried in British ships and routed through British ports.">Navigation Acts</dfn>, a tight set of trade laws designed to channel colonial wealth back to Britain. In practice, Britain barely enforced them. The chief minister Sir Robert Walpole, who dominated politics from 1721 to 1742, believed that prosperity at home depended on letting the colonies grow rich on their own terms. Later writers called this approach <dfn class="term" data-def="The early eighteenth-century British policy of loosely enforcing trade laws so colonial economies would expand without supervision.">salutary neglect</dfn> &mdash; healthy neglect &mdash; because, while the rules looked strict on paper, customs officers were few, badly paid and often happy to look the other way. Colonists got used to running their own economic affairs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what was salutary neglect, who oversaw it, and why does it matter for the later 1760s?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Salutary neglect was the early eighteenth-century British policy &mdash; associated with Walpole &mdash; of loose enforcement of the Navigation Acts. It let the colonies grow used to self-government and made later British attempts to tighten control feel intolerable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Smuggling Was Endemic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Smuggling thrived because everything about the geography and the law made it pay. The Atlantic seaboard runs for roughly 1,500 miles with hundreds of inlets, marshes and shallow rivers; a customs cutter could not patrol it all. Customs houses were concentrated at a handful of major ports such as Boston, New York, Philadelphia and Charleston, and even there enforcement was patchy. Meanwhile the goods worth smuggling carried huge profit margins. Molasses from the French and Dutch West Indies, used by New England distillers to make rum, was a third cheaper than the British Caribbean equivalent. Tea, brandy, wine and luxury cloth from continental Europe could be landed quietly along the coast and sold inland. Merchants in Boston and Newport openly built fortunes on the trade, and many of the same families dominated colonial assemblies.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">A New England merchant might bribe the local customs collector with a small share of the cargo to certify untaxed French molasses as a legal British import. False paperwork was easy to obtain, and juries of fellow colonists rarely convicted neighbours for breaching imperial trade rules. The Molasses Act of 1733 placed a sixpence-per-gallon duty on foreign molasses but was so widely evaded that the actual revenue collected in some years was less than the cost of running the customs service. Britain raised the rules but never seriously raised the enforcement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Failure of Customs Revenue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">London tried, half-heartedly, to make money from the Atlantic trade. The Treasury appointed a network of customs collectors and surveyors, but their salaries were low and their authority weak. Many of the better-paid posts were held by absentee officials in England who hired underpaid deputies in America. Bribery became routine. Colonial assemblies refused to fund extra revenue cutters or to pass laws strengthening the collectors&rsquo; hand. The result was a customs service that cost more to run than it brought in. Walpole accepted this. As long as British exports to the colonies kept growing &mdash; and they did, sharply, after 1720 &mdash; the loss of customs revenue mattered less than the gain in manufactured-goods sales.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two reasons customs revenue collection failed in the colonies before 1740.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Customs collection failed because officers were few and underpaid, juries refused to convict, and Walpole&rsquo;s government valued thriving colonial markets over enforcing Navigation Act revenue. Smuggling became a normal part of business.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Fur Trade and the Frontier</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Beyond the coast, a second contest for control played out over the <dfn class="term" data-def="The trade in beaver and other animal pelts collected by Indigenous hunters and exchanged for European goods.">fur trade</dfn>. The forests of New York, Pennsylvania and the Ohio Valley produced beaver, deer and other pelts in vast numbers, prized in Europe for hats and clothing. The trade ran through Indigenous middlemen &mdash; the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy on the New York frontier, the Lenape and Shawnee further south &mdash; who hunted, prepared and sold pelts at fortified trading posts. Britain&rsquo;s great rival here was France, whose traders pushed down from Canada and the Great Lakes offering better goods at lower prices and forming closer alliances with many Indigenous nations. London tried to regulate the trade through licensing schemes and through the Albany trading post in New York, but the licences were widely ignored and unlicensed traders met French competitors freely along contested rivers. The fur frontier was a place where British authority barely reached.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: was &lsquo;salutary neglect&rsquo; a popular term at the time?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">A common error is to write that Walpole &lsquo;announced&rsquo; a policy called salutary neglect. He never used the phrase. The term was coined later by the politician Edmund Burke, looking back in 1775, to describe the practical reality of how Britain had governed the colonies before the wars of mid-century. The point matters because the policy was an absence of action rather than a clear plan. That makes it harder for students to spot in the textbook, but more important to understand. Once Britain decided after 1763 that the colonies must be properly taxed and policed, the &lsquo;neglect&rsquo; the colonists had grown used to was withdrawn &mdash; and that change felt like an attack on liberties they thought they already had.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Before 1740 the British Empire in America looked stable. Trade flourished, settlers spread, and London left the colonies largely to themselves. Underneath, the foundations of the later crisis were already being laid. Smuggling had become so normal that two generations of colonial merchants regarded it as a right. The customs service had been allowed to wither. The frontier was contested ground where the King&rsquo;s writ ran weakly. When Britain emerged from the French and Indian War in 1763 with huge debts and a determination to make the colonies pay their share, every measure to do so &mdash; the Sugar Act, the Stamp Act, the new vice-admiralty courts &mdash; cut directly across habits the colonists had been allowed to form for half a century. The road to revolution begins not in 1765 but in the lax governance of the 1720s.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Walpole&rsquo;s policy of salutary neglect meant the Navigation Acts existed on paper but were rarely enforced, and colonial merchants ran much of the Atlantic trade themselves.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Smuggling thrived because of the long coastline, weak customs service, juries who would not convict, and the high profits of French molasses and European luxury goods.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The fur trade frontier was contested with France and Indigenous nations and largely escaped British regulation, foreshadowing the bigger struggle over western lands after 1763.</li>
</ul>
