<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Decade of Escalation, Not a Straight Line</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between 1765 and 1773 the relationship between Britain and her thirteen colonies went through a series of crises and calms. Each British measure provoked a colonial reaction; each colonial reaction provoked a tougher British response; and yet, between the storms, there were quiet years when both sides expected reconciliation. Reading this period as a smooth slide into revolution misses what made it dangerous &mdash; the way each new escalation came after a period when the trouble seemed to have passed. Examiners reward students who can show contingency: the Revolution was not yet inevitable, even in 1773.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list the four Acts that escalated the crisis between 1765 and 1774, with one short detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The pattern Act &mdash; reaction &mdash; calm &mdash; new Act runs through the period: Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Acts (1767) and Boston Massacre (1770), Tea Act and Boston Tea Party (1773), Intolerable Acts (1774). Quiet spells in 1766 and 1770&ndash;73 mean revolution was not yet inevitable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Stamp Act, 1765</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In March 1765 Parliament passed the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1765 British law requiring colonists to buy stamped paper for newspapers, legal documents, pamphlets and even playing cards.">Stamp Act</dfn>, the first internal tax ever levied directly on the American colonists. It required that newspapers, legal documents, almanacs, pamphlets and even playing cards be printed on stamped paper bought from a Crown agent. The Act shocked colonists for two reasons. First, the cost fell hardest on lawyers, printers and merchants &mdash; the very people whose voices carried in colonial assemblies. Second, the principle felt new: previous taxes had been external duties on trade, but a stamp on a will or a marriage licence reached into private life. Nine colonies sent delegates to a Stamp Act Congress in New York in October 1765, which produced a measured petition to the king. Out of doors the response was rougher.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Sons of Liberty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The street arm of resistance came from a network calling itself the <dfn class="term" data-def="A network of colonial activists formed in 1765 to organise resistance to the Stamp Act and later British policies, often through crowd action and intimidation.">Sons of Liberty</dfn>. The Boston cell, organised around the printer Benjamin Edes and the merchant Samuel Adams, set the pattern. On 14 August 1765 a Boston crowd hanged an effigy of the colony&rsquo;s stamp distributor, Andrew Oliver, and pulled down his office. Oliver resigned the next day. Similar actions followed in New York, Philadelphia and Charleston. By the end of 1765 every stamp distributor in the thirteen colonies had either resigned or fled. Boycotts of British imports cut London merchants&rsquo; sales sharply, and in March 1766 a new ministry under the Marquess of Rockingham repealed the Stamp Act &mdash; while passing a Declaratory Act that asserted Parliament&rsquo;s right to legislate for the colonies &lsquo;in all cases whatsoever&rsquo;. The repeal was celebrated in the colonies as a victory; the Declaratory Act was largely ignored. That mismatch mattered later.</p>

The Stamp Act crisis was about taxation, but it was also about jury trial (stamp offences could be tried in vice-admiralty courts without juries), the freedom of the press (printers paid the duty most often), and the right of colonial assemblies to vote their own taxes. The Sons of Liberty also drew on frontier anger over the Proclamation Act of 1763 and merchant frustration with customs enforcement under the Sugar Act of 1764. Higher-band answers show several of these grievances reinforcing each other rather than treating taxation as the whole story.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Townshend Acts and the Boston Massacre, 1767&ndash;70</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In 1767 the new Chancellor, Charles Townshend, levied duties on glass, paper, paint, lead and tea imported into the colonies. He thought he had been clever &mdash; these were external trade duties, not internal taxes. Colonists disagreed. John Dickinson&rsquo;s &lsquo;Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania&rsquo; argued that Parliament could regulate trade but could not tax for revenue. Non-importation agreements spread again, and customs disputes in Boston grew uglier as the Crown stationed two regiments of regulars in the town from October 1768. On the snowy night of 5 March 1770, a small crowd jeered and threw snowballs and ice at a sentry outside the Custom House. A relief party of eight redcoats arrived. In the confusion the soldiers fired into the crowd, killing five &mdash; including Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Indigenous heritage who became one of the first to die for the patriot cause. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The killing of five Boston civilians by British soldiers on 5 March 1770, used by patriot propagandists to symbolise British tyranny.">Boston Massacre</dfn> was a propaganda gift. Paul Revere&rsquo;s engraving showed a deliberate volley into a peaceful crowd; in fact the soldiers, defended in court by John Adams, were largely acquitted. But the print spread, and the dead became martyrs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the date of the Boston Massacre, how many died, and one named victim.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 5 March 1770 British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd outside the Custom House, killing five &mdash; including Crispus Attucks. Paul Revere&rsquo;s engraving made it a patriot rallying image, even though John Adams&rsquo;s defence won acquittals for most of the soldiers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Calm of 1770&ndash;73</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">On the very day of the Massacre, the new prime minister Lord North was already preparing to repeal most of the Townshend duties. Only the small duty on tea was kept &mdash; to preserve the principle. The result was a strange three-year quiet. Boycotts collapsed, imports rose, and many colonial merchants concluded that the worst was over. Sam Adams&rsquo;s Committees of Correspondence, set up in Massachusetts in November 1772 to share political news between towns, kept the embers warm, but most colonists in 1772 still expected reconciliation. The next escalation came not from a tax increase but from a tax cut.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Tea Act and the Boston Tea Party, 1773</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In May 1773 Parliament passed the Tea Act to rescue the East India Company from bankruptcy. The Act let the Company ship tea direct to America with the duty kept low, undercutting both legal colonial importers and smugglers from Holland. Colonists read it as a bribe: cheap tea now in exchange for accepting Parliament&rsquo;s right to tax. In Charleston tea was landed and locked in a warehouse; in Philadelphia and New York the ships were turned back; in Boston Governor Thomas Hutchinson refused to let the ships leave without paying duty. On the night of 16 December 1773 around 100 men dressed loosely as Mohawks &mdash; an act of patriot disguise that exploited a romanticised image of Indigenous resistance &mdash; boarded three Company ships at Griffin&rsquo;s Wharf and dumped 342 chests of tea into the harbour. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The destruction of 342 chests of East India Company tea in Boston harbour on 16 December 1773 by colonists protesting the Tea Act.">Boston Tea Party</dfn> destroyed around &pound;9,000 of property, the equivalent of more than &pound;1 million today.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Intolerable Acts, 1774</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">London this time refused to back down. In spring 1774 Parliament passed four laws colonists soon called the <dfn class="term" data-def="Colonial nickname for the four Coercive Acts of 1774, which closed Boston harbour and rewrote Massachusetts government in response to the Boston Tea Party.">Intolerable Acts</dfn> &mdash; officially the Coercive Acts. The Boston Port Act closed the harbour until the tea was paid for. The Massachusetts Government Act stripped the colony&rsquo;s charter and made the upper house and judges Crown appointments. The Administration of Justice Act allowed officials accused of capital crimes in Massachusetts to be tried in Britain. A new Quartering Act let the army billet troops on private property. To these four Coercive Acts colonists added the Quebec Act of June 1774 (which extended Quebec southwards into Ohio land claimed by Virginia and Connecticut) as a fifth grievance. The closure of Boston harbour, in a town whose population was 16,000, was meant to isolate Massachusetts from the other colonies. It did the opposite. Food, money and political support flowed in from across America. The First Continental Congress was already being planned.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">For an 8-mark analytical narrative the temptation is to chronicle: first the Stamp Act, then the Townshend Acts, then the Tea Act. Examiners cap that response in the middle bands. The skill being tested is causation: showing why each event fed the next. The repeal of the Stamp Act in 1766 mattered because it taught colonists that boycotts and crowd action worked &mdash; so when the Townshend duties followed in 1767, the same toolkit was reached for. The Boston Massacre mattered because it gave the patriot movement martyrs and an iconic image during the calm that followed Lord North&rsquo;s partial repeal. The Tea Act mattered because the calm of 1770&ndash;73 had convinced London the storm had passed; the destruction of the tea showed otherwise. The Intolerable Acts mattered because Britain answered an act of property destruction by suspending a colony&rsquo;s charter, which read in twelve other colonies as a warning that any of them could be next. That is why a First Continental Congress could meet in Philadelphia in September 1774.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Stamp Act of 1765 created the toolkit of resistance &mdash; non-importation, the Sons of Liberty, the Stamp Act Congress &mdash; and its 1766 repeal taught colonists that organised pressure could force London to retreat.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Townshend Acts and the Boston Massacre of 5 March 1770 deepened the conflict, but Lord North&rsquo;s partial repeal kept only the tea duty, producing a three-year calm in which most colonists still expected reconciliation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Boston Tea Party of December 1773 and London&rsquo;s response &mdash; the Intolerable Acts of 1774 closing Boston harbour and rewriting the Massachusetts charter &mdash; transformed a local quarrel into an inter-colonial crisis that produced the First Continental Congress.</li>
</ul>

Avoid the trap of treating the Revolution as inevitable: examiners reward answers that show calm spells in 1766 and 1770&ndash;73 when most colonists still wanted reconciliation.</p>
