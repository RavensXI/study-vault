What makes an act wrong; justice, retribution, deterrence and reformation across faiths.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Makes an Act Wrong?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every legal system in the world rests on a shared moral assumption: some acts are wrong and deserve punishment. But what exactly makes an act wrong? Religious and philosophical traditions give different answers. Some traditions hold that morality is <dfn class="term" data-def="A moral view that holds that right and wrong are fixed and do not change depending on circumstances.">absolute</dfn> &mdash; certain acts are always wrong regardless of context. Others argue for <dfn class="term" data-def="A moral view that holds that right and wrong depend on the situation, culture or consequences.">relative morality</dfn>, where context determines whether an action is acceptable. Many traditions also appeal to <dfn class="term" data-def="An inner sense of right and wrong that guides a person&rsquo;s moral decisions.">conscience</dfn> &mdash; the internal moral voice &mdash; as the key guide to ethical behaviour.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four aims of punishment. Write one sentence explaining each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main aims of punishment are: <strong>justice</strong> (giving offenders what they deserve), <strong>retribution</strong> (paying back for wrong done), <strong>deterrence</strong> (discouraging future offending) and <strong>reformation</strong> (changing the offender&rsquo;s character so they do not reoffend).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Christianity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Christian ethics sees sin as a fundamental category. An act is wrong if it breaks God&rsquo;s commands, damages the human community, or rejects love. The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5) pushes ethics beyond outward behaviour to inward intention &mdash; Jesus taught that anger and contempt can themselves be forms of moral failure. On punishment, Christians hold diverse views. Many emphasise reformation: the goal of punishment should be to restore the offender, not merely to satisfy public anger. Prison chaplains across Britain embody this &mdash; they visit prisoners, offer pastoral care, and support rehabilitation programmes. Some Christians draw on the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="Selfless, unconditional love for others, as taught in the New Testament.">agape</dfn> to argue that even offenders deserve compassion and the possibility of change.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">On the death penalty, Christian responses are divided. Exodus 20:13 commands &ldquo;You shall not murder,&rdquo; and many Christians interpret this as ruling out state execution. Matthew 5:38&ndash;39 reports Jesus challenging the principle of &ldquo;an eye for an eye,&rdquo; suggesting that retributive violence is not the final answer. Catholic teaching, expressed in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC) 2266&ndash;2267, acknowledges the state&rsquo;s right to defend society but holds that the death penalty is &ldquo;inadmissible&rdquo; because it attacks human dignity. The Catholic Bishops of England and Wales have consistently opposed capital punishment on these grounds.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Judaism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Jewish ethics grounds its understanding of crime in the Torah and rabbinic tradition. The Mishnah (Sanhedrin chapters 1 and 4) outlines the procedure for capital trials, requiring a majority of the Sanhedrin and two independent witnesses &mdash; standards so demanding that scholars such as <dfn class="term" data-def="A twelfth-century Jewish philosopher and legal scholar whose works systematised Jewish law and ethics.">Maimonides</dfn> observed that a court executing even one person in seventy years was considered severe. This caution reflects the principle that taking a human life is an irreversible act. The Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 states that saving one life is as if saving a whole world &mdash; a principle that shapes Jewish attitudes to both crime and punishment. Reform and Progressive Judaism today largely oppose the death penalty. Orthodox opinion is more varied but shares the Talmudic caution about irreversible punishment.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Islam teaches that justice (<em>&lsquo;adl</em>) is one of God&rsquo;s core attributes and that human societies must reflect divine justice. The Qur&rsquo;an (5:32) states that to save one life is as if saving all of humanity &mdash; a verse that underlines the seriousness of taking life. On punishment, the Qur&rsquo;an (16:90) commands justice, kindness and giving to relatives. Shariah law includes hudud (fixed penalties for certain crimes) and ta&rsquo;zir (discretionary penalties), but Islamic jurisprudence also requires very high standards of evidence before severe penalties can be applied. Many Muslim scholars argue that the conditions for hudud penalties can rarely be met in practice. On the death penalty, some Muslim scholars accept it for the most serious offences under precisely defined conditions; others argue the Qur&rsquo;an&rsquo;s emphasis on mercy should prevail.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Hinduism understands wrong action through the concepts of <dfn class="term" data-def="Wrong or sinful action in Hindu thought, which generates negative karma and hinders spiritual progress.">paapa</dfn> (sin) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle of righteous punishment or discipline in Hindu thought, seen as essential for maintaining social order.">danda</dfn> (punishment). The Dharmashastra texts justify punishment as necessary to maintain <em>dharma</em> &mdash; righteous order in society. The king had a duty to punish criminals so that the community remained in moral balance. <em>Ahimsa</em> (non-violence), however, complicates the question of capital punishment. Many Hindus argue that taking a life &mdash; even the life of a criminal &mdash; creates negative karma and is therefore wrong. The principle of karma means that wrongdoers will ultimately face the consequences of their actions across lifetimes, so the law of karma itself operates as a cosmic form of justice. On reformation, Hindu traditions often emphasise the possibility of spiritual renewal: a criminal who sincerely seeks to change can reduce the negative karma accumulated through their crimes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sikhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Sikhism teaches that every human being carries the divine light of God (the Waheguru) within them. This belief directly informs Sikh attitudes to criminals: even an offender retains their God-given dignity. The Guru Granth Sahib (1241) teaches about God&rsquo;s justice and mercy operating together. Sikhism values both justice and rehabilitation. On the death penalty, many Sikhs are influenced by <em>ahimsa</em> and the principle that life is sacred, though there is no single official Sikh position. The Sikh Rehat Maryada (code of conduct) does not explicitly address capital punishment, leaving room for diverse views within the community.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Buddhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Buddhism views wrong action through the lens of &ldquo;unskilful&rdquo; or &ldquo;unskilled&rdquo; behaviour &mdash; actions that arise from greed, hatred or ignorance and create suffering. The story of Milarepa, a Tibetan figure who committed terrible crimes before becoming one of Tibet&rsquo;s most revered spiritual teachers, illustrates the Buddhist conviction that transformation is always possible. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A British Buddhist charity that works with offenders in prisons, promoting rehabilitation through Buddhist principles.">Angulimala Society</dfn> (named after a notorious killer in the Pali scriptures who became a monk after meeting the Buddha) works in British prisons offering meditation and support to offenders. Buddhism strongly opposes the death penalty on the grounds of <em>ahimsa</em> (non-harm), <em>karuna</em> (compassion) and the Five Precepts, the first of which prohibits taking life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words: why do most Buddhist traditions oppose the death penalty? Give two reasons.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Angulimala Society is a Buddhist prison chaplaincy charity working across Britain. Named after the reformed murderer of the Pali scriptures, it supports offenders through meditation and compassionate care &mdash; embodying the Buddhist belief in rehabilitation over retribution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Non-Religious Perspectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Humanists and secular thinkers approach crime and punishment empirically &mdash; asking what works rather than what religious tradition prescribes. Most Humanists oppose capital punishment on the grounds that execution is irreversible, that miscarriages of justice cannot be corrected, and that the evidence for deterrence is weak. Humanists typically favour rehabilitation: if the purpose of punishment is to reduce harm, then changing the offender&rsquo;s behaviour is more effective than purely retributive measures. Secular criminologists draw on the same empirical evidence base when arguing for prison reform.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">A strong evaluation answer will weigh these aims against each other. Retribution satisfies a desire for justice but does not necessarily reduce reoffending. Deterrence assumes rational calculation by potential offenders, which may not always apply. Reformation is difficult to measure but has strong support from both religious traditions (which emphasise the person&rsquo;s capacity for change) and secular psychology. Justice is valued across traditions but defined differently: for some it means proportionate punishment; for others it means restoring the victim and community. Examiners reward answers that consider these tensions rather than simply listing the four aims.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The four aims of punishment are justice, retribution, deterrence and reformation &mdash; religious traditions differ in which they prioritise, with many emphasising reformation and the possibility of change.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Most major faith traditions oppose capital punishment, citing human dignity, mercy and the irreversibility of execution &mdash; though responses vary between and within traditions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Prison chaplains and charities such as the Angulimala Society (Buddhist) demonstrate how faith communities put their beliefs about rehabilitation into practice in the real world.</li>
</ul>
