--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Is There Evil and Suffering?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The existence of evil and suffering is one of the oldest and most difficult questions in philosophy and religion. If God is all-powerful (<em>omnipotent</em>), all-knowing (<em>omniscient</em>) and perfectly good (<em>omnibenevolent</em>), why does evil exist at all? This challenge is known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The philosophical problem of how a good, all-powerful God can allow evil and suffering to exist.">problem of evil</dfn>. Religious traditions have developed a range of responses &mdash; collectively called <dfn class="term" data-def="A philosophical or theological attempt to justify belief in God in the face of evil and suffering.">theodicy</dfn> &mdash; to explain why suffering exists and how it fits within a world created by God.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and from memory: what are the two main Christian theodicies and who is associated with each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The two major Christian theodicies are: (1) the <strong>Augustinian (free-will) defence</strong> &mdash; evil entered the world through human misuse of free will (the Fall); (2) the <strong>Irenaean / soul-making theodicy</strong> (associated with Irenaeus and developed by philosopher John Hick) &mdash; suffering is the environment in which humans grow into the moral image of God.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Christianity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Christian theology anchors its account of evil in Genesis 3, the story of the Fall. <dfn class="term" data-def="The theological doctrine that through Adam and Eve&rsquo;s disobedience in Genesis 3, all humanity inherited a tendency to sin and became separated from God.">Original Sin</dfn> entered the world when Adam and Eve disobeyed God by eating from the forbidden tree. Augustine (354&ndash;430 CE) argued in his <em>Enchiridion</em> that evil is not a substance in itself but a <em>privation</em> &mdash; an absence of good. God created everything good (Romans 5:12 notes that sin entered through one person), but humanity freely chose to turn away. The resulting corruption in human nature is Original Sin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A second response comes from the tradition of Irenaeus, developed into a full theodicy by philosopher John Hick in the twentieth century. Hick argued that humans are not created perfect but are created immature &mdash; in the &ldquo;image&rdquo; of God but not yet in the full &ldquo;likeness&rdquo; of God. Suffering and moral challenge are the conditions needed for human beings to grow spiritually. Without the possibility of pain, courage would be meaningless; without the risk of moral failure, virtue would be impossible. The Catholic Catechism (385&ndash;412) acknowledges both the reality of Original Sin and God&rsquo;s ultimate power to bring good out of evil.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Judaism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Jewish responses to evil and suffering are grounded in the Hebrew scriptures and rabbinic tradition. Deuteronomy 30:15&ndash;19 presents God setting before Israel a choice between life and death, good and evil &mdash; suggesting that human moral freedom is real and its exercise has consequences. The book of Job is a sustained exploration of innocent suffering: Job suffers despite his righteousness, and his friends&rsquo; attempts to explain suffering as punishment for sin are ultimately rejected by God. The text resists simple answers. The Talmud (Berakhot 9:5) contains a passage in which the rabbis discuss the obligations to bless God for both good and evil &mdash; a radical acceptance that God&rsquo;s purposes are not always transparent.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">For modern Jewish thinkers, the Holocaust (Shoah) represents the most searing confrontation with the problem of evil. Eva Clarke, who survived the Holocaust as a child, came to settle in Britain and has spoken publicly about memory and survival. Many Jewish thinkers after the Shoah have argued that traditional theodicy is inadequate in the face of such catastrophe, and that maintaining faith in a God of justice requires holding the question open rather than offering neat answers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Islam teaches that suffering and trials are part of God&rsquo;s test for humanity. The Qur&rsquo;an (2:155&ndash;156) explicitly frames hardship as a test: &ldquo;We will certainly test you with some fear and hunger... give good news to those who patiently endure, who say, when an affliction strikes them: &lsquo;Truly we belong to God and truly to Him we shall return.&rsquo;&rdquo; The existence of evil is explained partly through the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="In Islamic theology, the divine decree or predestination by which God has foreknowledge of all events.">al-Qadr</dfn> (divine predestination) &mdash; God has foreknowledge of all events, including suffering &mdash; and partly through the role of Iblis (the Devil), who tests humanity as described in Qur&rsquo;an 2:34. Suffering accepted with patient endurance (<em>sabr</em>) can become spiritually meritorious.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Hinduism offers a rich philosophical account of evil and suffering. <dfn class="term" data-def="In Hindu philosophy, the cosmic illusion that makes the material world appear as the ultimate reality, obscuring the soul&rsquo;s true nature.">Maya</dfn> (illusion) is one key concept: the world of appearances is not ultimate reality, and attachment to it &mdash; mistaking the temporary for the eternal &mdash; is the root of suffering. <dfn class="term" data-def="In Hindu thought, the law of moral cause and effect by which actions in this and previous lives determine future experiences.">Karma</dfn> explains why individuals suffer differently: one&rsquo;s present circumstances reflect the accumulated moral weight of past actions across many lifetimes. The Bhagavad Gita (14.17) and Bhagavata Purana (6:1.45) discuss the gunas &mdash; three fundamental qualities (goodness, passion and ignorance) &mdash; and how dominance of the lower gunas leads to wrong action and suffering. The Brahma Sutras (2.1.34&ndash;36) address the problem of evil philosophically, arguing that God (Brahman) is not the author of evil &mdash; individual souls bear responsibility for their own karma.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Sikhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Sikhism explains evil through the concept of the five evils that cloud the human soul: <dfn class="term" data-def="The five spiritual obstacles in Sikh teaching that separate the soul from God: lust, greed, attachment, anger and ego.">kam, lobh, moh, krodh and ahankar</dfn> (lust, greed, attachment, anger and ego). The Guru Granth Sahib (1342) addresses how these five evils trap the <em>manmukh</em> &mdash; the person who is self-centred rather than God-centred &mdash; in suffering. Karma also features in Sikh thought: suffering may be the result of past actions, but God&rsquo;s grace (<em>nadar</em>) can override karma and liberate the soul. The goal of Sikh life is to overcome the five evils through meditation on God&rsquo;s name (<em>Nam simran</em>) and move towards <em>mukti</em> (liberation).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Buddhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Buddhism&rsquo;s analysis of evil and suffering begins with the First Noble Truth: <dfn class="term" data-def="A Pali term meaning suffering, unsatisfactoriness or the inherent dissatisfaction in unenlightened life, identified as the First Noble Truth in Buddhism.">dukkha</dfn> (suffering) is an unavoidable feature of unenlightened existence. The origin of dukkha, explained in the Second Noble Truth, is <em>tanha</em> (craving or thirst). More specifically, Buddhism identifies the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three mental states that poison the mind and lead to suffering in Buddhist thought: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and delusion (moha).">Three Poisons</dfn> &mdash; <em>lobha</em> (greed), <em>dosa</em> (hatred) and <em>moha</em> (delusion or ignorance) &mdash; as the root causes of unskilful action and the suffering it creates. The figure of <em>Mara</em> (sometimes translated as the Evil One) represents temptation and the forces that pull the mind away from enlightenment. Dhammapada 12:165 teaches that by oneself evil is done, by oneself one is defiled; the path to freedom is internal transformation, not external rescue. Note: this lesson uses Pali terms as the spec specifies Pali primary.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the Three Poisons in Pali and their English meanings. Then explain how each leads to suffering.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Buddhist Three Poisons are: <strong>lobha</strong> (greed), <strong>dosa</strong> (hatred) and <strong>moha</strong> (delusion/ignorance). Together they are the root causes of all unskilful action and the suffering it generates &mdash; addressing them is the core purpose of Buddhist practice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Non-Religious Perspectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Humanists and atheists, including thinkers such as Richard Dawkins, argue that the existence of evil and suffering is itself evidence against the existence of a benevolent, all-powerful God. For Humanists, suffering has natural causes &mdash; disease, accident, human cruelty &mdash; and the appropriate response is compassion and practical action to reduce it, not theological explanation. Dawkins has argued that the apparent design of nature, including its capacity for suffering, is better explained by evolution than by a purposeful creator. This secular perspective sees the problem of evil as a genuine challenge to religious belief rather than a puzzle to be solved by theodicy.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">A strong evaluation will weigh the strengths and weaknesses of each theodicy. The free-will defence answers why moral evil exists but struggles to account for natural evil (earthquakes, disease) which is not caused by human choice. The soul-making theodicy explains why suffering might be necessary for moral growth but seems to require extreme suffering to be justified by the spiritual gains &mdash; a claim many find morally troubling. Buddhist and Hindu explanations are internally coherent but presuppose beliefs in karma and rebirth that not all people share. Humanist arguments show that the problem of evil remains a genuine intellectual challenge. Examiners look for this kind of critical engagement rather than a simple list of different views.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The two main Christian theodicies are the Augustinian free-will defence (evil entered through human choice at the Fall) and the Irenaean soul-making theodicy (suffering enables moral and spiritual growth).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Buddhist teaching identifies the Three Poisons &mdash; lobha (greed), dosa (hatred) and moha (delusion) &mdash; as the root of all suffering; Hindu thought emphasises maya (illusion) and karma as explanations for individual suffering.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The problem of evil remains a genuine philosophical challenge: Humanists and atheists such as Richard Dawkins argue that suffering is evidence against belief in a good, all-powerful God, making theodicy an essential topic for evaluation questions.</li>
</ul>
