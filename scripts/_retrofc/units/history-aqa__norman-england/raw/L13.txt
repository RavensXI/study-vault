<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why This Battlefield Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Battle of Hastings is your specified site for the 2027 exam. That means Paper 2 will ask you a 16-mark question that names the battlefield, and the examiner wants something specific: not just the story of the battle, but how the <em>place</em> &mdash; the ridge, the slopes, the marshy ground &mdash; shaped what happened on it. This lesson gives you the battle through the lens of its environment. The narrative background is in Lesson 2; here we walk the ground.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why the Armies Met Seven Miles from Hastings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The battle of 14 October 1066 was not fought at Hastings at all, but on a ridge about seven miles to the north-west, where the town of Battle now stands. Geography put both commanders there. William had landed at Pevensey and moved to Hastings, a port on a narrow peninsula of land &mdash; easy to defend, easy to supply by sea, but also easy to bottle up. He could not sit there through the winter, so he burned and plundered the surrounding villages to force a quick fight. Harold, marching south at speed after his victory at Stamford Bridge, took the bait &mdash; this was his own family heartland being harried. He halted his army across the London road, on a ridge blocking the only good route out of the Hastings peninsula.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where exactly was the battle fought, and why there?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The battlefield sits on a ridge seven miles north-west of Hastings, across the London road &mdash; the route William had to take to break out of his peninsula. The town of Battle grew up on the site afterwards. Some chroniclers called the ridge Senlac Hill.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the Ground: Ridge, Slope and Marsh</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Harold&rsquo;s position was chosen with a soldier&rsquo;s eye. His army formed up along the crest of the ridge, roughly seven hundred metres wide &mdash; about the frontage his men could fill standing shoulder to shoulder. In front of them the ground fell away, steeply in places, towards the low ground where William&rsquo;s army would have to form up beneath them. On either flank the slopes dropped into small stream valleys with soft, marshy ground, which protected the English from being outflanked. Behind the ridge lay the forest of the Weald, cover for a retreat if one was needed. An attacker had one realistic option: come straight up the hill.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">That suited Harold exactly, because his army had no cavalry and few archers. English warfare was infantry warfare: his elite <dfn class="term" data-def="A professional household warrior of the Anglo-Saxon kings and earls, typically armed with a two-handed battleaxe.">housecarls</dfn> and the part-time soldiers of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon militia of ordinary freemen, called up to fight for a limited period.">fyrd</dfn> fought on foot in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A defensive infantry formation in which men stand in close ranks, shields overlapping, presenting an unbroken wall to the enemy.">shield wall</dfn>. A shield wall on a hilltop with protected flanks was the strongest thing an Anglo-Saxon army could build. As long as it held its ground, it was very hard to break.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Armies on the Field</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Historians estimate perhaps seven thousand men on each side. Harold&rsquo;s were all infantry &mdash; housecarls in the front ranks with their two-handed axes, fyrdmen packed behind. William&rsquo;s army was a combined-arms force in three divisions: Bretons on his left, Normans in his centre, French and Flemish troops on his right. Each division layered archers in front, armoured infantry behind them, and mounted knights at the rear. That mix &mdash; missiles, foot and cavalry working together &mdash; was the Norman way of war, and the whole battle turned on whether the ground would let it work.</p>

Provoking a battle quickly was not aggression for its own sake &mdash; it was the only plan that worked. <h2 data-narration-id="n11">How the Environment Shaped the Fighting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The battle began in the morning and lasted until dusk &mdash; extraordinary for a medieval battle, which was usually decided in an hour or two. The length of the fight is itself evidence of what the ground did. Norman arrows, shot uphill, thudded into shields or sailed over the ridge. Norman knights charging uphill arrived at the shield wall slow and winded, and the wall held. Attack after attack rolled up the slope and broke.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The environment also produced the battle&rsquo;s great crisis. On the Norman left, the Bretons &mdash; fighting on the lowest, softest ground near the marshy stream &mdash; buckled and fled, and a rumour ran through the army that William was dead. Part of the English right charged down off the ridge in pursuit. William pushed back his helmet to show his face and rallied his knights, who wheeled round and cut down the pursuers on the low ground, away from the wall&rsquo;s protection. Chroniclers say the Normans then used <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate pretend flight, staged to tempt defending troops into leaving a strong position so cavalry can turn and destroy them in the open.">feigned retreats</dfn> to repeat the trick on purpose. Every man tempted downhill was a brick pulled out of the wall.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does the unusual length of the battle tell you about the site?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The battle lasted from morning until dusk &mdash; far longer than most medieval battles. The ridge is the reason: as long as the shield wall stayed on the crest with its flanks covered by marsh, Norman cavalry, archers and infantry could not combine effectively against it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The End: The Ridge Breaks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By late afternoon the arithmetic of attrition was telling. The wall was thinner; tired fyrdmen stood where housecarls had fallen; Harold&rsquo;s brothers Gyrth and Leofwine were already dead. William&rsquo;s archers kept shooting, his knights kept probing, and at last the line gave. Harold fell &mdash; killed, in the famous tradition shown in the Bayeux Tapestry, by an arrow in the eye, though historians still debate exactly how he died. With the king dead the army broke into the Weald behind the ridge. Even the pursuit carried the stamp of the ground: chroniclers describe Norman horsemen in the dark tumbling into a steep hidden ditch &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The name later chroniclers gave to a steep, concealed ditch behind the English position, into which pursuing Norman cavalry fell after the battle.">Malfosse</dfn> &mdash; where fleeing Englishmen turned and killed them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Site Afterwards: Battle Abbey</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The battlefield&rsquo;s history did not end at dusk. The Pope had backed William&rsquo;s invasion, but the Church still demanded penance for the bloodshed, and William&rsquo;s answer was built in stone: Battle Abbey, founded on the field itself, with the high altar placed &mdash; tradition says on William&rsquo;s own orders &mdash; on the spot where Harold fell. The abbey terraced the ridge and a town grew at its gates, so today&rsquo;s visitor sees a landscape partly reshaped by the monument to the battle. The site is in the care of English Heritage, and the surviving ridge, slopes and low ground are exactly the features your exam question is about.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Using the Site in the 16-Mark Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The question will give you a statement and ask how far a study of the battlefield supports it. The skill is pairing a physical feature with a consequence: the crest explains why the shield wall held all day; the marshy flanks explain why William had to attack frontally; the slope explains why cavalry and arrows underperformed; the low ground explains why leaving the wall was fatal; and the site&rsquo;s position astride the London road explains why the battle happened here at all. Site feature, then consequence, then link to the statement &mdash; that chain is what earns the marks.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The battle was fought seven miles from Hastings because geography demanded it: William had to break out of a peninsula along the London road, and Harold blocked it from a ridge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The site favoured the defender: a crest for the shield wall, marshy streams guarding both flanks, and slopes that blunted cavalry and archery &mdash; which is why the battle lasted an exceptional full day.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The environment also destroyed the English: the low ground killed every man tempted off the ridge, first in the real Breton crisis, then in the feigned retreats.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Harold&rsquo;s death at dusk broke the wall; William&rsquo;s penance built Battle Abbey on the field, with the altar on the traditional spot where Harold fell, preserving the site you are examined on.</li>
</ul>
