--- DESCRIPTION ---
Land for service, lord and vassal, knights’ fees: how William rebuilt English society.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Working Pyramid, Not a Blueprint</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Students often imagine William sitting down with a chart and inventing a &lsquo;feudal system&rsquo;. The reality is messier. The pattern of <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern of holding land in return for service &mdash; especially military service &mdash; that linked king, lords and peasants in Norman England. Modern historians often call it a model rather than a tidy system.">feudalism</dfn> was a working set of landholding-for-service relationships that had developed in northern France over the previous century, and William transplanted it to England after 1066. He did not build it from scratch; he intensified what Norman lords already knew. The historian Susan Reynolds (1994) has even argued that calling feudalism a &lsquo;system&rsquo; flatters its tidiness &mdash; what existed was a set of overlapping working relationships. AQA still teaches the model because students need a framework, but examiner reports reward those who can describe the real military and land obligations rather than chant a textbook hierarchy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">King at the Top</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">After 1066 William claimed all the land of England as his by right of conquest. He kept some &mdash; the royal demesne &mdash; for himself, including key forests, castles and towns, and granted the rest out to followers in return for service. The principle that all land was ultimately the king&rsquo;s was a sharper Norman idea than anything in pre-1066 England, where great Anglo-Saxon earls had held land in their own right. By 1086, every estate in England traced back to the king through a chain of holders.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the four levels of the Norman feudal pyramid in the right order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Norman pyramid had four levels: the king at the top, around 180 tenants-in-chief who held land directly from him, sub-tenants (often knights) who held from those, and at the bottom the peasants &mdash; mostly Anglo-Saxon &mdash; who worked the land. Land was held in return for specified service.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Tenants-in-Chief</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Beneath the king sat the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Norman lords (around 180 by 1086) who held land directly from the king, in return for military service and counsel.">tenants-in-chief</dfn>: the great Norman lords who held estates directly from William and owed him counsel and military service. Domesday Book records around 180 of them. The biggest holdings went to William&rsquo;s closest family and supporters &mdash; his half-brothers Odo of Bayeux and Robert of Mortain, his loyal commander William FitzOsbern, and a handful of others. Robert of Mortain alone held nearly 800 manors across thirteen counties; Odo of Bayeux was lord of vast estates centred on Kent. A relatively small group of men therefore held a remarkably large share of the kingdom &mdash; a deliberate concentration of military power in trusted hands.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Sub-Tenants and Knights&rsquo; Fees</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Tenants-in-chief in turn granted out portions of their estates to followers in a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="The granting of land by a tenant-in-chief to a sub-tenant in return for service, creating a chain of landholding beneath the king.">sub-infeudation</dfn>. The unit of grant was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of land considered enough to support one knight in armour and his household, who in return owed military service to his lord.">knight&rsquo;s fee</dfn> &mdash; an estate large enough to support a fully equipped knight and his household. In return the sub-tenant owed a fixed amount of military service, typically forty days a year. A great honour like Robert of Mortain&rsquo;s might be sub-divided into dozens or hundreds of knights&rsquo; fees, which is how England&rsquo;s small Norman ruling class managed to put thousands of armed knights into the field on demand.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Land was the currency of loyalty. A king who could grant a knight&rsquo;s fee, a manor or a great honour created a personal bond &mdash; the holder owed loyalty for what he held. The flow of <dfn class="term" data-def="The system by which a lord rewarded his followers with land, office or favour in return for loyalty and service.">patronage</dfn> ran down the pyramid: the king rewarded tenants-in-chief, who rewarded their own knights. When patronage broke down &mdash; as in the 1075 Earls&rsquo; Revolt or the 1088 rising against William II &mdash; the system shook because the chain of obligation snapped at one of its links.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Peasants at the Base</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The vast majority of people in England were peasants who worked the land. Domesday lists several categories: villeins (owing labour service to their lord), bordars and cottars (smaller holdings), freemen, and slaves &mdash; perhaps 10% of the population in 1086, although slavery was already declining. For most peasants, the conquest changed the language and name of the lord at the top of their manor, but daily labour patterns continued. They still ploughed the same strips, paid the same kinds of dues, and lived in the same villages. The novelty was the Norman lord on the hill.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Anglo-Saxon Aristocracy Replaced</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For the Anglo-Saxon landholding class &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="Anglo-Saxon nobles below earls who held substantial estates and provided the local leadership of pre-conquest England.">thegns</dfn> &mdash; the conquest was catastrophic. Domesday Book shows that by 1086 fewer than 10% of pre-conquest landholders still held land. The rest had been killed in the campaigns of 1066&ndash;71, exiled, or reduced to tenants of Norman incomers. The thegnage as a class effectively vanished within a generation. Where they survived, surviving thegns held smaller estates as sub-tenants of Norman lords &mdash; a sharp social demotion.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Robert of Mortain was William&rsquo;s half-brother. Domesday records him holding nearly 800 manors across thirteen counties, including coastal estates in Sussex and vast lands in Cornwall. He sub-infeudated dozens of his manors to his own knights, who held by knight-service. The pattern is typical of the new tenants-in-chief: huge holdings, multiple shires, a private network of sub-tenants who answered to Robert before they answered to the king. Such concentrations of land made conquest stick, but they also created the political muscle behind later rebellions, including the 1088 rising in which Robert of Mortain himself took part.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A Coalition, Not One Group</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Another misconception students fall into is treating &lsquo;the Normans&rsquo; as one homogeneous people. William&rsquo;s invasion army was a coalition: Normans, Bretons, Flemings and French. His post-conquest aristocracy reflected that mix &mdash; Bretons such as Alan Rufus held the honour of Richmond; Flemings settled in the south; tenants-in-chief came from across northern France and the Low Countries. Calling everyone &lsquo;Norman&rsquo; obscures the patronage politics that William had to manage at the top of the pyramid.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the Domesday statistic about Anglo-Saxon thegns&rsquo; survival as landholders by 1086.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1086, fewer than 10% of pre-conquest English landholders still held land. The Anglo-Saxon thegnage was effectively replaced as a class within twenty years &mdash; either dispossessed, killed, exiled or reduced to sub-tenants of Norman lords.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Norman pyramid linked king, around 180 tenants-in-chief, sub-tenants holding by knights&rsquo; fees, and the peasant base &mdash; with land held in return for specified service.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Sub-infeudation and patronage allowed a small Norman elite to put thousands of knights into the field while concentrating land in a few trusted families like Robert of Mortain&rsquo;s.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">By 1086 fewer than 10% of pre-conquest English landholders still held land &mdash; the Anglo-Saxon thegnage was effectively replaced as a class within a generation.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">For an &lsquo;Explain Significance&rsquo; question on feudalism, do not simply describe the pyramid. Choose two consequences and weigh them &mdash; for example, (1) how the small group of tenants-in-chief gave William the military reach to crush revolt and garrison castles, and (2) how the dispossession of Anglo-Saxon thegns recorded in Domesday transformed the social structure of England by 1086. Examiners reward causal links, not labelled diagrams. A thoughtful answer might also note that historians like Susan Reynolds question whether &lsquo;feudalism&rsquo; as a single system is even the right label.</p>
