--- DESCRIPTION ---
How c.500 motte-and-bailey castles and a few stone keeps held down a kingdom of two million people.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Kingdom Held Down by Earthworks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1066 there were perhaps fifty castles in the whole of England. By 1100 there were close to five hundred. The Normans were a tiny ruling minority &mdash; perhaps 8,000 fighting men sitting on top of two million Anglo-Saxons &mdash; and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A fortified residence built by a lord, designed to project authority over the surrounding area as well as defend against attack.">castle</dfn> was the technology that made that imbalance work. To understand Norman control, you have to understand the castle as an instrument of conquest first and a defensive structure second.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why England Had So Few Castles Before 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Anglo-Saxon England did not build castles in the Norman sense. Local defence rested on <dfn class="term" data-def="A fortified Anglo-Saxon town, usually walled or earth-banked, designed to shelter the surrounding population during attack.">burhs</dfn> &mdash; communal fortified towns first developed under King Alfred against Viking raids. Burhs protected whole communities. Castles, by contrast, were private fortresses owned by individual lords. The few pre-conquest castles in England (such as those built by Edward the Confessor&rsquo;s Norman favourites in Herefordshire) were resented by the English precisely because they looked foreign and threatening &mdash; they pointed inward at the local population, not outward at invaders.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many castles were there in England in 1066, and roughly how many by 1100?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The number of castles in England rose from about fifty in 1066 to roughly five hundred by 1100. This explosion of building was the physical infrastructure of the Norman Conquest.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Motte-and-Bailey: Speed Over Strength</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The most common Norman castle was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A castle design featuring a raised earth mound (motte) topped by a wooden tower, attached to an enclosed courtyard (bailey) protected by a ditch and palisade.">motte-and-bailey</dfn>. The motte was a steep earth mound, sometimes natural and shaped, sometimes built up from scratch using forced English labour. On top sat a wooden tower &mdash; the lord&rsquo;s residence and final stronghold. Beside the motte lay the bailey, a flat enclosed courtyard surrounded by a ditch and a palisade of sharpened timbers, holding stables, kitchens, a chapel, a hall and quarters for the garrison.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The point of the motte-and-bailey was speed. A wooden castle could be thrown up in a fortnight using local labour. William ordered one built at Hastings within hours of landing, using prefabricated timbers brought from Normandy. After his coronation he marched through southern England building motte-and-baileys at strategic points; after the rebellions of 1067&ndash;71 he flooded the disturbed areas with new ones.</p>

The Bayeux Tapestry shows English labourers being driven by Norman overseers to dig the motte at Hastings &mdash; one of the few visual records of a castle being built.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Stone Keeps: Permanence and Statement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Wood rotted, burned and could be chopped down. Where William wanted permanence &mdash; especially in major cities and royal centres &mdash; he replaced timber with stone. The greatest example is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The great stone keep of the Tower of London, begun in the 1070s under William I, designed as a royal stronghold and symbol of Norman authority over the city.">White Tower</dfn>, begun in the 1070s on the eastern edge of London. Its walls are over four metres thick at the base. Its scale was meant to be visible from the river: any merchant or rebel arriving at the capital saw a Norman fortress before they saw the city.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Other stone keeps followed at Colchester (built directly over the ruins of the Roman temple of Claudius, deliberately reusing imperial stonework), at Lincoln, and at Rochester. Stone construction was slow and expensive &mdash; only the king and the wealthiest tenants-in-chief could afford it. By 1100, stone keeps remained the exception; most of the c.500 castles in England were still timber on earth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Siting: Roads, Rivers and Rebels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Where you put a castle decided what it could do. Norman lords sited castles to control river crossings (Wallingford on the Thames, Cambridge on the Cam), road junctions (Berkhamsted on the route from London to the Midlands), the entrances to towns (York, Lincoln, Nottingham &mdash; sometimes two castles per town, on opposite banks), and rebellious regions (the lordships of the Welsh Marches, where Hereford, Shrewsbury and Chester anchored a frontier line).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four reasons a Norman lord would choose a particular spot for a castle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Norman castles were sited to control three things: movement (rivers, roads, town gates), people (rebellious regions, urban populations) and patronage networks (the centre of a tenant-in-chief&rsquo;s landholdings). Defence against invasion was rarely the first reason.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Castles as Instruments of Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">It is tempting to see castles as defensive shells &mdash; places to retreat into when attacked. They were, but only secondarily. Their primary function was projection: a permanent garrison of armed Norman knights stationed in the middle of an English town or shire, riding out to enforce lordship, collect rents, hold courts and intimidate the locals. Building one often meant demolishing English houses; <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal levy or duty by which Anglo-Saxon and Norman tenants were required to perform construction or repair work on castles, walls and bridges.">castle-work</dfn> meant forcing English peasants to dig the ditches and raise the motte without pay.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written by English monks, captures this resentment with bitter clarity. Castles, the Chronicler complained, &lsquo;filled the country&rsquo; and gave the Norman lords a base from which to oppress the English with taxes and labour. The very visibility of a motte rising over a market square was the message: this land has new owners, and they live on a hill watching you.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The number of castles in England rose from c.50 in 1066 to c.500 by 1100; this physical building programme was the infrastructure of the conquest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Motte-and-bailey castles could be built in days using forced English labour; stone keeps such as the White Tower were rarer, slower and reserved for major royal centres.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Castles were instruments of power first and defensive structures second &mdash; sited to control river crossings, towns and rebellious regions and resented by the English as visible symbols of Norman dominance.</li>
</ul>
