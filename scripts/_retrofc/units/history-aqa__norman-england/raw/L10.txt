--- DESCRIPTION ---
Manor, market and forest law: how Norman rule reshaped daily life for English people.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Norman Crust on an English Loaf</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The conquest changed who held land and who lived in the castle. For most people in England, however, daily life carried on. They still ploughed the same strips, paid the same kinds of dues, attended the same parish church, and ate the same food. The novelty was the Norman lord on the hill, the French quarter at the edge of town, and the royal hunting reserve at the top of the valley. Hugh Thomas calls this pattern &lsquo;continuity beneath rupture&rsquo; &mdash; ruling-class change on top of substantial peasant continuity. The misconception students fall into is that the conquest revolutionised peasant life immediately. It did not. The slow work of change unfolded over a century.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Anglo-Saxon Village and Manorial Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Most English peasants in 1066 lived in villages of perhaps 20 to 60 households clustered around a parish church and a lord&rsquo;s hall. They worked under the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon and Norman system of farming where a village&rsquo;s arable was divided into long thin strips spread across two or three large fields, with each peasant holding strips in each.">open-field system</dfn>: the village&rsquo;s arable land was divided into long thin strips spread across two or three great fields, and each peasant household held a portion in every field so that good and poor land was shared. Crops rotated &mdash; one field in winter wheat, one in spring barley, one fallow &mdash; to keep soil fertility. Beyond the arable lay common pasture, common meadow for hay, and common woodland for fuel and pannage (acorn-feeding pigs).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three main classes of unfree peasant in Domesday, and describe one duty each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Domesday Book records most peasants as villeins (working a substantial holding for the lord), bordars (smaller holdings, owing labour service) or cottars (cottagers with little land, working for daily wages or food). Around 10% of the population in 1086 were still slaves, mostly in the south-west, although slavery was already declining.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Roles, Seasons and Food</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Peasant work followed the agricultural calendar. Spring brought ploughing and sowing; summer, weeding and haymaking; August and September, harvest and threshing; autumn, ploughing again and slaughter of livestock that could not be fed through winter; winter, threshing, repairs, weaving and salting. Most peasants ate a coarse bread of barley, rye or maslin, pottage of dried peas and beans flavoured with onions or herbs, occasional cheese and meat at festivals. Norman lords ate better &mdash; wheat bread, more meat, imported wine &mdash; but the daily routine of the manor changed only slightly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Towns and Markets Before and After 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Late Anglo-Saxon England was unusually urbanised by 11th-century European standards. London, York, Winchester, Norwich, Lincoln and Thetford each had thousands of inhabitants, weekly markets, mints and craft quarters. The Normans intensified this urban growth: they granted new market charters, founded boroughs at strategic points (often beneath new castles), and established <dfn class="term" data-def="Distinct quarters of major English towns settled by French-speaking Norman incomers after 1066, often around the new castle and granted privileges by the lord.">French quarters</dfn> for incoming Norman merchants and settlers in major towns. At Norwich and Nottingham these French quarters had their own street plans alongside the Anglo-Saxon old town &mdash; visible in archaeology and in surviving street names today.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">When the Normans took Norwich after the conquest, they laid out a new market and street grid south of the Anglo-Saxon town centre. Domesday calls it the <em>novus burgus</em> &mdash; the new borough &mdash; and records a population of French-speaking incomers settled there to trade. The new market eclipsed the older Tombland and is still the main market square in modern Norwich. Similar French quarters appeared at Nottingham, Northampton, Southampton and elsewhere; in each case Norman merchants, builders and clergy congregated near the castle and the new cathedral, distinct from the existing English population.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Forest Law and Royal Hunting Reserves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For peasants on the edge of forests, Norman rule meant a sharp loss of rights. Vast tracts of countryside were declared royal hunting reserves under <dfn class="term" data-def="The protected status of land under Norman royal hunting law &mdash; commoners lost rights to take game, timber, firewood or graze livestock under threat of mutilation or worse.">forest law</dfn>: villagers who had taken firewood, gathered fallen timber, snared rabbits or grazed pigs in the woodland for generations could now be blinded, mutilated or executed for poaching. The most notorious creation was the New Forest in Hampshire, made by clearing villages of their inhabitants &mdash; a piece of bald rural displacement on which the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle remarks bitterly. Forest law existed alongside common law, and royal foresters policed it through their own courts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Changing Landscapes: Castles, Parks and Burhs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Norman lords reshaped the landscape in ways that ordinary villagers could see. New castles cleared rows of houses; at Lincoln, 166 English homes were demolished to make space for the new motte-and-bailey. Lordly <dfn class="term" data-def="Enclosed landscapes attached to a Norman lord&rsquo;s residence, set aside for hunting deer and demonstrating status &mdash; precursors of the medieval deer parks of the 12th and 13th centuries.">deer parks</dfn> began appearing around manor houses, separating lordly leisure from peasant work. The old Anglo-Saxon burhs (fortified towns) were modified or supplemented by Norman castles inside them, while many new market settlements grew up at castle gates. The visual impact was clear: where there had been timber halls and church towers, there were now stone keeps and motte mounds dominating the skyline.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain to a friend in two sentences how Norman forest law changed what peasants could do in the countryside.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Norman forest law placed huge tracts of countryside under royal hunting privilege. Peasants lost the right to take firewood, snare game or graze pigs without paying fines, and savage penalties &mdash; including blinding and mutilation &mdash; were imposed for breaches.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Continuity for Most</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Despite all these changes, daily life for most peasants kept its rhythm. The same fields were ploughed, the same parish church was attended, the same crops were rotated. Old English remained the language of the village; Latin and French ran the courts and the great houses. Marriage was still arranged within local communities. The big shifts were urban (French quarters, new markets), legal (forest law, the murdrum fine, primogeniture) and architectural (castles and cathedrals), but in the cottages of Yorkshire and Wessex, the conquest did not change the calendar of work.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">Anglo-Saxon law had given women relatively wide rights to hold land, make wills and seek divorce; surviving wills show women bequeathing estates in their own name. Norman customary law was less generous on inheritance, restricting widows&rsquo; rights to dower-portions of their husbands&rsquo; estates and channelling new estates through male primogeniture. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and other 11th-century sources record cases of widows of dispossessed thegns being married off to incoming Normans &mdash; a way of transferring land into Norman hands without further bloodshed. For peasant women, daily working life carried on more or less unchanged, but legally the position of higher-status English women was reduced.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Most peasants worked open-field arable around their village and parish church; Domesday recorded them as villeins, bordars, cottars, freemen and slaves, with daily routines largely continuous through the conquest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Norman lords reshaped towns &mdash; French quarters at Norwich, Nottingham and elsewhere, new markets, new castle-gate settlements &mdash; while their forest law restricted peasant access to woodland and game.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The conquest brought urban, legal and architectural change at the top of society, but rural daily life carried on largely unchanged for most ordinary English people through the rest of the 11th century.</li>
</ul>
