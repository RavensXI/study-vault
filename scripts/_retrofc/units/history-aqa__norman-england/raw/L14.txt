--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Building That Is Also an Argument</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The White Tower is your specified site for the 2028 exam. Unlike a battlefield, this site is a single building you can still walk through &mdash; and the 16-mark question will reward you for reading it like a document. Every feature of the Tower makes a claim about Norman power: where it stands, what it is made of, how you get in, and what William wanted the largest city in his new kingdom to feel every time it looked up. Lesson 7 gave you castles as a system; this lesson reads one building in depth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why There, Why Then</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">London was not conquered at Hastings. The city &mdash; by far the biggest and richest in England &mdash; submitted to William by negotiation in late 1066, and a city that chooses to submit can choose to change its mind. William&rsquo;s answer was to build castles inside it. Immediately after his coronation he ordered fortifications in the city&rsquo;s south-east corner, and around the mid-1070s work began there on something new in England: a colossal stone <dfn class="term" data-def="The great tower of a castle, serving as residence, stronghold and statement of lordship in one building.">keep</dfn>. The site was chosen with precision. It sat in the angle of the surviving Roman city walls, which protected two of its sides and let the builders work securely inside a defended corner &mdash; and it commanded the Thames, the river road by which trade, tribute and any hostile fleet would come.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when was the White Tower begun, and under whose supervision?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Work on the great stone keep began around the mid-1070s under William I, supervised by Gundulf, Bishop of Rochester, a churchman famed as a builder. It was substantially complete by around 1100. The name &lsquo;White Tower&rsquo; came later, when the walls were whitewashed in the thirteenth century.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the Building: Stone, Height and Thickness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Tower&rsquo;s fabric is itself evidence. Much of the stone is Kentish ragstone, but the dressings &mdash; the sharp corners and window surrounds &mdash; are fine pale limestone shipped across the Channel from Caen in Normandy. Importing stone by sea was staggeringly expensive; William did it anyway, because the building was meant to look Norman, to be visibly made of the conquerors&rsquo; own land. The keep rose roughly ninety feet, with walls up to fifteen feet thick at the base, corner turrets above the roofline, and small, high windows. In a city of timber and thatch, nothing remotely like it existed. Chroniclers and townsmen alike understood the message: this building was not going away, and neither were its owners.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Compare it with Lesson 7&rsquo;s motte-and-bailey castles and the difference in purpose is obvious. An earth-and-timber castle was fast war-time architecture &mdash; up in a fortnight, disposable. The White Tower took a generation to build. It was <em>permanence</em> made of stone: the conquest declaring itself finished.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Reading the Building: How You Get In</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The entrance tells its own story. The door stood at first-floor level, reached by an external wooden stair that could be removed in a crisis, leaving attackers facing fifteen feet of blank wall. Inside, the keep stacked its functions vertically: storage in the basement, garrison and business below, and above them the great hall and chambers where the king or his constable lived, held court and overawed visitors. The building was simultaneously a fortress, a palace, a treasury and a prison &mdash; the whole apparatus of Norman lordship under one roof.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">One Tower Among Three</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The White Tower did not control London alone. Two lesser castles &mdash; Baynard&rsquo;s Castle and Montfichet&rsquo;s Tower &mdash; were planted at the city&rsquo;s western end, so that Norman garrisons bracketed London from both sides. That pattern repeats what you saw across the kingdom: York got two castles; strategic towns got theirs driven through demolished houses. The difference at the Tower is scale and material. London was the prize of the kingdom, so it received the statement building &mdash; the keep that out-built anything the English had ever seen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which two other castles helped hold London, and what does that pattern show?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Baynard&rsquo;s Castle and Montfichet&rsquo;s Tower stood at London&rsquo;s western end, bracketing the city with the White Tower in the east &mdash; the standard Norman pattern of controlling towns from multiple strongpoints, applied to the greatest town of all.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">What the Site Shows About Norman England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Pull the features together and the Tower becomes a summary of the whole conquest. The location shows the Normans&rsquo; priorities: London and its river first. The Roman walls show their practicality: build where the ground already defends you. The Caen stone shows identity and expense deployed as propaganda. The first-floor door and fifteen-foot walls show a ruling minority that expected rebellion &mdash; this was built <em>during</em> the years of revolt you studied in Lessons 4 and 5. The chapel shows Church and Crown fused. And the sheer scale shows the ambition that separates William&rsquo;s conquest from a mere raid: he was building for centuries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Site Afterwards and in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The keep still stands at the heart of the Tower of London, wrapped in the walls later kings added, and the fortress around it has served as armoury, prison, mint and menagerie since. For the 16-mark question, work feature by feature and attach each to a purpose: Caen stone &rarr; Norman identity displayed; first-floor entrance &rarr; security against a hostile city; position on the Thames and Roman walls &rarr; control of trade and approach; scale in stone &rarr; permanence aimed at Londoners&rsquo; minds as much as their walls. Feature, purpose, consequence &mdash; then weigh it against your wider knowledge of how the Normans held England.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Begun around the mid-1070s under Gundulf of Rochester and substantially complete by c.1100, the great keep was William's answer to a rich city that had submitted but might rebel.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Every feature argues: Caen stone for Norman identity, a first-floor entrance for security, the Roman wall corner and the Thames for control, and unprecedented scale for permanence.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">It worked with Baynard's Castle and Montfichet's Tower in the west — the many-strongpoints pattern the Normans used on towns across England, at maximum strength for the greatest town.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The keep fused fortress, palace, treasury and chapel in one building — the whole machinery of Norman lordship, which is why it summarises the conquest for your 16-mark answer.</li>
</ul>
