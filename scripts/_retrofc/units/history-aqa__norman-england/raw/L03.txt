--- DESCRIPTION ---
How William turned a battlefield win into a crown — submissions, castles and the Christmas.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Battlefield to Throne</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A battlefield victory was not a country. After Hastings on 14 October 1066, William held only the field and the camp. London, Winchester, York and the Welsh borders were untouched. The remaining English leaders &mdash; Edwin and Morcar in the Midlands and North, the surviving <dfn class="term" data-def="Anglo-Saxon noblemen of the second rank below earls; landholding warriors who served the king and dominated local society.">thegns</dfn>, the bishops, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon council of leading nobles and bishops that advised the king and could elect or recognise his successor.">Witan</dfn> &mdash; could still raise an army or proclaim another king. The next ten weeks decided whether Hastings would be remembered as the start of a conquest or a one-off raid.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The March of Submissions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">William moved cautiously. He waited five days at Hastings for an English surrender that did not come. He then turned east along the coast, taking Romney and Dover. Dover&rsquo;s castle was strengthened in eight days. From there he pushed inland to Canterbury, the religious capital, which submitted in late October. He then swung west and north around London, ravaging Surrey and Hampshire to demonstrate the cost of resistance, before crossing the Thames at Wallingford. The strategy was not to assault London but to circle it, cut its supply lines and let the leaders inside come to him.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three towns that submitted to William between Hastings and his coronation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Between mid-October and December 1066, William circled London via Romney, Dover, Canterbury, Wallingford and Berkhamsted. He did not need to storm the capital &mdash; the encirclement and his ravaging of the surrounding shires forced the city&rsquo;s leaders to come out and submit.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Witan&rsquo;s Last Throw: Edgar Atheling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Inside London the surviving Witan briefly elected the teenage Edgar Atheling king. He was the last male of the West Saxon line and the only candidate left with royal blood. But he had no army. The northern earls Edwin and Morcar had already returned to their earldoms. Archbishop Stigand of Canterbury and Archbishop Ealdred of York could not raise the city to fight. By early December, with William&rsquo;s army at Berkhamsted just 30 miles north of London, the leading bishops, Edgar himself, and Edwin and Morcar rode out and submitted to William, offering the crown.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Berkhamsted was the moment the conquest passed from military victory to political legitimacy. The men who came out to submit included the two leading archbishops in England and the king-elect Edgar himself. Their submission meant that William&rsquo;s coronation, when it came, would be performed by Anglo-Saxon clergy, witnessed by Anglo-Saxon nobles, and presented as a continuation of the kingship of Edward the Confessor &mdash; not as a foreign takeover.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Coronation, Christmas Day 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">William was crowned at Westminster Abbey on 25 December 1066, ten weeks after Hastings and on the spot where Harold had been crowned the year before. Archbishop Ealdred of York performed the consecration; Stigand was passed over because his pallium (a vestment of office) had come from a disputed pope and his position was insecure. The ceremony followed the Anglo-Saxon coronation order, with the new king swearing to defend the Church, govern justly and uphold the laws of his predecessors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Outside the abbey something went badly wrong. When the congregation shouted out their assent, in both English and Norman French, the Norman guards stationed around the building thought a riot had broken out. They set fire to the surrounding houses to disperse the crowd. According to the Norman chronicler Orderic Vitalis, the church emptied as people ran out to fight the flames or save their property; only the bishops and a few clergy stayed to finish the ceremony. William was visibly shaken &mdash; an early sign that conciliation and force would have to run side by side.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Conciliation: keeping the English working</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">William&rsquo;s early policies were deliberately moderate. He confirmed Anglo-Saxon law and the rights of London in a charter to the citizens of London, written in English. He kept English bishops in their sees, including Stigand for now. Edwin and Morcar retained their earldoms, on terms. Several English thegns were allowed to keep their lands in return for renewed oaths. Conciliation was a tool of conquest: it bought time while William consolidated, built castles and brought over Norman lords. The harder line came later, after the revolts of 1067&ndash;71.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Castles: The First Tools of Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Even as William was crowned, his men were building. The first <dfn class="term" data-def="A Norman castle design with an artificial earth mound (the motte) topped by a wooden tower, and an enclosed courtyard (the bailey) at its base, surrounded by a ditch and palisade.">motte-and-bailey</dfn> castles went up in London, Dover, Canterbury, Winchester and Wallingford within months of Hastings. Each was a small wooden tower on an earth mound with a fenced courtyard. They could be thrown up in a few weeks using forced English labour and a small Norman garrison. Their function was to project Norman power into a town and dominate its markets, river crossings and roads. They were instruments of conquest first and defensive structures second &mdash; a misconception worth correcting in any answer about Norman control.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why William built castles in towns rather than just on borders.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By spring 1067 motte-and-bailey castles dominated London (the future Tower), Dover, Canterbury and other major towns. They were instruments of conquest &mdash; projection of Norman power into the heart of English civilian life &mdash; not just border defences.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">What Coronation Did Not Mean</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Coronation gave William legal title across the kingdom, but real control was thin. The Midlands, the North and the Welsh borderlands had submitted in writing only. Many shires had never seen a Norman soldier. William sailed back to Normandy in March 1067 with English hostages and treasure, leaving his half-brother Odo of Bayeux and William FitzOsbern as regents. Within months a wave of revolts would test how thin Norman control really was &mdash; the subject of the next lesson.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">William circled London via Dover, Canterbury, Wallingford and Berkhamsted between October and December 1066, forcing submissions rather than fighting another pitched battle.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The coronation at Westminster Abbey on 25 December 1066 used Anglo-Saxon ritual to legitimise Norman rule, but a riot outside the abbey foreshadowed how fragile the new regime really was.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Early conciliation &mdash; confirming Anglo-Saxon law, keeping English officials &mdash; ran alongside motte-and-bailey castles in major towns; conquest, not just defence, was the castles&rsquo; first purpose.</li>
</ul>
