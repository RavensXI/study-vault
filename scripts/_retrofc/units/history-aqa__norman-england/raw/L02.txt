--- DESCRIPTION ---
Two battles in three weeks — Stamford Bridge and Hastings — and why Norman victory was.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Year of Two Invasions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By September 1066 Harold Godwinson had been king for eight months. He had spent the summer with the largest army England could field on the south coast, watching for William&rsquo;s ships. Provisions ran out on 8 September and the army went home. Within a fortnight the wind he had been waiting for blew the wrong way: Harald Hardrada, not William, was the first to land.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Fulford Gate, 20 September 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Hardrada and Tostig Godwinson sailed up the Humber with around 300 ships and 9,000 men. The northern earls Edwin of Mercia and Morcar of Northumbria gathered the local <dfn class="term" data-def="The general military levy of able-bodied freemen called out by the king or earl in late Anglo-Saxon England, supplementing the elite professional housecarls.">fyrd</dfn> and met them at Fulford Gate, just south of York, on 20 September. The Norse line outlasted the English; Edwin and Morcar&rsquo;s army was broken and York opened its gates. Hardrada now controlled the north.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The March North</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Harold heard the news in London around 21 September. He summoned his <dfn class="term" data-def="The professional household troops of an Anglo-Saxon king or earl, paid a wage and equipped with mail, helmet, sword and a long two-handed Dane axe.">housecarls</dfn>, called out the southern fyrd, and rode north. He covered roughly 185 miles in four to five days &mdash; an exceptional pace for an 11th-century army. He arrived at Tadcaster on 24 September, picking up local levies as he came.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Stamford Bridge, 25 September 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Hardrada had agreed to meet hostages from York at Stamford Bridge, seven miles east of the city. His men had left their mail behind in the ships at Riccall, expecting nothing more than ceremony. Harold&rsquo;s fast march caught them by surprise. After heavy fighting, including a famous stand by a single Norse warrior on the bridge itself, the English broke through. Hardrada was killed by an arrow to the throat; Tostig was killed in the melee. Of around 300 ships only 24 returned to Norway. Stamford Bridge was a comprehensive English victory &mdash; and the last great battle of the Viking Age in Britain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two main commanders killed at Stamford Bridge and the date of the battle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Stamford Bridge on 25 September 1066, Harold&rsquo;s housecarls and southern fyrd destroyed Harald Hardrada&rsquo;s Norse army. Hardrada and Tostig Godwinson were both killed. Only 24 of about 300 Norse ships sailed home.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">William&rsquo;s Landing on the South Coast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Three days later, on 28 September, William&rsquo;s fleet landed unopposed on the Sussex coast at Pevensey. He moved his base to Hastings within days and threw up a wooden castle there. His men ravaged the local countryside, partly for supplies, partly to force Harold to march and fight before his army could fully recover. Harold marched south with his housecarls and the surviving fyrd, gathering reinforcements as he went, but his army was tired and depleted before the battle began.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Hastings, 14 October 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Harold reached the area on 13 October and chose his ground well. He drew up his men on Senlac ridge, blocking the Hastings&ndash;London road. The English formed a tight <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon defensive formation in which warriors locked their kite-shaped or round shields edge-to-edge and braced their spears, presenting a continuous wall to attackers.">shield wall</dfn> on the crest of the ridge: housecarls in the front rank with two-handed Dane axes, fyrd behind them. William attacked uphill with three lines &mdash; archers, infantry, then mounted knights.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Norman archers shot first but their arrows had little effect against the shield wall on higher ground. The infantry charge was thrown back. The cavalry charge stalled. A rumour ran through William&rsquo;s left flank that the duke had been killed; it began to break. William rode bare-headed along the line to show he was alive, rallied his men and charged again. Sometime in the afternoon a section of the English right pursued retreating Bretons down the slope &mdash; whether by genuine retreat or feigned retreat is debated &mdash; and were cut down by the cavalry on the open ground. The shield wall thinned. Late in the day Harold was killed; the Bayeux Tapestry shows him struck in the eye, then cut down by a mounted knight. With the king dead and his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine already fallen, the housecarls fought to the death around the standard. The fyrd dispersed into the woods.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why Hastings Was Close-Run</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">It is tempting to assume Norman cavalry and archers always beat an Anglo-Saxon shield wall, but the battle was decided in the last hour of daylight. The English had repulsed two major Norman attacks. Harold&rsquo;s army was depleted from Stamford Bridge and the rapid march, but the ridge negated William&rsquo;s cavalry advantage for most of the day. Modern historians stress the role of the feigned (or genuine) retreats and the death of Harold himself: had Harold survived to night, William&rsquo;s army &mdash; far from supplies, in hostile country, with no reinforcements &mdash; might have had to retreat to its ships. The myth of inevitable Norman victory grew up after the conquest, written by the winners.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Norman armies fought with three arms working together: archers softened the enemy line, infantry advanced under cover of the shooting, and mailed knights on warhorses charged on command. They also brought prefabricated castles &mdash; Hastings was fortified within days of landing. The Anglo-Saxons fought on foot in massed shield walls, with elite housecarls swinging two-handed Dane axes capable of cutting through mail. Both ways of war could win; the question was who controlled the ground.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory two reasons why Hastings was close-run rather than inevitable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hastings was decided in the last hour of daylight on 14 October 1066. The shield wall had repulsed Norman archers, infantry and the first cavalry charge. The combination of feigned retreats, the wearing-down of the housecarls and Harold&rsquo;s death &mdash; not Norman tactical superiority alone &mdash; turned the battle.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Harold&rsquo;s rapid march north and surprise victory at Stamford Bridge on 25 September 1066 destroyed Hardrada&rsquo;s invasion but left the southern army worn down before William landed three days later.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">At Hastings on 14 October the shield wall held against archers, infantry and the first cavalry charge; the battle was decided in the final hour by feigned retreats and the death of Harold.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Norman victory was close-run, not inevitable &mdash; the &lsquo;cavalry always beats shield wall&rsquo; framing is a post-conquest myth that ignores how nearly William was beaten.</li>
</ul>
