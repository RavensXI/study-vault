William’s scorched-earth winter answer to the 1069 revolt — and a debate still unsettled.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Reign-Defining Decision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By December 1069 William had bought off the Danish fleet and recaptured York, but the autumn revolt had been the most dangerous moment of his reign. He decided that mercy and conciliation had failed in the North. Through the winter of 1069 to 1070 his army moved across Yorkshire, Durham and beyond in a deliberate campaign of destruction known to historians as the <dfn class="term" data-def="William&rsquo;s deliberate winter campaign of 1069&ndash;70 across northern England, designed to crush rebellion by destroying villages, livestock and food stores.">Harrying of the North</dfn>. The misconception students fall into is that &lsquo;100,000 dead&rsquo; is a hard fact. It is a chronicle figure written more than fifty years later. What the Harrying actually was &mdash; and how brutal &mdash; is a debate active among historians today.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What William Did</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">William divided his army into smaller mobile bands and sent them out from a base at York. These detachments burned villages, slaughtered livestock and destroyed grain stores and farming tools wherever they went. The campaign focused on Yorkshire but reached into Durham, Cheshire and the Tees valley. It deliberately ran through winter, when food was scarcest and rebuilding was slowest. Survivors who escaped the raids faced famine; the chroniclers describe people eating dogs, horses and even, in shocked passages, each other.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in which winter did the Harrying happen, and which three regions were worst hit?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Harrying of the North took place in the winter of 1069&ndash;70. Yorkshire was hit hardest, with Durham, Cheshire and the Tees valley also devastated. Villages, livestock and food stores were destroyed deliberately to make rebellion impossible.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Chroniclers&rsquo; Voice: Orderic and John of Worcester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Most of what we know about the Harrying comes from two later monastic writers. The Norman chronicler <dfn class="term" data-def="An Anglo-Norman monk and historian (1075&ndash;c.1142) of the abbey of St Evroul, whose Ecclesiastical History is a major source for the Norman Conquest, written about fifty years after the events.">Orderic Vitalis</dfn> wrote his Ecclesiastical History around 1125, more than fifty years after the events. Although he served a Norman abbey, he condemned William&rsquo;s decision in unusually strong language: &lsquo;In his anger he commanded that all crops and herds, chattels and food of every kind should be brought together and burned to ashes.&rsquo; Orderic went on to claim that more than 100,000 people died of hunger in the months that followed and that William had committed an unforgivable crime against the innocent. <dfn class="term" data-def="An English monk (d. 1140) at Worcester Priory whose Latin chronicle of English history is one of the main contemporary sources for William&rsquo;s reign and the Harrying.">John of Worcester</dfn>, writing slightly earlier, gives a similar picture of mass starvation and depopulation across the North.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Domesday &lsquo;Waste&rsquo; Entries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">When the Domesday commissioners reached Yorkshire in 1086, they recorded enormous numbers of villages with the Latin entry <dfn class="term" data-def="A Domesday Book entry meaning &lsquo;wasted&rsquo; or worthless &mdash; villages or estates with no taxable value, often interpreted as showing destruction or depopulation.">vasta est</dfn>, &lsquo;it is waste&rsquo;. In some districts more than half the recorded settlements were marked waste, sixteen years after the Harrying. For older historians this was direct numerical proof of catastrophic destruction &mdash; whole regions wiped clean. The political effect is undisputed: large-scale revolt against William I never happened in the North again.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">More recent work has cautioned against reading every &lsquo;waste&rsquo; entry as proof of physical destruction. A village might be &lsquo;waste&rsquo; because it had no taxable value at the moment of survey, because tenants had fled, because the lord could not produce records, or because the estate had been deliberately reorganised by Norman tenants-in-chief. Some &lsquo;waste&rsquo; districts are far enough from the documented Harrying route that they cannot easily be blamed on William&rsquo;s soldiers. The destruction was real, but the Domesday data is fiscal as much as it is forensic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Modern Historiography: How Bad, How Big?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Modern historians sit between two earlier extremes. Older nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship took Orderic&rsquo;s 100,000 dead largely at face value and called the Harrying a genocide. More recent work, including Hugh Thomas&rsquo;s The English and the Normans (2003), reads it as catastrophic but smaller in scale than Orderic claimed &mdash; the figure inflated by a writer working in a tradition that condemned cruelty in princes. What no historian now denies is the political effect: terror that ended the threat of large-scale northern revolt for the rest of William I&rsquo;s reign.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A high-band answer does not just repeat &lsquo;100,000 dead&rsquo;. It explains where the figure comes from (Orderic, c.1125), notes that it is a chronicle estimate written decades after the events by a writer hostile to royal cruelty, and weighs it against the Domesday &lsquo;waste&rsquo; pattern of 1086. The point is not to dismiss the suffering &mdash; it was real and unprecedented &mdash; but to handle the evidence carefully, as the AQA mark schemes and examiner reports consistently reward.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Long-Term Effect on Northern Landholding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Harrying changed the map of northern landholding. Many Anglo-Saxon thegns who survived had no estates left to hold; their lands were redistributed to a small group of trusted Normans who would now garrison the North. The North-east was reorganised under figures such as the bishop of Durham and Alan Rufus of Richmond, who built castles to hold their new territories. By 1086, Yorkshire was one of the most thoroughly Normanised regions of England despite having been the most rebellious in 1069. That is the reign-defining trade William made in the winter of 1069&ndash;70.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory two reasons modern historians treat Orderic Vitalis&rsquo;s 100,000 figure with caution.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Orderic Vitalis wrote about the Harrying around 1125, more than fifty years after the events. His figure of 100,000 dead is treated by modern historians as evidence of perceived horror rather than a precise head-count, and Domesday &lsquo;waste&rsquo; entries can have many causes besides direct destruction.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Harrying of the North (winter 1069&ndash;70) was a deliberate winter campaign of destruction across Yorkshire, Durham, Cheshire and the Tees valley designed to make northern revolt impossible.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Orderic Vitalis&rsquo;s claim of 100,000 dead, written around 1125, sits alongside Domesday&rsquo;s 1086 &lsquo;waste&rsquo; entries as evidence &mdash; but modern historians treat both with caution.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The political and territorial effect is undisputed: northern landholding passed to a new Norman elite, and large-scale revolt did not return to the North under William I.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For an &lsquo;Explain Significance&rsquo; question on the Harrying, separate significance at the time from significance over time. At the time, the Harrying ended the 1069 revolt and bought off any repeat. Over time, it reshaped northern landholding, planted a network of new Norman lords and castles, and gave William the historical reputation that Anglo-Norman chroniclers themselves struggled with. Examiners reward students who weigh both moments and use historians&rsquo; treatment of the chronicle evidence rather than repeating &lsquo;100,000 dead&rsquo; as fact.</p>
