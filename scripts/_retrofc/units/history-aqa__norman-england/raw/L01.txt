--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Edward’s death in January 1066 left England with four rival claimants and a kingdom on the edge.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Kingdom Without an Heir</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">On 5 January 1066, King Edward the Confessor died in his new abbey church at Westminster. He had reigned for 23 years but had no children. England in 1066 was rich, well-organised and dangerously unstable: it ran a sophisticated system of <dfn class="term" data-def="The royal land tax of late Anglo-Saxon England, raised on every hide of land and used to fund armies, navies and tribute to the Vikings.">geld</dfn> tax, struck high-quality silver coin, and supported around two million people. That wealth made the throne worth fighting for. By the spring of 1066 four men were preparing to take it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why the Succession Was Disputed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Anglo-Saxon England had no fixed law of succession. The throne usually passed to a close male relative, but the council of leading nobles &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The council of leading earls, bishops and royal officials in late Anglo-Saxon England that advised the king and confirmed the choice of a new monarch.">Witan</dfn> &mdash; had to confirm the choice. A king could also promise the throne to someone in his lifetime. Edward had made several different gestures over his long reign, and each of them now mattered.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">On his deathbed, witnesses said Edward stretched out his hand to his most powerful earl, Harold Godwinson, and commended his queen and kingdom to him. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle accepted this as the decisive act. Norman writers, however, claimed Edward had already promised the crown to Duke William of Normandy back in 1051, and that Harold had sworn an oath to support William&rsquo;s claim while a guest in Normandy in around 1064. The oath, real or invented, became the moral spine of the Norman case.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the four men who claimed the English throne in 1066 and one reason each gave for his claim.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four claimants pursued the English throne in 1066: Harold Godwinson (deathbed gesture and Witan election), William of Normandy (1051 promise and Harold&rsquo;s oath), Harald Hardrada of Norway (a treaty inherited from King Cnut&rsquo;s line) and Edgar Atheling (royal Anglo-Saxon blood).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Harold Godwinson, Earl of Wessex</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Harold was the most powerful man in England outside the king. As Earl of Wessex he controlled the richest part of the kingdom; his brothers Tostig, Gyrth and Leofwine held Northumbria, East Anglia and Kent. The Godwins were also tied to royalty &mdash; Edward had married Harold&rsquo;s sister Edith. Harold had led English armies, kept the Welsh border under control and was popular with the southern thegns. The Witan met on 6 January, the day of Edward&rsquo;s funeral, and elected him king. He was crowned the same day at Westminster Abbey.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">By Anglo-Saxon rules Harold&rsquo;s position was strong. He had been chosen by the Witan, named by the dying king, and crowned in front of the leading nobles. The &lsquo;usurper&rsquo; label comes from Norman writers like William of Poitiers, who needed to justify the invasion. Modern historians stress that until October 1066 most contemporaries inside England saw Harold&rsquo;s rule as legitimate &mdash; the misconception that he was an obvious oath-breaker is post-conquest propaganda.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">William, Duke of Normandy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">William had ruled Normandy since 1035, fighting off rivals as a teenager and pacifying the duchy with castles and loyal lords. He claimed Edward had promised him the English throne during a visit in 1051, when Edward&rsquo;s relations with the Godwin family had broken down. William also claimed that Harold, shipwrecked on the Norman coast in around 1064, had sworn a sacred oath to back William&rsquo;s claim &mdash; an oath the Bayeux Tapestry shows being sworn over holy <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical remains of saints, or objects connected to them, kept in churches and used to make oaths binding before God.">relics</dfn>. To break such an oath was, by 11th-century standards, a serious sin, and William used this to win Pope Alexander II&rsquo;s blessing for his invasion. A papal banner was sent to Normandy in 1066.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Harald Hardrada of Norway</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Harald Hardrada (&lsquo;the Hard Ruler&rsquo;) was the most feared warrior in northern Europe. He claimed England through an old treaty between his predecessor Magnus and Harthacnut, the Anglo-Danish king who had ruled before Edward. The claim was thin in law but Hardrada had ships, men and a record of victories from Sicily to the Black Sea. He was joined in summer 1066 by Tostig Godwinson, Harold&rsquo;s exiled brother, who had been driven out of his Northumbrian earldom in 1065 and wanted revenge.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Edgar Atheling, the Forgotten Heir</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Edgar was the last male of the old West Saxon royal line &mdash; grandson of Edmund Ironside, great-nephew of Edward the Confessor. By blood his claim was the strongest of all. But Edgar was about 14 in January 1066, with no military following and no powerful earl behind him. The Witan needed an adult king who could fight, not a teenage prince. Edgar would matter later, after Hastings, when he was briefly proposed as king before submitting to William.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">By 1066 England had been raising the geld for over 60 years. Royal mints in around 70 towns produced standard silver pennies. Sheriffs collected fines and rents in every shire. The king&rsquo;s personal landholdings (the royal demesne) made him the largest landowner in the country. For an outsider like William or Hardrada, capturing the English crown meant capturing one of the wealthiest, most centralised states in western Europe &mdash; far richer than Normandy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory two reasons why the Witan chose Harold over the 14-year-old Edgar Atheling.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Witan elected Harold on 6 January 1066 because England needed an adult, battle-tested king. Edgar Atheling had royal blood but was too young; Harold had armies, allies and Edward&rsquo;s deathbed gesture. By Norman standards he was an oath-breaker; by Anglo-Saxon standards he was the legitimate king.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Edward the Confessor died childless on 5 January 1066, leaving four claimants &mdash; Harold Godwinson, William of Normandy, Harald Hardrada and Edgar Atheling &mdash; with overlapping claims and no fixed law to choose between them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Witan elected Harold on 6 January because he had Edward&rsquo;s deathbed gesture, a powerful family network and the military experience that the teenage Edgar lacked.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Norman writers later branded Harold an oath-breaker, but inside England in early 1066 his rule was treated as legitimate &mdash; the &lsquo;usurper&rsquo; framing belongs to post-conquest propaganda.</li>
</ul>
