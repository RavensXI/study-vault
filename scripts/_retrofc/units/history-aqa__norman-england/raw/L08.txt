--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sheriffs, hundred courts, ordeals, ‘murdrum’: what the Normans kept and what they added.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Continuity Beneath Rupture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A common misconception is that the Norman Conquest was a clean break in English government. It was not. William inherited one of the most administratively sophisticated monarchies in Europe, and he kept most of its machinery intact. He changed the personnel at the top &mdash; thegns out, Norman barons in &mdash; and added a few sharp innovations of his own. The historian Hugh Thomas calls this &lsquo;continuity beneath rupture&rsquo;: ruling-class transformation on top of substantial Anglo-Saxon stability underneath. Understanding what was kept and what was added is the way to answer the spec&rsquo;s comparison questions cleanly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What the Normans Inherited</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">By 1066 England was already divided into <dfn class="term" data-def="The administrative units of Anglo-Saxon and Norman England, broadly equivalent to modern counties, each headed by a sheriff and managed through hundred courts.">shires</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Subdivisions of an Anglo-Saxon shire, each with its own court that met monthly to handle local justice and collect royal dues.">hundreds</dfn> &mdash; the framework that lasted, in modified form, into modern times. Each shire was supervised by a royal officer called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The king&rsquo;s chief representative in a shire, responsible for collecting royal revenues, enforcing royal justice and commanding the shire militia.">sheriff</dfn> (Anglo-Saxon &lsquo;shire-reeve&rsquo;) who collected the king&rsquo;s revenues, presided over the shire court and led the local militia. Communication ran through the <dfn class="term" data-def="A sealed written order from the king (Anglo-Saxon and Norman), a quick administrative document used to instruct sheriffs and other officers.">writ</dfn> &mdash; a short sealed Latin or Old English document by which the king sent instructions to his sheriffs. England had also developed a sophisticated system of cash taxation through the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon land tax assessed in &lsquo;hides&rsquo; on shires and hundreds, originally collected to pay tribute to Viking raiders and continued by William as a regular royal income.">geld</dfn>, originally raised to pay off Viking raiders.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three Anglo-Saxon institutions of government that the Normans kept after 1066.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Normans kept the Anglo-Saxon framework of government largely intact: shires and hundreds, sheriffs as royal officers, the writ as the standard royal instruction, and the geld land tax. The framework continued; the personnel changed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Anglo-Saxon and Norman Aristocracies Compared</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The pre-1066 ruling class were the great earls and the thegns &mdash; landholders who provided counsel through the <dfn class="term" data-def="The council of leading Anglo-Saxon nobles, churchmen and royal officers who advised the king and elected new kings.">Witan</dfn>. They held lands in their own right, often dispersed across many shires, and their power rested on family networks as well as royal favour. The Norman tenants-in-chief who replaced them held land formally from the king, owed forty days&rsquo; military service per knight&rsquo;s fee, and operated through a tighter pyramid of patronage. Where Anglo-Saxon earls had been semi-independent regional powers, Norman tenants-in-chief were the king&rsquo;s sworn men first.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What the Normans Added</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">William also bolted on his own innovations. The most striking was <dfn class="term" data-def="Norman law that placed huge tracts of England under royal hunting law, with savage penalties for poaching, taking timber or clearing land.">forest law</dfn>: vast tracts of countryside were declared royal hunting reserves, with savage penalties &mdash; blinding, mutilation, sometimes execution &mdash; for poaching, taking firewood or clearing land. The New Forest in Hampshire was created by clearing villages of their inhabitants. Forest law existed alongside, not instead of, ordinary common law &mdash; royal officers called foresters policed it through their own courts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Even sharper was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavy collective fine imposed by the Normans on the hundred where a Norman was found murdered, unless the killer was produced or the dead man was proven to be English.">murdrum</dfn> fine. If a Norman was found dead with no clear killer, the hundred where the body lay had to pay a heavy collective fine to the king. The hundred could only escape this fine by producing the murderer &mdash; or by proving the dead man was English, a process called &lsquo;presentment of Englishry&rsquo;. The murdrum fine assumed Norman lives were under permanent threat from a hostile Anglo-Saxon population, and made every English community responsible for protecting Norman incomers.</p>

The New Forest was created in Hampshire on land cleared of villages and farmland; many of the displaced communities were never resettled. Forest law remained one of the bitterest English grievances against Norman rule for the next century, and it only began to be rolled back under Magna Carta and the Charter of the Forest in the early 13th century.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Justice and the Legal System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Most ordinary disputes still went to the local <dfn class="term" data-def="The court of a hundred, meeting monthly to handle theft, debt, minor violence and other routine matters of local justice.">hundred court</dfn> or shire court that had existed before 1066. There was no professional judiciary in the modern sense; the sheriff or a hundred reeve presided. When evidence was unclear, the courts used <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of trial that submitted the accused to a physical test (carrying hot iron, plunging an arm into boiling water, being bound and thrown into water) and read the outcome as God&rsquo;s judgement.">trial by ordeal</dfn>: the accused carried a hot iron a set distance, plunged an arm into boiling water, or was bound and thrown into a pool. The wound that healed cleanly meant innocence; one that festered, guilt. The Normans added <dfn class="term" data-def="A Norman judicial procedure in which two parties (or their champions) fought a duel; the winner was held to be in the right, supposedly with God&rsquo;s judgement deciding the outcome.">trial by combat</dfn>, a duel between accuser and accused (or paid champions) that imported a continental warrior practice into English courts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Inheritance and the New Norman Pattern</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Anglo-Saxon inheritance had often been partible &mdash; estates divided between sons. Normans favoured <dfn class="term" data-def="The Norman rule by which the eldest son inherited his father&rsquo;s entire estate, keeping landholding consolidated in one heir per generation.">primogeniture</dfn>: the eldest son inherited the lot. This kept knights&rsquo; fees and great honours intact across generations and was crucial to the long-term survival of the feudal pyramid. The cost was political: William&rsquo;s younger sons inherited less than the eldest. When William I died in 1087, his eldest son Robert Curthose got Normandy, his second son William II got England &mdash; and the resulting tension fuelled the 1088 rebellion.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">In The English and the Normans (2003), Hugh Thomas argues that beneath dramatic Norman rupture &mdash; battles, dispossession, castles &mdash; lay substantial Anglo-Saxon continuity. The shire and hundred courts kept meeting; sheriffs kept collecting revenue; villages kept their fields and churches. Marc Morris, by contrast, emphasises the violence of the change &mdash; thegns dispossessed, bishops replaced, the Harrying of the North. AQA examiners reward students who can hold both readings together: government structure largely continuous, government personnel and high politics violently new.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the murdrum fine to a friend in two sentences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The two sharpest Norman additions to English government were forest law (royal hunting reserves with savage penalties) and the murdrum fine (collective punishment imposed on hundreds where a Norman was killed). Both protected the new ruling class from a hostile Anglo-Saxon majority.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Normans inherited shires, hundreds, sheriffs, the writ and the geld &mdash; the framework of Anglo-Saxon government largely continued under new Norman personnel.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Forest law and the murdrum fine were the sharpest Norman additions, designed to protect royal privilege and Norman incomers from a hostile English majority.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Justice mostly happened in inherited hundred and shire courts using ordeals, with the Normans adding trial by combat; primogeniture replaced partible inheritance to keep knights&rsquo; fees intact.</li>
</ul>
