--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Anglo-Saxon England was governed, its economy worked, and the Church shaped daily life.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Power of the Anglo-Saxon Monarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1060, Anglo-Saxon England was one of the most advanced kingdoms in Europe. The king sat at the top of government and was expected to maintain law and order, defend the kingdom, and collect taxes. However, the king did not rule alone — he relied on a network of <dfn class="term" data-def="Powerful nobles who governed large regions of England on the king's behalf.">earls</dfn>, a council of advisors called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A council of important nobles, churchmen, and advisors who helped the king make major decisions, including choosing the next king.">Witan</dfn>, and the Church to govern effectively.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain the Witan's role and why it mattered in the 1066 succession crisis.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Witan was a council of around 30–60 leading nobles and churchmen who advised the king. They could influence major decisions and even had a role in choosing the next king when a monarch died.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The king’s authority was personal — he had to earn the loyalty of his earls through gifts of land and favour. If an earl grew too powerful, the balance of power could shift. This tension between royal authority and the power of the earls was a constant feature of Anglo-Saxon politics. Kings who lost the support of their leading nobles could face rebellion or even deposition, so maintaining good relationships was essential.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Earldoms and Local Government</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">England was divided into <dfn class="term" data-def="Large regions of Anglo-Saxon England, each governed by an earl on behalf of the king.">earldoms</dfn>, each governed by an earl. By the 1060s, the four main earldoms were Wessex, Mercia, Northumbria, and East Anglia. Earls collected taxes, raised armies, and enforced the law in their region. In return, they kept a share of taxes and legal fines. Below the earls, <dfn class="term" data-def="Wealthy landowners who served as local lords and owed military service to earls.">thegns</dfn> served as local lords, managing smaller estates and providing military service.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Justice was delivered through two main courts. <strong>Shire courts</strong> met twice a year to deal with serious crimes and land disputes, presided over by the earl or the king’s representative (the <dfn class="term" data-def="A royal official who represented the king's authority in each shire, collecting taxes and enforcing the law.">shire reeve</dfn>). <strong>Hundred courts</strong> met every four weeks and handled more routine matters like theft and minor disputes. The system relied on community involvement — local people acted as witnesses and helped decide guilt through oaths and, in some cases, <strong>trial by ordeal</strong> (such as holding hot iron), where God was believed to reveal the truth.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Economy and Social System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Anglo-Saxon England had a largely agricultural economy. Most people were <dfn class="term" data-def="Peasant farmers who worked the land and owed labour and rent to their lord.">ceorls</dfn> (free peasants) who farmed strips of land in open fields around their village. Below them were <strong>villeins</strong> and <strong>slaves</strong>, who had fewer rights and freedoms. The social hierarchy ran from the king at the top, through earls and thegns, down to ceorls and slaves at the bottom. A person’s <strong>wergild</strong> (literally “man price”) reflected their position in this hierarchy — if someone was killed, the compensation owed to their family was determined by their social rank. An earl’s wergild was far higher than a ceorl’s, reflecting the value placed on status.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the levels of Anglo-Saxon social structure from top to bottom.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>About 10% of England’s population were slaves in 1066. Slavery was an accepted part of Anglo-Saxon society, though the Church encouraged freeing slaves as an act of piety.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Towns like London, Winchester, and York were important centres of trade. England exported wool, metalwork, and embroidery, and imported wine, spices, and luxury goods from Europe. The coinage system was one of the most sophisticated in Europe — silver pennies were the standard currency, minted under royal authority at around 70 mints across the country. The king controlled the money supply by periodically recalling coins and issuing new ones, generating revenue in the process. This level of monetary control was unmatched in most of Europe and shows how advanced Anglo-Saxon government was.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Towns and Villages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Most Anglo-Saxons lived in small villages of 100–300 people. Farming followed the <strong>open-field system</strong>, where land was divided into strips and shared among villagers. Each village typically had two or three large fields that were farmed in rotation, with one left fallow each year to recover its fertility. Villages were largely self-sufficient, producing their own food, clothing, and tools. Towns were fewer and smaller than villages, but they served as centres for markets, crafts, and church administration. Major towns such as London had populations of around 10,000–15,000, making them significant urban centres by medieval European standards.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Church was central to Anglo-Saxon life. It shaped education, morality, and law. Bishops and abbots sat on the Witan and advised the king. Monasteries were centres of learning, producing manuscripts and preserving knowledge. The Church owned around a quarter of all land in England and collected <strong>tithes</strong> (a tenth of each person’s produce) to fund its work. Parish churches were the heart of village life — everyone was expected to attend, and the Church marked every stage of life from baptism to burial.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Archbishops of Canterbury and York were the most powerful churchmen. By 1060, the Church in England had some practices that differed from those in Rome, such as married clergy and the buying and selling of Church positions (<strong>simony</strong>). These issues would become significant after the Norman Conquest.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Anglo-Saxon England was governed through a system of earls, thegns, and local courts, with the Witan advising the king on major decisions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The economy was agricultural, based on the open-field system, with towns serving as centres of trade and craft. Silver pennies were the standard currency.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Church was deeply embedded in government, education, and daily life, owning around a quarter of all land and collecting tithes from the population.</li></ul>
