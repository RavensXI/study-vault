--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Lanfranc reformed the English Church and why the Domesday Book transformed Norman government.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Church’s Role in Norman England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Church was essential to William’s rule. It provided educated administrators, moral authority, and a network of parish churches that reached every community in England. Bishops and abbots held land as <dfn class="term" data-def="The most powerful Norman lords who held land directly from the king, including barons and bishops, owing military service and loyalty in return.">tenants-in-chief</dfn> under the feudal system, meaning they owed the king military service just like secular lords. The Church legitimised William’s rule — Pope Alexander II had blessed the invasion and sent a papal banner.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">However, the Anglo-Saxon Church had problems by Norman standards. Some practices differed from continental norms, and the most prominent example of this was Archbishop <dfn class="term" data-def="The controversial Archbishop of Canterbury who held the position irregularly and was seen by the Normans as illegitimate, eventually deposed in 1070.">Stigand</dfn>.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Significance of Stigand</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Stigand had been Archbishop of Canterbury since 1052, but his position was controversial. He held two bishoprics at once (Canterbury and Winchester), which was against Church rules (<strong>pluralism</strong>). He had received his pallium (the symbol of papal authority) from an antipope — a pope later declared illegitimate. The papacy considered Stigand’s appointment invalid.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why William replaced English Church leaders. Name the key figure he removed first.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Stigand’s irregular position was one reason Pope Alexander II supported William’s invasion. William used papal backing to present the Conquest as a religious mission to reform the corrupt English Church. Stigand was finally deposed in 1070 and replaced by the Norman <dfn class="term" data-def="Italian-born Norman monk who became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1070 and reformed the English Church, imposing stricter discipline and Norman practices.">Lanfranc</dfn>.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Lanfranc’s Reforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Lanfranc was an Italian-born scholar and monk who had been abbot of William’s monastery at Caen in Normandy. As Archbishop of Canterbury from 1070, he carried out sweeping reforms of the English Church:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Key Church Reforms Under Lanfranc</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Replacing English churchmen:</strong> By 1087, only one English bishop (Wulfstan of Worcester) remained. All other bishops and most abbots were Norman. This was <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of replacing English churchmen, customs, and practices with Norman ones across the English Church.">Normanisation</dfn> of the Church in practice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Ending corruption:</strong> Lanfranc attacked <strong>simony</strong> (buying and selling Church positions) and <strong>pluralism</strong> (holding multiple positions). He insisted on clerical <strong>celibacy</strong> (priests should not marry), though enforcement was patchy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Reorganisation:</strong> Lanfranc established a clear hierarchy with Canterbury as the supreme archbishopric above York. He moved some bishoprics from rural villages to towns (e.g. from Dorchester to Lincoln) and introduced regular Church councils to standardise practice. He also rebuilt Canterbury Cathedral and many other churches in the grander Romanesque style.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Church courts:</strong> Lanfranc separated Church courts from secular courts. Previously, bishops had sat in shire courts alongside the sheriff. Now, Church courts handled moral and religious offences (like adultery and oath-breaking) separately from royal courts. This gave the Church greater independence in its own sphere but also extended its authority.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Lanfranc was also a loyal political ally of William. He served as regent when William was in Normandy and helped suppress the revolt of the earls in 1075, coordinating the military response that crushed the rebellion before William even returned. He was both a churchman and a political operator — his loyalty to William was crucial to the stability of Norman England. The partnership between William and Lanfranc illustrates how the Normans used the Church as a tool of government: bishops held land as tenants-in-chief, owed military service, and served in administrative roles alongside their religious duties.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Domesday Survey and Domesday Book</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In 1085, William ordered a comprehensive survey of all land and resources in England. The immediate trigger may have been the threat of a Danish invasion — William needed to know exactly what resources he could call upon. Commissioners were sent to every shire to record who owned each piece of land, what it was worth, how many people lived there, and what livestock and equipment they had. To ensure accuracy, a second set of commissioners checked the first group’s findings. The survey covered almost all of England (though some northern areas and major cities like London were omitted). The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle recorded that “not even one ox, one cow, or one pig escaped notice” — reflecting both the thoroughness of the survey and the resentment it caused.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="From memory, state the two purposes of the Domesday Book and explain why each mattered to William.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The resulting <dfn class="term" data-def="A comprehensive survey of English land and resources completed in 1086, recording ownership, values, and resources for almost every estate in England.">Domesday Book</dfn> (completed 1086) recorded three snapshots: who held the land in 1066 (under Edward the Confessor), who held it when William granted it, and who held it in 1086. This allowed William to settle disputes, prevent fraud, and see exactly how much the Conquest had changed England’s landownership.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">

<span>Significance of the Domesday Book</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>For government:</strong> The Domesday Book gave William detailed knowledge of his kingdom’s resources. No medieval ruler had ever possessed such comprehensive information. It confirmed feudal landholding — once recorded in Domesday, ownership was legally fixed. It settled disputes over who owned what and prevented lords from claiming more than they were entitled to.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>For finance:</strong> The survey revealed exactly what each estate was worth, allowing William to set fair (and maximum) tax levels. It prevented lords from hiding wealth or underreporting their resources. It confirmed that William could extract more revenue from England than any Anglo-Saxon king had managed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>For historians:</strong> The Domesday Book is the most detailed record of any medieval society in Europe. It reveals the extent of the Norman land transfer (almost total), the impact of the Harrying of the North (“waste” entries), and the structure of English society twenty years after the Conquest.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Lanfranc reformed the English Church by replacing English bishops with Normans, attacking simony and pluralism, separating Church and secular courts, and asserting Canterbury’s supremacy over York.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Stigand’s irregular position gave William a justification for replacing the English Church leadership — Church reform served both religious and political purposes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Domesday Book (1086) gave William unprecedented knowledge of England’s land, wealth, and people — it was a tool of taxation, political control, and the definitive record of the Norman Conquest’s impact.</li></ul>
