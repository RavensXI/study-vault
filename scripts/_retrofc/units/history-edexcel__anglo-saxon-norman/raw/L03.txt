--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Harold defeated Hardrada at Stamford Bridge but lost to William at Hastings weeks later.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Harold’s Coronation and Early Reign</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Harold Godwinson was crowned king on 6 January 1066 — the day after Edward the Confessor’s death. The speed of the coronation was deliberate: Harold needed to establish his authority before rivals could challenge him. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A council of important nobles and churchmen who advised the king and helped choose the next monarch.">Witan</dfn> had approved Harold’s claim, and Archbishop Stigand performed the ceremony at Westminster Abbey.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Harold spent the spring and summer of 1066 preparing for invasion. He positioned his <dfn class="term" data-def="A part-time fighting force of free men called up by their local lord to defend the kingdom in times of danger.">fyrd</dfn> (part-time peasant army) along the south coast to watch for William’s fleet, while his fleet patrolled the English Channel. By September, supplies were running low and the fyrd’s service period had expired, so Harold was forced to disband them — just as the threats materialised.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Battle of Gate Fulford — 20 September 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">While Harold watched the south, <dfn class="term" data-def="King of Norway who invaded northern England in 1066, allied with Tostig, claiming the English throne.">Harald Hardrada</dfn> and Tostig landed in northern England with around 300 ships and 8,000–10,000 warriors. Earls Edwin and Morcar marched to meet them at <strong>Gate Fulford</strong>, near York, on 20 September. The English earls were decisively defeated. This victory gave Hardrada control of York and forced him to wait at Stamford Bridge for hostages and supplies.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain what happened at Gate Fulford and why it mattered for Hastings.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Battle of Gate Fulford (20 September 1066) was a significant Norman-era battle because the defeat of Edwin and Morcar’s forces meant the northern English army was destroyed. Harold had to march north with his own troops to deal with Hardrada, leaving the south coast undefended.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Battle of Stamford Bridge — 25 September 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Harold reacted with remarkable speed. He marched his <dfn class="term" data-def="The king's professional, full-time household troops, well-armed and trained warriors who formed the core of the Anglo-Saxon army.">housecarls</dfn> nearly 190 miles from London to Yorkshire in just four days, gathering local forces as he went. On 25 September, he caught Hardrada’s army completely by surprise at Stamford Bridge. The Norwegians were relaxing without their armour on a warm day, expecting no resistance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The battle was fierce and decisive. Both Harald Hardrada and Tostig were killed. The Norwegian army was shattered — of the roughly 300 ships that arrived, only around 24 were needed to carry the survivors home. It was a stunning victory for Harold. However, the battle cost Harold many experienced fighters, and his army was exhausted from the forced march.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">Just two days after Stamford Bridge, on 27 September, the wind changed and William’s fleet set sail from Normandy. William landed at Pevensey on 28 September with around 7,000 troops, including cavalry, archers, and infantry. Harold received the news and immediately turned his exhausted army around, marching roughly 250 miles south in about 10 days. He arrived near Hastings on 13 October, choosing to fight the next morning on a ridge at Senlac Hill.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Battle of Hastings — 14 October 1066</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Harold positioned his army on the high ground of Senlac Hill, forming a tight <dfn class="term" data-def="A defensive formation where soldiers stood shoulder-to-shoulder with overlapping shields, creating a protective wall.">shield wall</dfn>. His force of around 7,000–8,000 troops was mostly infantry: housecarls with axes and swords at the front, and fyrd men behind. Harold had no archers and no cavalry to speak of.</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n13">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Morning</div>
<h4>Norman attacks repelled</h4><p>William sent his archers, then infantry, then cavalry up the hill. The shield wall held firm. Norman infantry struggled against the steep slope and the devastating two-handed axes of the housecarls.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Midday</div>
<h4>Rumour of William’s death</h4><p>A rumour spread that William had been killed. He lifted his helmet to show his face and rallied his troops. The Bretons on the left flank broke and fled, and some English fyrd broke ranks to chase them down the hill.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Afternoon</div>
<h4>Feigned retreats</h4><p>William used feigned retreats to lure English troops out of their strong defensive position. Each time the English broke ranks to pursue, Norman cavalry turned and cut them down.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Evening</div>
<h4>Harold’s death</h4><p>As the shield wall thinned, a final combined assault of archers (firing high) and cavalry broke through. Harold was killed — tradition says by an arrow to the eye, though this is debated. The English army fled.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="List four reasons why William won at Hastings from memory, covering different types of factor.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>William won the Battle of Hastings because of a combination of factors: his use of combined arms (archers, infantry, cavalry), the effectiveness of feigned retreats, Harold’s tired and depleted army, the lack of English cavalry and archers, and Harold’s death on the battlefield which destroyed English morale.</p>
</div>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>William’s leadership:</strong> He was a skilled commander who adapted his tactics during the battle, using feigned retreats and rallying his troops when they thought he was dead. <strong>Norman troops and tactics:</strong> William used a combined force of archers, infantry, and cavalry, giving him flexibility that Harold lacked. <strong>Harold’s weaknesses:</strong> Harold’s army was exhausted from Stamford Bridge and the forced marches. He had no archers or cavalry. Some of his best housecarls had been killed or wounded in the north. <strong>Luck and timing:</strong> The wind changed at the worst possible moment for Harold. If William had sailed earlier, Harold would have had his full fyrd waiting. Harold’s death ended the battle — without their king, the English had no reason to fight on.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Harold won a stunning victory at Stamford Bridge (25 September 1066), destroying Hardrada’s army — but it left his forces exhausted and depleted before facing William.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">William won at Hastings (14 October 1066) through combined arms, feigned retreats, Harold’s tiredness, and Harold’s death — no single factor explains the outcome.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Battle of Hastings changed English history forever — it ended Anglo-Saxon rule and began the Norman Conquest.</li></ul>
