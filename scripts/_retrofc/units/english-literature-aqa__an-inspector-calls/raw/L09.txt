--- DESCRIPTION ---
The play’s three central themes and how Priestley uses them to argue for a more just society.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Responsibility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The state of being morally accountable for one’s actions and their consequences for others.">Responsibility</dfn> is the play’s most important theme. Priestley argues that every person has a duty to care for others, not just themselves. The Inspector’s message — “We are responsible for each other” — is the play’s thesis statement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Each character’s treatment of Eva represents a different failure of responsibility. Birling fails as an employer. Sheila fails as a consumer. Gerald fails as a lover. Mrs Birling fails as a charity worker. Eric fails as a man. Together, they form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A chain of connected events where each person’s action contributes to the final outcome.">chain of responsibility</dfn> — no single person killed Eva, but collectively they destroyed her.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain how Priestley builds responsibility through structure, naming the chain effect and two specific minor actions mentioned.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Priestley structures the play so that responsibility is <strong>cumulative</strong>. Each revelation adds another link in the chain. The audience sees how seemingly minor actions — sacking someone, complaining about a shop assistant — can have devastating consequences when combined.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Individual vs Collective Responsibility</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Birling believes in <dfn class="term" data-def="The view that each person is accountable only for their own actions and should look after themselves.">individual responsibility</dfn>: “a man has to look after himself.” The Inspector believes in <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that all members of a society share an obligation to care for one another’s wellbeing.">collective responsibility</dfn>: “we are members of one body.” The play is structured as a battle between these two philosophies, and Priestley ensures the Inspector’s view wins.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Theme 2: Social Class</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Divisions in society based on economic status, occupation and social background, determining access to power and resources.">Class</dfn> determines everything in the play. The Birlings’ wealth and status give them power over Eva. Birling can sack her, Sheila can have her fired, Gerald can keep her as a mistress, Mrs Birling can refuse her charity, and Eric can exploit her — all because of the class difference between them.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Class and Language</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mrs Birling’s language reveals her class prejudice most clearly. She calls Eva “a girl of that sort,” “impertinent” and “giving herself ridiculous airs.” This language reduces Eva from a person to a <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed, oversimplified idea about a group of people based on their class, gender, race or other characteristic.">stereotype</dfn> — a “type” rather than an individual. Priestley uses Mrs Birling’s language to expose how class prejudice dehumanises its victims.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and analyze Eva Smith's symbolic name, explaining what Eve and Smith represent, plus why she never appears on stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eva Smith represents the entire working class. Her name (“Eve” + “Smith”) suggests she is an everywoman. She is never seen on stage — she exists only through the accounts of others, just as the working class existed only as servants, workers and statistics in the eyes of the Edwardian upper class.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Theme 3: Gender</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The socially constructed roles, behaviours and expectations assigned to men and women by society.">Gender</dfn> intersects with class throughout the play. Eva is doubly disadvantaged — she is both working-class and female. As a woman in 1912, she cannot vote, has no legal protection against exploitative employers, and depends on men for economic survival.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Gerald’s affair with Eva exposes the sexual double standard of Edwardian society. A wealthy man can keep a mistress and return to his respectable life afterwards; the woman is left with nothing. Eric’s treatment of Eva is even more damaging — he forced himself on her when drunk, a clear abuse of power.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sheila and Gender</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Sheila’s transformation also has a gender dimension. At the start, she is a possession — engaged to Gerald as part of a business alliance between two families. By the end, she is an independent moral agent who refuses to accept the ring back on the old terms. Priestley uses Sheila to show that <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a person develops the ability to think and act independently, free from oppressive social expectations.">emancipation</dfn> requires not just legal rights but a fundamental change in attitudes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How the Themes Connect</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Responsibility, class and gender are interlinked. The Birlings refuse responsibility <em>because</em> of class prejudice — they do not see Eva as fully human. Gender compounds this: Eva is exploited sexually as well as economically. Priestley’s argument is that a just society must address all three — creating shared responsibility, dismantling class barriers, and achieving gender equality.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Responsibility is cumulative:</strong> Each character contributes one link in the chain that destroys Eva. No single person is wholly to blame, but all share responsibility.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Class determines power:</strong> The Birlings can exploit Eva because their wealth and status give them power she lacks. Class prejudice dehumanises its victims.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Gender doubles the disadvantage:</strong> Eva is exploited both as a worker and as a woman. Priestley argues that social justice requires addressing class and gender inequality together.</li></ul>
