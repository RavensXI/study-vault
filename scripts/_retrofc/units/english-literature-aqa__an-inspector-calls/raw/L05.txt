<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Eric’s Confession</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 3 opens with Eric returning to the room, now aware that the family knows his secret. He confesses that he met Eva/Daisy at the Palace bar, that he had been drinking heavily, and that he forced his way into her lodgings. He got her pregnant.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eric stole money from his father’s office to support Eva — roughly fifty pounds. Eva discovered the money was stolen and refused to accept any more, showing greater moral integrity than Eric himself. When Eric learns that his own mother refused Eva’s plea for help, he turns on her: “You killed her — and the child she’d have had too — my child — your own <dfn class="term" data-def="A child of one’s son or daughter.">grandchild</dfn>.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Eric's three serious actions towards Eva: what he did to her sexually, what happened as a result, and how he tried to res...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eric’s treatment of Eva is arguably the most serious. He used her sexually, got her pregnant, and tried to buy her off with stolen money. Yet like Sheila, Eric shows genuine remorse — he accepts responsibility and is horrified by his family’s behaviour.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n5">Eric’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition characterised by dependence on alcohol, often causing impaired judgement and destructive behaviour.">alcoholism</dfn> is hinted at throughout the play but ignored by his parents. Gerald notes early on that Eric is “squiffy” (drunk). When the truth finally emerges, Mr and Mrs Birling are shocked, revealing how little they know about their own son. Priestley uses Eric’s drinking to show that the Birling family’s obsession with appearances has blinded them to real problems within their own household.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Inspector’s Final Speech</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Before leaving, the Inspector delivers the play’s most famous speech. It is Priestley’s direct message to the audience:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">“One Eva Smith has gone — but there are millions and millions and millions of Eva Smiths and John Smiths still left with us, with their lives, their hopes and fears, their suffering and chance of happiness, all intertwined with our lives and what we think and say and do. We don’t live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the biblical book and chapter that contains the phrase 'members of one body' that the Inspector echoes in his final speech.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Inspector’s speech uses the phrase “members of one body.” This echoes the New Testament (1 Corinthians 12:27: “Now you are the body of Christ, and each one of you is a part of it”). By using religious language, Priestley gives his socialist message a moral and spiritual authority that transcends politics.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The speech uses several key techniques. <strong>Repetition</strong> (“millions and millions and millions”) emphasises the scale of suffering. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of three elements in sequence for rhetorical effect and emphasis.">tricolon</dfn> “their lives, their hopes and fears, their suffering and chance of happiness” humanises the working class. The direct address (“We are responsible for each other”) includes the audience in the message.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The speech ends with a warning: if people do not learn the lesson of <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that all members of a community share a moral obligation to care for one another.">collective responsibility</dfn>, they will be taught it in “fire and blood and anguish.” The 1945 audience would have heard this as a direct reference to the two world wars they had just endured.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Eric vs His Parents</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">After the Inspector leaves, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A difference in attitudes and values between older and younger people within a family or society.">generational divide</dfn> becomes even sharper. Eric, like Sheila, insists that it does not matter whether the Inspector was real: “he’s been here and we all know what we did to her — and I say the girl’s dead.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Eric moves from a troubled, heavy-drinking young man to someone who genuinely understands the consequences of his actions. His accusation against his mother (“You killed her”) shows he has crossed a line — he is willing to confront his own family. Like Sheila, Eric represents Priestley’s hope that the younger generation can learn from the mistakes of the past.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Eric shows genuine remorse:</strong> Like Sheila, he accepts responsibility and confronts his parents. His accusation against Mrs Birling is the play’s most emotionally raw moment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>The Inspector’s speech is Priestley’s manifesto:</strong> “We are members of one body” is a direct call for collective responsibility, echoing Christian teaching and socialist politics.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>“Fire and blood and anguish” warns of consequences:</strong> The 1945 audience knew this prophecy had come true in two world wars.</li></ul>
