--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Priestley establishes the Birling family’s world before the Inspector shatters their complacency.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Opening: A Celebration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The play opens with the Birling family celebrating <strong>Sheila’s engagement</strong> to Gerald Croft. The stage directions describe a “fairly large suburban house” with “good solid furniture” but “not cosy and homelike.” This detail is significant — the Birlings have wealth but lack genuine warmth. The room is dressed to impress, not to comfort.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The lighting is described as “pink and intimate” before the Inspector arrives, then becomes “brighter and harder.” This <dfn class="term" data-def="Instructions in a play that describe the setting, lighting, sound effects and characters’ actions.">stage direction</dfn> is a metaphor for the Inspector’s role: he brings harsh light to the family’s comfortable illusions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the exact lighting change described in stage directions and what it symbolises about truth versus self-deception.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The lighting change from “pink and intimate” to “brighter and harder” symbolises the shift from comfortable self-deception to uncomfortable truth. This is one of the most frequently examined stage directions in the play.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Birling’s Speeches</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Before the Inspector arrives, Arthur Birling makes a series of confident speeches. He predicts that war is impossible: “there isn’t a chance of war.” He dismisses community: “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” He boasts about the Titanic being “absolutely unsinkable.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Every prediction is wrong. Priestley uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where the audience knows the truth that the characters do not, creating tension, humour or pathos.">dramatic irony</dfn> to demolish Birling’s authority before the Inspector even arrives. If Birling is wrong about the Titanic and the war, the audience knows he is equally wrong about the importance of social responsibility.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Birling represents <dfn class="term" data-def="An economic system based on private ownership and profit, where individuals compete for wealth.">capitalism</dfn> at its most selfish. His speech about “looking after yourself” is a direct rejection of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that members of a society have a duty to help and protect one another.">collective responsibility</dfn> that Priestley championed. By making Birling so obviously wrong about everything, Priestley ensures the audience rejects his philosophy too.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Inspector Arrives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Inspector Goole arrives just as Birling is lecturing Gerald and Eric about self-reliance. His entrance interrupts Birling mid-speech — literally cutting off his capitalist philosophy. The timing is deliberate: Priestley positions the Inspector as the direct answer to Birling’s selfishness.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Inspector announces that a young woman — <strong>Eva Smith</strong> — has died in the infirmary after swallowing disinfectant. He shows a photograph to Birling (but not to the others). Birling admits that Eva Smith worked at his factory and that he sacked her for leading a <dfn class="term" data-def="A refusal to work organised by employees to protest against pay or conditions.">strike</dfn> demanding higher wages.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State Eva Smith's exact wages before and after her pay rise request, and quote Birling's phrase describing her.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling sacked Eva Smith because she asked for a pay rise from twenty-two shillings and sixpence to twenty-five shillings a week. He saw this as “one of those cranks” causing trouble. Priestley uses this to show how the capitalist class treated workers as disposable.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">“Eva” echoes Eve — the first woman in the Bible. “Smith” is the most common English surname. Together, they suggest she represents <strong>all working-class women</strong>. She is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A general name or concept representing a wider group or idea rather than a specific individual.">everywoman</dfn> figure: not just one victim, but every person exploited by the system the Birlings represent.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Birling shows no remorse for sacking Eva. He calls it a “perfectly straightforward case” and says he “can’t accept any responsibility.” He tries to intimidate the Inspector by mentioning his connections: the Chief Constable is “a friend of mine.” This reveals Birling’s belief that power and status should place him above moral scrutiny.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>The lighting change is symbolic:</strong> “Pink and intimate” to “brighter and harder” represents the shift from comfortable illusion to harsh truth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Birling is undermined by dramatic irony:</strong> His wrong predictions about the Titanic and war destroy his credibility before the Inspector even arrives.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Eva Smith is an everywoman:</strong> Her name suggests she represents all exploited working-class people, not just one individual.</li></ul>
