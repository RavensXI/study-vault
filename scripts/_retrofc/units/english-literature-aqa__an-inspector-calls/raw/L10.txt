--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Priestley uses stage directions, dramatic irony, the unities and the well-made play to convey his message.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Language: How Characters Speak</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Priestley uses language to reveal character and reinforce themes. Each character’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The level of formality or style of language a person uses, revealing their social position and attitudes.">register</dfn> tells us about their values.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Birling</strong> speaks in long, pompous speeches. He uses business language (“lower costs,” “higher prices”) even when discussing human lives. His <dfn class="term" data-def="A long speech by one person that dominates a conversation, often revealing their self-importance.">monologues</dfn> reveal a man who talks <em>at</em> people, not <em>with</em> them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Mrs Birling</strong> speaks in clipped, dismissive phrases: “a girl of that sort,” “ridiculous airs.” Her language categorises people by class, reducing individuals to types.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>The Inspector</strong> speaks plainly and directly. He uses short, simple sentences that cut through the Birlings’ evasions: “I’m not here to talk about your knighthood.” His language has moral authority that the Birlings’ more elaborate speech lacks.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the specific exclamation Sheila uses early in the play and quote her exact words from the end showing growth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila’s language changes across the play. Early on, she uses exclamations (“Oh — how horrible!”) that suggest emotional but shallow reactions. By the end, she speaks in measured, thoughtful sentences: “You began to learn something. And now you’ve stopped.” Her language tracks her moral growth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Stage Directions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Priestley’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Written instructions in a script that describe the physical aspects of production, including set design, lighting and characters’ movements.">stage directions</dfn> are as important as the dialogue. The most significant include:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Stage Directions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Lighting:</strong> “Pink and intimate” before the Inspector; “brighter and harder” after. Symbolises the shift from comfortable illusion to harsh truth.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Setting:</strong> “Not cosy and homelike” — the Birlings have wealth but not warmth. The single room creates a sense of <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of being trapped or confined, often used in drama to intensify tension.">claustrophobia</dfn>; the characters cannot escape the Inspector’s questioning.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>The Inspector’s entrance:</strong> He arrives just as Birling is lecturing about self-reliance, literally interrupting the capitalist philosophy. His physical presence is described as creating “an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness” — he is an immovable moral force.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Structure: The Three Unities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The play observes the classical <dfn class="term" data-def="Three principles of drama from ancient Greece: unity of time (one day), place (one location) and action (one main plot).">three unities</dfn> of drama:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Unity of time:</strong> The entire play takes place in a single evening.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Unity of place:</strong> Everything happens in the Birlings’ dining room.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Unity of action:</strong> There is one continuous investigation with no subplots.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why the Unities Matter</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The unities create relentless <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of sustained emotional excitement or anxiety about what will happen next.">tension</dfn>. There are no scene changes, no time jumps, no escape. The characters are trapped in the room with the Inspector, just as they are trapped by their own guilt. The audience experiences the investigation in real time, sharing the characters’ growing discomfort.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Dramatic Devices</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Dramatic Irony</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension, irony or pathos.">Dramatic irony</dfn> operates throughout the play. Birling’s predictions (Titanic, war) are wrong. Mrs Birling condemns her own son. The 1945 audience knows that the Edwardian confidence the Birlings display will be shattered by two world wars. Priestley uses dramatic irony to give the audience moral superiority over the characters, ensuring they reject Birling’s worldview.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what structural device involves a phone call and what type of story Priestley transforms the play from and into.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <strong>cyclical structure</strong> — the phone call at the end repeating the Inspector’s arrival — is Priestley’s most powerful structural device. It transforms the play from a detective story into a moral lesson: those who refuse to learn are condemned to repeat their mistakes.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Well-Made Play and Its Subversion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A style of play with a tightly constructed plot building towards a climactic revelation, popular in the 19th and early 20th centuries.">well-made play</dfn> follows a predictable structure: a secret is revealed, complications arise, and a neat resolution follows. Priestley uses this form but <strong>subverts</strong> it. The “resolution” (the Inspector is fake, no girl has died) is immediately undermined by the phone call. There is no neat ending — only an unsettling cycle.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">This subversion serves Priestley’s purpose perfectly. A neat resolution would let the audience feel comfortable. The ambiguous, cyclical ending forces them to keep thinking about the play’s questions long after the curtain falls.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Language reveals character:</strong> Birling’s pomposity, Mrs Birling’s class prejudice and the Inspector’s moral clarity are all conveyed through how they speak.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>The three unities create claustrophobia:</strong> One room, one evening, one investigation — the characters (and audience) cannot escape the truth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>The cyclical ending subverts the well-made play:</strong> Instead of a neat resolution, the phone call reopens everything, forcing the audience to keep questioning.</li></ul>
