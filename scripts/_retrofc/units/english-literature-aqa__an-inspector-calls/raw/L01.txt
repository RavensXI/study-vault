--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why Priestley set his play in 1912 but wrote it in 1945, and how both dates shape its meaning.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Key Dates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>An Inspector Calls</em> was written in <strong>1945</strong> but set in <strong>1912</strong>. Understanding both dates is essential to understanding the play. Priestley deliberately chose a setting two years before the First World War so that the audience — who had just lived through the Second World War — would recognise the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique where the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension or humour.">dramatic irony</dfn> in the characters’ confident predictions about the future.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What two catastrophically wrong predictions does Mr Birling make about war and the Titanic?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mr Birling predicts that war is impossible and that the Titanic is “unsinkable.” The 1945 audience knew both predictions were catastrophically wrong. Priestley uses this dramatic irony to undermine everything Birling represents: capitalism, self-interest and complacency.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">1912: The Edwardian World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In 1912, Britain was divided by rigid <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of social organisation based on divisions of wealth, occupation and inherited status.">class</dfn> boundaries. The wealthy upper and middle classes enjoyed enormous privilege, while the working class — especially women — had few rights and little protection. There was no welfare state, no National Health Service and no unemployment benefit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Women could not vote. Working-class women like Eva Smith had almost no power — they depended on employers for survival and had no legal recourse if they were sacked or exploited. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The campaign for women’s right to vote, active in Britain from the late 19th century until 1928.">suffragette movement</dfn> was growing but had not yet achieved change.</p>

Arthur Birling is a factory owner and former mayor. His wife Sybil comes from a higher social class and looks down on her husband’s origins. Gerald Croft’s family are old money — landed aristocracy. This social hierarchy matters because it determines how each character treats Eva Smith.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">1945: The Post-War World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">By 1945, Britain had endured two devastating world wars. Millions had died. The old class certainties had been shattered. People across all classes had sacrificed together, and there was a powerful mood for social change.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In the <strong>1945 general election</strong>, the Labour Party won a landslide victory over Winston Churchill’s Conservatives. The new government created the <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government programmes providing social protection including healthcare, housing and unemployment support.">welfare state</dfn>: the NHS, national insurance, council housing and free secondary education. Priestley’s play directly supported this vision of a more equal, caring society.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What political ideology did Priestley follow and what phrase does the Inspector use about 'one body'?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Priestley was a committed socialist. He believed that people had a <strong>collective responsibility</strong> to look after one another. The Inspector is his mouthpiece — his final speech (“We are members of one body”) directly echoes Priestley’s own political beliefs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">J.B. Priestley’s Life and Beliefs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">John Boynton Priestley was born in <strong>Bradford</strong> in 1894 into a middle-class family. He fought in the First World War and was badly injured. This experience shaped his political views: he saw how working-class soldiers sacrificed their lives while the wealthy profited from the war.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">During the Second World War, Priestley made hugely popular radio broadcasts (“Postscripts”) calling for social justice and equality. He was so influential that the government took him off the air, fearing he was too left-wing. Priestley was a founder of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A political philosophy advocating collective ownership, workers’ rights and the redistribution of wealth for a more equal society.">socialist</dfn> Common Wealth Party and campaigned for nuclear disarmament.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Priestley and the “Time Plays”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Priestley was fascinated by theories of time. He wrote several “time plays” exploring the idea that past, present and future are connected. In <em>An Inspector Calls</em>, the Inspector seems to exist outside normal time — he knows about events before they happen and arrives before Eva Smith has actually died. This supernatural element reinforces Priestley’s message: if we do not learn from the past, we are doomed to repeat it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Well-Made Play</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><em>An Inspector Calls</em> follows the conventions of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of play with a tightly constructed plot, logical cause-and-effect, and a dramatic revelation or twist.">well-made play</dfn>: it takes place in a single room on a single evening, building towards a climactic revelation. But Priestley subverts the form by leaving the ending ambiguous — the final phone call restarting the cycle of investigation suggests the characters (and society) have not learned their lesson.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Set in 1912, written in 1945:</strong> The time gap creates dramatic irony. The audience knows the characters’ confident predictions are wrong.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Priestley was a socialist:</strong> He used the play to argue for collective responsibility and against the selfish individualism of the Edwardian upper classes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>The 1945 audience was ready for change:</strong> Having survived two wars, they elected a Labour government that built the welfare state. The play supported this political moment.</li></ul>
