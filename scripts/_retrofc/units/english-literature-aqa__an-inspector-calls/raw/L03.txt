<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sheila and Milwards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Inspector reveals that after being sacked by Birling, Eva Smith found work at <strong>Milwards</strong>, a fashionable department store. She was happy there until <dfn class="term" data-def="A character in the play who undergoes significant moral change, representing hope for the younger generation.">Sheila Birling</dfn> had her sacked out of jealousy and spite.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Sheila admits that she complained about Eva because the girl had held up a dress against herself and smiled — a smile Sheila interpreted as mocking. Sheila used her family’s status as valued customers to pressure the manager into firing Eva. It was an act of <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of resentment or spite triggered by someone else’s perceived advantages.">jealousy</dfn>, driven by class privilege.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Sheila's exact quote about feeling bad that separates her from her parents who never accept responsibility.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila is the first character to show genuine <strong>remorse</strong>. She immediately recognises her cruelty: “I felt rotten about it at the time and now I feel a lot worse.” This separates her from her parents, who never truly accept responsibility.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Sheila’s response to the Inspector marks the beginning of her <dfn class="term" data-def="A fundamental change in a character’s understanding, values or behaviour over the course of a narrative.">character arc</dfn>. She starts the play as a privileged, somewhat shallow young woman (“Oh — how horrible!”). But she quickly moves beyond guilt to genuine understanding. She begins to challenge her parents, question Gerald, and accept that social responsibility extends beyond her own comfort.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Priestley uses Sheila to represent the <strong>younger generation</strong> — capable of learning, changing and building a better society. She is the character most aligned with Priestley’s own values by the end of the play.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Gerald and Daisy Renton</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Inspector reveals that Eva changed her name to <strong>Daisy Renton</strong> after leaving Milwards. At the mention of this name, Gerald reacts visibly — Sheila notices and realises he knew her.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Gerald eventually confesses. He met Daisy at the Palace Variety Theatre bar, where she was being harassed by Alderman Meggarty (a local councillor). Gerald rescued her, gave her money and set her up in a friend’s flat. They began an <dfn class="term" data-def="A romantic or sexual relationship outside of marriage, often conducted in secret.">affair</dfn> that lasted several months.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Was Gerald a ‘Hero’?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Gerald’s account presents him as a rescuer — he saved Daisy from Meggarty and gave her security. But Priestley complicates this. Gerald had the power in the relationship: he was wealthy, male and upper-class. Daisy was homeless, desperate and entirely dependent on him. When Gerald ended the affair, he gave her money and ended the affair so he could focus on his business commitments</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Gerald’s behaviour exposes the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imbalance of control or influence between individuals or groups, often determined by class, gender or wealth.">power imbalance</dfn> between men and women in Edwardian society. Even his “kindness” was a form of control. Daisy had no real choice — she was a kept woman dependent on Gerald’s goodwill.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what Sheila does with Gerald's engagement ring after his confession and why this rejects Edwardian expectations.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila gives Gerald back his engagement ring after hearing his confession. This is symbolically important — it shows Sheila is no longer willing to accept comfortable lies. She chooses truth over the security of marriage, rejecting the Edwardian expectation that women should tolerate men’s behaviour.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Sheila as the Inspector’s Ally</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">As the investigation progresses, Sheila increasingly sides with the Inspector. She warns Gerald not to try to hide anything: “he knows. And I hate to think how much he knows that we don’t know yet.” She becomes a <dfn class="term" data-def="A second investigator or moral conscience who reinforces the Inspector’s authority within the family.">proxy</dfn> for the Inspector within the family, pressing others to confess.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Generational Divide</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A clear divide emerges between the older Birlings (Arthur and Sybil) and the younger generation (Sheila and Eric). The older characters refuse to change; the younger ones are transformed by the Inspector’s visit. Priestley uses this divide to suggest that <strong>social change will come from the young</strong>, who are still capable of empathy and moral growth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Sheila shows genuine remorse:</strong> She is the first character to accept responsibility, and her transformation represents Priestley’s hope for the younger generation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Gerald’s rescue was also exploitation:</strong> The power imbalance between a wealthy man and a desperate working-class woman complicates his apparently kind actions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>The engagement ring symbolises choice:</strong> By returning it, Sheila rejects comfortable lies and chooses honesty over social expectation.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When writing about Sheila, always track her <strong>transformation</strong> across the play. Show how she moves from shallow guilt (“I felt rotten”) to genuine moral understanding. For Gerald, discuss the <strong>power imbalance</strong> — his “rescue” of Daisy was also a form of exploitation. Examiners want nuanced readings, not simple hero/villain judgements.</p>
