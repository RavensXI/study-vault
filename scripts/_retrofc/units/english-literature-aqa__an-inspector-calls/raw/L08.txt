--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Priestley uses each character to represent different attitudes to guilt, class and social change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sheila Birling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sheila undergoes the most complete <dfn class="term" data-def="A fundamental change in a character’s attitudes, values or understanding across the course of a narrative.">transformation</dfn> in the play. She begins as a privileged, somewhat shallow young woman — delighted by her engagement ring, dismissive of her father’s boring speeches. But the Inspector’s visit transforms her into the play’s moral centre.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Sheila is the first to show genuine guilt (“I felt rotten about it”), the first to understand the Inspector’s power (“he knows”), and the one who most clearly grasps Priestley’s message: what they did was wrong regardless of consequences.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what Sheila's return of the engagement ring symbolises and how her choice differs from her parents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sheila’s return of the engagement ring symbolises her rejection of the comfortable, unquestioning life her parents represent. She chooses <strong>truth over security</strong> — the opposite of her parents, who choose security over truth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Eric Birling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Eric is a troubled young man with a drinking problem that his parents have refused to acknowledge. His treatment of Eva is the most <dfn class="term" data-def="Deeply personal and private; relating to intimate matters that are usually kept hidden.">intimate</dfn> and arguably the most damaging — he used her sexually and got her pregnant.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Yet Eric, like Sheila, shows genuine remorse. His accusation against his mother (“You killed her”) is the play’s most emotionally powerful moment. After the Inspector leaves, Eric refuses to celebrate: “he’s been here and we all know what we did.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Eric as a Product of His Environment</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Priestley suggests that Eric’s behaviour is partly caused by his upbringing. He has been raised in a household where money buys silence, appearances matter more than feelings, and difficult truths are ignored. His alcoholism is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An outward expression of a deeper, underlying problem.">symptom</dfn> of a family that prioritises image over genuine connection. Eric is not excused for his actions, but he is presented as a product of the very values Priestley criticises.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Gerald Croft</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Gerald occupies an ambiguous position. He genuinely cared for Daisy/Eva — his affair was driven by attraction and some compassion, not just exploitation. But his class and gender gave him all the power. When the relationship ended, Daisy was left with nothing while Gerald returned to his comfortable life.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Gerald’s Alignment</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">After the Inspector leaves, Gerald aligns with the <strong>older generation</strong>. He leads the investigation into the Inspector’s identity, telephones the infirmary, and tries to convince Sheila that everything can return to normal. By doing so, he reveals that his emotional response was temporary — ultimately, he prioritises social order over moral accountability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Priestley uses Gerald to show that <dfn class="term" data-def="A social class characterised by inherited wealth, land ownership and long-established family status.">aristocratic</dfn> privilege is just as resistant to change as middle-class capitalism. Gerald’s higher social status does not make him more moral — it simply gives him more power to avoid consequences.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Mrs Sybil Birling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Mrs Birling is the play’s most unyielding character. She represents the <dfn class="term" data-def="A system in which men hold primary power and women are excluded from positions of authority and influence.">patriarchal</dfn> upper class at its most rigid: she is cold, snobbish and completely lacking in empathy for anyone outside her social circle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">She refused Eva’s plea for charity help, calling her “impertinent” for using the name Birling. She insists the father of the child should be punished — unknowingly condemning her own son. She shows no remorse at any point in the play.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why Mrs Birling is unique among all characters and what Priestley's message is about generational change.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Birling is the only character who never accepts any responsibility. She represents the <strong>complete failure of the older generation</strong> to learn from their mistakes. Priestley uses her inflexibility to strengthen his argument that real change can only come from the young.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Mrs Birling and Gender</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Despite being a woman herself, Mrs Birling shows no solidarity with Eva. Her charity work is not driven by empathy but by social duty and status. She judges Eva by class, not gender. Priestley uses Mrs Birling to show that <dfn class="term" data-def="Prejudice or discrimination in favour of one’s own social class, even against members of one’s own gender.">class loyalty</dfn> can override gender solidarity — upper-class women can be just as oppressive towards working-class women as upper-class men.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Sheila is the play’s moral centre:</strong> Her transformation from shallow privilege to genuine understanding represents Priestley’s hope for social change.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Gerald aligns with the older generation:</strong> Despite his emotional response, he ultimately prioritises social order over moral accountability.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Mrs Birling never changes:</strong> Her absolute refusal to accept responsibility represents the entrenched attitudes Priestley believed must be overcome.</li></ul>
