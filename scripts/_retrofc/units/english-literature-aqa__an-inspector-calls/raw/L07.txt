--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Priestley uses the Inspector as his mouthpiece and Mr Birling as the embodiment of capitalist selfishness.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Inspector Goole</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Inspector is the play’s most mysterious character. He arrives uninvited, conducts an interrogation that dismantles the Birling family’s self-image, delivers a powerful moral speech, and disappears. His name — <dfn class="term" data-def="A word that sounds the same as another word but has a different meaning; here, Goole sounds like ghoul, a supernatural being.">Goole</dfn> — sounds like “ghoul,” hinting at something supernatural.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Inspector is Priestley’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who directly expresses the author’s views and beliefs within a literary work.">mouthpiece</dfn>. His views on collective responsibility, social justice and the consequences of selfishness are Priestley’s own. He functions not as a realistic police inspector but as a moral force — a figure of conscience who compels the characters to confront what they have done.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the Inspector's exact words about his investigation method and why he shows the photograph to only one person at a time.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Inspector controls the pace of the investigation. He shows the photograph to only one person at a time, preventing the characters from comparing stories. His method is deliberate — he says “one person and one line of inquiry at a time” — ensuring each character must face their guilt individually.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Inspector’s Language and Method</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">The Inspector speaks with <dfn class="term" data-def="Power and right to give orders, make decisions and enforce obedience.">authority</dfn> that transcends his supposed rank. He interrupts Birling’s capitalist speeches, dismisses Mrs Birling’s attempts to pull rank, and refuses to be intimidated by threats. His language is direct, simple and uncompromising — the opposite of the Birlings’ evasive, self-justifying talk.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">He uses graphic descriptions of Eva’s death (“burnt her inside out”) to shock the characters out of their complacency. This vivid, uncomfortable language forces them — and the audience — to confront the real human cost of their actions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Mr Arthur Birling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Birling is a “heavy-looking, rather portentous man” who is “rather provincial in his speech.” He is a prosperous factory owner, a former Lord Mayor, and hopes to receive a knighthood. He is defined by his <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive pride in one’s achievements, status or possessions, often accompanied by a desire for admiration.">self-importance</dfn> and his absolute belief in capitalism.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Birling’s Philosophy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Birling’s core belief is that “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” This is a direct rejection of collective responsibility. He sees workers as costs to be managed, not people to be cared for. He sacked Eva for asking for a pay rise of two and a half shillings a week — a tiny sum to him, survival to her.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Priestley makes Birling ridiculous as well as cruel. His wrong predictions (the Titanic, the impossibility of war) ensure the audience sees him as foolish, not just selfish. This is important: Priestley wants the audience to <em>reject</em> Birling’s philosophy, not just disagree with it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what Birling fears most after the Inspector leaves and what this reveals about his priorities compared to Eva's death.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling’s primary concern after the Inspector leaves is not Eva’s death but his <strong>knighthood</strong>. He fears a “public scandal.” This reveals that status matters more to him than human life — exactly the attitude Priestley wrote the play to condemn.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Inspector vs Birling: The Central Conflict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The play is structured as a confrontation between two worldviews. Birling represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that each person is responsible only for themselves and their own family, rejecting broader social obligations.">individualism</dfn>: self-interest, profit and personal advancement. The Inspector represents <dfn class="term" data-def="A political and moral philosophy emphasising shared responsibility, mutual aid and the common good.">collectivism</dfn>: shared responsibility, empathy and social justice.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Who Wins?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">On the surface, Birling appears to “win” — the Inspector is exposed as a fake. But the final phone call reverses this apparent victory. The Inspector’s message was true even if his identity was not. Priestley ensures that the audience sides with the Inspector, not Birling. The play’s structure is designed to make Birling’s triumph hollow and temporary.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>The Inspector is Priestley’s mouthpiece:</strong> His views on collective responsibility represent Priestley’s socialist beliefs. His supernatural qualities give him moral authority beyond the law.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Birling embodies selfish capitalism:</strong> His wrong predictions, sacking of Eva, and obsession with his knighthood make him both foolish and cruel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>The play is a battle between individualism and collectivism:</strong> The Inspector wins morally, even if Birling appears to win tactically.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">When writing about Birling, always connect his dramatic irony to Priestley’s <strong>purpose</strong>. Birling is not just wrong about the Titanic — Priestley makes him wrong so the audience will reject his capitalist philosophy. Discuss the character as a <strong>symbol</strong>, not just a person.</p>
