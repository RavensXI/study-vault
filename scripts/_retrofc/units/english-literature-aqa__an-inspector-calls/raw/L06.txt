--- DESCRIPTION ---
What happens after the Inspector leaves, the family’s divided reactions, and the devastating final phone call.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">After the Inspector Leaves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Once the Inspector departs, the Birling family splits into two camps. <strong>Mr Birling, Mrs Birling and Gerald</strong> immediately begin trying to prove the Inspector was a fraud. Gerald makes independent enquiries and discovers there is no Inspector Goole on the local police force. The family also rings the Infirmary to confirm no girl has died. They call the <dfn class="term" data-def="A hospital or clinic providing medical treatment, often associated with care for the poor.">infirmary</dfn>, which confirms no suicide has been admitted.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Mr Birling is triumphant: “The whole thing’s different now.” He believes that if the Inspector was a fake, no real harm was done and there is nothing to worry about. Gerald returns the engagement ring to Sheila, expecting everything to go back to normal.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What does Gerald suggest about the photographs and how does this affect the identity of Eva Smith?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gerald suggests the Inspector may have used <strong>different photographs</strong> to each family member, meaning they might not all have mistreated the same girl. This raises the possibility that “Eva Smith” is not one woman but several — strengthening the interpretation that she represents <strong>all</strong> exploited working-class people.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sheila and Eric Stand Firm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Sheila and Eric</strong> refuse to celebrate. Sheila insists: “It doesn’t make any difference — you know. Between us, we drove that girl to commit suicide — and that’s what matters.” Eric agrees: the Inspector’s identity is irrelevant because what they did was <em>wrong</em>, regardless of whether the consequences were real.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This is the play’s most important <dfn class="term" data-def="A fundamental difference in moral or ethical understanding between two groups, often between generations.">moral divide</dfn>. The older generation cares only about consequences (punishment, scandal). The younger generation understands that morality is about <strong>actions</strong>, not outcomes.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">When Gerald offers the ring back, Sheila does not accept it immediately. She says: “No, not yet. It’s too soon.” This signals that Sheila has genuinely changed — she will not simply return to the comfortable, unquestioning life she had before. The engagement ring, which symbolised the <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement or partnership based on social status and mutual benefit rather than genuine feeling.">social alliance</dfn> between the Birling and Croft families, can no longer be accepted without thought.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Final Phone Call</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Just as Mr Birling is celebrating their escape, the telephone rings. He answers, listens, and his face changes. He announces: “That was the police. A girl has just died — on her way to the Infirmary — after swallowing some disinfectant. And a police inspector is on his way here — to ask some — questions.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The play ends with the family staring in shocked silence. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A circular structure where the ending returns to or echoes the beginning, suggesting repetition or inescapability.">cyclical ending</dfn> suggests that the events are about to repeat — the Inspector will arrive again, the interrogation will begin again. The characters who refused to learn will be given another chance — or another punishment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What are the two main interpretations of the final phone call and what message do both convey?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The final phone call is the play’s most debated moment. It suggests either that a real girl has now died (the Inspector was a warning that went unheeded) or that time is repeating itself. Either way, the message is the same: <strong>if you refuse to learn the lesson of responsibility, the consequences will find you.</strong></p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Interpretations of the Ending</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>The Inspector as time traveller:</strong> He arrived before the death occurred, warning the family of what their actions would cause. They ignored the warning, and now the consequences are real.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>The Inspector as supernatural figure:</strong> His name — “Goole” (homophone of “ghoul”) — hints at something ghostly or otherworldly. He represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The voice of right and wrong; an inner sense of moral judgement.">conscience</dfn> itself, visiting those who have silenced their own.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Cyclical time:</strong> Priestley was influenced by J.W. Dunne’s theory that time is not linear. The ending suggests the family is trapped in a cycle they can only escape by changing their behaviour.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Audience’s Role</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The ambiguous ending forces the audience to make their own moral judgement. Will the Birlings change this time? Should they be punished? Priestley deliberately leaves these questions open because the real question is directed at the audience: <strong>will <em>you</em> take responsibility?</strong> The play is not just about the Birlings — it is about everyone watching.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>The older generation learns nothing:</strong> Birling, Mrs Birling and Gerald celebrate when they think the Inspector was fake, showing they care about punishment, not morality.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Sheila and Eric have genuinely changed:</strong> They insist that what they did was wrong regardless of whether the Inspector was real.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>The phone call restarts the cycle:</strong> The ending suggests that ignoring the lesson of responsibility leads to real consequences. The play’s warning is directed at the audience as much as the characters.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">The ending is a popular exam topic. Always discuss the <strong>cyclical structure</strong> (events repeat), the <strong>generational divide</strong> (who has learned and who has not), and at least one interpretation of the Inspector’s identity. Link the ending to Priestley’s message: the play is a warning to the <em>audience</em>, not just the characters.</p>
