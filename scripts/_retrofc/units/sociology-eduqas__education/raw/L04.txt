--- DESCRIPTION ---
Material deprivation, cultural capital, labelling, the ethnocentric curriculum and masculinity in schools.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Some Groups Achieve More Than Others?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The previous lesson described the patterns: working-class pupils perform below average, girls outperform boys since the 1990s, and ethnic groups show varied attainment. This lesson asks <em>why</em>. Sociologists organise explanations at two levels: factors <em>outside</em> school (home background, material resources, cultural attitudes) and factors <em>inside</em> school (labelling, streaming, curriculum content). No single perspective has the complete answer &mdash; competing explanations offer different mechanisms.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two outside-school factors and two inside-school factors that sociologists identify as causing attainment gaps. For each, name the mechanism and a relevant sociologist.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Outside-school factors include <dfn class="term" data-def="A lack of the financial resources needed for comfortable participation in society, affecting housing, diet, access to educational materials and study conditions.">material deprivation</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The knowledge, skills, qualifications and cultural behaviours that middle-class families possess, giving their children a systematic advantage in educational settings.">cultural capital</dfn>. Inside-school factors include <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which teachers attach definitions or categories to pupils, shaping how those pupils are treated and how they see themselves.">labelling</dfn>, streaming, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A curriculum that reflects the values, knowledge and perspectives of one ethnic group, usually White European, marginalising the experiences of minority ethnic groups.">ethnocentric curriculum</dfn>, and catchment area effects linked to school type.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Social Class: Material and Cultural Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A lack of the financial resources needed to participate fully in education, affecting housing conditions, access to equipment and the ability to pay for tutoring.">Material deprivation</dfn> describes the economic disadvantages working-class pupils face: overcrowded housing with no quiet study space, inability to afford uniform or equipment, needing part-time jobs that reduce study time, and parents unable to pay for private tuition. These material conditions constrain performance regardless of ability or effort.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The concept of cultural capital was developed by Pierre Bourdieu. Bourdieu argued that middle-class families possess cultural capital &mdash; familiarity with the language, values and behaviours that schools reward. Middle-class children enter school already knowing how to address teachers, how to present written work and which cultural references carry currency. Working-class children often lack this, putting them at a systematic disadvantage even when equally able. The concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="A lack of the knowledge, skills, attitudes and values that education systems treat as normal and valued, associated with working-class backgrounds.">cultural deprivation</dfn> takes a related but different approach: it argues that some working-class family cultures transmit values that are misaligned with school expectations, such as short-term thinking over long-term academic investment. Critics argue this explanation &ldquo;blames the victim&rdquo; rather than addressing structural disadvantage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Halsey: Class Barriers and Origins and Destinations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A.H. Halsey&rsquo;s study <em>Origins and Destinations</em> (with Heath and Ridge, 1980) investigated the relationship between social class and educational achievement in Britain. Halsey found that despite the expansion of state education and the introduction of comprehensive schooling, class remained a powerful barrier to educational opportunity. Even when pupils of similar measured ability were compared, those from working-class backgrounds were significantly less likely to gain high-level qualifications or attend university. Halsey&rsquo;s research is a direct challenge to the functionalist meritocracy claim: it demonstrates that achieved status in education reflects social background as much as individual talent.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ball: Parental Choice, Catchment Areas and Marketisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Stephen Ball&rsquo;s research on <dfn class="term" data-def="The application of market principles such as competition between schools, parental choice and performance measures like league tables to the organisation of education.">marketisation</dfn> and parental choice examined how league tables, Ofsted inspections and open enrolment reshaped inequality. Ball argued that middle-class families with cultural capital can navigate the system &mdash; reading league tables, visiting multiple schools and moving into desirable <dfn class="term" data-def="The geographical area from which a school draws most of its pupils; proximity often shapes entry to oversubscribed schools.">catchment areas</dfn>. Working-class parents care equally about their children&rsquo;s education but lack the same information and resources to act on their preferences. Counter-school cultures (Willis, 1977) also contribute: working-class boys who develop anti-school peer cultures limit their own qualifications, reproducing their class position through apparent free choice rather than coercion.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Explanations for ethnic attainment gaps divide into two broad camps. The <strong>cultural deprivation thesis</strong> argues that some minority ethnic families transmit cultural values misaligned with school expectations, such as different language practices or lower emphasis on formal academic qualifications. Critics regard this as &ldquo;victim-blaming&rdquo; and prefer structural explanations located in the school itself.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The <strong>ethnocentric curriculum</strong> describes a school curriculum that predominantly reflects White European culture and knowledge, potentially alienating minority ethnic pupils whose heritage and experience is not represented. <dfn class="term" data-def="Discrimination against minority ethnic groups embedded in the policies, practices and cultures of an institution, without necessarily involving individual prejudice.">Institutional racism</dfn> refers to discrimination embedded in school policies and practices rather than in individual teacher attitudes &mdash; for example, disproportionate exclusion of Black Caribbean boys, or lower teacher expectations. The Macpherson Report (1999), following the Stephen Lawrence Inquiry, introduced institutional racism into mainstream British public debate and prompted reviews of school practices.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Feminist sociologists explain girls&rsquo; improved performance since the 1990s through changes in female aspirations, growth in service-sector employment for women, and curriculum reforms that moved away from male-dominated content. Girls have benefited from a broader expectation that academic qualifications lead to careers, rather than preparation for domestic roles.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The argument that schools have adopted feminised teaching styles, assessment methods and values that disadvantage boys, whose learning styles differ.">feminisation of schools</dfn> refers to the claim that schools have become dominated by female teachers, coursework-based assessment and cooperative learning styles that suit girls more than boys, whose socialised learning behaviour differs. This remains a contested explanation among sociologists &mdash; some argue it pathologises boys rather than addressing wider structural inequality.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The argument that the decline of traditional male manual industries has left some working-class boys without a clear masculine identity that values academic achievement.">crisis of masculinity</dfn> describes the impact of de-industrialisation on working-class boys: as traditional male manual industries such as steel, coal and manufacturing declined from the 1980s onwards, some working-class boys came to see academic success as incompatible with masculine identity. With no clear vocational destination on the horizon, academic effort became associated with femininity and passivity. This explanation connects to Willis&rsquo;s (1977) observation that &ldquo;the lads&rdquo; valued manual labour above academic work, anticipating futures in the factory rather than the classroom.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this and distinguish material deprivation from cultural deprivation in your own words. Give one example of each affecting a real pupil's achievement.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Material deprivation = lack of financial resources (no study space, no equipment, needing a part-time job). Cultural capital = lack of the middle-class knowledge and behaviours schools reward (unfamiliarity with teacher language, school conventions). Cultural deprivation = lack of the values and attitudes education systems expect. All three are associated with working-class underachievement but operate through different mechanisms &mdash; a pupil may experience one without the others.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Social class shapes achievement through material deprivation (lack of economic resources) and cultural capital (lack of middle-class knowledge schools reward). Halsey&rsquo;s <em>Origins and Destinations</em> confirmed class remains a barrier even when ability is controlled for; Ball&rsquo;s research shows marketisation advantages middle-class families in school choice.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Ethnic underachievement is explained by the ethnocentric curriculum and institutional racism (structural factors in schools) or by the contested cultural deprivation thesis (family culture). Both sides must be presented, not just one.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Gender factors include girls&rsquo; rising aspirations and expanded employment, the feminisation of schools argument, and the crisis of masculinity &mdash; the decline of male manual industries leaving some working-class boys without a masculine identity compatible with academic achievement.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">A very common error on questions about factors affecting achievement is to state that class affects achievement without explaining <em>how</em>. The specification explicitly requires the <em>mechanism</em>: material deprivation (how does a lack of money reduce grades?), cultural capital (what specifically does the middle-class child know that the working-class child does not?), marketisation (how does parental choice advantage middle-class families?). Stating a correlation earns minimal marks. Name the mechanism and explain how it operates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">On a 12-mark Evaluate question about factors affecting achievement, the strongest answers distinguish between outside-school factors (material deprivation, cultural capital, cultural deprivation, catchment areas) and inside-school processes (labelling, streaming, ethnocentric curriculum), evaluate evidence for each, name Halsey and Ball as sociologists and reach a clear judgement about whether class, gender or ethnicity is most significant &mdash; or whether all three intersect.</p>
