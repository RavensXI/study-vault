Rochester’s proposal, the aborted wedding, Bertha revealed, and Jane’s moral choice.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Proposal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The proposal scene in Chapter 23 is one of the most famous in English literature. Rochester and Jane are walking in the garden at sunset. Rochester teases Jane, pretending he will marry Blanche Ingram and send Jane away to Ireland. Jane, unable to contain her feelings any longer, bursts out with the declaration that defines her character: “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless?” She continues: “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me; I am a free human being with an independent will.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This speech is revolutionary for a Victorian heroine. Jane does not wait to be chosen — she asserts her equality. She does not speak of love in terms of submission or gratitude, but of mutual respect between two spirits: “it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God’s feet, equal.” Brontë strips away class, gender, and appearance to present love as a meeting of souls.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what the key symbol or technique represents and why it matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“I am no bird; and no net ensnares me” — Jane uses the metaphor of a caged bird to reject the idea that she can be trapped or controlled. The imagery of freedom runs throughout the novel, from the Red Room to the moors, and culminates in Jane’s insistence that she will only love as an equal.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Chestnut Tree</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">On the night of the proposal, a great chestnut tree in the garden is struck by lightning and split in two. This is Brontë’s most powerful use of <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique in which the natural world reflects or mirrors the emotions of the characters or the moral state of the story.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>. The tree symbolises the relationship: apparently strong, but about to be violently torn apart by a force neither Jane nor Rochester can control. The storm that follows the proposal foreshadows the catastrophe to come.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Brontë uses nature as a moral barometer throughout the novel. Moments of crisis are accompanied by storms, fire, or extreme weather; moments of peace coincide with calm landscapes. The split tree is a visual image the examiner will expect you to analyse if it appears in an extract.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Aborted Wedding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The wedding is interrupted at the most dramatic possible moment: a solicitor, Mr Briggs, announces that Rochester is already married. His wife, <dfn class="term" data-def="Rochester’s first wife, a Creole woman from Jamaica who has been confined in the attic of Thornfield Hall due to her violent madness.">Bertha Mason</dfn>, is alive and imprisoned in the third storey of Thornfield. Rochester does not deny it. Instead, he leads the wedding party upstairs to see Bertha for themselves. She is described in violent, animalistic terms: “the clothed hyena” who runs at Rochester with “something of vengeance” and must be physically restrained.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">This is the novel’s turning point. Every mystery is explained — the laughter, the fire, the torn veil — and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of having two spouses at the same time, which was a criminal offence in Victorian England.">bigamy</dfn> attempt is exposed. Rochester argues that his marriage to Bertha was forced on him by his family for money, that Bertha was already showing signs of madness, and that he has suffered for years. He begs Jane to stay with him, to live abroad as his partner even if they cannot legally marry.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Bertha Mason is one of the most debated figures in English literature. On one level, she is a Gothic horror: a violent, animalistic creature imprisoned in the attic. Brontë describes her as a “mad cat” and a “clothed hyena,” language that strips her of humanity. On another level, Bertha can be read as a <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that represents or stands for something else, especially an abstract idea represented by a concrete object or figure.">symbol</dfn> of everything Victorian society repressed — female anger, sexuality, and the consequences of a patriarchal system that locked women away when they became inconvenient.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Modern critics (notably Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar in <em>The Madwoman in the Attic</em>, 1979) read Bertha as Jane’s dark double — the rage and passion that Jane herself must suppress to survive in polite society. Bertha tears Jane’s wedding veil in two, an act that symbolically destroys the false marriage before it can take place. Whether Brontë intended this reading is debatable, but it is a powerful analytical tool for the exam.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Jane’s Moral Choice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Rochester’s plea is agonising. He offers Jane love, comfort, travel, and passion. Everything she has ever wanted is within reach. All she has to do is compromise her principles. Jane refuses. She tells him: “I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central turning point in a narrative, the moment of greatest tension after which the outcome becomes inevitable.">climax</dfn> of the novel’s moral argument. Jane does not leave because she stops loving Rochester — she leaves because she loves herself more. Her decision is rooted in Christian morality (she will not live in sin) and in personal integrity (she will not become a kept woman, dependent on a man who has already lied to her). It is the hardest and bravest thing she does in the entire novel.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and summarise the writer’s message or argument from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“I care for myself” is Jane’s defining moral statement. She chooses self-respect and integrity over love and comfort. Brontë presents this not as cold rejection but as an act of profound strength — Jane preserves her identity by refusing to become Rochester’s mistress.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Leaving Thornfield</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Jane leaves Thornfield at dawn, taking almost nothing with her. She describes the departure as a kind of death: “I had already gained the door; but, reader, I walked back.” She looks at Rochester one last time, then forces herself away. Brontë uses the structure of the departure — the hesitation, the backward glance, the physical pain of separation — to show that Jane’s moral strength does not come from indifference. It comes from choosing principle over desire, even when the cost is devastating.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Jane’s departure is the pivot of the entire novel. Everything before it leads to this choice; everything after it flows from the consequences. She enters the world alone, penniless, and starving — but she has kept her integrity intact.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The proposal, the aborted wedding, and Jane’s departure form the emotional and moral core of the novel. They test whether Jane can hold to her principles when everything she desires is offered to her — and she can.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Jane’s declaration “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me” is a defining moment — she asserts her equality and refuses to be trapped by gender or class expectations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The chestnut tree split by lightning is Brontë’s most powerful use of pathetic fallacy, symbolising the relationship that is about to be violently torn apart.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Jane’s choice to leave Rochester after the bigamy revelation is the moral climax of the novel — she chooses self-respect over love, proving that her independence is her most fundamental value.</li></ul>
