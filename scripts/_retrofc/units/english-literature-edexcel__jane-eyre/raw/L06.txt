--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s return to the burned Thornfield, Rochester blinded, and the marriage of equals.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Voice on the Moors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">At the moment Jane is closest to accepting St John’s proposal, she hears Rochester’s voice calling her name across the moors: “Jane! Jane! Jane!” She responds: “I am coming! Wait for me!” This is one of the novel’s most debated moments. Is it genuinely supernatural — a spiritual connection between two souls? Or is it a projection of Jane’s own desire, breaking through the repression St John has imposed on her? Brontë leaves the question open, allowing both readings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">What matters structurally is that the voice pulls Jane out of St John’s orbit and back towards Rochester. It reasserts the claims of emotion over duty, of the heart over cold reason. Jane leaves Moor House and travels to Thornfield, driven by an instinct she cannot fully explain but will not ignore.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Thornfield in Ruins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Jane arrives to find Thornfield Hall destroyed. Bertha Mason set fire to the house and then jumped from the roof to her death. Rochester tried to save the servants and Bertha herself, but the building collapsed. He survived, but was left blinded and with one hand amputated. Thornfield is a blackened ruin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The destruction of Thornfield is rich in <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that represents or stands for something else, especially an abstract idea represented by a concrete object or figure.">symbolism</dfn>. The grand house with its secrets and its locked attic is obliterated — and with it, all the deception, inequality, and concealment that it represented. Bertha’s death removes the legal barrier to Jane and Rochester’s marriage, but it also removes the moral taint. Rochester has been punished for his sins: his blindness and mutilation are a form of <dfn class="term" data-def="Suffering or loss experienced as a consequence of wrongdoing, which leads to moral or spiritual improvement.">penance</dfn>. He is humbled, dependent, and stripped of the power that once made him Jane’s superior.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what the key symbol or technique represents and why it matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thornfield’s destruction symbolises the end of deception and power imbalance. Rochester’s blindness and injuries are a form of penance — he must be diminished before he and Jane can meet as true equals. The fire purges the relationship of its moral corruption.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Reunion at Ferndean</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Rochester has retreated to <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, secluded manor house in a forest where Rochester lives after the fire at Thornfield, symbolising simplicity and genuine love.">Ferndean</dfn>, a small, secluded manor deep in a forest. The setting is deliberately humble — no grand hall, no servants, no secrets. When Jane finds him, he is broken: blind, maimed, and deeply depressed. He believes Jane will never return.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The reunion scene reverses the power dynamic of their entire relationship. Rochester is now the dependent one — he needs Jane physically, emotionally, and practically. Jane, meanwhile, arrives with her own money, her own family connections, and her own fully formed identity. She is no longer the penniless governess seeking employment. She is a woman of independent means who chooses to be with Rochester freely, not out of necessity or obligation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">“Reader, I married him” is perhaps the most famous sentence in the novel. Its power comes from its simplicity and its <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a writer addresses or involves the reader directly, breaking the barrier between the fictional world and the real world.">direct address</dfn> to the reader. Notice the structure: Jane is the subject of the sentence. She married <em>him</em>. She is not “married by” Rochester or “given away” by anyone. The active voice places Jane in control of her own destiny. After a novel in which she has been controlled, confined, deceived, and tested, she finally acts with complete agency.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Brontë describes their marriage as one of perfect companionship: “I am my husband’s life as fully as he is mine.” They are intellectually, emotionally, and spiritually equal. Rochester gradually recovers some sight in one eye, but the novel makes clear that the relationship does not depend on his physical recovery. It depends on the equality that Jane’s departure, her inheritance, and Rochester’s suffering have finally made possible.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">A Marriage of Equals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The ending of <em>Jane Eyre</em> is not a conventional happy ending. Rochester is diminished: blind, scarred, and dependent. The grand house is gone. The fairytale trappings of wealth and beauty have been stripped away. What remains is something Brontë considers more valuable: a partnership based on mutual respect, genuine love, and shared experience. Jane does not need Rochester for money, status, or security. She is with him because she wants to be — and that, for Brontë, is the only acceptable basis for marriage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The novel ends not with Jane and Rochester but with St John Rivers, who is dying as a missionary in India. Brontë gives the last paragraph to him, describing his devotion to God. This is a deliberate structural choice. By placing St John’s cold, self-sacrificing faith at the end, Brontë implicitly asks the reader to compare it with Jane’s warm, human love. Both paths involve sacrifice; but Jane’s path leads to life and connection, while St John’s leads to death.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe Reader development across the text in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Reader, I married him” places Jane as the active subject. She is not “married by” Rochester — she marries him. The direct address to the reader asserts Jane’s control over her own narrative, completing the journey from voiceless orphan to woman with full agency.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Rochester’s Redemption</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Rochester’s blindness and injuries are often interpreted as divine punishment or penance. He lied, attempted bigamy, and kept a woman imprisoned in his attic. The fire — set by the woman he wronged — destroys everything he built on deception. His suffering humbles him, and when Jane returns, he acknowledges his faults openly. He tells Jane he has learned to pray and to depend on God, something his arrogance previously prevented.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The partial restoration of his sight — he can eventually see their firstborn child — suggests redemption is possible but not complete. Rochester is a better man, but he bears the permanent marks of his moral failures. Brontë does not let him off lightly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The resolution of <em>Jane Eyre</em> is about balance: love tempered by principle, passion checked by integrity, power equalised through suffering and generosity. Jane achieves what she has wanted since childhood — to be loved and respected as an equal — and she achieves it entirely on her own terms.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">“Reader, I married him” uses active voice and direct address to place Jane in complete control of her own narrative and destiny.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Thornfield’s destruction and Rochester’s injuries symbolise the removal of deception and the power imbalance — only then can a true marriage of equals occur.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The novel ends with St John Rivers, implicitly contrasting his cold, self-sacrificing faith with Jane’s warm, human love — Brontë values connection over duty.</li></ul>
