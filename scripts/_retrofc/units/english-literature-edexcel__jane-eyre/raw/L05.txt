--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s independence at Moor House, St John’s cold proposal, and the inheritance.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Alone on the Moors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After leaving Thornfield, Jane wanders the moors for three days, starving and destitute. She sleeps outside, begs for food, and is turned away from doors. Brontë strips Jane of everything — home, love, money, security — to test whether her principles can sustain her when she has nothing else. The moors are a powerful <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique in which the natural world reflects or mirrors the emotions of the characters or the moral state of the story.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>: wild, open, and beautiful, but also dangerous and indifferent. Jane describes them as “a ridge of lighted heath” — a landscape that is both illuminated and exposed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This is the lowest point of the novel. Jane is completely alone, physically weakened, and emotionally devastated. Yet Brontë shows that Jane’s spirit survives. She does not regret leaving Rochester. She tells herself: “The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself.” Her suffering is the price of her integrity, and she is willing to pay it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself” — this tricolon builds in emotional intensity, showing that Jane’s self-respect grows stronger as her external circumstances worsen. Independence is not a luxury for Jane; it is a survival mechanism.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Moor House and the Rivers Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane collapses at the door of Moor House (also called Marsh End) and is taken in by the Rivers siblings: <dfn class="term" data-def="Jane’s cousins who take her in when she is destitute, representing intellectual companionship and familial love.">Diana and Mary Rivers</dfn>, and their brother St John. This section of the novel provides Jane with something she has never had: a family. Diana and Mary are educated, kind, and intellectually matched with Jane. Their friendship is warm and equal — a contrast to every relationship Jane has previously experienced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Brontë uses Moor House to show that Jane can thrive when she is valued for her mind and character rather than her beauty or wealth. Jane takes a position as a village schoolteacher — a humble role, but one she performs with dedication. She discovers she can build a meaningful life through her own efforts, without depending on Rochester or anyone else.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Jane learns that her uncle, John Eyre, has died and left her a fortune of twenty thousand pounds. She also discovers that the Rivers siblings are her cousins — the family she has longed for her entire life. Jane’s response to the inheritance is characteristic: she immediately divides it equally among all four cousins, keeping only five thousand for herself. This act of generosity transforms her from a dependent into a benefactor — and it shows that money, for Jane, is valuable only as a means to independence and connection, not as an end in itself.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The inheritance is also structurally significant. It removes the class barrier between Jane and Rochester. When Jane returns to him, she comes as a woman of independent means, not as a penniless governess. Brontë ensures that the final reunion is between equals in every sense.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">St John Rivers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A clergyman and Jane’s cousin who represents cold, dutiful religion and proposes a loveless marriage based on missionary work.">St John Rivers</dfn> is Rochester’s opposite in almost every way. Where Rochester is passionate, impulsive, and emotionally excessive, St John is controlled, calculating, and emotionally repressed. He is handsome, brilliant, and devoted to his calling as a missionary — but he is also cold. His ambition is entirely directed towards God’s service, and he views human emotions as weaknesses to be overcome.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">St John proposes to Jane — not out of love, but because he needs a wife to accompany him on his mission to India. He tells her that her skills and temperament make her suitable for the role. It is a proposal based entirely on utility and duty. Jane recognises that accepting St John would mean surrendering her identity just as completely as becoming Rochester’s mistress would have. She tells him: “I scorn your idea of love.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>St John’s proposal is the mirror image of Rochester’s: Rochester offered passionate love without moral integrity; St John offers moral purpose without emotional warmth. Jane rejects both because true fulfilment requires both love and self-respect — neither alone is enough.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>St John and Religious Duty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">St John represents a different kind of religious character from Brocklehurst. Where Brocklehurst is a hypocrite (preaching austerity while living in luxury), St John genuinely practises what he preaches. His <dfn class="term" data-def="Severe self-discipline and avoidance of all forms of physical pleasure, often practised for religious reasons.">asceticism</dfn> is real, and his commitment to his missionary work is total. Yet Brontë presents his faith as ultimately life-denying. St John suppresses his feelings for the beautiful Rosamond Oliver because she would not make a suitable missionary wife. He views love as a distraction from duty.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Jane almost succumbs to St John’s persuasion. His moral authority is powerful, and she genuinely respects his dedication. But at the crucial moment, she hears Rochester’s voice calling her name across the moors — a mysterious, possibly supernatural event that pulls her back to her own desires. Brontë suggests that Jane’s instinctive, emotional response is more trustworthy than St John’s calculated reason.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The Moor House chapters complete Jane’s journey to independence. She gains a family, a fortune, and a clear understanding of what she wants from life. She has tested her principles against both passionate love and cold duty, and she knows that she will accept nothing less than a relationship that honours both her heart and her conscience.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Jane’s time on the moors tests her principles to the limit — she endures starvation and desolation rather than compromise her integrity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">St John Rivers is Rochester’s opposite: cold duty versus passionate love. Jane rejects both proposals because each requires her to sacrifice part of herself.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The inheritance removes the class barrier between Jane and Rochester, ensuring their reunion is between equals. Jane shares her wealth because money is a means to independence, not an end in itself.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">St John Rivers is often overlooked in exam answers, but he is crucial for a Part (b) response on religion, duty, or types of love. Compare his cold proposal with Rochester’s passionate one — both are rejected because each demands that Jane sacrifice her identity. The examiner rewards students who can analyse the structural parallel between these two proposals.</p>
