--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane as governess at Thornfield, Rochester’s Byronic qualities, and the growing relationship.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Arriving at Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After eight years at Lowood, Jane advertises for a position as a governess and is hired to teach Adèle Varens at Thornfield Hall. Her arrival marks a pivotal shift in the novel: from childhood suffering to adult independence, and from institutional life to the complex social world of a great house. Thornfield is grand but unsettling — it has dark corridors, a locked third storey, and an atmosphere that Jane herself describes as strange.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Mrs Fairfax, the housekeeper, is kind but ordinary. Adèle is lively but superficial, obsessed with gifts and dresses (Brontë uses her to gently satirise French vanity). Jane finds Thornfield comfortable but unstimulating — until the master of the house arrives.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Rochester: The Byronic Hero</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Edward Rochester is Brontë’s most complex male creation. He is not handsome in a conventional sense — Jane describes his face as “dark” and “stern,” his features “harsh.” Yet he possesses an intense magnetism that draws Jane (and the reader) in. Rochester fits the archetype of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary character type (named after Lord Byron) who is dark, brooding, morally ambiguous, passionate, and haunted by a secret past.">Byronic hero</dfn>: brooding, passionate, morally ambiguous, and burdened by a secret that torments him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Their first meeting is strikingly unconventional. Rochester falls from his horse on the icy lane and Jane helps him up without knowing who he is. There is no gallantry, no formality — just two people meeting as equals in a moment of physical need. Brontë deliberately strips away the usual power dynamics of master and servant, establishing from the start that this relationship will not follow conventional rules.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rochester is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary character type (named after Lord Byron) who is dark, brooding, morally ambiguous, passionate, and haunted by a secret past.">Byronic hero</dfn> — charismatic but deeply flawed. His moral ambiguity (concealing his marriage, asking Jane to become his mistress) makes him both attractive and dangerous. Brontë asks the reader to question whether passion can excuse deception.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Growing Relationship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Rochester is drawn to Jane precisely because she is unlike anyone he has met. She is honest, direct, and unafraid to challenge him. Their conversations crackle with intellectual energy. Rochester tells her she is “not naturally austere” and that he detects “something of vengeance” in her nature — a perceptive reading of the fierce spirit Jane has carried since childhood. Jane, in turn, sees past Rochester’s gruffness to the wounded man beneath.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brontë builds the relationship through dialogue rather than action. There are no romantic set-pieces; instead, long, probing conversations in which both characters test and reveal themselves. Rochester confesses fragments of his past — his dissolute years in Europe, his <dfn class="term" data-def="A person’s lover or partner outside marriage.">mistresses</dfn>, his self-disgust — and Jane listens without judgement but also without flattery. She tells him plainly when he is wrong, a quality he finds both infuriating and irresistible.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">From the moment Jane arrives, Thornfield is haunted by strange sounds. She hears “a curious laugh — distinct, formal, mirthless” from the third storey. Grace Poole, a servant, is blamed for the disturbances, and Jane accepts this explanation — for a time. But the noises intensify: Rochester’s bed curtains are set on fire while he sleeps, and Jane saves his life. Later, a mysterious figure tears the wedding veil. These events use <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique in which the writer gives hints or clues about events that will happen later in the story.">foreshadowing</dfn> to build suspense and signal that Thornfield conceals a terrible truth.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Brontë uses the Gothic convention of the haunted house, but the source of the terror is not supernatural — it is a living woman, Bertha Mason, locked in the attic by her husband. The “ghost” is a real person, which makes the horror more disturbing, not less.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Blanche Ingram and Jealousy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Rochester invites a party of wealthy guests to Thornfield, including the beautiful and aristocratic Blanche Ingram. Jane is tormented by jealousy, believing Rochester will marry Blanche for her beauty and status. Blanche is everything Jane is not: tall, glamorous, confident, and well-born. Yet Brontë makes clear that Blanche is shallow — she is <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessively proud of or concerned with one’s own appearance, qualities, or achievements.">vain</dfn>, snobbish, and interested only in Rochester’s wealth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Blanche episode serves several purposes. It forces Jane to confront her own feelings for Rochester. It exposes the superficiality of high society. And it reveals Rochester’s manipulation — he later admits he used Blanche to provoke Jane’s jealousy, a morally questionable tactic that foreshadows his larger deception.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and summarise this key fact in your own words from memory, then check.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rochester deliberately uses Blanche Ingram to make Jane jealous — a manipulative tactic that reveals his willingness to deceive those around him. This foreshadows the much greater deception of his hidden marriage to Bertha Mason.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jane’s Self-Assessment</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">When Jane realises she loves Rochester, she forces herself to draw two portraits: a plain self-portrait in chalk, titled “Portrait of a Governess, disconnected, poor, and plain,” and an imagined miniature of Blanche in ivory and colour. She commands herself to compare them whenever she feels hopeful. This scene reveals Jane’s painful self-awareness and her internalisation of the class system — she believes that wealth and beauty should determine who deserves love, even though her own story argues the opposite.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Brontë uses this moment to show that even the most independent-minded characters are shaped by the values of their society. Jane must learn to reject not just external oppression, but the oppressive beliefs she has absorbed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The Thornfield chapters establish the central tension of the novel: Jane loves Rochester, but she will not sacrifice her principles or her selfhood for that love. The question is whether Rochester — with all his charm, his secrets, and his moral ambiguity — deserves her.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Rochester is a Byronic hero — charismatic, brooding, and morally ambiguous. His concealment of Bertha and his manipulation of Jane through Blanche Ingram reveal serious flaws beneath the charm.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The attic noises create Gothic suspense and foreshadow the revelation of Bertha Mason. Brontë makes the terror more disturbing by grounding it in reality rather than the supernatural.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Jane and Rochester’s relationship is built on intellectual honesty and emotional directness, but it is threatened by the social inequality between them and by Rochester’s deception.</li></ul>
