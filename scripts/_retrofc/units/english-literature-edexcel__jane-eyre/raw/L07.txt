--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Character as Argument</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In <em>Jane Eyre</em>, every major character embodies a set of values or ideas that Brontë wants the reader to examine. Understanding what each character represents — and how they relate to the novel’s themes — is essential for a strong exam response. This lesson analyses the key characters as vehicles for Brontë’s arguments about class, gender, morality, and love.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Jane Eyre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Jane is the moral centre of the novel. She is defined by her insistence on equality, her fierce independence, and her refusal to compromise her principles. As a child, she confronts Mrs Reed with terrifying honesty; as an adult, she walks away from the man she loves rather than accept a morally corrupt arrangement. Her declaration “I care for myself” is not selfish — it is the foundation of her identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">What makes Jane exceptional as a <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character in a story whose actions and development drive the narrative forward.">protagonist</dfn> is that she is also the <dfn class="term" data-def="The voice telling the story, which in first-person narration is a character within the story who shapes how the reader understands events.">narrator</dfn>. She addresses the reader directly (“Reader, I married him”), controls the pacing of revelations, and shapes our understanding of every other character. Brontë gives Jane complete authority over her own story — a radical act for a Victorian heroine.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane is both protagonist and narrator, giving her double authority. She embodies Brontë’s argument that a woman’s worth is determined by her character and intellect, not her beauty, wealth, or social position.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Edward Rochester</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Rochester is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary character type (named after Lord Byron) who is dark, brooding, morally ambiguous, passionate, and haunted by a secret past.">Byronic hero</dfn>: passionate, charismatic, morally ambiguous, and burdened by guilt. He genuinely loves Jane, but his love is possessive and controlling. He conceals his marriage, manipulates Jane through the Blanche Ingram scheme, and attempts bigamy. His flaws are not superficial — they are deeply embedded in his character and in the patriarchal system that gives men power over women.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Rochester’s arc is one of humbling and redemption. The fire at Thornfield strips him of wealth, power, sight, and physical strength. He is forced into the dependence he once imposed on others. Only in this diminished state can he become Jane’s equal. Brontë suggests that Rochester’s suffering is both punishment and purification — he becomes a better man through loss.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Bertha Mason</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Bertha is the most complex and controversial figure in the novel. Brontë describes her in dehumanising terms: a “clothed hyena,” a “mad cat,” a creature of “something of vengeance.” On the surface, she is a Gothic monster — violent, animalistic, and terrifying. But modern critics have read her differently. As Rochester’s wife, she was brought from Jamaica, married for money, and then locked away when she became inconvenient. She can be seen as a victim of <dfn class="term" data-def="A social system in which men hold primary power, authority, and privilege, and women are subordinate.">patriarchy</dfn> and colonialism — a woman whose rage is entirely justified, even if Brontë does not present it that way.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Bertha also functions as Jane’s dark double. She expresses the anger and passion that Jane must suppress to survive in polite Victorian society. When Bertha tears the wedding veil, she symbolically destroys the false marriage before it can happen. Her death by fire clears the way for Jane’s return, but it also raises uncomfortable questions about whose freedom comes at whose expense.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">St John Rivers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">St John is Rochester’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character in a story who contrasts with another character, usually the protagonist, in order to highlight particular qualities of each.">foil</dfn> — where Rochester is all passion and no restraint, St John is all restraint and no passion. He is handsome, brilliant, and deeply committed to his faith, but he is also cold, controlling, and emotionally repressive. He proposes to Jane not out of love but out of utility: she would make a good missionary’s wife. His version of love is duty without warmth, service without joy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Brontë admires St John’s dedication but ultimately rejects his philosophy. The novel’s final paragraph describes his missionary death as noble but lonely. Jane’s path — love, connection, family — is presented as the fuller, more human life.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Helen Burns, Mrs Reed, and Mr Brocklehurst</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Helen Burns</strong> represents Christian patience and acceptance. She forgives her persecutors, endures suffering without complaint, and believes in a heavenly reward. Her death forces Jane (and the reader) to question whether passive goodness is enough to survive in a cruel world. Brontë honours Helen’s faith but suggests that Jane’s fiercer nature is what the world demands.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Mrs Reed</strong> embodies the cruelty of the upper classes towards those they consider beneath them. She resents Jane for being a burden and a reminder of her husband’s preference for his sister’s child. Even on her deathbed, she confesses to a final act of spite: she told Jane’s uncle that Jane was dead, denying her the inheritance and family she deserved. Mrs Reed represents the vindictiveness that can fester when power goes unchecked.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Mr Brocklehurst</strong> is Brontë’s satirical portrait of religious hypocrisy. He starves and freezes the Lowood girls in the name of Christian austerity while his own family lives in comfort. He publicly humiliates Jane to assert his authority. He represents the abuse of institutional power — using religion as a tool of control rather than compassion.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brontë uses characters as contrasting pairs: Rochester/St John (passion vs duty), Jane/Helen (rebellion vs acceptance), Brocklehurst/Miss Temple (hypocrisy vs integrity). Understanding these pairings helps you write structurally about how Brontë constructs her argument across the novel.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Minor Characters: Adèle, Mrs Fairfax, and Rosamond Oliver</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Adèle Varens</strong> is Rochester’s ward, a French girl who may or may not be his biological daughter. She is superficial and materialistic, obsessed with gifts and dresses. Brontë uses her to contrast with Jane’s depth of character and to gently satirise French vanity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Mrs Fairfax</strong> is the kindly housekeeper at Thornfield who warns Jane against marrying Rochester, sensing that the relationship is unequal. She represents conventional Victorian morality — well-meaning but limited.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Rosamond Oliver</strong> is the beautiful, wealthy woman St John loves but refuses to marry because she would not suit his missionary life. She illustrates St John’s willingness to sacrifice human happiness for abstract duty.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n22">In the exam, always connect characters to Brontë’s wider purpose. Ask: what does this character represent? How do they compare or contrast with other characters? What argument is Brontë making through them?</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25">Brontë uses characters as embodiments of ideas: Jane (independence), Rochester (flawed passion), Bertha (repressed rage), St John (cold duty), Helen (patient faith).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Characters work in contrasting pairs: Rochester/St John, Jane/Helen, Brocklehurst/Miss Temple. These pairings structure Brontë’s moral argument.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Bertha Mason can be read as a Gothic monster, a victim of patriarchy, or Jane’s dark double — any of these readings is valid if supported by evidence.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">For Part (b), structuring your answer around character contrasts is highly effective. Instead of discussing characters in isolation, compare them: Rochester’s passion versus St John’s coldness, Jane’s rebellion versus Helen’s patience. This shows the examiner you understand how Brontë constructs meaning through juxtaposition. Always use the author’s name: “Brontë presents Rochester as...” not “Rochester is...”</p>
