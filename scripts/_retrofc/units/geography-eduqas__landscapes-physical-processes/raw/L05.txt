--- DESCRIPTION ---
Stores and flows in UK drainage basins, hydrographs, and why rivers flood today.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Drainage Basin?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of land drained by a single river and its tributaries.">drainage basin</dfn> is the area of land where every drop of rain eventually finds its way into the same river system. The boundary of a basin is the <strong>watershed</strong> — usually a high ridge of land — and inside the watershed, water moves through a series of stores and flows. The drainage basin is the basic unit geographers use to study where water comes from, where it goes, and why floods happen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four flows that move water through a drainage basin and one store at each end of the system.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Water enters a basin as <strong>precipitation</strong>, sits temporarily in <strong>stores</strong> (vegetation, soil, groundwater, lakes), moves through <strong>flows</strong> (interception, infiltration, throughflow, overland flow) and finally leaves as <strong>river discharge</strong> at the mouth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Stores and Flows</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">When rain falls on a drainage basin, the first thing it usually meets is vegetation. Trees and shrubs catch droplets on their leaves — called <dfn class="term" data-def="The catching of rainfall by leaves and stems before it reaches the ground.">interception</dfn>. Some evaporates straight back into the air, some drips slowly to the ground. Once on the soil, water can soak in (infiltration), move sideways through the upper soil (<dfn class="term" data-def="The slow movement of water sideways through soil towards a river channel.">throughflow</dfn>), or run across the surface as overland flow. Deep below, water enters cracks in the rock (groundwater flow) and feeds rivers slowly throughout the year.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">A healthy basin slows water down. Forests intercept up to a third of rainfall before it reaches the soil. Grassland and well-aerated farmland soils let water infiltrate. Rivers respond gradually, peaking days after rain falls. A damaged basin — ploughed bare, compacted by livestock, or paved over — rejects water at the surface. Overland flow dominates, water reaches the river within hours, and discharge spikes sharply. The same rainfall produces a small flood in the first basin and a serious flood in the second.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Hydrographs Tell the Story</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that shows river discharge over time, often after a single storm.">hydrograph</dfn> records the response of a river to a storm. Rainfall is shown as bars and discharge as a line. The <strong>rising limb</strong> shows water arriving in the channel, the <strong>peak discharge</strong> is the highest point, and the <strong>falling limb</strong> shows the river slowly returning to its base flow. The time gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge is the <strong>lag time</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Close this and sketch a flashy hydrograph and a low hydrograph, labelling rising limb, peak and lag time on each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A <strong>flashy</strong> hydrograph has a short lag time and steep rising limb — common after intense rain in urban or deforested basins. A <strong>low</strong> hydrograph has a long lag and gentle limbs — common in forested rural basins with permeable rock.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Physical Causes of UK Flooding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Rivers flood when discharge exceeds channel capacity and water spills onto the floodplain. Several physical conditions push UK rivers towards this point.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Heavy or prolonged rainfall is the obvious trigger. Saturated soil cannot absorb new rain, so almost everything runs straight into the river. Steep upland slopes — common in north-west Britain — speed water downhill. Impermeable rocks like granite stop water soaking in deep underground. Sparse vegetation cover reduces interception. When several of these line up at once, even a routine storm can flood a town downstream.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Human Causes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Land-use change tilts the balance further. Building on a floodplain replaces grass and soil with roofs, roads and concrete — a typical city centre rejects 70–90 % of rainfall as overland flow. Drains move that water to the river within minutes. Deforestation in upland catchments removes the trees that would have intercepted rain. Ploughing fields up and down the slope creates ready-made channels for surface flow. Even small changes — a new car park here, a hedge removed there — add up.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Case study — the Calder Valley floods of 2015</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">On Boxing Day 2015, Storm Eva dumped over 100 mm of rain on the upper Calder Valley in West Yorkshire in a single day. Soils were already saturated from a wet December, much of the upland moor had been drained for grouse shooting, and the steep narrow valley funnelled water rapidly downhill. The River Calder rose 2 metres in a few hours, overtopping defences in Hebden Bridge and Mytholmroyd. Around 2,000 homes and 1,000 businesses were flooded across the Calderdale district, and damage exceeded £100 million. Almost every cause — physical and human — played a role.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Annual Patterns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">UK rivers also have a yearly rhythm. In winter, low evaporation and saturated soils mean even moderate rain causes high discharge. In summer, plants intercept and transpire much more water, soils have spare capacity, and discharge stays low. The annual hydrograph — a year-long record — shows clear winter peaks. Climate change is making winter rainfall more intense and summer droughts longer, sharpening both ends of the cycle.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">A drainage basin moves water through stores and flows; how it balances them controls how a river responds to rainfall.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Hydrographs show rising limb, peak discharge, falling limb and lag time; flashy hydrographs follow heavy rain in urban or deforested basins.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">UK floods are usually triggered by saturated soils plus a heavy storm, but human pressures like building on floodplains and removing trees magnify the impact.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">Eduqas examiner reports flag two recurring errors with hydrographs. First, students confuse peak rainfall with peak discharge — remember they happen at different times, separated by the lag. Second, students describe the graph but never explain it. For 6-mark questions, link a basin’s features (steep slopes, urban surfaces, deforested ground) to the shape of its hydrograph. Always name a UK case study (Calder Valley 2015, Cumbria 2009, Cornwall 2004) when asked about a specific flood.</p>
