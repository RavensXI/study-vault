--- DESCRIPTION ---
How aid, NGOs and fair trade try to close the gap between richer and poorer countries.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Closing the Development Gap</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Uneven development is not inevitable. Governments, charities and international agreements have tried for decades to narrow the gap between richer and poorer countries. Three responses dominate: <dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of money, goods, services or expertise from one country or organisation to another to help reduce poverty or respond to crisis.">aid</dfn>, the work of <dfn class="term" data-def="A non-governmental organisation; a charity or non-profit body that operates independently of governments, often providing aid or development support.">NGOs</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A trading system that pays producers in poorer countries a fair, stable price and invests a premium in their community.">fair trade</dfn>. Each works in a different way, and each has strengths and weaknesses.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three main responses to uneven development with a one-line description of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three main global responses to uneven development are international aid, the work of NGOs and the fair trade movement. They differ in scale, in who delivers them and in how long they last.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Types of Aid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Aid splits into two broad types. <dfn class="term" data-def="Short-term aid given immediately after a disaster such as a flood, earthquake or famine.">Emergency aid</dfn> arrives quickly after a disaster — food, water, tents, medical teams. After the 2010 Haiti earthquake, the UN appealed for $1.5 billion to feed 3 million survivors. <dfn class="term" data-def="Aid that supports lasting projects such as schools, clinics, water supplies and farming improvements.">Long-term development aid</dfn> is slower and steadier — it builds schools, clinics, wells and roads, and trains local people to run them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Aid can flow in three ways. <strong>Bilateral aid</strong> goes from one government directly to another, sometimes with conditions attached such as buying products from the donor country. <strong>Multilateral aid</strong> is pooled through international bodies like the United Nations and World Bank and shared between many recipients. <strong>Voluntary aid</strong> is raised by the public for charities such as Oxfam or the Red Cross.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">In 2015 the United Nations agreed 17 <strong>Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)</strong> to be reached by 2030. They include ending extreme poverty, ensuring all children complete primary school, and providing clean water and sanitation for all. The SDGs replaced the earlier Millennium Development Goals and act as a shared scoreboard for governments, NGOs and donors. Progress has been uneven — child mortality has fallen sharply, but climate change is now pushing some targets further out of reach.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Strengths and Weaknesses of Aid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Aid saves lives in emergencies and can transform schools, clinics and water supplies. But aid is also criticised. Large amounts can be lost to corruption before they reach the people who need them. <dfn class="term" data-def="Aid that comes with conditions, such as a requirement to buy goods or hire firms from the donor country.">Tied aid</dfn> may force recipients to buy from the donor country, undermining local businesses. Some economists argue that decades of aid have left countries dependent rather than self-reliant. Others reply that without aid, life expectancy in many LICs would be far lower today.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two strengths and two weaknesses of long-term aid for development.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aid saves lives in emergencies and funds schools, clinics and water supplies. But it can be lost to corruption, tied to donor-country purchases and create dependency if not paired with local capacity-building.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">NGOs — Working Closer to the Ground</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">NGOs include large international charities such as Oxfam, WaterAid, Save the Children and Médecins Sans Frontières, as well as thousands of small local organisations. Because they are not run by governments, NGOs can often work in remote villages or war zones where official aid agencies struggle. Their projects are usually small-scale: a hand-pump well for a village in Mali, a girls’ school in rural Bangladesh, training health workers in Sierra Leone.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">NGO projects tend to favour <strong>bottom-up development</strong> — working with local communities to choose what they need rather than imposing top-down solutions from outside. WaterAid’s rope-pump wells in Malawi, for example, can be built and maintained by local people using parts available in the nearest market town. The technology is appropriate to local skills and budgets.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Fair Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Fair trade tackles the unfairness in global trade rather than relying on aid. Farmers and producers who join a fair trade scheme are guaranteed a <strong>fair, stable minimum price</strong> for their crop — for example, Fairtrade-certified cocoa farmers in Ghana receive at least US$2,400 per tonne, plus an extra premium of US$240 per tonne. The premium goes into a community fund that pays for schools, clinics or new equipment.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Fair trade products earn higher prices for farmers and have funded schools and water projects across coffee, cocoa and banana communities in Latin America and Africa. But critics argue the premium reaches only a small fraction of the world’s farmers, and certification fees can shut out the very poorest. Others worry it props up production of crops the world already has too much of. The most balanced view is that fair trade helps the farmers it reaches but cannot, on its own, fix global trade rules.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Putting Responses Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">No single response will close the development gap. Emergency aid keeps people alive after a hurricane; long-term aid and NGO projects build the schools, water systems and clinics that lift the next generation; fair trade pays farmers a price closer to what their work is worth. The countries that have moved up the development continuum most quickly — Vietnam, Bangladesh, Rwanda — have combined all three with their own government investment and sound policy.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Emergency aid tackles short-term crises while long-term development aid funds schools, clinics and water supplies; both can be bilateral, multilateral or voluntary.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">NGOs often work bottom-up with local communities using appropriate technology, reaching villages where governments cannot, but they operate at smaller scale than official aid.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Fair trade pays producers in poorer countries a stable minimum price plus a community premium; it helps participating farmers but cannot, on its own, rewrite global trade rules.</li>
</ul>
