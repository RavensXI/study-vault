--- DESCRIPTION ---
How trade rules, multinationals and tourism create uneven development between richer and poorer countries.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Are Some Countries Richer Than Others?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Development is uneven across the world, and the causes are rarely just bad luck. Three forces shape today’s pattern: the rules of <dfn class="term" data-def="The buying and selling of goods and services between countries.">global trade</dfn>, the choices of <dfn class="term" data-def="A company that owns or controls operations in two or more countries.">multinational companies</dfn> (MNCs), and the spread of international tourism. Each force creates winners and losers — and rarely splits them evenly between high-income countries (HICs), newly industrialised countries (NICs) and low-income countries (LICs).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three main drivers of uneven development with one sentence on each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three main drivers of uneven development at the global scale are international trade, multinational companies and tourism. All three connect richer and poorer countries but do so on unequal terms.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Trade and the Rules of the Game</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Countries grow richer when they sell more than they buy. <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods or services sold to other countries.">Exports</dfn> bring money in; <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods or services bought from other countries.">imports</dfn> take it out. Many LICs export <strong>primary products</strong> — cocoa, coffee, copper, oil — whose prices swing wildly on world markets. HICs and NICs export <strong>manufactured goods</strong> and services, which earn far higher and steadier prices. A tonne of raw cocoa earns Ghanaian farmers a fraction of what Swiss chocolate makers earn from the same beans turned into bars.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The rules of trade can deepen this gap. <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of countries that agree to trade freely with one another, often charging tariffs on goods from outside.">Trade blocs</dfn> like the European Union remove tariffs between members but charge them on imports from outside. <dfn class="term" data-def="A tax placed on goods imported from another country.">Tariffs</dfn>, <strong>quotas</strong> (limits on how much can be imported) and <strong>subsidies</strong> (government payments to a country’s own farmers or producers) all tilt the playing field. EU sugar subsidies, for example, made it cheaper for African producers to sell cane sugar to Europe than for Europeans to buy it — until the subsidies were reformed.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Around 60% of Zambia’s export earnings still come from copper. When the world copper price falls, Zambia’s government has less tax income for schools, roads and hospitals overnight. A country that exports a wider range of finished goods — cars, electronics, clothing — spreads its risk. This is one reason China and Vietnam climbed faster up the development continuum than Zambia or Burkina Faso: they shifted from raw exports into manufacturing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Multinational Companies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">An MNC has its head office in one country and factories, offices or shops in many others. Apple is American, but its iPhones are assembled in China and Vietnam from parts made in South Korea, Japan and Taiwan. MNCs locate plants where labour is cheaper, taxes are lower or raw materials sit close by. The biggest MNCs, like Walmart or Toyota, earn more than the GNI of most LICs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">For host LICs and NICs, MNCs bring real benefits: jobs, training, technology, infrastructure and tax revenue. The clothing industry helped lift millions of Bangladeshi women out of household poverty. But the costs are real too. Wages are often low, working conditions can be harsh, profits flow back to the home country and MNCs may leave when costs rise — closing factories overnight. The 2013 collapse of the <strong>Rana Plaza</strong> garment factory in Dhaka, which killed over 1,100 workers, became a symbol of these trade-offs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two advantages and two disadvantages of MNCs locating in an LIC or NIC.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>MNCs bring jobs, infrastructure and technology to host countries but often pay low wages, repatriate profits and can relocate suddenly. The balance depends on the strength of the host government and labour laws.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Tourism as a Development Driver</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Tourism is now one of the world’s biggest industries, and many LICs and NICs treat it as a fast track to growth. Cheaper flights, the internet and rising HIC incomes mean places like Kenya, Thailand and the Maldives now host millions of international visitors a year. Tourism creates jobs in hotels, transport, food, guiding and crafts, and it brings in foreign currency that can be spent on schools and hospitals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">But tourist money does not always stay where it is spent. <dfn class="term" data-def="Tourism in which visitors stay within a self-contained resort and most spending leaves the host country.">Enclave tourism</dfn> — where visitors stay inside a foreign-owned resort, eat imported food and book through an HIC travel firm — can mean as little as 20% of the price they pay reaches the local economy. Cultural impacts add to the picture: traditional ways of life can become a performance for tourists, and rising prices push locals out of their own neighbourhoods.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Kenya — Tourism’s Two Faces</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Tourism contributes around 10% of Kenya’s GDP and supports more than a million jobs around the Maasai Mara safari parks and Mombasa coast. New airports, roads and water supplies follow. Yet many resorts are foreign-owned, terrorist attacks and political unrest can collapse bookings overnight, and Maasai communities have lost grazing land to private game reserves. The challenge is to spread tourism’s gains while protecting the people and wildlife that draw visitors in the first place.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Putting the Pieces Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Trade rules, MNCs and tourism are not separate stories — they reinforce each other. An MNC opens a textile factory in Bangladesh because trade rules favour exporting clothes to Europe. A trade bloc lowers air-fare taxes, so tourists fly to Kenya instead of holidaying at home. Each decision in a head office in London, Tokyo or New York reshapes opportunities in Lusaka, Hanoi or Mumbai. The development gap is not natural — it is built and rebuilt every day by these connections.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Uneven global development is driven by trade rules (tariffs, quotas, subsidies, primary-product dependence), MNC location decisions and the rise of international tourism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">MNCs bring jobs, technology and infrastructure but often pay low wages, send profits home and can relocate suddenly — the host country’s laws shape the balance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Tourism boosts GDP and employment in countries like Kenya, but enclave resorts and cultural change can limit how much benefit reaches local communities.</li>
</ul>
