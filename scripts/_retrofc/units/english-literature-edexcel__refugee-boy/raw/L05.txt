--- DESCRIPTION ---

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Alem Kelo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Alem is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character in a play or story, through whose eyes the audience experiences the events.">protagonist</dfn> and the emotional centre of the play. He is fourteen years old, of mixed Ethiopian and Eritrean heritage, and is brought to England by his father before being left alone. Everything about Alem is designed to challenge the audience’s assumptions about asylum seekers. He is intelligent, polite, curious, brave, and kind — qualities that make it impossible to dismiss him as a stereotype.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Alem’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a character changes and develops over the course of a story or play.">character arc</dfn> moves from innocence (the hotel with his father), through confusion and grief (the abandonment), to gradual adaptation (school, the Fitzgeralds), and finally to quiet resilience (the tribunal). He does not become a different person — he remains fundamentally the same kind, thoughtful boy throughout. What changes is his understanding of the world and his place in it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: why does Zephaniah present Alem as an ordinary, likeable teenager rather than an extraordinary one?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alem is deliberately presented as an ordinary, likeable teenager placed in an extraordinary situation. Zephaniah’s purpose is to show that refugees are not “other” — they are people just like the audience, whose lives have been disrupted by forces beyond their control.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mr Kelo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Alem’s father is one of the play’s most complex characters. He is a loving, devoted parent who makes the agonising decision to leave his son in a foreign country. His <dfn class="term" data-def="An internal struggle within a character, often between competing desires, duties, or values.">inner conflict</dfn> is visible in the early hotel scenes: he is warm and attentive, but also restless and emotional. The audience senses his pain before understanding its cause.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Mr Kelo embodies the play’s central paradox — that abandonment can be an act of love. He sacrifices his relationship with his son to save Alem’s life. His letter reveals the depth of his anguish and his awareness that Alem will feel betrayed. In essays, analyse Mr Kelo as a character who is torn between his role as a father (wanting to be with his son) and his duty as a protector (knowing Alem is safer alone in England).</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Alem’s mother, Mariam, is largely absent from the play’s present-day action. She may appear in <dfn class="term" data-def="Scenes in a play that show events from the past, interrupting the present-day action to provide background information.">flashback</dfn> sequences, but her primary role is as a figure of loss. Mariam is Eritrean, and her death during the conflict represents the ultimate human cost of the Ethiopian-Eritrean war. Her absence shapes everything: it is part of why Mr Kelo brings Alem to England, and it deepens Alem’s grief and isolation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">In exam essays, Mariam can be discussed in terms of what she represents rather than what she does on stage. She symbolises the innocent civilians destroyed by political violence. Her death also means that Alem has lost both parents in different ways — one to violence, one to sacrifice — making his situation uniquely devastating.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Fitzgeralds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Mr and Mrs Fitzgerald are Alem’s foster parents. They are presented as warm, generous, and genuinely caring. Mrs Fitzgerald in particular takes on a maternal role, providing the emotional support that Alem desperately needs. The Fitzgeralds represent the argument that ordinary people can make a difference — they cannot fix the asylum system, but they can offer one boy a safe, loving home.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">However, the Fitzgeralds are not presented as saviours. They have limitations: they cannot control the outcome of the tribunal, they cannot replace Alem’s family, and their care is always temporary and conditional. Sissay avoids the trap of the “<dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative pattern where a white character rescues or helps a person of colour, reinforcing racial power dynamics.">white saviour</dfn>” narrative by showing the Fitzgeralds as kind but not all-powerful. They help Alem, but they do not solve his problems.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: why is the Fitzgeralds' kindness not enough on its own — what else does Alem need?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Fitzgeralds demonstrate that individual compassion matters, but it is not enough. Alem needs both the Fitzgeralds’ love <em>and</em> a fair asylum system. Sissay uses them to argue that personal kindness must be accompanied by political justice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Mustapha and Sweeney</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Mustapha and Sweeney represent opposing responses to difference. Mustapha, a fellow outsider, offers Alem friendship and solidarity based on shared experience. He understands what it means to be different and chooses empathy. Sweeney, by contrast, responds to difference with hostility and racial abuse. He is not a complex character — he functions as a representative of the prejudice that exists in British society.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">These two characters work as a pair. Together, they present the audience with a choice: which response do <em>you</em> identify with? Sissay does not let the audience remain neutral. By positioning us to empathise with Alem, we are drawn towards Mustapha’s compassion and repelled by Sweeney’s cruelty. This is part of the play’s activist purpose — it does not just depict prejudice, it challenges the audience to reject it.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Ruth: The Human Face of the System</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Ruth is Alem’s case worker within the asylum system. She is compassionate, professional, and genuinely invested in Alem’s welfare. Ruth represents the possibility of decency within institutions — she treats Alem as a person, not just a case file. However, her power is limited. She can advocate for Alem, but she cannot override the tribunal’s decision. Ruth shows that good people exist within flawed systems, but individual goodness alone cannot fix structural problems.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">In essays, Ruth can be compared with the Fitzgeralds: both offer personal compassion within institutional constraints. The difference is that the Fitzgeralds operate in the private sphere (the home) while Ruth operates in the public sphere (the state). Together, they suggest that Alem needs both personal care and systemic support.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Character Relationships and the Play’s Message</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The characters in Refugee Boy are not just individuals — they represent different attitudes and systems. Mr Kelo represents the impossible choices forced on parents by conflict. The Fitzgeralds represent compassion. Mustapha represents solidarity. Sweeney represents prejudice. Ruth represents institutional decency. The tribunal represents systemic power. By examining how these characters relate to Alem, Sissay builds a complete picture of the refugee experience: the personal, the social, and the political.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Alem is deliberately presented as an ordinary, likeable teenager to challenge stereotypes about refugees — his consistency of character is Zephaniah’s most important tool.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Mr Kelo embodies the play’s central paradox: abandonment as an act of love. His inner conflict makes him one of the most complex characters.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Each character represents an attitude or system — compassion, prejudice, solidarity, institutional power — and together they build a complete picture of the refugee experience.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">In character-based essay questions, always link character to theme and purpose. Do not just describe what a character does — explain what they represent and why Zephaniah and Sissay chose to present them that way. For example, argue that the Fitzgeralds show the limits of individual compassion without systemic justice. This kind of analysis demonstrates AO1 (knowledge of the text) and AO3 (understanding of context and purpose) simultaneously.</p>
