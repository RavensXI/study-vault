--- DESCRIPTION ---
Capulet forces Paris on Juliet, driving her to the Friar’s desperate sleeping-potion plan.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Capulet’s Ultimatum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After Tybalt’s death, Lord Capulet decides that Juliet must marry <dfn class="term" data-def="A young nobleman and kinsman of the Prince. Capulet chooses him as Juliet’s husband, unaware that she is already secretly married to Romeo.">Paris</dfn> immediately. Earlier in the play, Capulet seemed a reasonable father — he told Paris to “woo her, gentle Paris, get her heart; / My will to her consent is but a part.” But grief and anger over Tybalt’s death have hardened him. He now gives Juliet no choice: the wedding will happen on Thursday.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">When Juliet refuses, Capulet explodes with terrifying rage: “Hang thee, young baggage! Disobedient wretch! … An you be mine, I’ll give you to my friend; / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets.” He threatens to disown her entirely. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of a list of words or phrases in quick succession, building intensity and emotional impact.">listing</dfn> of “hang, beg, starve, die” is brutal — each verb is a short, violent stab. Shakespeare reveals the reality beneath Capulet’s earlier courtesy: Juliet is his property, and her obedience is not optional.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall what three specific consequences an Elizabethan daughter faced for defying her father's marriage choice."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Capulet’s fury reflects Elizabethan <dfn class="term" data-def="A social system in which the father or oldest male is the head of the family and authority passes through the male line.">patriarchal</dfn> attitudes. A daughter who defied her father risked being disowned, left penniless, or even sent to a convent. Shakespeare exposes the cruelty of a system that treats daughters as objects to be traded in marriage.</p></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lady Capulet & the Nurse Abandon Juliet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Juliet turns to her mother for support, but Lady Capulet refuses: “Talk not to me, for I’ll not speak a word. / Do as thou wilt, for I have done with thee.” Lady Capulet’s cold rejection is devastating. She sides with her husband, not her daughter. Shakespeare presents Lady Capulet as a woman trapped by the same patriarchal system — she may sympathise with Juliet privately, but she cannot oppose her husband publicly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Nurse’s betrayal is even more painful. Juliet’s closest confidante advises her to forget Romeo and marry Paris: “I think it best you married with the County … Romeo’s a dishclout to him.” The Nurse’s advice is pragmatic but emotionally crushing. From Juliet’s perspective, every adult has now failed her — her father threatens her, her mother abandons her, and her Nurse betrays her. She is completely alone.</p>

<div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Juliet’s Aside: “Ancient Damnation”</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n8">After the Nurse leaves, Juliet delivers a bitter <dfn class="term" data-def="Words spoken by a character that are heard by the audience but not by the other characters on stage.">aside</dfn>: “Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend! … Go, counsellor. / Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain.” She calls the Nurse a “wicked fiend” and vows never to confide in her again. This is a decisive moment — Juliet cuts off her last source of support and turns to the Friar instead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Shakespeare uses the Nurse’s betrayal to isolate Juliet completely. Without any ally except the Friar, she becomes vulnerable to his risky, untested plan. The adults who should protect Juliet are the ones who push her towards death.</p></div></div></div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Friar’s Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Juliet goes to Friar Lawrence in desperation. She arrives carrying a knife and threatens to kill herself rather than marry Paris. The Friar, alarmed, offers an alternative: a <dfn class="term" data-def="A liquid preparation used as a medicine, poison, or magical substance. The Friar gives Juliet a sleeping potion that mimics death for forty-two hours.">potion</dfn> that will make her appear dead for forty-two hours. Her family will place her in the Capulet tomb, the Friar will send word to Romeo, and Romeo will rescue her when she wakes. They will flee to Mantua together.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The plan is dangerously complex and relies on perfect timing. If Romeo does not receive the message, if Juliet wakes too early or too late, if the potion does not work exactly as described — disaster is inevitable. Shakespeare highlights the fragility of the Friar’s scheme to create unbearable suspense. The audience, who already know from the Prologue that the lovers will die, can see exactly how the plan will fail.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain how the Friar's potion connects to his earlier speech about herbs containing both 'poison' and 'medicine'."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The Friar’s sleeping potion echoes his earlier speech about herbs containing both “poison” and “medicine.” The potion is designed to save Juliet, but it will ultimately cause Romeo’s suicide. Shakespeare shows that the Friar’s good intentions produce catastrophic results.</p></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Juliet Takes the Potion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Alone in her chamber the night before the wedding, Juliet delivers a powerful soliloquy. She imagines the horrors of waking in the tomb: “Shall I not then be stifled in the vault, / To whose foul mouth no healthsome air breathes in, / And there die strangled ere my Romeo comes?” She pictures the rotting corpse of Tybalt, imagines going mad with fear, and wonders if the Friar has given her real poison to cover up his involvement in her secret marriage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Despite her terror, Juliet drinks the potion. Her courage is extraordinary — she faces the prospect of suffocating in a tomb surrounded by dead bodies, alone and in darkness. Shakespeare presents Juliet’s bravery as far exceeding Romeo’s: while Romeo threatens suicide in a fit of emotion (Act 3), Juliet methodically plans and executes a terrifying act of self-preservation. Her final words before drinking are: “Romeo, Romeo, Romeo! Here’s drink. I drink to thee.”</p>

<div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Dramatic Irony: The Capulets’ Grief</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n17">When the Capulets discover Juliet apparently dead on her wedding morning, their grief is intense. Capulet cries: “Death lies on her like an untimely frost / Upon the sweetest flower of all the field.” The simile is beautiful, but the audience knows Juliet is not dead — she is merely sleeping. This creates agonising dramatic irony.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Shakespeare also uses this moment to criticise the Capulets. Their grief is genuine, but it comes too late — Capulet’s cruelty and Lady Capulet’s coldness drove Juliet to this desperate act. The Friar, meanwhile, must act sorrowful while knowing the truth. The entire scene is layered with deception, showing how secrecy has corrupted every relationship in the play.</p></div></div></div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the four adults who fail Juliet in Act 4, leaving her completely isolated except for one person's reckless help."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Act 4 shows Juliet completely isolated. Her father, mother, and Nurse all fail her. Only the Friar offers help, and his plan is reckless. Shakespeare uses Juliet’s solitude to build sympathy and to show that the tragedy is caused not by the lovers’ flaws, but by the failures of the adult world.</p></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Capulet’s fury and the threats of disownment reveal the brutal reality of patriarchal control. Lady Capulet and the Nurse both abandon Juliet, leaving her completely isolated.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Friar’s sleeping-potion plan is dangerously complex and relies on perfect timing. The audience already knows it will fail, creating unbearable dramatic irony.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Juliet’s soliloquy before taking the potion shows extraordinary courage. Shakespeare presents her as braver and more decisive than any adult in the play.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">Juliet’s potion soliloquy (Act 4, Scene 3) is an excellent passage for analysing fear, courage, and isolation. If asked about Juliet as a character, use this scene to show her bravery — but always link it to Shakespeare’s purpose. He presents Juliet’s courage to condemn the patriarchal system that forces a thirteen-year-old girl into such a desperate situation.</p>
