Breaking problems apart, hiding unnecessary detail and using subprograms to model the real world.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Computational Thinking?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A problem-solving approach that involves breaking down complex problems, removing unnecessary detail, and designing step-by-step solutions so they can be carried out systematically.">Computational thinking</dfn> is a structured way of approaching problems so they can be solved clearly and efficiently. It is not about using a computer &mdash; it is about <em>thinking</em> in a way that makes complex problems manageable. Two of the most important techniques are <strong>decomposition</strong> and <strong>abstraction</strong>. Together, they form the foundation of everything you will study in GCSE Computer Science.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the definitions of decomposition and abstraction. Write one sentence for each without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Decomposition and abstraction are two complementary techniques. Decomposition breaks a large problem into smaller sub-problems. Abstraction removes unnecessary detail so you can focus on what actually matters. Both are used by professional developers every time they start a new project.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Decomposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Breaking a complex problem down into a number of smaller sub-problems, each of which accomplishes an identifiable task that can be solved more easily on its own.">Decomposition</dfn> means breaking a large, complex problem into smaller, more manageable sub-problems. Each sub-problem accomplishes a specific, identifiable task. Sub-problems can themselves be broken down further, creating a hierarchy of manageable tasks &mdash; a technique sometimes called <em>stepwise refinement</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Consider building a mobile banking app. At the top level it is one enormous problem. Decomposed, it becomes: user authentication, account balance display, payment processing, transaction history, and security alerts. Each of those can be decomposed further &mdash; payment processing breaks into input validation, fraud checks, bank transfer protocols, and confirmation messages. Each piece is now small enough for a single developer (or a team) to tackle independently.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Suppose you are asked to design an online GP appointment booking system. Decomposing the problem gives at least four identifiable sub-problems:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Patient login</strong> &mdash; verify the patient&rsquo;s identity securely</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Show available slots</strong> &mdash; fetch free appointment times from the system</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Book a slot</strong> &mdash; record the booking and prevent double-booking</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Send confirmation</strong> &mdash; email or SMS reminder to the patient</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Each sub-problem is small enough to design, code and test independently. A team of four developers could work on all four sub-problems simultaneously, cutting development time.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Abstraction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing unnecessary detail from a problem, keeping only the information that is relevant to solving it.">Abstraction</dfn> means removing unnecessary detail so you can focus on the information that actually matters. It is not about making things simpler than they are &mdash; it is about identifying which details are relevant and filtering out everything else.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The London Underground map is a classic example. Real tube lines curve, dip underground, and vary widely in the spacing of stations. A geographically accurate map would be too cluttered to use. The Underground map abstracts away distances and precise geography, keeping only the connections between stations and their order. That is all a passenger needs to plan a journey.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In programming, abstraction happens constantly. A variable named <code>playerScore</code> hides the actual memory address where the value is stored. A subprogram called <code>sendPaymentConfirmation()</code> hides dozens of lines of networking and formatting code behind a single meaningful name. Programmers use abstraction to manage complexity at every level of a system.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Give one everyday example of abstraction and explain what detail has been removed and why it is not needed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When designing an algorithm, you make abstraction decisions: what are the <strong>inputs</strong>? What <strong>processing</strong> happens on them? What are the <strong>outputs</strong>? Everything else &mdash; the hardware, the user interface appearance, the storage format &mdash; is abstracted away from the algorithm itself.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Benefits of Using Subprograms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Decomposition naturally leads to <dfn class="term" data-def="A named, self-contained block of code that performs a specific task and can be called from different points in a program.">subprograms</dfn> &mdash; named, self-contained blocks of code that each solve one sub-problem. Your specification identifies four key benefits of structuring programs using subprograms.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Four Benefits of Subprograms &mdash; Explained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>1. <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to call a subprogram from multiple points in a program without rewriting the code it contains.">Reusability</dfn>.</strong> A subprogram is written once but can be called from many different points in a program. A <code>validateEmail()</code> subprogram used in the registration form can be called again on the login page and on the password-reset page without rewriting the logic. This reduces the total amount of code and the likelihood of inconsistencies.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>2. Testing in isolation.</strong> A subprogram can be tested on its own, with known input values, before the rest of the program is written. If <code>calculateDiscount()</code> is wrong, the bug is contained in one place and is straightforward to find and fix. Testing individual subprograms separately is far easier than trying to find an error in thousands of lines of combined code.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>3. Reducing complexity.</strong> Breaking a program into subprograms means that any developer reading the code sees short, clearly-named blocks rather than one enormous sequence. The overall structure is immediately visible at a high level, and each detail is hidden inside its relevant subprogram.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>4. Division of labour.</strong> A team can divide the subprograms between developers and work in parallel. One developer writes the payment-processing subprogram; another writes the notification subprogram. As long as both subprograms take the agreed inputs and return the agreed outputs, they can be developed simultaneously and integrated afterwards.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Modelling Real-World Problems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Decomposition and abstraction together allow programmers to build models of real-world systems. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A simplified representation of a real-world system that captures its essential features and behaviours while ignoring irrelevant detail.">model</dfn> captures the essential features of a system without representing every physical detail. A sat-nav models a road network as a graph of nodes (junctions) and edges (roads) &mdash; it abstracts away the width of the lane, the colour of the tarmac, and the trees at the roadside, retaining only distances, speed limits, and connectivity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The same principle applies at every scale. A school registration system models students as records containing a name, year group, and a set of attendance flags &mdash; the model does not need to know students&rsquo; heights or favourite subjects. Identifying what to include and what to leave out is an act of abstraction, and it is the first design decision in any software project.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example &mdash; Decomposing and Abstracting a Social Media Notification System</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Problem: build the part of a social media app that sends a notification when someone likes your post.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Decomposition:</strong> (1)&nbsp;Detect a like event on a post. (2)&nbsp;Look up the post owner&rsquo;s notification preferences. (3)&nbsp;Format the notification message. (4)&nbsp;Deliver the notification (push alert, email, or in-app badge). Each of these becomes a separate subprogram.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Abstraction applied:</strong> The &ldquo;detect like&rdquo; subprogram does not need to know the post&rsquo;s content, the liker&rsquo;s age, or what device the post owner is using. It only needs: the post&rsquo;s ID and the ID of the user who liked it. Everything else is irrelevant detail that can safely be abstracted away.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Inputs:</strong> post ID, liker&rsquo;s user ID. <strong>Processing:</strong> retrieve owner, check preferences, format message. <strong>Output:</strong> notification delivered to the post owner.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Decomposition breaks a complex problem into smaller sub-problems, each accomplishing an identifiable task that can be solved, coded and tested independently.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Abstraction removes unnecessary detail, keeping only the information relevant to solving the problem &mdash; inputs, processing and outputs are identified; everything else is filtered out.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Subprograms are named, self-contained blocks of code that deliver four key benefits: reusability, testing in isolation, reduced complexity, and division of labour across a development team.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">For a 4-mark <strong>Explain</strong> question, make sure every factual point is followed by a consequence or reason &mdash; examiners deduct marks for descriptions that simply state what happens without explaining <em>why</em> it matters. When a question asks you to describe the benefits of subprograms, identify each benefit by name (reusability, testing in isolation, reducing complexity, division of labour) and then explain how that benefit applies in the scenario given. For a 6-mark <strong>Discuss</strong> question, you must explore <em>both</em> sides &mdash; for example, subprograms are beneficial but they add an overhead of designing clear interfaces between them, which requires careful planning.</p>
