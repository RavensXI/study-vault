--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Series Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected end to end in a single loop, so the same current flows through every component.">series circuit</dfn>, all components are connected end to end in a single loop. There is only one path for the current to follow.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">In a series circuit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Current</strong> is the same through every component. There is only one path, so the same charge flows through each part: \(I_{\text{total}} = I_1 = I_2 = I_3\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Potential difference</strong> is shared between the components. The total potential difference of the supply equals the sum of the potential differences across each component: \(V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Resistance</strong> adds up. The total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances: \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the three rules for current, voltage, and resistance in a series circuit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a series circuit: current is the same everywhere, potential differences add up to the supply voltage, and resistances add up.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Parallel Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected across each other, creating separate branches so the current can take different paths.">parallel circuit</dfn>, components are connected across each other on separate branches. The current has more than one path to follow.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">In a parallel circuit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Current</strong> splits at junctions. The total current from the supply equals the sum of the currents through each branch: \(I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3\). Current is <dfn class="term" data-def="The total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it.">conserved</dfn> at every junction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Potential difference</strong> is the same across each branch. Every component connected in parallel has the same potential difference across it: \(V_{\text{total}} = V_1 = V_2 = V_3\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Resistance</strong> decreases. Adding more branches gives the current more paths to flow through, so the total resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the three rules for current, voltage, and resistance in a parallel circuit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a parallel circuit: the potential difference is the same across each branch, currents through each branch add up to the total current, and the total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Energy in Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">When charge flows through a resistor, the electrical energy is transferred to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy related to the temperature of an object — when a resistor heats up, energy is transferred to its thermal store.">thermal store</dfn> of the resistor, which heats up. This happens because the moving electrons collide with the metal ions in the lattice, transferring energy to them and making them vibrate more. This energy is then dissipated to the thermal store of the surroundings.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n25">We can reduce unwanted heating by using wires with lower resistance. Thicker wires and shorter wires have lower resistance, which means less energy is wasted as heat.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Electrical Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred — measured in watts (W). One watt equals one joule per second.">Power</dfn> is the rate at which energy is transferred, measured in watts (W). One watt equals one joule of energy transferred per second. There are three key power equations:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>\(P = \frac{E}{t}\)</strong> — power equals energy transferred divided by time</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>\(P = I \times V\)</strong> — power equals current times potential difference</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>\(P = I^2 \times R\)</strong> — power equals current squared times resistance</p>

</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n34">
  <p><strong>Exam Tip:</strong> Learn the series and parallel rules as a set of contrasts. In series, current stays the same and voltages add up. In parallel, voltage stays the same and currents add up. Examiners frequently test whether you can apply these rules to unfamiliar circuits.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion" data-narration-id="n35">
  <p>Series and parallel circuits follow clear, predictable rules. Knowing how current, potential difference, and resistance behave in each type of circuit — and being able to calculate power and energy transfer — is essential for the electricity section of the exam.</p>
</div>
