<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Electric Charge and Current</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of flow of electric charge around a circuit — measured in amperes (A).">Electric current</dfn> is the rate of flow of <dfn class="term" data-def="A property of particles such as electrons and protons — measured in coulombs (C).">charge</dfn> around a circuit. In metals, the current is carried by electrons, which are negatively charged. When a potential difference is applied across a circuit, electrons flow from the negative terminal of the battery through the wires and components to the positive terminal.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Current is measured in <dfn class="term" data-def="The SI unit of electric current — symbol A. One ampere is one coulomb of charge flowing per second.">amperes</dfn> (A) using an <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument used to measure electric current — connected in series with the component being measured.">ammeter</dfn>, which is always connected in series with the component being measured.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the charge equation with all its units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Charge, current, and time are linked by the equation: \(Q = I \times t\), where \(Q\) is charge in coulombs (C), \(I\) is current in amperes (A), and \(t\) is time in seconds (s).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Potential Difference</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred per unit charge passed between two points in a circuit — measured in volts (V). One volt equals one joule per coulomb.">Potential difference</dfn> (often called voltage) is the energy transferred per unit charge that passes between two points in a circuit. One volt means one joule of energy is transferred for every coulomb of charge that passes. It is measured using a <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument used to measure potential difference — connected in parallel across the component being measured.">voltmeter</dfn>, which is always connected in parallel across the component being measured.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The equation linking energy transferred, charge, and potential difference is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>\(E = Q \times V\)</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where \(E\) is energy transferred in joules (J), \(Q\) is charge in coulombs (C), and \(V\) is potential difference in volts (V).</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Resistance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how much a component opposes the flow of charge — measured in ohms (Ω).">Resistance</dfn> is a measure of how difficult it is for charge to flow through a component. A high resistance means less current flows for a given potential difference. Resistance is measured in ohms (\(\Omega\)).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">The relationship between potential difference, current, and resistance is given by:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>\(V = I \times R\)</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Where \(V\) is potential difference in volts (V), \(I\) is current in amperes (A), and \(R\) is resistance in ohms (\(\Omega\)). This can be rearranged to \(I = \frac{V}{R}\) or \(R = \frac{V}{I}\).</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A resistor has a resistance of 20 \(\Omega\). The current through it is 0.5 A. Calculate the potential difference across it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">\(V = I \times R = 0.5 \times 20 = 10\) V</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down V = IR, Q = It, and E = QV from memory with all units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three core circuit equations are: \(V = I \times R\) (Ohm’s law), \(Q = I \times t\) (charge), and \(E = Q \times V\) (energy transferred).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">I–V Characteristics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The relationship between current and potential difference can be shown on an <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph showing how the current through a component varies with the potential difference across it.">I–V graph</dfn>. Different components produce different characteristic graphs:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fixed resistor, filament lamp, and diode</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Fixed resistor:</strong> The I–V graph is a straight line through the origin. Current is directly proportional to potential difference, meaning the resistance stays constant. This component obeys <dfn class="term" data-def="At constant temperature, the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.">Ohm’s law</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Filament lamp:</strong> The graph is a curve that becomes less steep at higher potential differences. As the current increases, the filament heats up, which increases its resistance. The lamp does not obey Ohm’s law.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Diode:</strong> Current flows in one direction only (forward bias). In reverse bias, essentially no current flows. The I–V graph shows current increasing rapidly once the threshold voltage (about 0.7 V for silicon) is reached in the forward direction, and zero current in the reverse direction.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Thermistors and LDRs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Some components have a resistance that changes with conditions:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A resistor whose resistance decreases as temperature increases (negative temperature coefficient type).">Thermistor</dfn></strong> (NTC type) — resistance decreases as temperature increases. Used in temperature sensors and thermostats. <strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A resistor whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases.">LDR</dfn></strong> (light-dependent resistor) — resistance decreases as light intensity increases. Used in automatic lighting systems and light meters.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state how resistance changes with temperature for a thermistor and with light for an LDR.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thermistor (NTC): resistance decreases as temperature increases. LDR: resistance decreases as light intensity increases. Both are used as sensors in control circuits.</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n27">
  <p><strong>Exam Tip:</strong> Always connect an ammeter in series and a voltmeter in parallel. If asked to draw a circuit to investigate resistance, include a battery, ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel across the component, and a variable resistor to change the current.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion" data-narration-id="n28">
  <p>Current, potential difference, and resistance are the three fundamental quantities in circuit physics. Master the equations \(V = I \times R\), \(Q = I \times t\), and \(E = Q \times V\), and understand how different components (fixed resistors, filament lamps, diodes, thermistors, and LDRs) behave.</p>
</div>
