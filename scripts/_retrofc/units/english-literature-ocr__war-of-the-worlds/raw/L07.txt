<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Imperialism and Colonialism</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel’s central theme is <dfn class="term" data-def="A country extending its power over other territories through colonisation and military force.">imperialism</dfn> reversed. The narrator explicitly draws the parallel: “Before we judge [the Martians] too harshly, we must remember what ruthless and utter destruction our own species has wrought.” Wells forces Victorian readers to see the invasion as what Britain does to other nations. The Martians’ treatment of humans mirrors European treatment of colonised peoples.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Wells extends this critique to the Tasmanian genocide, citing it directly. The Martians view humans as Europeans viewed “primitive” peoples: as obstacles to be removed. This was a radical challenge to Victorian imperial confidence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does Wells use the Martian invasion as a critique of British imperialism?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells explicitly compares the invasion to British colonialism, citing the Tasmanian genocide. The Martians treat humans exactly as Europeans treated colonised peoples. By reversing the power dynamic, Wells forces readers to empathise with those Britain colonised.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Evolution and Human Insignificance</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Wells draws on <dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific theory that species change over time through natural selection.">Darwinian evolution</dfn> to suggest that humanity is not the pinnacle of creation. The Martians are more evolved but more horrifying. The bacteria that defeat them are less evolved but equally powerful. Wells positions humanity in the middle — not special, not permanent, just one species among many.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The theme of evolution connects to Victorian anxiety about degeneration. If species can evolve, they can also devolve. The Martians, reduced to brains feeding on blood, represent evolution taken to a disturbing extreme.</p>

His question — “Why are these things permitted?” — receives no answer. Wells, an atheist, suggests that religious frameworks are inadequate when confronted with a universe that is indifferent to human suffering.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Fragility of Civilisation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Wells shows <dfn class="term" data-def="The established social structures, laws, institutions and norms that organise human society.">civilisation</dfn> collapsing with terrifying speed. Social hierarchies dissolve, laws become meaningless, and survival depends on individual resourcefulness rather than social position. The London evacuation scenes show respectable Victorians reduced to panicking refugees — a deliberate inversion of the colonial relationship.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Class in Crisis</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Wells shows class distinctions becoming irrelevant during the invasion. The wealthy cannot buy safety; the poor are no more vulnerable than anyone else. The artilleryman’s fantasy of selecting only the “strong” satirises Social Darwinism — the idea that class hierarchy is natural and necessary.</p>

  </div></div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how Wells presents civilisation as fragile.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Civilisation collapses within days: social hierarchies dissolve, laws become meaningless, millions panic. Wells shows that the social order Victorians took for granted is a thin veneer over chaos. Only individual resourcefulness, not social position, determines survival.</p>
</div>
