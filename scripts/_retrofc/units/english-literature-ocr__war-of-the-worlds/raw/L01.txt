--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">H.G. Wells — Life and Ideas</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">H.G. Wells (1866–1946) published <em>The War of the Worlds</em> in 1898, at the height of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The policy of extending a country’s power through colonisation, often justified by beliefs about racial and cultural superiority.">British Empire</dfn>. Wells was a trained scientist and socialist who used science fiction to critique Victorian society. The novel imagines what would happen if Britain — the world’s greatest colonial power — were itself colonised by a technologically superior force.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Wells was influenced by Charles Darwin’s theory of <dfn class="term" data-def="Darwin’s theory that species evolve through natural selection, where the best-adapted organisms survive and reproduce.">evolution</dfn> and by anxieties about the future of humanity. The Martians represent what humans might become — hyper-intelligent but emotionless, reduced to pure intellect. The novel is both a thrilling adventure and a warning about the dangers of unchecked technological and imperial power.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is the central analogy of The War of the Worlds?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells uses the Martian invasion as a reverse colonialism analogy. Victorian Britain, the world’s greatest imperial power, is itself colonised by a technologically superior force. Wells forces readers to empathise with colonised peoples.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Imperialism and the Reverse Invasion</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The central <dfn class="term" data-def="A comparison between two otherwise unlike things, used to illuminate a point.">analogy</dfn> of the novel is imperialism in reverse. The narrator explicitly compares the Martian invasion to British colonialism: “Before we judge them too harshly, we must remember what ruthless and utter destruction our own species has wrought.” Wells forces Victorian readers to see themselves as the colonised, not the colonisers.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The Martians treat humans exactly as Europeans treated colonised peoples: as inferior beings to be displaced or destroyed. Wells deliberately sets the invasion in Surrey — the comfortable heart of middle-class England — to make the horror personal and immediate.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Martians are described as mostly brain: “huge round bodies—or rather heads—about four feet in diameter, each body having in front of it a face.” They have evolved beyond physical bodies, feeding directly on blood. Wells suggests they represent humanity’s future if intellect develops at the expense of compassion. Their technology is devastating but their biology is repulsive — progress without humanity.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Science and Victorian Anxiety</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">The late Victorian period was marked by scientific optimism but also deep anxiety. Darwin’s theories raised uncomfortable questions about human significance. If species evolve and go extinct, humanity might not be permanent. The Martians embody this fear: they are more evolved but also more horrifying, suggesting that progress does not guarantee improvement.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Wells’s Socialist Critique</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Wells was a committed <dfn class="term" data-def="A political ideology advocating collective ownership and equal distribution of wealth.">socialist</dfn> who believed Victorian class divisions were as destructive as any alien invasion. The novel shows society collapsing under stress: the wealthy flee, social hierarchies dissolve, and survival depends on adaptability, not privilege. The curate’s cowardice and the artilleryman’s fantasies of rebuilding society both satirise different responses to crisis.</p>

  </div></div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three Victorian anxieties that Wells draws on in the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells draws on anxieties about imperialism (what if we were colonised?), evolution (what if a superior species exists?), and technology (what if progress makes us inhuman?). The Martians represent the dark side of all three.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Wells uses the Martian invasion as reverse colonialism, forcing Victorian readers to empathise with colonised peoples.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">The Martians embody anxieties about evolution, technology, and progress without compassion.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Wells critiques Victorian class divisions by showing how social hierarchies collapse under alien attack.</li></ul>
