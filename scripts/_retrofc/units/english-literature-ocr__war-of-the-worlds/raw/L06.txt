--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">First-Person Narration and Reliability</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Wells chooses a <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative told from the ‘I’ perspective, limiting the reader’s knowledge to what the narrator experiences.">first-person narrator</dfn> to create immediacy and identification. We share the narrator’s confusion, terror and gradual understanding. But this perspective is deliberately limited — the narrator cannot explain the Martians’ technology, understand their motives, or see the full picture of the invasion. This ignorance mirrors the experience of colonised peoples facing incomprehensible foreign technology.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The narrative switches to the brother’s perspective for the London evacuation chapters. This structural shift widens the scope while maintaining the personal, eyewitness quality that makes the novel so compelling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does Wells use a first-person narrator with limited knowledge?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The narrator’s limited perspective mirrors the experience of colonised peoples facing incomprehensible technology. His confusion creates immediacy and identification. Wells deliberately restricts information to make the reader share the terror of the unknown.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Episodic Structure and Pacing</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The novel is structured as a series of episodes: the landing, the Heat-Ray, the tripods, flight, entrapment, survival, resolution. Each episode escalates the threat and strips away another layer of the narrator’s (and the reader’s) confidence. Wells paces the novel carefully, alternating between action and reflection, chaos and claustrophobia.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary genre that uses imagined scientific or technological developments as a framework for exploring social and philosophical ideas.">science fiction</dfn> genre allows Wells to examine real-world issues (imperialism, class, religion, technology) through a fantastical lens. The alien invasion is the vehicle; the social critique is the purpose.</p>

It allows Wells to show the London evacuation without moving the narrator out of his storyline. The two perspectives create a more complete picture while maintaining the personal quality of eyewitness testimony.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Documentary Realism</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Wells writes with a documentary quality that gives the novel its power. He names real places (Woking, Chertsey, London), uses precise technical language, and describes events with scientific detachment. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary approach that aims to represent events accurately and objectively, as if documenting reality.">realism</dfn> makes the impossible feel plausible — it is one of the earliest examples of science fiction grounded in contemporary geography and technology.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Science Fiction as Social Critique</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Wells uses the genre to ask: what if a more powerful civilisation treated us the way we treat others? Science fiction provides distance — readers engage with ideas about imperialism and class through the lens of alien invasion, making the critique less confrontational but more effective.</p>

  </div></div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how Wells uses real place names to create documentary realism.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wells sets the invasion in real Surrey and London locations, using precise geographical detail. This makes the impossible feel plausible and forces Victorian readers to imagine their own familiar world destroyed. It is one of the earliest examples of science fiction grounded in contemporary reality.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Wells uses science fiction conventions as vehicles for social critique, not just entertainment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Documentary realism forces readers to engage with the political allegory.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The two-book structure creates both suspense and thematic depth.</li></ul>
