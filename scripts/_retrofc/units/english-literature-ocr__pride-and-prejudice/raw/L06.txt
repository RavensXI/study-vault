<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Austen’s Ironic Narrative Voice</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Austen’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The distinctive voice through which a story is told, shaping the reader’s interpretation.">narrative voice</dfn> operates through irony: the narrator often states the opposite of what she means. The opening sentence presents social prejudice as “universal truth.” This ironic distance runs through every page, shaping how we judge characters without ever directly telling us what to think.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The narrator is often close to Elizabeth’s perspective but is not identical to it. We share Elizabeth’s amusement and anger, but the narrator subtly distances herself when Elizabeth’s judgment fails. This creates <dfn class="term" data-def="When the reader understands more than the character, creating tension or humour.">dramatic irony</dfn> — the reader sees what Elizabeth cannot.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is free indirect discourse and how does Austen use it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Free indirect discourse blends the narrator’s voice with a character’s thoughts, creating dramatic irony. The reader recognises Elizabeth’s errors before she does because the narrator’s ironic distance reveals what the character’s limited perspective cannot.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Free Indirect Discourse</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique blending the narrator’s voice with a character’s thoughts without direct speech markers.">Free indirect discourse</dfn> is Austen’s signature technique. When we read “Darcy had never been so bewitched by any woman,” we are inside Darcy’s consciousness but hearing the narrator’s words. This blending creates dramatic irony: we simultaneously experience feelings and recognise limitations.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Consider: “Elizabeth, feeling really anxious, was determined to go.” The word “really” feels like Elizabeth’s own thought inserted into the narrator’s sentence. This creates intimacy while maintaining analytical distance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Dialogue as Characterisation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Austen uses dialogue more than description to reveal character. Collins’s proposal exposes his pomposity without narrator commentary. Darcy’s first proposal reveals his pride through his own language. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre that satirises social behaviour and conventions of a particular class.">comedy of manners</dfn> relies on the gap between social performance and private reality.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Collins’s Proposal as Comedy of Manners</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Collins lists his “reasons for marrying” without mentioning love. His language is pompous: “Almost as soon as I entered the house, I singled you out.” The word “singled out” makes Elizabeth sound like an item selected from a shelf. Austen needs no narrator commentary — Collins condemns himself.</p>

  </div></div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and give two examples of Austen’s irony.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The opening sentence presents social prejudice as “universal truth.” Collins’s proposal lists “reasons” without love. Darcy’s first proposal insults more than it flatters. Irony is Austen’s primary tool for social critique.</p>
</div>
