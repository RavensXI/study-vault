<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Format Overview</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The Pride and Prejudice question will appear on your 19th-century novel paper. It assesses <dfn class="term" data-def="Assessment Objectives: AO1 (response), AO2 (methods and effects), AO3 (context).">AO1, AO2 and AO3</dfn>. This is typically a closed-text exam — you will not have the novel in front of you. You may face an extract-based or discursive question. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact format, mark weightings and timing.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Both question types require clear argument, embedded quotations, and woven Assessment Objectives. The examiner rewards responses that analyse Austen’s methods (irony, free indirect discourse, dialogue, symbolism) and embed Regency context within that analysis.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What are the three Assessment Objectives?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AO1 = ideas and textual references. AO2 = analysis of methods and effects. AO3 = understanding of context. The examiner wants all three woven together, not as separate sections.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Structuring an Analytical Response</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Use the WHAT-HOW-WHY model: WHAT (state your point, AO1), HOW (analyse method with quotation, AO2), WHY (embed context, AO3). Begin with a clear <dfn class="term" data-def="The central argument running through an entire essay.">thesis</dfn> and aim for 4–5 analytical paragraphs, adjusting to the time you are given for the question.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Common mistakes: retelling the plot, bolting on context as a separate paragraph, writing about the novel without naming techniques. Every sentence should analyse Austen’s methods and their effects within a sustained argument.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Quotation Skills</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Memorise short <dfn class="term" data-def="A quotation woven seamlessly into your own sentence.">embedded quotations</dfn> per theme: pride (“tolerable, but not handsome enough”), prejudice (“I could easily forgive his pride”), self-knowledge (“Till this moment I never knew myself”), marriage (“I ask only a comfortable home”), class (“Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?”).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Common Mistakes to Avoid</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Plot retelling: The examiner knows the story. Bolted-on context: embed it within analysis. Missing AO2: always name techniques (irony, free indirect discourse, dialogue) and explain their effect.</p>

  </div></div>
</div>


<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write five key quotations from memory, each linked to a different theme.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Short embedded quotations are more effective than long passages. Memorise 2–3 per theme and practise weaving them into analytical sentences.</p>
</div>
