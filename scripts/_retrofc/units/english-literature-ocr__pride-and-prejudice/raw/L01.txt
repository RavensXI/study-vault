<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jane Austen — Life and Influences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane Austen (1775–1817) published <em>Pride and Prejudice</em> in 1813. She was the daughter of a country clergyman and lived within the <dfn class="term" data-def="The social class below the aristocracy who owned land and lived on rental income.">landed gentry</dfn> — the class she writes about with precision and irony. Austen never married, and her novels explore the limited choices available to women with sharp social commentary.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For the OCR J352 exam, context (AO3) must be embedded within analysis. Austen’s world is one of strict hierarchy, where a woman’s future depended almost entirely on the man she married. Every conversation about money in the novel is a matter of survival, not mere romance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why was marriage so crucial for women of Elizabeth Bennet’s class?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Bennet estate is <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal restriction requiring property to pass to a specific male heir.">entailed</dfn> to Mr Collins, meaning the five Bennet sisters will have no home when their father dies. Mrs Bennet’s desperation to marry her daughters off is economic survival, not mere social ambition.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Marriage Market</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The famous opening — “It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife” — is deeply <dfn class="term" data-def="Where the intended meaning is the opposite of the literal meaning.">ironic</dfn>. The “truth” is actually the assumption of mothers like Mrs Bennet. Austen immediately signals her novel will examine the gap between what society claims and what motivates people.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Austen presents four models of marriage: Charlotte/Collins (pragmatic), Lydia/Wickham (reckless), Jane/Bingley (gentle affection), Elizabeth/Darcy (intellectual equality). Each reflects a different response to <dfn class="term" data-def="The period 1811–1820 when the Prince Regent ruled in place of King George III.">Regency</dfn> pressures.</p>

Charlotte Lucas’s decision to marry Collins is entirely rational: “I am not romantic, you know; I never was. I ask only a comfortable home.” Austen asks whether Elizabeth’s idealism is a luxury only a younger, prettier woman can afford.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four marriages in the novel and what each represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four marriages explore the range of choices: Charlotte/Collins = survival, Lydia/Wickham = recklessness, Jane/Bingley = affection, Elizabeth/Darcy = intellectual equality achieved through overcoming pride and prejudice.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n12">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n13">Austen uses irony to expose the gap between social ideals and economic realities in Regency England.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n14">Marriage was the primary route to financial security for women — the entail makes Mrs Bennet’s urgency a survival matter.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n15">The four marriages represent different responses to class, money and social expectation.</li></ul>
