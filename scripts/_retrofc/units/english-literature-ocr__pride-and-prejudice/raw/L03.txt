--- DESCRIPTION ---

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Wickham’s False Charm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Mr Wickham arrives in Meryton as a handsome, charming militia officer. He quickly befriends Elizabeth and tells her that Darcy cheated him out of a valuable <dfn class="term" data-def="A position as clergyman with an attached income, typically offered by a wealthy landowner.">living</dfn> left to him by Darcy’s father. Elizabeth believes him immediately because his story confirms her existing prejudice against Darcy. Austen shows how prejudice makes us gullible: we trust people who tell us what we already want to believe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Wickham is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose qualities contrast with another’s, highlighting their differences.">foil</dfn> to Darcy: charming on the surface but morally bankrupt underneath. Darcy appears proud but is genuinely honourable. Austen’s central irony is that first impressions reverse the truth: the pleasant man is dangerous, the rude man is worthy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does Elizabeth believe Wickham’s lies, and what does this reveal about prejudice?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth trusts Wickham because his story confirms her existing prejudice against Darcy. Austen shows that prejudice is not just about disliking people — it also means being too ready to trust those who flatter us. Elizabeth’s error mirrors Darcy’s: both judge by first impressions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Collins’s Proposal and Charlotte’s Choice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Mr Collins proposes to Elizabeth with comic pomposity, listing his “reasons” (Lady Catherine’s approval, his duty as clergyman, the entail) but never mentioning love. Elizabeth refuses firmly, but Collins cannot comprehend her refusal. Austen uses Collins to <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of humour, irony or exaggeration to mock and criticise social behaviour.">satirise</dfn> the transactional nature of Regency marriage — he treats a proposal like a business negotiation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Charlotte Lucas then accepts Collins, choosing financial security over romantic happiness: “I am not romantic, you know; I never was. I ask only a comfortable home.” Elizabeth is shocked, but Charlotte’s decision is entirely rational within the Regency system. Austen asks the reader to consider whether Elizabeth’s idealism is a luxury only a younger, prettier woman can afford.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Darcy’s first proposal is more insult than declaration. He dwells on Elizabeth’s inferior connections rather than expressing love: “In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.” The word “struggled” and “will not do” frame his love as an affliction. Elizabeth is furious and rejects him passionately: “You are the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Darcy’s Letter — The Turning Point</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Darcy’s letter after the proposal is the novel’s structural turning point. It reveals that Wickham is a liar who attempted to elope with Darcy’s fifteen-year-old sister Georgiana, and that Darcy separated Bingley from Jane to protect his friend from what he considered an unsuitable family. Elizabeth is forced to acknowledge her prejudice: “Till this moment I never knew myself.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Elizabeth’s Self-Recognition</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Elizabeth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A moment of sudden insight or realisation about oneself or the world.">epiphany</dfn> — “Till this moment I never knew myself” — is the novel’s emotional climax. She recognises that her intelligence and wit made her overconfident in her judgments. The letter splits the novel into before (prejudice) and after (self-knowledge). Austen suggests that knowing yourself is harder and more valuable than knowing others.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what Darcy’s letter reveals about both Wickham and Darcy himself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The letter is the novel’s most important structural device. It provides new information (Wickham’s true character, Georgiana’s near-seduction) while forcing Elizabeth to recognise her own prejudice. Both Darcy and Elizabeth must overcome their flaws — his pride and her prejudice — before they can come together.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n15">Wickham’s charm conceals dishonesty, while Darcy’s rudeness conceals genuine worth — Austen’s central irony about first impressions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Darcy’s first proposal fails because his pride prevents him from expressing love without class condescension.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Elizabeth’s self-recognition after the letter is the novel’s emotional and structural turning point.</li>
</ul>
