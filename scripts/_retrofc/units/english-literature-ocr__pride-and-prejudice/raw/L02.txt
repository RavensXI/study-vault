--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Bennet family, Darcy’s pride at Meryton, and how first impressions shape the plot.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Bennet Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens by introducing the Bennet family of Longbourn: five daughters, a sardonic father and an anxious mother. Mr Bennet retreats into his library and his irony; Mrs Bennet schemes to marry her daughters to wealthy men. Austen uses their contrasting reactions to the arrival of Mr Bingley at Netherfield Park to establish the novel’s comic tone and the tension between romantic idealism and economic pragmatism that runs through every chapter.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The five sisters represent a spectrum of Regency womanhood: Jane is gentle and trusting, Elizabeth is witty and independent, Mary is pedantic, Kitty is easily led, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The youngest Bennet sister whose reckless, flirtatious behaviour eventually causes a scandal that threatens the whole family.">Lydia</dfn> is reckless. Austen positions Elizabeth as the heroine not because she is perfect, but because she combines intelligence with moral seriousness — qualities the other sisters lack in different ways.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does Darcy say about Elizabeth at the ball, and what does it reveal about his character?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Darcy dismisses Elizabeth as “tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me.” This public insult reveals his class pride — he considers the Bennets beneath his notice. Elizabeth’s response (“I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine”) shows her <dfn class="term" data-def="A preconceived opinion formed without sufficient evidence or reason.">prejudice</dfn> forming: she judges Darcy on this single remark for much of the novel.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Meryton Ball — First Impressions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Meryton ball functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A world in miniature; a small-scale version of a larger system.">microcosm</dfn> of Regency social dynamics. Dancing is a public performance of courtship: Bingley dances with Jane twice, signalling serious interest, while Darcy refuses to dance with anyone outside his party. To refuse a dance is a social insult — it signals that someone is beneath your notice. Darcy’s behaviour immediately establishes his pride as a social barrier.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Meanwhile, Mrs Bennet loudly boasts about Jane’s prospects, Lydia and Kitty flirt with officers, and Mary performs an embarrassing piano piece. The Bennet family’s social embarrassments undermine Jane and Elizabeth’s chances. Austen shows that in Regency England, you were judged not just by your own behaviour but by your family’s — a reality that will become critical when Lydia elopes.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The word “tolerable” is carefully chosen. It reduces Elizabeth from a person to an object to be assessed — she is not intolerable, merely not worth the effort. The conditional “not handsome enough to tempt me” implies Darcy sees himself as the prize, not the suitor. Austen uses this moment to establish the central irony: the man who seems most unpleasant will prove most worthy, and the woman he dismisses will become the one person who challenges him to grow.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Darcy and Elizabeth’s Early Dynamic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The contrast between Darcy and Bingley mirrors the contrast between Elizabeth and Jane. Bingley is open and generous; Darcy is reserved and judgmental. Jane sees the best in everyone; Elizabeth is perceptive but quick to condemn. Austen positions the reader to share Elizabeth’s amusement at Darcy’s expense, but later events will reveal that both Elizabeth and the reader have been as prejudiced as Darcy has been proud.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Jane and Bingley’s straightforward mutual attraction serves as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character or subplot that contrasts with the main one, highlighting differences.">foil</dfn> to Elizabeth and Darcy’s combative relationship. Their romance appears simple but is disrupted by Darcy and Caroline Bingley, who separate them to protect Bingley from a “disadvantageous” match. Austen shows that even gentle love is vulnerable to class interference.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three ways Austen uses the ball scene to establish the novel’s key themes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Meryton ball introduces every major theme: pride (Darcy’s refusal to dance), prejudice (Elizabeth’s instant judgment), class (the Bennet family’s social embarrassments), and marriage as a public performance. Austen establishes that first impressions in this world are powerful, often wrong, and very difficult to overcome.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n15">The Meryton ball establishes the novel’s central dynamic: Darcy judges by class, Elizabeth by character, and both are partially wrong.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Darcy’s “tolerable” insult and Elizabeth’s witty response introduce pride and prejudice as mutual flaws.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The Bennet family’s social embarrassments show that in Regency England, you were judged by your family, not just yourself.</li>
</ul>
