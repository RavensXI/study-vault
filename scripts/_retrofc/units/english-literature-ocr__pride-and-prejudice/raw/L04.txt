--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pemberley — Seeing the Real Darcy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth visits <dfn class="term" data-def="Darcy’s grand estate in Derbyshire, symbolising his true character: ordered, beautiful and generous.">Pemberley</dfn> with her aunt and uncle, the Gardiners. The estate is magnificent but not ostentatious — it reflects taste rather than vulgar display. Darcy’s housekeeper Mrs Reynolds praises him as “the best master” and the kindest man she has ever known. This third-party testimony cannot be dismissed as bias, and it profoundly changes Elizabeth’s view.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">When Elizabeth encounters Darcy unexpectedly, he is transformed: warm, attentive and genuinely welcoming to the Gardiners — the very <dfn class="term" data-def="Money earned through business rather than inherited land, considered socially inferior to landed wealth.">trade</dfn> connections he previously disdained. Austen shows his transformation through action, not declaration. He proves he has overcome his pride by treating people he would once have snubbed with genuine respect.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does the Pemberley visit change Elizabeth’s understanding of Darcy?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pemberley functions as both setting and symbol. The estate’s beauty and order reflect Darcy’s true character: generous, tasteful and responsible. Elizabeth’s famous thought — “to be mistress of Pemberley might be something!” — is comic, but it also signals her recognition that Darcy’s qualities are real.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lydia’s Elopement — Scandal and Survival</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Lydia’s elopement with Wickham is a catastrophe. In Regency society, an unmarried woman living with a man was ruinous, and one sister’s scandal tainted the whole family’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The general opinion held about a person or family, which determined their social standing and marriage prospects.">reputation</dfn>. All five sisters’ marriage prospects would be destroyed. Mr Bennet’s failure to control Lydia is his greatest parental failure — his detached irony has had real consequences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Darcy secretly pays Wickham’s debts and arranges the marriage, saving the Bennets from social destruction. He asks for no credit and wants Elizabeth never to know. Austen shows that true love is expressed through selfless action, not grand declarations. The contrast with his insulting first proposal demonstrates genuine transformation.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Darcy’s second proposal is humble and direct: “You are too generous to trifle with me. If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once.” The language is entirely different from his first attempt — no mention of her inferior connections, no struggle against his feelings. Elizabeth accepts, and both acknowledge their earlier errors. Austen shows that true love requires mutual growth and honest self-knowledge.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Resolution — Four Marriages Compared</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The novel’s resolution brings together four contrasting marriages. Charlotte and Collins represent pragmatic survival. Lydia and Wickham represent reckless passion — their marriage is unhappy, held together only by Darcy’s money. Jane and Bingley represent gentle mutual affection. Elizabeth and Darcy represent Austen’s ideal: a union of intellectual equals who have both overcome significant flaws.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Lady Catherine’s Failed Intervention</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Lady Catherine storms to Longbourn to demand that Elizabeth refuse Darcy. Her language reveals pure class snobbery: “Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?” Elizabeth’s refusal to yield — “I am only resolved to act in that manner which will constitute my happiness” — is her finest moment of independence. Ironically, Lady Catherine’s report of Elizabeth’s defiance encourages Darcy to propose again.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how Darcy proves his transformation through actions rather than words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Darcy’s transformation is proven through three selfless actions: welcoming the Gardiners at Pemberley, paying Wickham’s debts, and arranging Lydia’s marriage without seeking credit. Austen argues that genuine change shows in behaviour, not in speeches.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n15">Pemberley symbolises Darcy’s true character: ordered, beautiful and generous — the opposite of his public manner.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">Darcy proves his transformation through selfless action (saving the Bennets), not through declarations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The four marriages represent different combinations of love, pragmatism and social pressure in Regency England.</li>
</ul>
