--- DESCRIPTION ---
Logical, physical and policy-based methods used to keep data and systems safe from threats.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Protecting Information Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every organisation stores sensitive information — customer records, financial data, staff details and confidential business plans. If that information is lost, stolen or tampered with, the consequences can be severe: financial penalties, damaged reputation and loss of customer trust. Protecting information is therefore one of the most important responsibilities of any ICT professional. There are three broad categories of protection: <strong>logical</strong>, <strong>physical</strong> and <strong>security policies</strong>. Each layer addresses a different type of threat, and the strongest protection combines all three.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Logical Protection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Software-based controls that restrict or regulate access to digital information, systems and networks.">Logical protection</dfn> refers to software and system-based controls that prevent unauthorised access to data. Unlike physical locks, logical controls work by verifying identity and enforcing rules within the digital environment itself. The most common logical measures are access levels, authentication, firewalls, anti-malware applications, password protection and encryption.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Access levels</strong> ensure that users can only see and edit the information they need for their role. A sales assistant, for example, might be able to view customer orders but not alter payroll records. This limits the damage a compromised account can do. <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of verifying the identity of a user, device or system before granting access, typically through passwords, tokens or biometrics.">Authentication</dfn> is the process of confirming that someone is who they claim to be. It can be single-factor (a password alone) or multi-factor, where a second check — such as a one-time code sent to a mobile phone — is also required. Multi-factor authentication is far harder for an attacker to bypass because stealing a password is not enough on its own.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A security system that monitors and filters incoming and outgoing network traffic based on a set of defined rules, blocking unauthorised connections.">Firewalls</dfn> act as gatekeepers between a trusted internal network and untrusted external networks such as the internet. They examine data packets arriving at or leaving the network and block any traffic that does not match the permitted rules. A firewall can be hardware-based (a dedicated device), software-based (installed on individual computers) or a combination of both. Anti-malware applications scan the system for harmful software — viruses, spyware, ransomware — and either quarantine or delete threats before they can cause damage. They also monitor activity in real time, flagging suspicious processes immediately.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Password protection</strong> is one of the simplest but most widely used logical controls. Strong passwords — long, combining letters, numbers and symbols — are significantly harder to crack by brute-force attacks. Organisations often enforce a password policy that sets minimum complexity requirements and forces regular changes. <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of converting readable data into a scrambled format that can only be decoded by someone with the correct key, preventing unauthorised parties from reading the information.">Encryption</dfn> goes further: even if an attacker gains access to data, they cannot read it without the decryption key. Data can be encrypted at rest (stored on a server or device) and in transit (as it travels across a network). HTTPS — visible in your browser address bar — uses encryption to protect web traffic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list all six logical protection methods from memory: access levels, authentication, firewalls, anti-malware applications, password protection and encryption. Try to give one sentence explaining how each one works.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six logical protection methods are: access levels (limit what users can do), authentication (verify identity), firewalls (filter network traffic), anti-malware applications (detect and remove threats), password protection (control login access) and encryption (scramble data so it cannot be read without a key).</p>
</div>

Access levels answer the question "what are you allowed to do?" — they define what an already-authenticated user is permitted to see or change. Both work together: authentication is the door, and access levels are the rooms within the building that each person is allowed to enter. A school network, for example, might authenticate every pupil with a username and password, but access levels ensure that pupils cannot edit teacher mark books or administrative records.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Physical Protection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Even the strongest logical controls cannot help if an unauthorised person can walk up to a server and physically remove the hard drive. <strong>Physical protection</strong> addresses this by controlling and monitoring access to the real-world spaces and hardware where data is stored. Physical measures include locks, biometrics, the location of hardware, backup systems and security staff.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Locks</strong> are the most straightforward physical control — server rooms, filing cabinets and computer cases can all be locked to prevent unauthorised access. <dfn class="term" data-def="Security technology that uses unique biological characteristics — such as fingerprints, iris patterns or facial features — to identify or verify a person's identity.">Biometrics</dfn> offer a higher level of physical access control by using unique bodily characteristics: fingerprint scanners, iris recognition and facial recognition are all examples. Unlike keys or access cards, biometric data cannot easily be passed to another person or forgotten. The <strong>location of hardware</strong> also matters: servers and network equipment should be housed in dedicated secure rooms rather than shared offices, and ideally not on ground floor or basement levels where flooding or break-in risk is higher.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Backup systems</strong> protect against the permanent loss of data due to hardware failure, fire, flood or malicious deletion. A good backup strategy follows the 3-2-1 rule: keep three copies of data, on two different types of storage, with one copy held off-site. Off-site backups ensure that a single disaster — a fire destroying an office, for example — cannot wipe all copies simultaneously. <strong>Security staff</strong> provide a human layer of protection: patrolling premises, monitoring CCTV, checking visitor identity and responding to incidents in real time. Human oversight catches threats that automated systems might miss, such as an employee behaving suspiciously or a tailgating attempt at a secure door.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Exam questions sometimes ask you to distinguish logical from physical protection. A quick rule: if you can touch the protection measure, it is physical; if it is software or a system rule, it is logical. Backup systems count as physical even though they involve digital files, because they involve hardware and off-site storage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five physical protection methods are: locks (restrict physical access), biometrics (verify identity using body characteristics), location of hardware (keep servers in secure, appropriate environments), backup systems (maintain copies of data in multiple locations) and security staff (human monitoring and response). Physical and logical measures work best in combination.</p>
</div>

Even cloud backups rely on physical servers in a different location. The protection they provide is resilience against physical threats (fire, flood, hardware failure) rather than against logical threats like malware, though they do incidentally protect against ransomware too. In an exam, listing backup systems under physical protection is correct; listing it under logical would typically lose the mark.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Security Policies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Technology alone cannot protect an organisation if people do not use it correctly. <strong>Security policies</strong> are documented rules and procedures that govern how staff handle information and respond to incidents. The three main policies covered in this topic are: disaster recovery, staff responsibilities and the acceptable use policy.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">A <strong>disaster recovery plan</strong> is a documented procedure that sets out exactly what an organisation will do if a major incident — a cyberattack, a fire or a flood — disrupts normal operations. It identifies which systems must be restored first, who is responsible for each action, where backup data is held and how long restoration should take. Without a disaster recovery plan, organisations often make chaotic decisions under pressure, taking far longer to recover than necessary. <strong>Staff responsibilities</strong> policies make clear what each employee is expected to do to protect information — for example, not sharing passwords, locking their computer screen when leaving their desk and reporting any suspicious emails or activity immediately.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">An <strong>acceptable use policy (AUP)</strong> is a written agreement that tells users what they are and are not allowed to do with an organisation's ICT systems. It typically covers rules on personal use, prohibited websites, the installation of unauthorised software and the handling of sensitive data. Employees usually sign an AUP when they join an organisation. <strong>Staff training</strong> is essential because many security breaches are caused by human error rather than sophisticated technical attacks — clicking on a phishing link, using a weak password or accidentally emailing a file to the wrong person. Regular training keeps staff aware of current threats and reminds them of their responsibilities.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Logical protection uses software-based controls — access levels, authentication, firewalls, anti-malware, password protection and encryption — to prevent unauthorised digital access to data.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Physical protection uses tangible measures — locks, biometrics, hardware location, backup systems and security staff — to prevent unauthorised physical access to equipment and data.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Security policies — disaster recovery plans, staff responsibility guidelines, acceptable use policies and staff training — ensure that people use technology correctly and respond effectively when something goes wrong.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">In questions that ask you to identify a method of protection, always be precise about which category it belongs to. A firewall is a logical measure; a biometric scanner is a physical measure; a disaster recovery plan is a security policy. Mixing up the categories is a common mistake that loses marks. For extended-answer questions, the strongest responses explain how two or three measures work together — for example, explaining how encryption (logical) protects data even if a backup drive (physical) is stolen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">When evaluating which measures are most important, be careful not to dismiss any category completely. Full marks require a balanced argument: logical controls are useless if a thief walks out with the server; physical controls are useless if a remote hacker can access the system over the internet; and both are undermined by staff who ignore the acceptable use policy. The best protection is always layered.</p>
