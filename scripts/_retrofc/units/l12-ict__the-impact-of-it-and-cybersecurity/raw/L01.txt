Explore risks to computer-held data and the impact of data loss, theft or manipulation on individuals and businesses.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Risks to Information Held on Computers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every organisation that uses computers stores information that can be damaged, stolen or misused. Understanding the different types of risk is essential for protecting data and avoiding serious harm to individuals and businesses alike.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Accidental Damage</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Not every threat is deliberate. <dfn class="term" data-def="Unintentional harm caused to data or hardware, such as spilling liquid on a device, deleting a file by mistake, or hardware failure.">Accidental damage</dfn> can occur when a user accidentally deletes important files, a hard drive fails without warning, or a power surge corrupts data. Human error is one of the most common causes of data loss in the workplace.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Another accidental risk comes from incorrectly assigned access levels. If a system administrator gives a member of staff access to files they should not see, that employee may unintentionally disclose sensitive information to others or use data in ways that were never intended. This is sometimes called unintended disclosure, and it can have serious legal consequences under data protection law.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Remember accidental damage includes both hardware failures and human error — not just deliberate attacks.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Incorrectly assigning access levels is a non-malicious risk that can still result in data breaches, GDPR violations and serious reputational harm for an organisation.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Malicious Software</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Software designed to disrupt, damage or gain unauthorised access to a computer system. Short for malicious software.">Malware</dfn> is an umbrella term for any software deliberately created to cause harm. There are several distinct types that you need to know.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A <strong>virus</strong> is a program that attaches itself to a legitimate file and spreads when that file is copied or shared. A <strong>worm</strong> is similar but spreads automatically across networks without needing a host file. A <strong>Trojan horse</strong> disguises itself as useful software to trick users into installing it, then carries out harmful actions in the background.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Spyware</strong> secretly monitors a user's activity, collecting passwords, browsing history or financial details without consent. <dfn class="term" data-def="Malicious software that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment in exchange for the decryption key.">Ransomware</dfn> goes further — it locks or encrypts the victim's files and demands a payment, often in cryptocurrency, before restoring access. High-profile ransomware attacks have crippled hospitals, schools and businesses worldwide.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <strong>Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS)</strong> attack floods a server or website with so much traffic that it becomes overwhelmed and unavailable to legitimate users. This is often carried out using a network of infected computers called a botnet. Finally, <dfn class="term" data-def="Software that records every key pressed on a keyboard, enabling attackers to capture passwords, card numbers and other sensitive input.">key-logging</dfn> software silently records every keystroke a user makes, allowing attackers to harvest login credentials and financial data over time.</p>

A worm is self-replicating and spreads automatically across a network without any human action required. Both can cause significant damage, but worms tend to spread much faster because they do not rely on user behaviour.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Hacking</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hacking refers to gaining unauthorised access to a computer system or network. However, not all hackers have the same intentions. Security professionals distinguish between three groups using a colour-coded classification.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <strong>white hat</strong> hacker works ethically and legally, often employed by organisations to find security vulnerabilities before criminals can exploit them. This is known as penetration testing. A <strong>black hat</strong> hacker breaks into systems without permission, motivated by financial gain, malice or curiosity, and their actions are illegal. A <strong>grey hat</strong> hacker sits between the two — they may access systems without permission but typically notify the organisation of vulnerabilities rather than exploiting them maliciously.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why might a business hire a white hat hacker?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">A business might hire a white hat hacker to carry out a penetration test — a controlled, legal attempt to break into their own systems. This reveals weaknesses in security before a malicious attacker can find and exploit them. It is a proactive approach that can save an organisation from far more damaging breaches in the future.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Social Engineering</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Manipulating people into revealing confidential information or performing actions that compromise security, rather than exploiting technical weaknesses.">Social engineering</dfn> exploits human psychology rather than technical flaws. The most common form is phishing — sending emails that appear to come from trusted sources, such as a bank or a manager, to trick recipients into clicking malicious links or entering login credentials. Other techniques include pretexting (inventing a believable scenario to extract information), baiting (leaving infected USB drives where victims will find them) and vishing (voice phishing over the phone).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The Impact of Data Loss, Theft or Manipulation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">When data is lost, stolen or altered without authorisation, the consequences can be severe and wide-ranging. Both individuals and businesses face a range of financial, legal, moral and operational impacts.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Financial Implications</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Businesses may face enormous costs following a data breach. These include paying ransom demands, funding IT forensics investigations, compensating affected customers and investing in new security infrastructure. A competitor who gains access to confidential pricing strategies or product plans gains an unfair financial advantage that can permanently damage the victim organisation's market position.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="For exam questions on impact, try to link financial, legal and reputational harm together — they often compound each other.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and the Data Protection Act 2018, organisations can face fines of up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover for serious data breaches — whichever is higher.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Moral and Legal Implications</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Organisations have a moral responsibility to protect personal data entrusted to them by employees, customers and partners. Breaching this trust is not only ethically wrong but also illegal. Failing to comply with UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 can result in regulatory fines, prosecution and mandatory reporting obligations. Individuals whose data is leaked may become victims of identity theft, fraud or blackmail — harm that the organisation is morally accountable for enabling.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Data Manipulation and Loss of Service</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Data manipulation occurs when an attacker alters records rather than simply stealing or deleting them. This can be especially damaging because the changes may go undetected for some time. For example, if a hospital's patient records are silently altered, treatment decisions could be based on incorrect information with life-threatening consequences. Loss of service — as seen in DDoS attacks — prevents customers from accessing websites or online platforms, leading to lost sales and eroded trust.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Loss of Intellectual Property and Reputation</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Intellectual property (IP) such as product designs, source code, research data or creative content represents a business's competitive advantage. If this is stolen, competitors can replicate innovations without the investment, destroying the original creator's market edge. Alongside this, a business that suffers a publicised data breach often experiences lasting reputational damage. Customers lose confidence, partners withdraw and the business may struggle to recover even after the technical issue is resolved.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Risks to data include accidental damage, incorrectly assigned access levels, malware (viruses, worms, Trojans, spyware, ransomware, DDoS, key-logging), hacking and social engineering.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Hackers are classified as white hat (ethical), black hat (criminal) or grey hat (ambiguous intent); only white hat hacking is lawful.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Data loss, theft or manipulation can lead to financial loss, legal penalties under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, competitor advantage, data manipulation, loss of service, loss of intellectual property and lasting reputational damage.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">Exam questions often ask you to identify a threat and explain its impact. Always try to link your answer to a specific consequence — for example, a ransomware attack causes loss of service AND financial implications (ransom payment) AND potential loss of reputation. Multi-point answers that show how consequences connect will score higher marks.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">When discussing legal implications, refer specifically to UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 by name — vague references to 'the law' or 'data protection rules' will not score as well in extended-response questions.</p>
