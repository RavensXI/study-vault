<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Happens After Death?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every religious tradition has developed teachings about what happens after physical death. These beliefs shape how adherents understand the purpose of earthly life, how they treat the dying, how they mourn and how they think about justice in a world where wrongdoing often appears to go unpunished. Humanists, by contrast, hold that death is the end of personal existence. This lesson examines beliefs about death and the afterlife across six religious traditions and a non-religious worldview, following the canonical order: Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism, and Non-religious.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Christianity</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Christians believe in life after death grounded in the resurrection of Jesus. The New Testament affirms that Jesus rose from the dead and that this promises resurrection for all believers. Jesus tells Martha in John 11:24&ndash;27: &ldquo;I am the resurrection and the life. The one who believes in me will live, even though they die.&rdquo; Paul&rsquo;s first letter to the Corinthians (1 Corinthians 15:42&ndash;44) describes the resurrection body as imperishable, glorious and spiritual, in contrast to the physical body that is perishable and weak.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Christian beliefs about the afterlife include <dfn class="term" data-def="In Christian belief, a state of eternal joy and closeness to God enjoyed by those who have lived righteously and been saved — the ultimate destination of the blessed.">heaven</dfn> (eternal life with God), <dfn class="term" data-def="In Christian theology, a state of eternal separation from God, understood as the consequence of rejecting God's love and living contrary to his will.">hell</dfn> (separation from God) and, in Catholic theology, <dfn class="term" data-def="In Catholic theology, a state of purification after death for souls who are destined for heaven but need to be cleansed of remaining sin before they can enter fully into God's presence.">purgatory</dfn> (a state of purification after death for souls not yet ready for heaven). The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1020&ndash;1023) describes the particular judgement each person faces at death and the final vision of God in heaven. Catechism 1030 addresses purgatory and 1033 affirms the possibility of damnation for those who permanently reject God&rsquo;s love. Protestant denominations generally reject purgatory, holding that the believer passes directly from death to the presence of God.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: (a) the John 11:25 statement Jesus makes about resurrection; (b) the three Christian afterlife beliefs; (c) what the Catechism says about purgatory (paragraph number).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Christian afterlife beliefs: heaven (eternal life with God), hell (eternal separation from God), purgatory (Catholic: state of purification). Scripture: John 11:24&ndash;27 (resurrection promise); 1 Corinthians 15:42&ndash;44 (nature of the resurrection body). Catholic teaching: CCC 1020&ndash;1023 (judgement and heaven); CCC 1030 (purgatory); CCC 1033 (damnation).</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Judaism</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Jewish beliefs about the afterlife are more diverse than Christian ones, and in many strands of Judaism this life takes priority over speculation about the next. The Hebrew Bible contains the concept of <dfn class="term" data-def="In the Hebrew Bible, a shadowy underworld where the dead go — an early Jewish concept that precedes later, more developed ideas about heaven and hell.">sheol</dfn> &mdash; a shadowy, undifferentiated realm of the dead rather than a place of reward or punishment. Later Jewish thought, especially in the period of the Second Temple, developed concepts of resurrection, judgement and <dfn class="term" data-def="Hebrew for 'the world to come' — the Jewish afterlife belief in a renewed world of justice and peace, or the spiritual realm in which the righteous enjoy closeness to God.">olam ha-ba</dfn> (the world to come).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Orthodox Jews affirm bodily resurrection and divine judgement; Reform Jews tend to understand olam ha-ba more metaphorically or focus on legacy and the memory of the righteous. Jewish funerary practice (see the lesson on funeral rites) reflects these beliefs &mdash; burial rather than cremation is traditional, expressing the hope of resurrection.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n10">Islam</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Islamic concept of the afterlife is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The Islamic belief in the afterlife — a comprehensive term encompassing the period from death through to the Day of Judgement and the eternal states of paradise (Jannah) and hell (Jahannam).">akhirah</dfn>. Islam teaches that after death, the soul enters a state of waiting (Barzakh) until the Day of Judgement. On that day, all people will be raised and judged by Allah according to their deeds and faith. The Qur&rsquo;an teaches that Allah has the power to resurrect the dead: Qur&rsquo;an 46:33 affirms that the One who created the heavens and the earth is not unable to resurrect the dead. Qur&rsquo;an 3:16 speaks of those who pray for forgiveness and protection from the punishment of the fire &mdash; affirming both the reality of hell (Jahannam) and the hope of paradise (Jannah).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Islamic funeral practice is closely connected to the belief in akhirah: the body is washed and shrouded, buried promptly and placed facing Makkah. The speed and simplicity of Islamic burial reflect the belief that the body should be returned to the earth from which it was created, ready for resurrection.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Hinduism</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hindu beliefs about death centre on the cycle of <dfn class="term" data-def="In Hindu and Buddhist teaching, the cycle of birth, death and rebirth (reincarnation) driven by karma — the accumulated consequences of past actions.">samsara</dfn> (the cycle of birth, death and rebirth). The Bhagavad Gita (2:12&ndash;13) teaches that the atman (the eternal self or soul) is never truly destroyed: just as a person changes clothes, the soul moves from one body to another at death. The ultimate goal is <dfn class="term" data-def="In Hindu teaching, liberation from the cycle of samsara — the soul's union with Brahman (the ultimate reality/God), achieved through spiritual practice, devotion and the exhaustion of karma.">moksha</dfn> &mdash; liberation from the cycle of rebirth and union with Brahman (the ultimate reality). The path to moksha may take many lifetimes. Karma (the moral law of cause and effect) determines the conditions of each reincarnation.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Sikhism</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Sikh beliefs about death are closely related to Hindu ideas but with a distinctive theological emphasis. Like Hindus, Sikhs believe in samsara (the cycle of rebirth) and karma. The soul&rsquo;s goal is <dfn class="term" data-def="In Sikh teaching, liberation from the cycle of samsara and union with Waheguru (God) — the soul's ultimate homecoming, achieved through nam japna, honest living and God's grace.">mukti</dfn> &mdash; liberation from the cycle of rebirth and union with Waheguru (God). The Guru Granth Sahib (page 13) affirms that the soul that truly meditates on God&rsquo;s name achieves liberation. In Sikhism, mukti comes not through ritual or caste but through God&rsquo;s grace (nadar), devotion (bhakti) and righteous living. Death is not feared but understood as the soul&rsquo;s return to its source.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the Sikh term for liberation, the Guru Granth Sahib page reference, and what determines whether a soul achieves mukti according to Sikh teaching.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sikh afterlife belief: samsara (cycle of rebirth driven by karma), with mukti (liberation and union with Waheguru) as the goal. Guru Granth Sahib 13 affirms that meditating on God&rsquo;s name leads to liberation. Mukti is achieved through God&rsquo;s grace, devotion and righteous living &mdash; not through caste or ritual.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Buddhism</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Buddhist afterlife teaching is distinctive because of the teaching of anatta (no fixed self). If there is no permanent self, what is it that is &ldquo;reborn&rdquo;? Buddhists explain this through the analogy of a flame passed from candle to candle: something is transmitted, but it is not the same flame, nor a completely different one. What carries forward is kamma (the Pali term for the moral force of intentional actions) and the mental formations (sankh&#257;ras) that arise from it &mdash; not an unchanging soul.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The goal of Buddhist practice is <dfn class="term" data-def="The Pali term for the complete extinguishing of craving, hatred and delusion — the cessation of the cycle of rebirth (samsara) and the ultimate goal of Buddhist practice.">nibb&#257;na</dfn> &mdash; the complete cessation of the cycle of rebirth, achieved when kamma is exhausted and craving is extinguished. The Triratna tradition includes members who interpret rebirth not as literal life-to-life reincarnation but as moment-to-moment arising and passing away of experience &mdash; a more psychological reading of the doctrine.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n21">All three traditions &mdash; Hinduism, Sikhism and Buddhism &mdash; teach some form of samsara (the cycle of rebirth). However, they differ in what is reborn. Hinduism affirms the atman (eternal, unchanging soul) as what transmigrates. Sikhism also affirms the soul&rsquo;s journey across lives toward God. Buddhism denies a permanent soul (anatta) &mdash; what transmigrates is a stream of kamma and consciousness, not an unchanging entity. The goals also differ: Hinduism seeks moksha (union with Brahman), Sikhism seeks mukti (union with Waheguru), Buddhism seeks nibb&#257;na (cessation of rebirth through extinguishing of craving).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Non-Religious and Humanist Views</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Humanists understand death as the end of personal existence. There is no afterlife, no soul that survives the body, no resurrection or reincarnation. The Humanist tradition emphasises making the most of the one life we have and finding meaning in relationships, achievements, contribution to society and the legacy we leave behind. Grief is real and important; Humanist funeral ceremonies mark the significance of the life that has ended without religious language. The emphasis is on &ldquo;life before death&rdquo; rather than consolation from promises of an afterlife.</p>

Critics argue the opposite: that focus on the next life can devalue this life and that the Humanist emphasis on &ldquo;life before death&rdquo; provides a stronger motivation to improve the world now. <h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Christianity: heaven, hell and (Catholic) purgatory &mdash; grounded in John 11:24&ndash;27 and 1 Corinthians 15:42&ndash;44. Judaism: sheol (early), olam ha-ba (the world to come) and resurrection. Islam: akhirah, Barzakh (waiting), Day of Judgement, Jannah and Jahannam (Qur&rsquo;an 46:33, 3:16).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Hinduism: atman transmigrates through samsara toward moksha (Bhagavad Gita 2:12&ndash;13). Sikhism: soul journeys through samsara toward mukti by God&rsquo;s grace (Guru Granth Sahib 13). Buddhism: kamma and consciousness transmigrate without a fixed soul, aiming for nibb&#257;na.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Humanists believe death is the end of personal existence &mdash; the emphasis is on &lsquo;life before death&rsquo;. Beliefs about the afterlife shape funeral practices, ethical motivation and attitudes toward the dying across all traditions.</li>
</ul>
