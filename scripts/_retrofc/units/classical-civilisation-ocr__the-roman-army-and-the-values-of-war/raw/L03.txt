--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Rome fought and won an empire — key campaigns, siege tactics and the battle of Actium that changed history.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rome at War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Rome did not acquire its empire by accident. Over five centuries of nearly continuous warfare, the Roman army developed and adapted tactics, organisation and technology that allowed it to defeat opponents as varied as Macedonian phalanxes, Celtic warbands, desert guerrillas and Eastern cavalry. This lesson focuses on Roman warfare in the Imperial period and on two key conflicts: Trajan&rsquo;s campaigns against the Dacians in the early second century AD, and the naval battle of Actium in 31 BC that determined who would rule the Roman world.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Tactics and Adaptability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Roman army&rsquo;s greatest strength was its capacity to adapt. When facing enemies who excelled in archery, Rome recruited auxiliary archers from Syria. When fighting desert nomads, it reorganised supply lines. The fundamental tactical approach, however, was consistent: use the pilum volley to disorder the enemy, close to sword range, and overwhelm with disciplined pressure from the scutum-wall.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Roman generals understood that battle was often decided before it started &mdash; by choosing ground, by tiring the enemy, by cutting off supply lines. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The art of supplying and maintaining an army in the field; Roman logistical capability was central to the success of long-distance campaigns.">logistics</dfn> of a Roman campaign were extraordinary: granaries at fortresses along the Rhine and Danube, a network of roads that allowed rapid troop movements, and a river fleet that carried supplies deep into enemy territory. Trajan&rsquo;s Column records this infrastructure vividly &mdash; among its carved scenes are soldiers unloading supplies from boats and engineers constructing pontoon bridges.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what were Trajan's two campaigns against the Dacians called, when did they take place, and name two material sources that record these wars?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Trajan fought two major campaigns against the Dacians: the First Dacian War (101&ndash;102 AD) and the Second Dacian War (105&ndash;106 AD). Both are recorded in detail on Trajan&rsquo;s Column in Rome and commemorated on the Arch of Trajan at Benevento &mdash; two of the most important prescribed visual sources for Roman warfare.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Trajan&rsquo;s Dacian Campaigns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Dacia, roughly equivalent to modern Romania, was a powerful kingdom north of the Danube ruled by a capable king named Decebalus. Decebalus had humiliated Rome in previous conflicts, extracting tribute money and capturing Roman prisoners. Trajan invaded in 101 AD to recover Roman prestige and to seize Dacian gold reserves that were among the largest in the ancient world. The first war ended with Decebalus surrendering and agreeing to Roman terms; when he broke those terms, Trajan launched a second, more brutal campaign that ended with the destruction of the Dacian capital Sarmizegetusa and Decebalus&rsquo;s suicide rather than capture.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Trajan&rsquo;s Column, standing in Rome, is a hollow marble column roughly 30 metres tall, around whose shaft winds a continuous carved relief depicting both campaigns in over 150 individual scenes. The column begins with river-gods and troops crossing a pontoon bridge and moves through sieges, battle scenes, negotiations and triumphal processions to the final conquest. The Arch of Trajan at Benevento, a triumphal arch decorated with relief panels, shows Trajan receiving submission from Dacian enemies, distributing grain to Roman citizens, and associating his victories with divine favour. Both monuments present the campaigns as evidence of Roman greatness and Trajan&rsquo;s exceptional leadership.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Trajan&rsquo;s Column is an invaluable source for Roman military practice &mdash; it shows fort construction, river crossings, artillery use, allied auxiliary troops and siege operations in remarkable detail. However, it was a piece of state propaganda. Trajan is always shown in a commanding position; Roman soldiers are always disciplined and orderly. The Dacians are depicted as brave enemies to make the conquest more impressive, but they are also shown in defeat and submission. The column does not show Roman atrocities, looting or the suffering of civilian populations &mdash; it presents an idealised narrative of Roman power. Students must weigh this selectivity when using the column as evidence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Siege Warfare</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Roman success against fortified towns and hillfort strongholds depended on specialised siege techniques. The standard approach involved surrounding the enemy position with a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circumvallation is a continuous wall or rampart built by a besieging army around an enemy position to prevent escape; a contravallation faced outward to prevent relief armies breaking through.">circumvallation</dfn> &mdash; a wall or ditch that encircled the entire position and cut off all resupply. This was then supplemented by direct assault: battering rams mounted in wheeled towers, artillery firing stones and bolts, and approach ramps of earth built up to the level of the enemy walls. Trajan&rsquo;s Column shows all of these techniques in use against Dacian hillforts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Battle of Actium, 31 BC</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The naval battle of Actium was fought off the coast of north-western Greece on 2 September 31 BC. On one side was Octavian (the future Augustus) commanding a fleet under his brilliant admiral Marcus Agrippa. On the other were the combined forces of Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII of Egypt. The battle decided who would control the entire Roman world. Octavian&rsquo;s victory made him undisputed master of Rome and led directly to the establishment of the Roman Empire.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A large warship of the ancient world, propelled by three banks of oars and used for ramming enemy vessels.">trireme</dfn> had been the dominant warship of the classical Greek period, but by Actium the principal warships were larger vessels called quinqueremes and triremes built on a grander scale. Agrippa&rsquo;s fleet used grappling hooks and boarding tactics to neutralise Antony&rsquo;s heavier ships. When Cleopatra&rsquo;s squadron broke off the engagement and retreated towards Egypt, Antony followed &mdash; and his remaining fleet, left without leadership, surrendered. The battle showed that naval supremacy could decide the fate of an entire empire.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three key facts about the battle of Actium, including the date, the main participants, and the outcome, and name a material source that commemorates the battle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The battle of Actium (31 BC) saw Octavian&rsquo;s fleet under Agrippa defeat the combined forces of Mark Antony and Cleopatra. The victory is commemorated by a relief in the Vatican Museum and on the shield depicted on the Augustus of Prima Porta statue, where the scene of Actium is carved in miniature on the emperor&rsquo;s breastplate armour.</p>
</div>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The Romans invested enormous effort in commemorating military victory in permanent public form. Triumphal arches &mdash; like the Arch of Trajan at Benevento &mdash; were built at key road entry points so that every traveller would be reminded of Roman greatness. Victory columns like Trajan&rsquo;s Column placed the narrative of a campaign literally at the heart of the city, in the forum that bore the emperor&rsquo;s name. Coins were struck to broadcast the victory to every corner of the empire: the Mark Antony Legionary denarius (minted c. 32 BC) shows a warship on the obverse and an eagle between two military standards on the reverse, advertising his naval and military strength to his soldiers. Even defeat was commemorated: Augustus recovered the eagles lost by Varus in the Teutoburg Forest disaster (9 AD) decades later and displayed them publicly as proof that Rome always demanded and achieved satisfaction.</p>

</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Trajan fought two Dacian campaigns (101&ndash;102 AD and 105&ndash;106 AD); both are recorded on Trajan&rsquo;s Column and the Arch of Trajan at Benevento, which are key prescribed visual sources presenting victories as imperial propaganda.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The battle of Actium (31 BC) saw Octavian&rsquo;s fleet defeat Antony and Cleopatra, making Octavian master of the Roman world and leading to the creation of the Roman Empire under Augustus.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Roman military success rested on adaptable tactics, engineering capability (camps, siege works, roads) and the political use of commemorative monuments to project Roman power.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When answering essay questions about Roman warfare and expansion, avoid simply listing battles and dates. The examiner wants you to <em>argue</em> &mdash; for example, arguing that engineering was more important than weapons, or that propaganda (Trajan&rsquo;s Column, the Arch at Benevento) tells us as much about Roman values as about what actually happened. Use named sources as evidence for your argument, not just as decoration. Common pitfall: treating Trajan&rsquo;s Column as a neutral record rather than as a monument designed to glorify the emperor.</p>
