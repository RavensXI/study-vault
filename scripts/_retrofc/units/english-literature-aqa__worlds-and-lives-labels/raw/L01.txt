<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Nature, Place & Belonging — Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">This lesson explores three poems that use the natural world to examine ideas about belonging, spiritual connection, and the longing for home. <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary movement (late 18th–early 19th century) that valued emotion, nature, and individual experience over reason and industrial progress.">Romanticism</dfn> shaped Wordsworth and Brontë’s approaches, while Liz Berry writes from a contemporary perspective — yet all three poets find meaning, comfort, or challenge in the places they come from.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">William Wordsworth — “Lines Written in Early Spring”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Written in 1798, this poem captures Wordsworth sitting in a grove near Alfoxden, observing nature’s beauty while reflecting on humanity’s capacity for cruelty. The poem’s central tension lies between the <dfn class="term" data-def="The attribution of human emotions or intentions to non-human things, especially nature.">personification</dfn> of a joyful natural world and the speaker’s “sad thoughts” about “what man has made of man.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Wordsworth believed nature could teach moral lessons. The birds, flowers, and budding twigs are presented as experiencing genuine pleasure: “The birds around me hopped and played.” This is not mere description — it is a philosophical claim that nature possesses a kind of soul or spirit, reflecting Wordsworth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that the natural world is infused with a divine spirit or animating force, central to Wordsworth's philosophy.">pantheism</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is the central contrast in Wordsworth's poem, and what repeated line expresses it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The refrain “what man has made of man” creates a stark contrast between nature’s harmony and human destructiveness. Wordsworth uses the ballad form (ABAB quatrains) to give the poem a folk-song simplicity that reinforces its message about returning to natural values.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Key techniques:</strong> The poem uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A phrase or line repeated at intervals throughout a poem, often to reinforce a central theme.">refrain</dfn>, personification, and rhetorical questions. The final stanza’s question — “Have I not reason to lament?” — invites the reader to share Wordsworth’s grief at humanity’s disconnection from nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Context:</strong> Wordsworth wrote during the Industrial Revolution, when rural communities were being destroyed by factory labour. His Romantic belief was that nature offered spiritual renewal that industrial society could not provide.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Emily Brontë — “Shall Earth no More Inspire Thee”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In this poem, nature itself speaks directly to the human subject, asking whether it has lost the power to inspire. The Earth acts as a nurturing presence, almost a mother figure, who has watched the addressee closely and wants them back. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The voice or character who speaks in a poem, which may or may not represent the poet themselves.">persona</dfn> of Earth asks: have you outgrown me?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Brontë reverses the typical Romantic dynamic: instead of the poet celebrating nature, nature addresses the poet with a mixture of tenderness and reproach. Earth calls the addressee a “lonely dreamer” whose “mind is ever moving”, while the question in the title — “Shall earth no more inspire thee” — implies that the old bond is now at risk.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what is apostrophe, who speaks in Brontë’s poem, and how does this differ from Wordsworth’s approach to nature?">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The entire poem is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a speaker addresses an absent person, abstract idea, or inanimate object as if it could respond.">apostrophe</dfn> — the Earth speaks to a human who may or may not be listening. This technique creates intimacy and urgency: nature is not a passive backdrop but an active, almost desperate voice calling for reconnection. The poem’s emotional power comes from the sense that something precious is being lost.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Context:</strong> Brontë grew up on the Yorkshire moors, and her poetry consistently presents nature as a spiritual companion rather than merely a setting. Unlike Wordsworth’s gentle pantheism, Brontë’s nature is fiercer and more possessive — it demands attention rather than simply offering it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Liz Berry — “Homing”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Berry’s poem is addressed to a dead relative who was taught to hide her Black Country accent. The title suggests the instinct of returning — like a homing pigeon — and the poem traces the speaker’s wish to bring that lost voice back.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Berry opens with an extended metaphor: the relative “kept your accent / in a box beneath the bed”, the lock “rusted shut by hours of elocution”. Later the speaker prises that box open and lets “years of lost words spill out”. This <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of being rooted in a specific, identifiable place rather than a generic or idealised setting.">specificity of place</dfn> makes the longing feel concrete rather than abstract.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Key techniques:</strong> Berry uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Language that appeals to the five senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell) to create vivid mental images.">sensory imagery</dfn>, dialect words such as “bibble” and “fittle”, and enjambment that contrasts with the formal structures of the Romantic poets.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name one technique each poet uses to present nature, and explain how they differ.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All three poems present nature as emotionally significant, but their approaches differ: Wordsworth uses nature to critique humanity, Brontë gives nature its own voice and agency, and Berry focuses on the accent and dialect that tie a person to a specific place.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Connections Between the Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">These three poems span over two centuries but share a conviction that the natural world matters to human wellbeing. Wordsworth and Brontë both write within the Romantic tradition but differ in perspective: Wordsworth speaks <em>about</em> nature, while Brontë lets nature speak for itself. Berry, writing in the 21st century, focuses less on philosophy and more on the sound of a place in a person’s voice.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="After reading, write one sentence for each poet explaining what ‘belonging’ means to them: Wordsworth (moral harmony), Brontë (reciprocal need), Berry (accent and voice).">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing Attitudes to Belonging</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">For Wordsworth, belonging means being in harmony with a moral universe — nature teaches us how to live. For Brontë, belonging is a reciprocal relationship: the Earth needs us as much as we need it. For Berry, belonging is tied to memory and voice — the dialect words of a loved place that draw us back.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">A useful comparison point: all three poets present disconnection as a form of loss. Wordsworth laments humanity’s moral decline; Brontë’s Earth fears abandonment; Berry’s speaker aches for a voice that was shut away. The shared idea is that something essential in us diminishes when that connection is lost.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
