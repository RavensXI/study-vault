<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Technique & Key Quotations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">This lesson brings together the key quotations you should memorise from across the “Worlds and Lives” cluster and provides revision strategies for the anthology comparison essay. Strong quotation knowledge combined with confident comparison skills will give you the tools to tackle any question.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Key Quotations — Nature, Place & Belonging</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Wordsworth</strong> (“Lines Written in Early Spring”): “what man has made of man” — the refrain that captures humanity’s moral failure against nature’s harmony. Also: “Have I not reason to lament / What man has made of man?” — the rhetorical question inviting shared grief.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Brontë</strong> (“Shall earth no more inspire thee”): “I’ve watched thee every hour” — Earth insisting on a long, patient bond. The title itself is a key quotation: Earth’s urgent question about whether it has lost its power to inspire.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Liz Berry</strong> (“Homing”): “you kept your accent / in a box beneath the bed” and “vowels ferrous as nails” convey the <dfn class="term" data-def="A deep, persistent longing for something, especially a place or time associated with happiness.">yearning</dfn> for a place you can hear in a voice. The title “Homing” itself — suggesting instinct, return, and the pull of where you are from.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write from memory: one key quotation from each of the three Nature poems, with a one-line explanation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You only need 2–3 short quotations per poem for the exam. Choose quotations that are versatile — ones you can use for multiple themes. “What man has made of man” works for nature, power, protest, and humanity’s failings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Key Quotations — Identity & Heritage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Mundair</strong> (“Name Journeys”): “infertile English soil” and “a stumble / that filled English mouths” show what England did to her first language. A name as a journey is the core idea.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Seneviratne</strong> (“A Wider View”): “He craved the comfort of a wider view” — a mill worker’s horizon, and a long view back through a family.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Nichols</strong> (“Like an Heiress”): The title simile frames a Guyanese childhood as wealth; “a lone / wave of rubbish” then spoils it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Key Quotations — Migration & Displacement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Berry</strong> (“On an Afternoon Train…”): The title’s documentary precision (exact route, date) is itself a key quotation. Focus on “startled me”, “racial brotherhood” and “Where are you from?”.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Robinson</strong> (“A Portable Paradise”): The title is the poem’s central concept — paradise as something internal and transportable. Focus on “white sands, green hills and fresh fish”.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Dharker</strong> (“A Century Later”): “the school-bell is a call to battle” and “You cannot kill a book / or the buzzing in it”. The title marks the centenary of the First World War.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Revision Strategies for the Anthology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>1. Quotation cards:</strong> Create a card for each poem with 2–3 short quotations, the poet’s name, and key themes. Test yourself by pulling a theme and recalling relevant quotations from multiple poems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>2. Comparison grids:</strong> Draw a grid with poems across the top and themes down the side. Fill in each cell with a brief note on how that poem relates to that theme. This reveals unexpected connections.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>3. Practice under timed conditions:</strong> The comparison essay has a recommended time of 45 minutes. Practice planning (5 minutes), writing (35 minutes), and checking (5 minutes). Speed comes from knowing your quotations — you should not need to think about <em>what</em> to quote, only <em>how</em> to use it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Draw a quick comparison grid from memory: 5 poems across the top, 3 themes down the side. Fill in one point per cell.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The exam will name ONE poem and ask you to compare it with another of your choice. Prepare 3–4 <dfn class="term" data-def="Pre-prepared poem pairings with identified similarities and differences, ready to deploy regardless of which poem the exam names.">flexible pairings</dfn> in advance so that whatever poem is named, you have a strong comparison ready. Each pairing should share a theme but differ in approach.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">The Day of the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Read the question carefully:</strong> Identify the theme or idea it asks about. Identify which poem is named. Choose your comparison poem based on which pairing gives you the richest argument for <em>this specific question</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Plan before writing:</strong> 5 minutes planning saves time overall. Note 3 comparative points, each with quotations from both poems. Decide your evaluative conclusion — which poem is more effective and why?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Write with confidence:</strong> Use the integrated structure (both poems in every paragraph). Embed quotations into your sentences. Link analysis to context. Evaluate throughout — don’t save your opinion for the end.</p>
