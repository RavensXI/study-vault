--- DESCRIPTION ---
Pasteur’s swan-necked flask, Koch’s microbe hunting, and how Germ Theory transformed the fight against disease.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Introduction: The Invisible Enemy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For centuries, nobody knew what caused disease. People blamed “bad air” (<dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that disease was caused by 'bad air' or foul-smelling vapours rising from rotting matter, swamps, or sewage.">miasma</dfn>), punishment from God, or imbalances in the <dfn class="term" data-def="An ancient Greek medical theory which claimed the body contained four liquids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) that needed to be in balance for good health.">Four Humours</dfn>. In the 1860s, two scientists — one French, one German — revealed the true cause: tiny, invisible organisms called <dfn class="term" data-def="Microscopic organisms such as bacteria and viruses that can cause disease.">germs</dfn>.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Louis Pasteur and the Germ Theory (1861)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Louis Pasteur</strong> was a French <strong>chemist</strong>, not a doctor. He was asked to investigate why <strong>beer and wine</strong> were going sour in the barrel — a serious problem for France’s wine industry. This practical, industrial problem — not a medical question — led to one of the biggest discoveries in medicine.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Pasteur discovered that tiny living organisms — <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny living organisms, also called germs, that are too small to see with the naked eye.">microbes</dfn> or “germs” — were floating in the air and causing the beer to spoil. This was revolutionary. Most scientists believed in <dfn class="term" data-def="The widely held (but incorrect) belief that living organisms could arise from non-living matter.">spontaneous generation</dfn> — the idea that decay and life simply appeared out of nowhere.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Pasteur’s swan-necked flask experiment</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">Pasteur placed broth in a flask with a long, curved “swan neck.” Air could enter, but the curve trapped any <dfn class="term" data-def="Microscopic organisms such as bacteria that float in the air and can cause contamination or disease when they come into contact with food, liquids, or wounds.">airborne microbes</dfn> before they reached the broth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The broth stayed <strong>fresh</strong>. But when the neck was broken off, letting microbes fall in, it quickly went <strong>cloudy and sour</strong>. This proved that germs in the air caused decay — not spontaneous generation.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Close this and state the 3 key ideas of the Germ Theory from 1861.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Pasteur’s Germ Theory (1861)</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n25">
<li><strong>Germs are present in the air.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Germs cause decay.</strong></li>
<li><strong>These same germs could also be responsible for causing disease in humans.</strong></li>
</ul>
</div>
<p>Pasteur then received <strong>government funding</strong> to investigate a disease killing French <dfn class="term" data-def="Caterpillars of the Bombyx mori moth that produce silk thread.">silkworms</dfn>. He proved germs were causing it — a key step in linking germs to disease in <strong>living organisms</strong>, not just decay in liquids.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Robert Koch: The Microbe Hunter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Pasteur had the big idea — that germs cause disease. The German doctor <strong>Robert Koch</strong> provided the practical proof. Koch studied under <strong>Professor Friedrich Henle</strong>, one of the first scientists to challenge <dfn class="term" data-def="The widely held (but incorrect) belief that living organisms could arise from non-living matter.">spontaneous generation</dfn>. He also served as a surgeon during the <strong>Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)</strong>, giving him first-hand experience of wounds and infection. Koch took Pasteur’s theory and developed a way to identify the <strong>specific microbes</strong> that caused <strong>specific diseases</strong>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n11">Koch’s Methods</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Koch developed key techniques for studying bacteria. He established the principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea, established by Robert Koch, that each specific disease is caused by a specific type of germ.">specificity</dfn> — that <strong>specific germs cause specific diseases</strong>. This meant each disease could be identified, targeted, and treated individually.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name Koch's 3 techniques for studying bacteria.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Koch’s Techniques</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n26">
<li><strong>Growing bacteria:</strong> Koch was the first to grow bacteria on a solid medium using <dfn class="term" data-def="A jelly-like substance used as a solid surface for growing bacteria in a laboratory.">agar jelly</dfn> plates. This allowed him to create <dfn class="term" data-def="A laboratory sample containing only one specific type of bacterium, with no contamination from other microbes.">pure cultures</dfn> — samples containing only one type of bacterium — so he could study each germ individually.</li>
<li><strong>Staining bacteria:</strong> Koch used industrial chemical <dfn class="term" data-def="Chemical substances used to colour bacteria so they could be seen more clearly under a microscope.">dyes</dfn> to stain bacteria, making them visible under a microscope. Different bacteria absorbed different dyes, allowing him to tell them apart.</li>
<li><strong>Photographing bacteria:</strong> Koch developed methods of <strong>photographing microbes</strong> under the microscope, creating permanent visual records that other scientists around the world could study and verify.</li>
<li><strong>Proving infection:</strong> Koch proved bacteria caused disease by <strong>injecting mice with anthrax bacteria</strong> and observing them fall ill. This provided direct experimental evidence that a specific germ caused a specific disease in a living organism.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n14">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1861</div>
<h4>Pasteur publishes Germ Theory</h4>
<p>Pasteur proves that microbes in the air cause decay, proposing that germs could also cause disease in humans.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1876</div>
<h4>Koch identifies the anthrax bacterium</h4>
<p>Using his new techniques of growing and staining bacteria, Koch identified the exact bacterium that caused <dfn class="term" data-def="A serious infectious disease caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis.">anthrax</dfn> — the first time a specific germ was linked to a specific disease.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1882</div>
<h4>Koch identifies the tuberculosis bacterium</h4>
<p>Koch identified the bacterium responsible for <dfn class="term" data-def="A deadly infectious disease that primarily affects the lungs, causing coughing, fever, and wasting.">tuberculosis</dfn> (TB), one of the biggest killers of the 19th century. This was proof that different germs caused different diseases.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1883</div>
<h4>Koch identifies the cholera bacterium</h4>
<p>Koch identified the bacterium that caused <dfn class="term" data-def="A waterborne disease caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae that causes severe diarrhoea and dehydration.">cholera</dfn>, confirming the earlier findings of John Snow, who had linked cholera to contaminated water in 1854.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1905</div>
<h4>Koch awarded the Nobel Prize</h4>
<p>Koch was awarded the <strong>Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine</strong> for his work on tuberculosis, recognising his enormous contribution to medical science.</p>
</div>
</div>
<p>Koch’s team — nicknamed <strong>“the Microbe Hunters”</strong> — identified over <strong>21 disease-causing bacteria</strong>, including those responsible for cholera, typhoid, meningitis, and plague.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Impact on Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Germ Theory was not accepted straight away in Britain. Many doctors resisted changing their beliefs. Improved <dfn class="term" data-def="Optical instruments used to magnify tiny objects.">microscope</dfn> technology was a key enabling factor — without better lenses, scientists could not have seen bacteria clearly enough to study them. Several British doctors helped promote the new ideas:</p>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17">British doctors like <strong>William Roberts</strong> and <strong>William Cheyne</strong> helped promote Pasteur and Koch’s ideas in Britain.</p>
<p>A key turning point came during the <strong>cattle plague of 1866</strong>. Professor <strong>Lionel Beale</strong> investigated and found the disease was caused by microscopic living particles — strong evidence for Germ Theory. From <strong>1870</strong>, physicist <strong>John Tyndall</strong> used his reputation and speaking skills to convince the British scientific establishment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Germ Theory had a chain of consequences:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n27">
<li data-narration-id="n19">It <strong>confirmed John Snow’s findings</strong> about cholera spreading through contaminated water (1854).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">It inspired <strong>Joseph Lister</strong> to develop <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of using chemical substances (such as carbolic acid) to kill germs and prevent infection during surgery.">antiseptic surgery</dfn> in 1867, cutting deaths from infection after operations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">It enabled Koch’s student <strong>Paul Ehrlich</strong> to develop <dfn class="term" data-def="Chemical drugs designed to target and kill specific disease-causing bacteria without harming the rest of the body.">“magic bullets”</dfn> — drugs that could target and destroy specific germs in the body.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="From memory, how did the Franco-Prussian War fuel Pasteur and Koch's rivalry?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The Rivalry</div>
<p>Pasteur (France) and Koch (Germany) were fierce rivals, driven partly by <strong>national competition</strong> between France and Germany after the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71). This rivalry actually <strong>accelerated progress</strong>, as each tried to outdo the other in identifying germs and developing vaccines.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Pasteur gave the world the theory — that germs cause disease — and Koch gave the practical methods to prove it. Together, they turned medicine from guesswork into science, opening the door to targeted treatments, vaccines, and public health reforms.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n28">
<li><strong>Pasteur provided the theory:</strong> His 1861 Germ Theory proved microbes cause decay and proposed they cause disease. His swan-necked flask experiment disproved spontaneous generation.</li>
<li><strong>Koch provided the proof:</strong> Using agar jelly plates and chemical dyes, he identified bacteria causing anthrax (1876), TB (1882), and cholera (1883).</li>
<li><strong>The impact was huge:</strong> Germ Theory confirmed Snow’s cholera findings, inspired Lister’s antiseptic surgery, and enabled Ehrlich’s “magic bullets.”</li>
</ul>
