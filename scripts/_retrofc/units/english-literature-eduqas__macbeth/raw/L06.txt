--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Macbeth — Tragic Hero or Villain?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Macbeth begins as a loyal warrior — “brave Macbeth” (Act 1, Scene 2) — celebrated for defending Scotland. His <dfn class="term" data-def="A character flaw that leads to the downfall of a tragic hero. Macbeth’s hamartia is his unchecked ambition, described as ‘vaulting ambition, which o’erleaps itself.’">hamartia</dfn> (tragic flaw) is ambition. The witches’ prophecies do not create his desire for power — they reveal an ambition that already existed (“horrid image”, Act 1, Scene 3). His trajectory moves from moral consciousness (Act 1, Scene 7: reasons not to kill) through guilt (Act 2: “Neptune’s ocean”) and paranoia (Act 3: “full of scorpions”) to nihilism (Act 5: “signifying nothing”).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The exam debate is whether Macbeth is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A noble character whose downfall is caused by a combination of fate and personal flaw. Macbeth fits this pattern: a respected warrior destroyed by his own unchecked ambition.">tragic hero</dfn> deserving pity or a villain who chose evil. Evidence for the tragic hero: he hesitates, he feels guilt, he recognises what he has lost (“I have lived long enough”, Act 5, Scene 3). Evidence for the villain: he murders innocents (Macduff’s family), he becomes a tyrant by choice (“I am in blood stepped in so far”).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three pieces of evidence that Macbeth is a tragic hero and three that he is a villain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Macbeth’s character arc: loyal warrior → tempted nobleman → guilt-stricken murderer → paranoid tyrant → nihilistic villain. The key exam question is whether he deserves sympathy (tragic hero with a fatal flaw) or condemnation (a man who chose evil despite knowing it was wrong).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lady Macbeth — From Controller to Victim</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Lady Macbeth’s arc is the inverse of her husband’s. She begins as the dominant, controlling partner — “unsex me here” (Act 1, Scene 5), manipulation tactics (Act 1, Scene 7), practical calm after the murder (“a little water”, Act 2, Scene 2). By Act 3, she is sidelined: “Nought’s had, all’s spent, / Where our desire is got without content” (Act 3, Scene 2) reveals her disillusionment — they have gained everything and feel nothing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Her sleepwalking in Act 5 shows complete psychological collapse. She speaks in prose (not verse), her speech fragments across multiple memories, and the guilt she once dismissed (“a little water”) now overwhelms her (“All the perfumes of Arabia”). Her offstage death is reported by Macbeth’s cold “She should have died hereafter.” Shakespeare presents her as both transgressor and victim: she defied Jacobean gender norms and paid the ultimate price.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Banquo serves as Macbeth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who contrasts with another to highlight their qualities. Banquo’s loyalty and moral resistance expose Macbeth’s weakness.">foil</dfn>. He hears the same prophecies but warns against trusting the witches (“instruments of darkness”, Act 1, Scene 3). He experiences temptation — “cursed thoughts that nature / Gives way to in repose” (Act 2, Scene 1) — but resists it. His loyalty to Duncan’s memory survives where Macbeth’s does not. His murder (Act 3, Scene 3) and ghostly return (Act 3, Scene 4) make him the play’s symbol of inescapable guilt and legitimate succession (his descendants = the Stuart dynasty = James I).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Macduff — The Avenger and True Man</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Macduff is Macbeth’s nemesis — the agent of divine justice. His suspicion begins early: “Wherefore did you so?” (Act 2, Scene 3) challenges Macbeth’s story. He refuses to attend Macbeth’s coronation, demonstrating moral courage. His grief for his family (“All my pretty ones?”, Act 4, Scene 3) and his redefinition of masculinity (“I must also feel it as a man”) present an alternative to Macbeth’s toxic version. He fulfils the apparition’s prophecy as the man “not of woman born” who restores order by killing the tyrant.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10b">Critically, Macduff’s flight to England (leaving his family unprotected) adds complexity. His anguished guilt when he learns of their murder — “Sinful Macduff, / They were all struck for thee” — makes him more than a simple revenge hero. He carries guilt alongside righteousness, contrasting with Macbeth’s unrepentant tyranny. Shakespeare shows that even virtuous characters make flawed decisions, but unlike Macbeth, Macduff channels his guilt into justice rather than further violence.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Duncan</strong> represents the ideal king: “hath borne his faculties so meek” (Act 1, Scene 7). He uses nurturing language (“I have begun to plant thee”, Act 1, Scene 4) and trusts his nobles. His fatal flaw is excessive trust: “There’s no art to find the mind’s construction in the face.” His murder disrupts the Chain of Being and triggers nature’s rebellion.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Malcolm</strong> is the play’s legitimate heir. His testing of Macduff (Act 4, Scene 3) shows political wisdom. He lists the “king-becoming graces” that a ruler needs, contrasting himself with Macbeth’s tyranny. His restoration at the end repairs the Chain of Being.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>The Witches</strong> are agents of chaos. They speak in trochaic tetrameter (unnatural rhythm), use equivocation, and exist outside the Chain of Being. The key debate: do they control Macbeth’s fate, or merely reveal desires already within him? Shakespeare never answers this definitively — the ambiguity is the point.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each of the six characters, write one key quotation and its significance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The character web: Macbeth (tragic hero or villain), Lady Macbeth (controller turned victim), Banquo (moral foil), Macduff (avenger and redefined masculinity), Duncan (ideal but naive king), Malcolm (wise legitimate heir), Witches (agents of chaos and equivocation). Each character illuminates a different aspect of the play’s themes.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Macbeth’s arc (loyal warrior → nihilistic tyrant) raises the exam debate: tragic hero or villain? Both readings are valid with evidence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Lady Macbeth’s inverse arc (controller → victim) reflects Shakespeare’s punishment of Jacobean gender transgression. Banquo serves as the moral foil.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Macduff redefines masculinity (“I must also feel it as a man”), Malcolm defines legitimate kingship, and the Witches remain deliberately ambiguous as agents of fate or revealers of existing desire.</li></ul>
