<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Meena Kumar — Our Narrator</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Meena is nine years old, sharp, funny, and a compulsive liar. The novel opens with her stealing a collection tin outside Mr Ormerod’s shop and immediately lying about it. Syal establishes Meena as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator whose perspective cannot be fully trusted because of their age, bias, or limited understanding — the reader must look beyond what they say to understand the full truth.">unreliable narrator</dfn> — she exaggerates, invents stories, and sees the world through a child’s limited understanding. This creates dramatic irony: the reader often understands more than Meena about the racism and social tensions around her.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Meena’s lying is not malicious. She creates stories to make herself more interesting, to fit in, and to process a world that does not always make sense to her. Syal uses Meena’s imagination to show how a child navigates between two cultures — inventing identities because her real one does not fit neatly into either world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why does Meena lie, and what does her lying reveal about her identity?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena’s storytelling and lying reflect her search for identity. She creates fictional versions of herself because she does not yet know who she is. Syal uses the unreliable child narrator to create humour, dramatic irony, and emotional depth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Kumar Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Papa (Shyam Kumar)</strong> is an educated, dignified man who works at a factory despite being overqualified. He never complains about the racism he faces and maintains his family’s pride and cultural traditions. He represents the <dfn class="term" data-def="The generation of immigrants who came to Britain as adults, maintaining strong connections to their homeland's culture, language, and traditions.">first generation</dfn>’s quiet resilience — enduring prejudice with grace while building a life for his children.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Mama (Daljit Kumar)</strong> is warm, sharp-tongued, and homesick. She maintains Punjabi traditions, cooks Indian food, and gathers with the “Aunties and Uncles” (the wider Indian community who visit regularly). She is pregnant throughout much of the novel, and the arrival of Meena’s baby brother Sunil represents continuity and hope. Mama is also the family’s moral compass — she sees through Meena’s lies and understands the social dynamics of the village.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The “Aunties and Uncles” are not blood relatives but members of the wider Indian diaspora community who gather regularly at the Kumar house. These gatherings are filled with music, food, storytelling, and Punjabi language. Syal presents them as a vital support network — they share the experience of immigration and provide a sense of belonging that Tollington cannot.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Meena is sometimes embarrassed by these gatherings (they mark her as “different”) but also deeply comforted by them. The contrast between the warmth and cultural richness of the Indian community and the sometimes hostile English village is one of the novel’s central <dfn class="term" data-def="The placing of two things close together to highlight their differences.">juxtapositions</dfn>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Tollington’s Characters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Nanima</strong> (Meena’s grandmother) arrives from India later in the novel and becomes a transformative figure. She does not speak English but communicates through gesture, warmth, and traditional stories. Nanima represents the heritage and wisdom that Meena has been rejecting in her desire to fit in. Her presence helps Meena reconnect with her Punjabi identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Sam Lowbridge</strong> is a local white teenager who represents overt racism. He is attracted to Meena but also capable of racist violence. His complexity is important — Syal does not present racism as belonging only to obvious villains. Sam is also a product of poverty, neglect, and limited opportunity, showing that racism and social deprivation are intertwined.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name four key characters and explain what each represents thematically.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Kumar family represents the immigrant experience: Papa’s quiet dignity, Mama’s cultural preservation, and Nanima’s traditional wisdom contrast with Meena’s desire to assimilate. Sam Lowbridge represents how racism intersects with class deprivation.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The novel opens with Meena outside Mr Ormerod’s shop, stealing a charity collection tin. The scene establishes Meena’s character (impulsive, mischievous, imaginative), the village setting (small, everyone knows everyone), and the novel’s tone (humorous but with underlying tension). When Meena spots Anita Rutter for the first time, she is immediately fascinated — Anita represents everything Meena wants to be: confident, English, and free from parental supervision.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The opening also introduces the theme of storytelling. Meena’s version of events is always more exciting than reality. Syal invites us to enjoy Meena’s voice while recognising that the child does not fully understand the world around her.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Syal’s Tollington is populated with vividly drawn characters who reflect different aspects of 1960s working-class life: the nosy neighbours, the local hard man, the neglected children, and the well-meaning but clueless white liberals who treat the Kumars as exotic. Each character contributes to the novel’s richly textured portrayal of community, class, and race.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Meena is an unreliable child narrator whose lying and storytelling reflect her search for identity between two cultures.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Kumar family represents different aspects of the immigrant experience: dignity, cultural preservation, and the tension between heritage and assimilation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Tollington’s characters reveal how racism operates at every level — from casual assumptions to overt hostility.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When writing about Meena as a narrator, always discuss the effect of her <strong>unreliability</strong>. The gap between what Meena understands and what the reader understands creates dramatic irony. For example, Meena does not initially recognise casual racism for what it is — but the reader does. This technique allows Syal to present racism as both subtle and devastating, a key point for AO1 and AO3.</p>
