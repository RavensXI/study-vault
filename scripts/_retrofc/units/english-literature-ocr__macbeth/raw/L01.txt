--- DESCRIPTION ---
James I, the Gunpowder Plot, witchcraft and the Divine Right of Kings — the world behind Macbeth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Play Written for a King</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Macbeth</em> was first performed around 1606, three years after James VI of Scotland became King James I of England. Shakespeare wrote it partly as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate gesture of respect and flattery towards a monarch.">royal compliment</dfn>. James claimed to be descended from the real-life Banquo, so casting Banquo as noble and Macbeth as a villain flattered the new king. Every detail — Scottish setting, royal lineage, witches, regicide — is shaped by the fact that James would be watching.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and write, in three bullet points, how James I’s life directly shaped the plot of Macbeth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>James I was obsessed with witchcraft (he wrote <em>Daemonologie</em> in 1597), had survived the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, and believed kings were chosen by God. All three obsessions — witches, regicide, Divine Right — drive the plot of Macbeth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Divine Right of Kings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">James believed in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that a monarch is chosen by God and his authority cannot be challenged.">Divine Right of Kings</dfn> — the idea that a king is appointed by God and answerable only to him. Killing a rightful king was therefore not only murder but sacrilege. Macbeth’s assassination of Duncan is presented as an act that upends nature itself: horses eat each other, day turns to night, and an owl kills a falcon. These <dfn class="term" data-def="Unnatural or supernatural events that show the world is out of balance.">portents</dfn> show the audience that Macbeth has broken the moral order.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Duncan is deliberately drawn as a saintly, generous king — “his virtues / Will plead like angels” — so that his murder is a clear offence against God. When Malcolm is finally crowned at the end, the Divine Right order is restored and harmony returns. Audiences in 1606 would read this as moral reassurance after the shock of the Gunpowder Plot.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">A small group of English Catholics led by Robert Catesby (and including Guy Fawkes) attempted to blow up Parliament while the Protestant king and his lords sat inside. The plot was discovered on 5 November 1605. Shakespeare’s play arrives in the theatre less than a year later, and <em>Macbeth</em> is sometimes called his ‘Gunpowder play’.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Porter’s speech in Act 2 Scene 3 is a direct topical reference. He imagines an “equivocator” who “could swear in both the scales against either scale” — a pointed reference to Father Henry Garnet, a Jesuit priest who defended the Catholic doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="Deliberately misleading speech that is technically true, used to hide meaning from authority.">equivocation</dfn> and was executed in 1606. The Weird Sisters’ deliberately ambiguous prophecies are a dramatic version of the same idea.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Witches in Jacobean England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">James had personally interrogated witches he blamed for raising a storm against his ship in 1590. His book <em>Daemonologie</em> (1597) set out the king’s view of witchcraft: witches were servants of the Devil, able to predict the future and manipulate weather, but always subordinate to God. Shakespeare’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The three supernatural sisters in Macbeth who deliver prophecies and manipulate the title character.">Weird Sisters</dfn> fit this model: they can predict but cannot compel, and their words always require Macbeth’s willing action to come true.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In 1604, Parliament passed a new Witchcraft Act that made witchcraft a capital crime. Between 1590 and 1680 over 500 people were executed in Scotland alone for witchcraft. When the Weird Sisters say “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” they are literally threatening an upside-down moral universe — for a Jacobean audience, that was terrifyingly real, not metaphor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in one sentence why the Weird Sisters’ prophecies would feel more dangerous to a 1606 audience than a modern one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For a Jacobean audience, witches were not fictional. The belief that spoken prophecy had the power to shape events gave the Weird Sisters genuine menace — they risked the moral order every time they spoke.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Gender, Honour and the Warrior Code</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Jacobean Scotland is dramatised as a violent, masculine world. Macbeth is first introduced to us by the wounded Captain as the soldier who “unseam’d” the rebel Macdonwald “from the nave to the chaps.” Violence is equated with masculinity, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The expected behaviours, qualities and roles society attaches to being a man or woman.">gender</dfn> is used by Lady Macbeth as a weapon: she accuses Macbeth of being unmanly when he hesitates to kill Duncan, telling him to “screw your courage to the sticking-place.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Honour in this world depends on your loyalty to the king. When Macbeth breaks that loyalty, he betrays not just Duncan but the whole Scottish warrior code. By the end, Macduff — loyal, faithful and a husband and father — re-establishes honour through lawful violence. Shakespeare uses this arc to argue that masculinity without virtue becomes tyranny.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Shakespeare’s main source was Raphael Holinshed’s <em>Chronicles</em> (1587), a popular history of Britain. The real Macbeth was an 11th-century Scottish king who took the throne after killing Duncan in battle and ruled for 17 years. Shakespeare darkens the story: compresses the timeline, invents the witches’ prophecies and makes Duncan saintly rather than weak. Knowing this helps you discuss Shakespeare’s <em>dramatic choices</em> rather than treating the play as history.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why Context Matters in Exam Answers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Weak answers drop context into an essay as a paragraph of biography. Strong answers use context to <em>explain a line</em>. When you quote Macbeth’s “two truths are told / As happy prologues”, you can immediately link it to Jacobean ideas about equivocation, the Gunpowder Plot and the danger of ambiguous speech. Context should sharpen your reading, not replace it.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Shakespeare wrote Macbeth for James I: the king’s obsessions with witchcraft, regicide and Divine Right shape the plot, characters and themes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Gunpowder Plot of 1605 makes regicide and equivocation urgent Jacobean fears — the Porter and the witches both play on them.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Context earns marks when it is woven into a sentence of analysis, not presented as a separate biographical paragraph.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When examiners ask you to explore ideas in Macbeth, never list context as a separate block. Weave a short contextual phrase into an analysis sentence: ‘Shakespeare’s Jacobean audience, terrified of equivocation after the Gunpowder Plot, would hear the witches’ language as genuine threat.’ One sentence, fully integrated, earns more than a whole paragraph of unconnected historical background.</p>
