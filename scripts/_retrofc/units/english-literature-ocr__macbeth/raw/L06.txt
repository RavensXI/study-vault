<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Macbeth: From Hero to Tyrant</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">At the start of the play, Macbeth is a celebrated warrior. The Captain describes how he 'unseamed' the rebel Macdonwald 'from the nave to the chops' — a brutally violent image, but one framed as heroic loyalty. Duncan rewards him: 'O valiant cousin! Worthy gentleman!' This establishes Macbeth as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose nobility, status, and admirable qualities make their eventual downfall more tragic. Macbeth begins as a respected warrior before ambition corrupts him.">tragic hero</dfn> whose initial virtue makes his fall more devastating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">His <dfn class="term" data-def="A fatal flaw in a tragic hero that leads to their downfall. Macbeth's hamartia is his 'vaulting ambition.'">hamartia</dfn> is ambition. Even before meeting the witches, he imagines murder: the 'horrid image' unfixes his hair. After the witches' prophecies, he oscillates between action and guilt, needing Lady Macbeth's manipulation to commit the deed. But by Act 3, he plans Banquo's murder alone and by Act 4, he orders the massacre of innocents. Shakespeare traces a clear arc: reluctant murderer → paranoid king → full tyrant.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and trace Macbeth's character arc through five key moments, one from each act.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Macbeth's character arc: Act 1 — celebrated warrior with dangerous ambition. Act 2 — reluctant murderer consumed by guilt. Act 3 — paranoid king who plans murder independently. Act 4 — tyrant who massacres innocents. Act 5 — nihilistic, despairing figure who dies fighting.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lady Macbeth: Control and Collapse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Lady Macbeth is one of Shakespeare's most complex female characters. In Act 1, she is the driving force behind Duncan's murder. Her 'Unsex me here' soliloquy asks dark spirits to remove her femininity and fill her with 'direst cruelty.' She manipulates Macbeth by questioning his manhood ('When you durst do it, then you were a man') and demonstrates cold practicality after the murder, returning the daggers and dismissing guilt: 'A little water clears us of this deed.'</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">But Shakespeare constructs a devastating <dfn class="term" data-def="A reversal of fortune or situation, often from good to bad. Lady Macbeth's peripeteia is her shift from composed manipulator to guilt-destroyed sleepwalker.">peripeteia</dfn> (reversal). By Act 5, she has lost all control. Her sleepwalking, fragmented speech, and obsessive hand-washing reveal that the guilt she suppressed has consumed her entirely. The Doctor's verdict — 'More needs she the divine than the physician' — frames her collapse as divine punishment. Her offstage death (implied suicide) completes the reversal: the character who demanded power over others ultimately has no power over herself.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Banquo functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who contrasts with another character, highlighting their qualities by comparison. Banquo's loyalty and moral restraint highlight Macbeth's ambition and corruption.">foil</dfn> to Macbeth. Both hear the witches' prophecies, but their responses diverge. Banquo is cautious: 'oftentimes, to win us to our harm, / The instruments of darkness tell us truths, / Win us with honest trifles, to betray's / In deepest consequence.' He recognises the danger of trusting the supernatural. Macbeth, by contrast, is immediately seduced.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">However, Banquo is not entirely innocent. In Act 3, Scene 1, he admits 'Thou hast it now: King, Cawdor, Glamis, all' and suspects foul play, yet he does nothing to act on his suspicions. Shakespeare shows him thinking about the prophecy that his descendants will be kings. This complexity prevents Banquo from being a simple moral paragon, but his restraint still contrasts powerfully with Macbeth's action.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Macduff: The Avenger</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Macduff represents legitimate opposition to tyranny. He is the first to discover Duncan's body and to voice suspicion of Macbeth. He flees to England to join Malcolm, putting Scotland's liberation above his own family's safety — a decision that costs him everything when Macbeth has his wife and children murdered.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Macduff's most significant moment is his redefinition of masculinity in Act 4, Scene 3. When Malcolm tells him to 'Dispute it like a man,' Macduff replies: 'I shall do so; / But I must also feel it as a man.' This challenges the toxic version of manhood that drives the Macbeths — the idea that real men suppress emotion and act through violence. Macduff shows that courage and grief can coexist.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Malcolm's character develops primarily in Act 4, Scene 3. His loyalty test of Macduff — pretending to be lustful, greedy, and lacking every kingly virtue — shows political shrewdness. He lists the 'king-becoming graces': 'justice, verity, temperance, stableness, / Bounty, perseverance, mercy, lowliness, / Devotion, patience, courage, fortitude.' These are the qualities Macbeth lacks, and they define what legitimate kingship looks like in the play.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Malcolm's final speech reduces the Macbeths to 'this dead butcher and his fiend-like queen.' While this oversimplifies their complexity, it serves a political function: Malcolm needs to establish moral clarity to legitimise his rule. His promise to restore order echoes the Great Chain of Being and reassures the audience that divine justice has been served.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each of the five main characters, name one key quotation and explain what it reveals about them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shakespeare uses character contrasts to sharpen his themes. Banquo (restraint) foils Macbeth (ambition). Macduff (emotional honesty) contrasts with Lady Macbeth (suppression). Malcolm's kingly virtues contrast with Macbeth's tyranny. These pairings are essential for the OCR exam's character-based questions.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Macbeth's arc — from reluctant murderer to nihilistic tyrant — makes him a tragic hero whose hamartia (ambition) destroys everything he values.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Lady Macbeth's peripeteia from composed manipulator to guilt-destroyed sleepwalker shows that suppressed emotion cannot be contained forever.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Banquo, Macduff, and Malcolm serve as foils to the Macbeths, offering contrasting models of loyalty, masculinity, and kingship.</li></ul>
