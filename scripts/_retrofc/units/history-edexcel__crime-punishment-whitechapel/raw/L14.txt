How detectives, the press and the Vigilance Committee chased Jack the Ripper.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Canonical Five</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between 31 August and 9 November 1888, five women were murdered in or around Whitechapel by an unidentified killer. Most historians refer to these as the &lsquo;canonical five&rsquo;: Mary Ann Nichols (Buck&rsquo;s Row, 31 August), Annie Chapman (Hanbury Street, 8 September), Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes (the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="The murders of Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes within an hour of each other on 30 September 1888, in Met and City of London Police territories respectively.">double event</dfn>&rsquo; on 30 September) and Mary Jane Kelly (Miller&rsquo;s Court, 9 November). All five lived in lodging houses around Flower and Dean Street and Dorset Street; all but Kelly were killed in the street; all suffered injuries that suggested some anatomical knowledge. The case became known internationally because of the way the press reported it, not just because of the murders themselves.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the five canonical Ripper victims with the dates of their murders.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five canonical Ripper murders ran from 31 August to 9 November 1888. They were investigated jointly by H Division of the Metropolitan Police, Scotland Yard&rsquo;s CID and the City of London Police &mdash; the &lsquo;double event&rsquo; of 30 September straddled the Met/City boundary, complicating evidence-sharing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Detective Methods in 1888</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">By 1888 detective work in Britain was less than a decade old in its modern form. The CID, founded 1878 after a corruption scandal, was small and Scotland Yard-centred. The investigation was led on the ground by <dfn class="term" data-def="H Division detective inspector who led the on-the-ground Whitechapel murders investigation in autumn 1888.">Inspector Frederick Abberline</dfn>, who knew the East End well, and overseen by the head of CID, <dfn class="term" data-def="Lawyer and intelligence official; head of Metropolitan Police CID from August 1888 and overseer of the Ripper detective work.">Robert Anderson</dfn>. Their toolkit was limited.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Detectives interviewed witnesses door-to-door across Whitechapel, taking written statements that survive in the H Division case papers. They commissioned <dfn class="term" data-def="A drawing of a person, drawn from witness descriptions, used in late-Victorian investigation before photography of suspects was routine.">police sketches</dfn> of suspects from witness descriptions and circulated them in the press. They photographed crime scenes &mdash; for the first time in a Met investigation, Catherine Eddowes&rsquo; body in Mitre Square was photographed by the City of London Police on 30 September 1888 &mdash; though photographs of suspects in custody were not yet routine. Police surgeons such as <dfn class="term" data-def="Police surgeon to the Metropolitan Police; produced detailed forensic notes and the first known offender profile during the 1888 Whitechapel investigation.">Thomas Bond</dfn> conducted post-mortems and produced what is now considered an early offender profile, suggesting characteristics of the killer based on the wounds.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">What was missing is just as important. There was no fingerprinting in 1888 (introduced by the Met in 1901), no blood-typing (developed in 1900), no central database of suspects, and only a paper record system. Door-to-door interviews in Whitechapel ran into the same problem the lodging houses created elsewhere: residents moved frequently, and witnesses might be untraceable a week later. The investigation depended overwhelmingly on tips, witness statements and luck.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Goulston Street Graffito</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In the early hours of 30 September 1888 &mdash; the &lsquo;double event&rsquo; &mdash; a piece of Catherine Eddowes&rsquo; bloodstained apron was found in the entrance to a flat block on Goulston Street, just inside H Division territory. Above it on the wall, in chalk, was the message: &lsquo;The Juwes are the men That Will not be Blamed for nothing.&rsquo; The misspelling and the line break in the original were noted by the City of London Police officer who first found it.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Sir Charles Warren arrived in person at dawn. The City of London Police wanted to photograph the writing as evidence; Warren ordered it scrubbed off before sunrise. He feared that, if Whitechapel residents read it on their way to work, anti-Jewish riots would break out &mdash; the area had a long record of antisemitic violence and the Ripper rumours had already named Jewish migrants as suspects. The decision still divides historians: it may have saved lives, but it destroyed potentially crucial evidence and outraged the City Police, who had not been consulted. It is also a textbook example of how the Met/City boundary turned a single crime scene into a jurisdictional problem.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">A common error is to assume Victorian police routinely circulated photographs of suspects. They did not. Photography was used at crime scenes &mdash; Catherine Eddowes&rsquo; body in Mitre Square was photographed in 1888 &mdash; and prison authorities were beginning to keep photographic registers of convicted offenders. But circulating a suspect photograph to identify someone before charge was not yet routine procedure in the Met. Detectives still relied on witness descriptions and police sketches.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Press and the &lsquo;Dear Boss&rsquo; Letters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The Whitechapel murders were the first major British crime story to play out in a fully developed mass-circulation press. <em>The Star</em> was a halfpenny evening paper with a circulation of around 300,000 by 1888, and <em>The Pall Mall Gazette</em> under W.T. Stead pioneered sensational social-investigation journalism. Headlines such as &lsquo;Another Horrible Murder&rsquo; ran daily; <em>Punch</em> magazine ran satirical cartoons mocking Sir Charles Warren and the Met as bumbling.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">On 27 September 1888 the Central News Agency received a letter signed &lsquo;Jack the Ripper&rsquo;, addressed &lsquo;Dear Boss&rsquo;. The name stuck. A second letter and a postcard followed in early October. Police came to suspect, then to conclude, that the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="Letter received by the Central News Agency on 27 September 1888 signed &lsquo;Jack the Ripper&rsquo;; named the killer in the press but is widely believed by police to have been a journalist&rsquo;s hoax.">Dear Boss letter</dfn>&rsquo; was a journalist&rsquo;s hoax to drive sales. By then it had named the killer in every paper in the country. A separate parcel arrived on 16 October at the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, addressed to its chairman <dfn class="term" data-def="Master builder of Mile End and chairman of the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, formed in September 1888 to organise civilian patrols.">George Lusk</dfn>; it contained half a human kidney and a taunting letter signed &lsquo;From Hell&rsquo;. Whether the &lsquo;From Hell&rsquo; letter was genuine remains contested.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Whitechapel Vigilance Committee</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In September 1888, after the Chapman murder, local tradesmen formed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Civilian organisation formed in Whitechapel in September 1888 to patrol streets, hire detectives and offer rewards during the Ripper murders.">Whitechapel Vigilance Committee</dfn> under George Lusk, a master builder. The Committee organised civilian patrols of the rookeries at night, hired private detectives and lobbied the Home Office to offer a reward (which Henry Matthews refused to authorise from public funds). It is a good example of late-Victorian civil society stepping in where the state was perceived to have failed. Critics in the press argued the patrols were duplicating police work; defenders argued they were a legitimate response to a force that had lost public trust.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The pressure of the autumn of terror brought down both Sir Charles Warren and his Commissioner predecessors&rsquo; reform agenda. After a sustained press campaign, Warren resigned on 8 November 1888 &mdash; the day before Mary Jane Kelly was murdered in Miller&rsquo;s Court. No one was ever charged with the canonical five murders, and the case remains officially unsolved.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Sources for the Historian</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Whitechapel matters to GCSE History because it requires students to handle real Victorian sources. Local sources include H Division police casebooks and witness statements (Met archive), Whitechapel Union workhouse admission registers, coroners&rsquo; reports from inquests on the victims, Booth&rsquo;s 1889 poverty notebooks and the records of the Vigilance Committee. National sources include newspaper coverage in <em>The Times</em>, <em>The Star</em>, <em>The Daily Telegraph</em> and <em>Punch</em>, the Old Bailey records and Hansard debates on the Home Secretary&rsquo;s decisions. Each source type has strengths and weaknesses. A coroner&rsquo;s report is precise on injury but says nothing about public mood; a newspaper cartoon captures public mood but tells you little about what police actually did. Examiners reward students who pick the right source for the right enquiry &mdash; and who weigh content, provenance and contextual knowledge together when judging usefulness.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">A frequent error is to judge a source&rsquo;s usefulness purely on provenance &mdash; &lsquo;it&rsquo;s a sensational newspaper, so it&rsquo;s biased, so it&rsquo;s not useful&rsquo;. The highest mark band on the source utility question specifically rewards integrating content, provenance and contextual knowledge. A sensational <em>Punch</em> cartoon is biased &mdash; it is an opinion piece &mdash; but it is highly useful for an enquiry into public attitudes towards the Met in 1888, precisely because it captures the mood. Provenance shapes how you read a source; it does not, on its own, make the source useless.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why a source&rsquo;s usefulness depends on the question you are asking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A source&rsquo;s usefulness depends on the enquiry. A police casebook is excellent for what officers actually did, weaker for public mood; a <em>Punch</em> cartoon is excellent for public mood, weaker for procedural fact. Examiners reward students who weigh content, provenance and own knowledge together rather than treating one of the three as decisive.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The five canonical Ripper murders ran from 31 August to 9 November 1888 and were investigated jointly by H Division CID, Scotland Yard and the City of London Police; cooperation was complicated by the Met/City boundary, especially during the &lsquo;double event&rsquo; of 30 September.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Detective methods in 1888 were limited to witness statements, sketches, crime-scene photography and post-mortems; there was no fingerprinting, no blood-typing and no central suspect database, and Sir Charles Warren&rsquo;s decision to erase the Goulston Street graffito remains historiographically contested.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The mass press named the killer (the &lsquo;Dear Boss&rsquo; letter, probably a hoax), satirised the police (<em>Punch</em> cartoons of Warren) and shaped public mood; the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee under George Lusk shows late-Victorian civil society stepping in where the state was thought to have failed.</li>
</ul>
