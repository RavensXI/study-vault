--- DESCRIPTION ---
How village communities, tithings and the hue and cry kept order before 1066.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Society Without Police</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Late Anglo-Saxon England, around the year 1000, was a small farming society of perhaps two million people. There were no police, no prisons in the modern sense, and no professional courts. Yet villages still needed to keep order &mdash; theft of livestock, violent quarrels and disputes over land could not be ignored. The kings of Wessex and Mercia, including <dfn class="term" data-def="King of the English from 924&ndash;939; issued some of the earliest written Anglo-Saxon law codes.">King &AElig;thelstan</dfn>, set out written law codes to defend what was called the king&rsquo;s peace. The system depended on neighbours watching neighbours: order came from the community, not from the state.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Crimes fell into three broad groups. Crimes against the person included assault and murder. Crimes against property covered theft and arson. Crimes against authority included treason against the king and refusal to pay taxes. The most serious offences &mdash; treason and murder &mdash; were punished by death, but most everyday disputes were settled with fines and compensation rather than execution.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three categories of Anglo-Saxon crime, with one example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Anglo-Saxon law recognised three categories of crime: against the person (assault, murder), against property (theft, arson) and against authority (treason, tax evasion). The king&rsquo;s peace covered all of them.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Tithings and Collective Responsibility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Every free man over the age of twelve belonged to a <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of ten Anglo-Saxon men jointly responsible for each other's behaviour and for bringing wrongdoers to court.">tithing</dfn> &mdash; a group of ten men who were collectively responsible for each other&rsquo;s behaviour. If one member of the tithing committed a crime, the others had to bring him to court or pay a heavy fine themselves. This was known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon principle that a community was jointly responsible for the behaviour of its members.">collective responsibility</dfn>, and it forced communities to police themselves.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">When a crime was witnessed, anyone could raise the <dfn class="term" data-def="A loud public call for help when a crime was committed; everyone hearing it had to drop what they were doing and join the chase.">hue and cry</dfn>. Every man in the village was legally obliged to drop his work and chase the suspected criminal. Failure to join the chase meant the whole village could be fined. Above the village, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A royal official in charge of a shire (county) who collected fines and presided over local courts.">shire reeve</dfn> &mdash; the origin of the modern word &lsquo;sheriff&rsquo; &mdash; collected fines and presided over the shire court on behalf of the king.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">It is tempting to describe the hue and cry as if it were a uniform, nationwide police force. It was not. Whether the chase was successful depended entirely on the local community &mdash; how alert neighbours were, whether they were willing to abandon their work, and whether the criminal had local support. In tightly knit villages it could be effective; in towns or along busy roads it was often easily evaded. Examiners reward students who treat the hue and cry as a community-driven system that varied in effectiveness, not as a national police force.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Trial by Jury and Oath-Helping</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Once a suspect was caught, the case went to a local court. There were no professional lawyers. Instead, the accused was tried by a jury of local men who already knew them. A central feature was oath-helping: the accused gathered &lsquo;oath-helpers&rsquo; who swore in court that the accused was telling the truth. The more respected the oath-helpers, the stronger the defence. This was a system based on personal reputation, not on physical evidence as we would understand it today. If a verdict could not be reached, more dramatic methods such as trial by ordeal could follow &mdash; a topic for a later lesson.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Wergild: Compensation, Not Just Punishment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">If someone was killed or injured, the wrongdoer often had to pay <dfn class="term" data-def="&lsquo;Man-price&rsquo;: compensation paid by an offender to the victim or victim's family in Anglo-Saxon law.">wergild</dfn>, literally &lsquo;man-price&rsquo;. This compensation was paid not to the king but to the victim or the victim&rsquo;s family. The amount depended on the social rank of the victim. By the laws of <dfn class="term" data-def="King of England 1016&ndash;1035; his law codes refined the wergild scale and tightened the king's peace.">King Cnut</dfn>, the death of a nobleman could cost roughly 1,200 shillings; a free peasant&rsquo;s life was valued at around 200 shillings; a slave&rsquo;s, less still. Even injuries had a price &mdash; loss of an ear, a finger or an eye each had a fixed payment.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">The point of wergild was to head off the <dfn class="term" data-def="A cycle of revenge killings between two families when a wrong was not settled.">blood feud</dfn>. Without compensation, an injured family might take revenge by killing the offender, which would trigger another revenge killing in return. Wergild allowed a wrong to be settled in cash and stopped feuds from escalating. It is therefore a mistake to read Anglo-Saxon punishment as simply brutal: it combined harsh penalties for serious crime with a pragmatic system of restitution that kept communities together.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: was the wergild paid to the king?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common error is to describe wergild as a fine paid to the king or to the courts. It was not. It was compensation paid directly to the victim or, if the victim had been killed, to their surviving family. Fines paid to the king (called the <em>wite</em>) did exist alongside wergild and were sometimes added on top, but the wergild itself was a private payment that recognised the victim&rsquo;s loss. Treating it as a private compensation, not a state fine, is what unlocks the higher mark band.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Deterrence and Retribution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For serious crimes that were not settled by wergild, Anglo-Saxon punishment was deliberately public and harsh. Corporal punishments included branding, flogging and the cutting off of a hand for repeated theft. Capital punishments &mdash; hanging or beheading &mdash; were reserved for treason, arson and the worst murders. Under <dfn class="term" data-def="King of England 1042&ndash;1066, whose codes drew on earlier Anglo-Saxon traditions of the king's peace.">Edward the Confessor</dfn>, the law continued to mix money compensation, mutilation and execution depending on the nature of the offence and the rank of the victim.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Two ideas guided this system. The first was deterrence: punishments were carried out in the open so that other villagers would think twice before offending. The second was retribution &mdash; the idea that a wrong done to a person or community had to be visibly answered. Neither idea was about reforming the criminal, an aim that would only emerge centuries later. In a small farming society where survival depended on neighbours trusting one another, the priority was to restore order quickly and clearly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why wergild was used &mdash; what problem did it solve?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wergild was paid to the victim or the victim&rsquo;s family, not to the king. By providing compensation for death or injury it was designed to prevent the blood feud, in which families took revenge through more killings.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Anglo-Saxon law enforcement was community-based: tithings of ten men shared collective responsibility, and the hue and cry obliged every villager to chase suspected criminals.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Wergild was compensation paid to the victim&rsquo;s family, scaled by social rank, and existed mainly to prevent blood feuds &mdash; punishment was therefore both restorative and retributive, not just brutal.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Above the village, the shire reeve enforced the king&rsquo;s peace and presided over shire courts; corporal and capital punishments were reserved for the most serious offences against person, property and authority.</li>
</ul>
