How Howard, Fry and the separate system replaced public hangings and transportation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Spectacle to Cell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1800 punishment in Britain still meant the rope, the ship and the public crowd. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The body of capital statutes in eighteenth-century England that imposed the death penalty for over 200 offences, many minor; named for its severity rather than for the volume of executions, which fell as juries refused to convict.">Bloody Code</dfn> listed more than 200 capital offences, from murder down to stealing five shillings from a shop. Convicts spared the gallows were shipped to Australia under a sentence of <dfn class="term" data-def="Sentence requiring a convict to be shipped to a penal colony &mdash; mainly Australia between 1787 and 1868 &mdash; for a fixed term, often seven or fourteen years, or for life.">transportation</dfn>. Yet by 1870 the gallows had been moved inside prison walls, transportation had ended, and the new prison at <dfn class="term" data-def="London prison opened in 1842 as a model penitentiary; first prison built specifically for the &lsquo;separate system&rsquo; and copied across Britain.">Pentonville</dfn> dominated the system. This lesson explains how that change happened &mdash; the campaigners, the experiments and the failures of the new prison regimes.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The shift began with reformers asking a different question. Eighteenth-century justice asked how harshly an offender should be punished. The reformers asked what punishment was for. Their answers &mdash; deterrence, retribution, but also <dfn class="term" data-def="The Victorian aim of reshaping a prisoner&rsquo;s morals and habits, usually through religious instruction, work and silent reflection, so that they would not reoffend.">reform</dfn> &mdash; reshaped the prison estate.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">John Howard: The Inspector of Prisons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Bedfordshire High Sheriff and prison reformer; toured European prisons in the 1770s and published &lsquo;The State of the Prisons&rsquo; in 1777, exposing English gaol conditions.">John Howard</dfn> became High Sheriff of Bedfordshire in 1773 and visited his county gaol. What he found horrified him: prisoners kept together regardless of crime, gaolers paid by fees so debtors who had served their time could not be released, no fresh water, no heating, fevers spreading from cell to cell. He spent the next four years touring prisons across England, Wales and Europe, and in 1777 published <em>The State of the Prisons in England and Wales</em>. The book was a meticulous catalogue: cell sizes, food rations, the names of corrupt gaolers.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Howard&rsquo;s recommendations were practical. Prisoners should be paid for, not extort fees. They should be classified by crime and by sex. Cells should be clean, ventilated and inspected. Chaplains and surgeons should attend. Parliament responded with the 1774 Health of Prisoners Act and the 1779 Penitentiary Act, although neither was enforced thoroughly. Howard mattered because he turned prison reform into a national, evidence-based campaign rather than a moral complaint.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down four problems Howard exposed in English gaols and the year his book was published.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>John Howard published <em>The State of the Prisons</em> in 1777 after touring English and European gaols. He exposed disease, fee-paying gaolers, lack of classification and the absence of inspection &mdash; turning prison conditions into a parliamentary issue.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Elizabeth Fry at Newgate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Quaker prison reformer; began visiting women at Newgate in 1813 and founded the Association for the Improvement of the Female Prisoners in Newgate in 1817.">Elizabeth Fry</dfn> was a Quaker from Norwich. In 1813 she visited the women&rsquo;s side of <dfn class="term" data-def="London&rsquo;s main criminal prison from the twelfth century until its demolition in 1902; site of public executions outside its walls until 1868.">Newgate</dfn> prison and found roughly 300 women and their children crammed into two wards, sleeping on straw, drinking gin and gambling. There was no work, no schooling, no prison clothes; some women had been there for years awaiting transportation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Fry&rsquo;s response was to set up a school inside the prison for the children, to bring sewing work for the women, and to read the Bible to them. In 1817 she founded the Association for the Improvement of the Female Prisoners in Newgate, which paid for matrons, separate accommodation, religious instruction and useful work. She visited convict ships before they sailed for Australia, supplying clothes and Bibles. By the 1820s she was advising parliamentary committees and meeting Queen Victoria. Her influence shaped the principle that women prisoners needed female matrons and separate facilities &mdash; a principle the 1823 Gaols Act tried to enforce.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Modern students often hear &lsquo;reform&rsquo; and think therapy &mdash; counselling, education, addiction treatment. For Howard and Fry it meant something narrower and harder: moral and religious change. The reformed prisoner read the Bible, learned a trade, attended chapel and accepted the discipline of silent labour. Modern rehabilitation came later, with twentieth-century probation, parole and treatment programmes. Examiners reward students who can name what Victorian &lsquo;reform&rsquo; actually involved: religious instruction, work, separation from corrupting company &mdash; not modern therapeutic ideas.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Scaling Back the Bloody Code</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">As Home Secretary from 1822, Robert Peel led a parliamentary attack on the Bloody Code. Between 1823 and 1827 he repealed several hundred capital statutes, reducing the offences for which a person could legally be hanged. Death remained on the books for murder and treason; the Gaols Act 1823 required justices to inspect county gaols, pay gaolers a salary, separate prisoners by sex and crime, and provide chaplains. By 1837 only about a dozen offences carried the death sentence in practice. The Bloody Code did not end with a single Act; it was dismantled by Peel and his successors in stages, alongside Peel&rsquo;s creation of the Metropolitan Police (1829) which made proportionate punishment enforceable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Pentonville and the Separate System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In 1842 Pentonville opened in north London. Designed by Joshua Jebb, it was a radial prison: five wings of single cells running off a central inspection hall, the cells designed for one man each, with a hammock, a small workbench and a high barred window. Pentonville was the flagship of the <dfn class="term" data-def="Prison regime in which each prisoner was kept in a single cell with no contact with others; reading, work and chapel happened separately, on the theory that isolation forced moral reflection and prevented contamination by other criminals.">separate system</dfn>: a regime in which each prisoner was kept apart from every other.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The day was timetabled. Prisoners worked alone in their cells &mdash; picking <em>oakum</em> (unpicking old rope), making mailbags or weaving. They wore masks when leaving the cell to attend chapel, where each man stood in a small wooden cubicle so he could see only the chaplain. Exercise was taken in single yards, in single file, in silence. The theory: separation prevented hardened criminals corrupting first offenders, and forced each prisoner to confront his conscience without distraction.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A different reform regime, used at Wakefield and Coldbath Fields from the 1830s, in which prisoners worked together but were forbidden to speak; enforced with hard labour like the treadwheel and the crank.">silent system</dfn>, used at prisons such as Wakefield and Coldbath Fields, was a rival approach: prisoners worked together but in total silence, enforced by hard labour on the treadwheel or the crank. Both regimes claimed to be the route to reform. By the 1850s Pentonville-style separation was the dominant model and 54 new prisons had been built on its plan across Britain.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four design features of Pentonville prison and the year it opened.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pentonville (1842) was the flagship of the separate system: a radial prison with single cells, masked exercise, silent labour and chapels with individual cubicles. Joshua Jebb&rsquo;s design was copied at 54 new prisons by the 1850s.</p>
</div>

    <span>Strengths and weaknesses of the separate system</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Contemporaries praised Pentonville for order, cleanliness and the disappearance of disease and disorder of the old gaols. Religious instruction was systematic. But within a decade prison doctors were reporting alarming numbers of mental breakdowns: hallucinations, suicide attempts and what Victorians called &lsquo;mania&rsquo;. Between 1842 and 1850 over twenty Pentonville inmates were certified insane and sent to Bedlam. Historians such as Michael Ignatieff have argued that the separate system &mdash; presented as humane reform &mdash; could be as cruel as the Bloody Code, simply in slower and quieter ways. By the 1860s the regime was being relaxed, with more communal labour and shorter cell terms.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">1868: The Year of Two Endings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Two long-running punishments ended in 1868. Public execution was abolished by the Capital Punishment Amendment Act, after decades of complaint that the spectacle attracted huge drunken crowds and brutalised onlookers rather than deterring crime. The last public hanging, of the Fenian Michael Barrett, took place outside Newgate on 26 May 1868; from then on, hangings were carried out inside prison walls. In the same year, the last convict ship to Australia, the <em>Hougoumont</em>, sailed: the Australian colonies had refused further transports of convicts and the Penal Servitude Acts of 1857 and 1864 had already shifted long sentences to British prisons. By 1870 the British penal system was prison-based, behind walls, and the visible eighteenth-century theatre of punishment had ended.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">John Howard&rsquo;s 1777 <em>State of the Prisons</em> and Elizabeth Fry&rsquo;s work at Newgate from 1813 turned prison reform into a national, evidence-based campaign that shaped the 1823 Gaols Act.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Pentonville opened in 1842 as the flagship of the separate system: single cells, masked exercise, silent labour. The model was copied at 54 prisons but caused serious mental breakdown.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">In 1868 public execution was moved inside prison walls and transportation to Australia ended &mdash; a clear shift from public spectacle to closed, prison-based punishment.</li>
</ul>
