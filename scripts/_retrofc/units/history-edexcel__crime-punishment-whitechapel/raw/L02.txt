--- DESCRIPTION ---
How 1066 changed crime: new Forest Laws, Murdrum and Norman royal authority.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Conqueror Layered on Top of Anglo-Saxon Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After his victory at Hastings in 1066, <dfn class="term" data-def="Duke of Normandy who became King of England in 1066; introduced Forest Laws, the Murdrum fine and trial by combat to English law.">William I (the Conqueror)</dfn> faced a problem familiar to any conqueror: he was a French-speaking ruler imposing himself on roughly two million English-speaking people. Wholesale replacement of English law would have provoked rebellion. Instead William kept much of what he found &mdash; the king&rsquo;s peace, tithings, the hue and cry, the shire reeve and the local courts &mdash; and layered Norman additions on top. This is a key idea: the Conquest brought significant change in <em>what counted as a crime</em>, but considerable continuity in how communities still helped to police themselves.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The Norman additions had two purposes. The first was to protect Norman lords, who were a small armed minority in a hostile country. The second was to enrich the king and project his power over land, hunting and movement. Forest Laws, the Murdrum fine and trial by combat all served those aims.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two things the Normans changed about crime, and two things they kept from Anglo-Saxon law.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Norman Conquest is a story of change AND continuity. New crimes (Forest Laws, Murdrum) and a new trial method (trial by combat) were layered on top of an older system that kept tithings, the hue and cry, the shire reeve and local courts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Royal Forest and the Forest Laws</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">From 1071 onwards, William and his sons designated huge areas of woodland and farmland as <dfn class="term" data-def="Land legally reserved for the king's hunting; subject to special Forest Law rather than ordinary law.">royal forest</dfn>. By the reign of <dfn class="term" data-def="Son of William I; King of England 1100&ndash;1135. Extended Forest Law and used it as a major source of royal revenue.">Henry I</dfn>, around a third of England was royal forest. These were not necessarily wooded &mdash; whole villages could find themselves enclosed by forest boundaries.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Inside this land the <dfn class="term" data-def="Norman laws (from 1071) reserving royal forest land for the king's hunting and criminalising poaching, gathering wood and grazing animals there.">Forest Laws</dfn> applied. It became a crime to hunt deer or wild boar, to fell trees, to gather firewood, to graze pigs or to own a dog capable of catching game without first having three of its claws cut. Punishments were severe: fines that could ruin a peasant family, mutilation (the loss of fingers, an eye or worse) and, in extreme cases, death.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">For an English peasant who had previously gathered wood or hunted small game on what they thought was common land, this was a sudden criminalisation of everyday life. Historians describe such offences &mdash; illegal in law but widely accepted by ordinary people &mdash; as <dfn class="term" data-def="An offence that breaks the law but is accepted, even approved, by the local community; poaching under Forest Law is the classic example.">social crime</dfn>. <dfn class="term" data-def="Hunting or taking animals, fish or wood from land where you have no legal right; under Norman Forest Law this could be punished by death or mutilation.">Poaching</dfn> is the textbook example: most villagers helped poachers and refused to inform on them, so the law was widely broken with community approval.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Modern students often dismiss poaching as a small offence. Under Norman Forest Law it was anything but minor. Killing a royal deer could cost an offender a hand, an eye or, in some cases, his life. The change in law &mdash; not the change in behaviour &mdash; is what created the crime. The same act of hunting that had been ordinary in 1065 could be punished with mutilation in 1075. Examiners reward students who explain that the Conquest changed the legal definition of what counted as crime; the food remained the same, only the laws around it changed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Murdrum Fine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the years immediately after 1066, English resistance to Norman rule sometimes meant the assassination of Norman lords. William responded with the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Norman fine imposed on the local Anglo-Saxon community when a Norman was found murdered and the killer could not be produced.">Murdrum fine</dfn>. If a Norman was found dead in a village or hundred and the killer could not be produced, the entire local community had to pay a heavy collective fine. The fine could be ruinous, and it gave English villagers a strong incentive to identify the murderer themselves rather than shield him.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">It is important not to confuse the Murdrum with a general murder law. It applied specifically when the victim was Norman; it did not apply when an Englishman was killed. Like the Forest Laws, it was designed to protect the new ruling class. It also extended the older Anglo-Saxon idea of collective responsibility: the community was now answerable not only for its own members but for any unsolved Norman death within its bounds.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception: was Murdrum a punishment for all murders?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Students often write that the Murdrum fine punished murder in general. It did not. It was a specific tool to protect Normans from English attack. If the killer of a Norman was named and produced for trial, the fine was avoided. If not, the local community paid &mdash; whether they had any hand in the killing or not. The point was practical, not moral: it pressured English communities into policing each other on behalf of the new king.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Trial by Combat &mdash; A New Way to Decide a Verdict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Normans also brought trial by combat, in which the accused and accuser fought one another with swords or staves; God was believed to grant victory to the truthful. It supplemented &mdash; rather than replaced &mdash; the Anglo-Saxon trials by jury and oath-helpers. Trial by ordeal continued too, until the Church withdrew clerical participation in 1215 (the subject of the next lesson). Crucially, the existing tithings, the hue and cry and shire courts continued to function alongside these Norman additions: the village constable still chased criminals, but those criminals could now be charged with offences invented since 1066.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Henry II and the Royal Courts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Almost a century after Hastings, <dfn class="term" data-def="King of England 1154&ndash;1189; the Assize of Clarendon (1166) strengthened royal courts and created the early common law.">Henry II</dfn> went further. The Assize of Clarendon (1166) required local juries to report serious offences to royal judges who travelled the country. These judges applied the same rules in every shire, creating what would later be called the common law. The result was that, while Anglo-Saxon community policing remained the day-to-day reality, royal justice now reached deeper into the countryside than it had in 1066. Continuity at the bottom of the system; substantial change at the top.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences who the Murdrum fine was designed to protect, and how it was avoided.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Murdrum fine was charged on a whole community when a Norman was found murdered and the killer could not be produced. It was specifically designed to protect Normans, and could be avoided by handing over the killer.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Conquest brought change AND continuity: tithings, the hue and cry and shire courts continued, while Forest Laws, the Murdrum fine and trial by combat were layered on top.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Forest Laws criminalised everyday peasant activities such as gathering wood and hunting; poaching became the classic &lsquo;social crime&rsquo; &mdash; illegal but supported by the local community.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Murdrum fine specifically protected Normans by punishing whole English communities when a Norman killing went unsolved; under Henry II, royal courts (Assize of Clarendon, 1166) extended royal justice further still.</li>
</ul>
