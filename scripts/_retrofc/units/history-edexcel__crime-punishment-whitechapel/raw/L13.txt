How H Division, beat constables and the Met faced the rookeries of 1888.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Met and H Division</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1888 the <dfn class="term" data-def="London-wide police force created in 1829 by Robert Peel, divided into lettered territorial divisions covering the capital.">Metropolitan Police</dfn> had policed London for almost sixty years. Robert Peel&rsquo;s 1829 Act had created a salaried, uniformed force, and successive Home Secretaries had expanded it. London was divided into lettered territorial divisions, each covering several square miles. Whitechapel and Spitalfields fell under <dfn class="term" data-def="Lettered Metropolitan Police division covering Whitechapel and Spitalfields, headquartered at Leman Street.">H Division</dfn>, a force of roughly 500 men based at Leman Street police station. H Division was responsible for one of the busiest, poorest and most heavily migrant slices of the capital, with the City of London just over the boundary at Aldgate.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">At the top of the Met sat the Commissioner. From 1886 the post was held by <dfn class="term" data-def="Career soldier and Commissioner of the Metropolitan Police 1886&ndash;88; resigned in November 1888 during the Ripper investigation.">Sir Charles Warren</dfn>, a former army general appointed to bring military discipline to the force. Above him in turn was the Home Secretary, <dfn class="term" data-def="Conservative Home Secretary 1886&ndash;92; ultimate political authority over the Metropolitan Police during the Whitechapel murders.">Henry Matthews</dfn>, who held political responsibility for policing in Parliament. The Met also contained a separate plain-clothes detective branch, the <dfn class="term" data-def="Criminal Investigation Department, the plain-clothes detective branch of the Metropolitan Police, founded in 1878.">CID</dfn>, founded in 1878 after a corruption scandal had discredited the earlier detective force. Robert Anderson, head of CID from 1888, was directly responsible for managing detective work during the autumn murders.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down four facts about H Division: location, size, headquarters and one challenge it faced.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>H Division of the Metropolitan Police, based at Leman Street, covered Whitechapel and Spitalfields with roughly 500 officers in 1888. It bordered the separate City of London Police, an organisational divide that complicated investigation when crimes crossed boundaries.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Beat Constable</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The backbone of the Met was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A uniformed Metropolitan Police constable assigned to walk a fixed route on foot at a set pace, typically two and a half miles per hour, day and night.">beat constable</dfn>. Each constable was assigned a fixed route, usually marked on a map at the station. He walked it at two and a half miles per hour, day and night, in shifts of nine hours. A standard beat in central Whitechapel was around half a mile of streets and alleys. The constable wore a heavy blue uniform, helmet and cape, carried a wooden truncheon, a whistle for raising the alarm and a bullseye lantern with a sliding shutter. He was unarmed by force policy &mdash; firearms were not standard issue.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Discipline was strict: a sergeant met every constable on his beat at set times, and any deviation was punished. The hours were exhausting, the pay was low and turnover was high. A new recruit&rsquo;s wage in 1888 was around 24 shillings a week &mdash; less than a skilled tradesman &mdash; and a quarter of all new constables left within their first year. Drunkenness on duty was the most common dismissal offence. The result was an under-resourced force where many constables were inexperienced, some were exhausted and a minority were unfit for the work.</p>

    <span>Misconception: Met constables in 1888 were a polished, professional force</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Examiners often see students assume that, by 1888, beat constables were highly trained professionals. They were not. Recruits served only a few weeks of basic training before being put on a beat. Pay was low enough that around a quarter of new constables left within twelve months. Detective work was even thinner: H Division had only a small handful of CID officers compared with hundreds of uniformed constables. Treating the force as &lsquo;Peel&rsquo;s well-drilled professionals&rsquo; misses the structural pressures &mdash; recruitment problems, drunkenness on duty, exhaustion &mdash; that examiners reward you for naming.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Policing the Rookeries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Patrolling Whitechapel was structurally difficult. Beat constables walked at a fixed pace, so locals could time the patrols and avoid them. The rookeries &mdash; Flower and Dean Street, Dorset Street, Thrawl Street &mdash; were full of narrow alleys and enclosed courts where gas lamps did not reach. Even on lit streets, fog and coal-smoke routinely cut visibility to a few yards. A beat constable rounding a corner could not see the far end of his own beat.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">Crime in Whitechapel was diverse. Drunkenness and street fights filled the casebooks. Prostitution was widespread, and the lodging-house economy made it almost untraceable. <dfn class="term" data-def="Criminal extortion in which gangs demanded regular payments from shopkeepers and pub landlords in exchange for not damaging their premises.">Protection rackets</dfn> were run by gangs such as the Bessarabian Tigers and the Odessians. Domestic violence was both common and rarely prosecuted. Pickpocketing and street theft were endemic in busy markets like Petticoat Lane. Most of this was misdemeanour-level work that occupied uniformed constables full-time, leaving very little capacity for specialist investigation when something serious happened.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Cooperation problems with the City of London Police</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Whitechapel sat just east of the boundary with the City of London, which had its own separate police force. The two forces had different commanders, different ranks and different procedures. They cooperated routinely but reluctantly. When a crime crossed the boundary &mdash; as the &lsquo;double event&rsquo; murders of 30 September 1888 would &mdash; one body lay in Met territory (Berner Street) and another in City territory (Mitre Square). Information had to pass through two separate chains of command, and rivalry could slow the sharing of evidence. The split was a structural weakness of London policing in 1888.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Public Trust and Bloody Sunday</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">By 1888 public trust in the Met was already shaky. Sir Charles Warren had been brought in as a no-nonsense ex-soldier, but his approach made him controversial. On 13 November 1887 &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Demonstration in Trafalgar Square on 13 November 1887 broken up by Met constables under Sir Charles Warren; over 100 demonstrators injured, two killed.">Bloody Sunday</dfn> &mdash; he sent constables and mounted units to break up a Trafalgar Square demonstration over Irish home rule and unemployment. More than 100 demonstrators were injured and two later died of their injuries. The Met was accused in the press of brutality. Working-class Londoners, including many in Whitechapel, treated Warren&rsquo;s force with suspicion long before the murders began.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Antisemitic violence in Whitechapel added another front. After 1881 attacks on Jewish residents in the rookeries became more frequent &mdash; insults, broken windows, occasional assaults. They were not new in 1888, and they did not stop after; but during the Ripper investigation rumours that the killer was a Jewish migrant inflamed the atmosphere. Sir Charles Warren ordered the famous <dfn class="term" data-def="Phrase chalked on a wall in Goulston Street, Whitechapel, after the 30 September 1888 &lsquo;double event&rsquo;; ordered erased by Sir Charles Warren before it could be photographed.">Goulston Street graffito</dfn> &mdash; chalked on a wall in the early hours of 30 September 1888 &mdash; to be erased before dawn, fearing it would trigger anti-Jewish riots if the public read it at first light. We will look at how that decision affected the investigation in the next lesson.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why H Division had so little detective capacity by 1888.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Met in 1888 was overwhelmingly a uniformed beat-constable force. The CID had existed only since 1878, was small, and was concentrated at Scotland Yard rather than spread evenly across divisions. H Division therefore had limited detective capacity even before the autumn of terror began.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">H Division of the Metropolitan Police covered Whitechapel and Spitalfields from Leman Street with around 500 officers, nearly all in uniform; the small CID detective branch had existed only since 1878.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The beat-constable system &mdash; fixed route, fixed pace, low pay, basic training &mdash; struggled in the rookeries: alleys cut visibility, locals could time the patrols, and high turnover left many constables inexperienced.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Public trust in the Met under Sir Charles Warren had already been damaged by Bloody Sunday in November 1887; by 1888 working-class Londoners and migrant communities were sceptical even before the Ripper murders began.</li>
</ul>
