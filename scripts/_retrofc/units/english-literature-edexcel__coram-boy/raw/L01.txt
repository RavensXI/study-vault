<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Context Matters for Coram Boy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jamila Gavin’s <em>Coram Boy</em> (2000) is set in <dfn class="term" data-def="The historical period covering the reigns of the first four King Georges, roughly 1714–1830.">Georgian England</dfn>, a period of stark contrasts between wealth and poverty, refinement and cruelty. The novel spans approximately 1741–1750 and draws heavily on real historical events and places. For the Edexcel exam, you must weave contextual knowledge throughout your essay — not bolt it on as a separate paragraph — to show you understand how the setting shapes characters and themes.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name two specific historical facts about 18th-century England that Gavin uses in the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Edexcel assesses AO3 (context) heavily in Section B. You must connect historical facts about 18th-century England directly to how Gavin presents characters, settings and themes in the novel.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Thomas Coram and the Foundling Hospital</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Captain <dfn class="term" data-def="A real historical figure (1668–1751) who campaigned for decades to establish the Foundling Hospital in London.">Thomas Coram</dfn> was a retired sea captain who was horrified by the sight of abandoned babies and children on London’s streets. After seventeen years of campaigning, he persuaded wealthy patrons to fund the <dfn class="term" data-def="An institution established in 1739 in London to care for children whose mothers could not look after them.">Foundling Hospital</dfn>, which opened in 1739 in Bloomsbury, London. It was the world’s first incorporated charity and the UK’s oldest children’s charity. Mothers who could not care for their babies could leave them at the Hospital, where they would be given a new name, educated and trained for employment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In the novel, the Foundling Hospital becomes a place of salvation for Aaron (Toby), contrasting sharply with the fate of babies who fall into Otis Gardiner’s hands. Gavin uses this real institution to explore the theme of how society chooses which children deserve protection and which are disposable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Abandoned Children and Child Trafficking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In 18th-century England, thousands of unwanted children were abandoned each year. Illegitimate children faced social disgrace and were often left to die. Before the Foundling Hospital, there was virtually no institutional support for these children. Some were taken in by <dfn class="term" data-def="People who, for a fee, took in unwanted children — many of whom neglected or killed the children in their care.">baby farmers</dfn> who charged a fee but frequently neglected or killed the children entrusted to them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Gavin draws on this dark reality through the character of Otis Gardiner, who collects babies from desperate mothers for a fee, promising to deliver them to the Foundling Hospital. In reality, Otis and his son Meshak bury most of these babies in the countryside. This child trafficking plot is not invention — it reflects documented 18th-century practices where unscrupulous individuals exploited vulnerable mothers for profit.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: what was baby farming, and how does Gavin dramatise it through Otis Gardiner?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Baby farming was a real and widespread practice in 18th-century England. Gavin uses Otis Gardiner’s child-collecting scheme to dramatise how the most vulnerable members of society — infants and unmarried mothers — were exploited for profit.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Class, Wealth and the Social Hierarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Georgian England had a rigid <dfn class="term" data-def="A system in which people are ranked according to their birth, wealth or social position.">class system</dfn>. The landed gentry like the Ashbrooks lived in grand country estates, while the urban poor lived in squalor. The gap between rich and poor is central to <em>Coram Boy</em>: Alexander Ashbrook grows up in privilege at Ashbrook House in Gloucestershire, while Meshak and the foundling children exist at the very bottom of society. Gavin uses these contrasts to explore whether a person’s worth is determined by birth or by character.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">The novel is set during the height of the transatlantic <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced transportation and sale of African people as slaves, particularly between the 16th and 19th centuries.">slave trade</dfn>. Gavin includes Toby, a Black child at the Foundling Hospital, to remind the reader that the exploitation of children was part of a wider culture of treating human beings as property. The character of Mrs Lynch also references the slave trade through her connections. By weaving slavery into the narrative, Gavin broadens her exploration of who in 18th-century society was considered fully human and who was not.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Gavin’s Purpose: Why This Context Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Gavin wrote <em>Coram Boy</em> in 2000, drawing on years of historical research. She was born in India and grew up in England, and her work frequently explores the experiences of outsiders and marginalised groups. By choosing to set her novel in 18th-century England, Gavin invites the modern reader to see parallels between historical and contemporary attitudes to children, poverty and race. The novel was adapted for the stage by the National Theatre in 2005, receiving four Olivier Award nominations including Best New Play, which brought Gavin’s themes to an even wider audience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Understanding this historical context is not optional for the exam — it is how you unlock the deeper meanings of Gavin’s novel. Every character, every setting and every plot event connects back to the realities of 18th-century England. When you write about the Foundling Hospital, the class system, child trafficking or slavery, you are engaging with AO3 — and demonstrating that you understand the world Gavin recreates and why she chose to recreate it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20"><em>Coram Boy</em> is set in Georgian England (1741–1750), a period of extreme inequality, where abandoned children, baby farming and the slave trade were everyday realities.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The Foundling Hospital, founded by Thomas Coram in 1739, is central to the novel — it represents both salvation and the question of which children society chooses to save.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Handel’s music connects the novel’s themes of beauty, redemption and transcendence, and his real historical involvement with the Foundling Hospital grounds the fiction in fact.</li></ul>
