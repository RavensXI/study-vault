<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Character Analysis in Coram Boy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For the Edexcel exam, you may be asked to write about how Gavin presents a specific character or how characters are used to explore a theme. In a <dfn class="term" data-def="An exam without access to the text, requiring students to recall quotations and details from memory.">closed-book</dfn> exam, you do not need to remember exact quotations word-for-word, but you must be able to refer to specific moments, describe key scenes and explain how characters develop across the novel. This lesson examines the seven most important characters.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Meshak</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Meshak is arguably the novel’s most important character. He has a learning disability, is entirely dependent on his father Otis, and is haunted by the babies he has been forced to bury. Despite this, he is the character who shows the greatest <dfn class="term" data-def="The strength to do what is morally right, even when it involves personal danger or suffering.">moral courage</dfn>. When he saves Alexander and Melissa’s baby, he defies the only authority he has ever known. His act of protecting baby Aaron — defying his murderous father Otis — demonstrates that goodness is not dependent on intelligence, social status or wealth.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Close this and answer: why is Meshak a subversive character — what does society assume about him versus what he actually does?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gavin presents Meshak as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose actions challenge or overturn the reader's expectations about their type or group.">subversive character</dfn> — the person society would dismiss as least capable is the one who performs the novel’s most heroic acts. This is Gavin’s central argument about human worth.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Otis Gardiner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Otis is the novel’s primary <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who opposes the protagonist and creates the central conflict in a narrative.">antagonist</dfn>. He is calculating, ruthless and without remorse. Unlike many literary villains, Otis is not driven by grand ambition or ideology — he simply wants money. His evil is mundane, which makes it more frightening. He treats child trafficking as a business transaction and controls Meshak through fear and violence. Gavin uses Otis to show that the worst evil often hides behind ordinariness.</p>

Alexander eventually defies Sir William to study under Handel in London. His relationship with Melissa produces Aaron, though Alexander believes the child died. Alexander represents the theme of duty versus individual desire, and his story shows how even the privileged are constrained by the class system’s rigid expectations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Aaron (Toby)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Aaron grows up at the Foundling Hospital under the name Toby, unaware of his true parentage. His extraordinary singing voice — inherited from Alexander — is what eventually draws attention to him and leads to the revelation of his identity. Aaron represents the novel’s exploration of identity: he is defined by others throughout the story (as a foundling, as a singer, as Alexander’s son) and must ultimately reconcile these identities. His character asks the question: who are we if we do not know where we come from?</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Melissa</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Melissa is the daughter of a local family who falls in love with Alexander. When she becomes pregnant, the shame falls entirely on her — in 18th-century England, an unmarried mother faced social ruin. The baby is taken from her and given to Otis. Melissa bears the emotional cost of the class system’s double standards: Alexander’s reputation survives, while she carries the grief in silence. Gavin uses Melissa to expose the <dfn class="term" data-def="A social system where women are held to different moral standards than men, particularly regarding sexual behaviour.">sexual double standard</dfn> of Georgian society, where the consequences of an illegitimate child fell almost exclusively on the mother.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Thomas Ledbury</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Thomas is Alexander’s childhood friend and a key figure in connecting the Ashbrook world to the wider narrative. He represents loyalty and decency — values that contrast with Otis’s treachery. Thomas is shot and killed by Gaddarn (Otis) during the rescue of Aaron; his death is one of the novel's most shocking moments and’s storylines together. While not as dramatically complex as Meshak or Otis, Thomas functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who serves the plot by connecting storylines or enabling key events, rather than being the focus of thematic exploration.">plot connector</dfn> whose reliability throws the dysfunction of other characters into sharp relief.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what is Mrs Lynch's role in Otis's crimes, and what does her complicity show about how evil operates?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Lynch is complicit in Otis’s crimes. She helps collect babies and maintain the pretence that they are being taken to the Foundling Hospital. Gavin uses Mrs Lynch to show that evil requires accomplices — Otis could not have operated alone. Her character demonstrates that turning a blind eye to injustice is itself a form of moral failure.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Sir William is not a villain, but he represents the rigid values of the Georgian upper class. He expects Alexander to follow a respectable career and dismisses his son’s musical ambitions. His insistence on duty and respectability reflects a class system where individual desires are subordinated to family reputation. Gavin uses Sir William to show that even well-meaning parents can damage their children by imposing the values of their social class.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Character as Theme</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Each character in <em>Coram Boy</em> functions as part of Gavin’s wider argument about class, morality and human worth. When writing about characters in the exam, always connect your analysis to these broader themes. The strongest exam responses do not just describe what a character does — they explain what the character <em>represents</em> and why Gavin chose to present them in that way.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Notice how Gavin pairs and contrasts characters to develop her argument. Meshak and Otis are father and son, yet they represent opposite moral poles. Alexander and Aaron are father and son separated by class, yet united by music. Melissa and Mrs Lynch are both women caught in systems that exploit them, yet one is a victim and the other a willing accomplice. These pairings are deliberate — they allow Gavin to test her themes from multiple angles and create a morally complex world.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Meshak is the novel’s moral centre — the character society dismisses as worthless performs the most courageous acts, challenging assumptions about human value.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Otis represents the banality of evil — his villainy is mundane and profit-driven, made possible by a society that turns a blind eye to the exploitation of children.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Each character embodies a facet of Gavin’s argument about class and morality: Alexander (duty vs. passion), Melissa (the sexual double standard), Aaron (identity without origins), Mrs Lynch (complicity in evil).</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">In a closed-book exam, you cannot look up quotations. Instead, refer to specific moments: ‘When Meshak sees the baby and describes it as an angel…’ or ‘At the point where Otis orders Meshak to bury the child…’ This shows the examiner you know the text well without needing exact wording. Always analyse what the character <em>reveals</em> about Gavin’s themes, not just what they do. For example: ‘Gavin uses Meshak to argue that moral worth is independent of social status, challenging the 18th-century class hierarchy.’</p>
