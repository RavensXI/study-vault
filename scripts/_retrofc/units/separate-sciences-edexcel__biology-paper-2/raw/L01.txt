--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Photosynthesis: The Equation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An endothermic reaction in which plants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, taking place in the chloroplasts.">Photosynthesis</dfn> is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, in this case absorbing light energy.">endothermic</dfn> reaction: it absorbs light energy and stores it as chemical energy in glucose. It takes place in the <strong>chloroplasts</strong> of plant cells, where the green pigment <strong>chlorophyll</strong> absorbs light.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The word equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{glucose} + \text{oxygen}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The balanced symbol equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$$</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the balanced symbol equation for photosynthesis from memory, including all coefficients.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Photosynthesis is endothermic: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\). It occurs in chloroplasts, uses light energy absorbed by chlorophyll, and produces glucose (for energy and biosynthesis) plus oxygen (released as a by-product).</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Uses of Glucose in Plants</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Plants use photosynthetically produced glucose for multiple purposes: <strong>respiration</strong> (releasing energy for cell processes); converting to <strong>starch</strong> for storage in roots and leaves; producing <strong>cellulose</strong> for cell wall synthesis; combining with mineral ions (especially nitrates from the soil) to make <strong>amino acids</strong> and then proteins; and producing <strong>lipids</strong> for storage in seeds and cell membranes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<p data-narration-id="n7b">Leaves are adapted to maximise photosynthesis: a broad, flat surface area captures light; a thin leaf means CO&#8322; does not have to diffuse far to reach chloroplasts; a network of veins supplies water and removes glucose. The waxy cuticle on the upper surface reflects excess light at very high intensities, preventing photoinhibition of the chloroplast enzymes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Limiting Factors</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A factor whose low concentration or value restricts the rate of a reaction, even if other factors are plentiful. For photosynthesis: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature.">limiting factor</dfn> is one that, when in short supply, prevents photosynthesis from proceeding faster regardless of how much of the other factors is available. Three main factors limit the rate of photosynthesis:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Light intensity</strong> &mdash; as light increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases (more energy for the light-dependent reactions), until another factor becomes limiting.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Carbon dioxide concentration</strong> &mdash; CO&#8322; is a raw material; increasing its concentration raises the rate until light or temperature limits it.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Temperature</strong> &mdash; higher temperatures speed up enzyme-controlled reactions in the chloroplast up to an optimum (around 25&ndash;35°C for most plants); above the optimum, enzymes denature and the rate falls sharply.</li>
</ul>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common exam graph shows rate of photosynthesis on the y-axis against light intensity on the x-axis, at two different CO&#8322; concentrations. The curve for high CO&#8322; rises more steeply and reaches a higher plateau. Where the curve flattens, the current factor (light) is no longer limiting &mdash; another factor (CO&#8322; or temperature) has become limiting. Adding more CO&#8322; shifts the plateau upwards; raising temperature (to a safe level) also lifts the plateau. These graphs test whether you can identify which factor is limiting at each part of the curve.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Inverse Square Law</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n16">Light intensity decreases with distance from the source according to the <dfn class="term" data-def="Light intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source: doubling the distance reduces intensity to one quarter.">inverse square law</dfn>:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">$$\text{light intensity} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">where \(d\) is the distance from the light source. This means that if you move a lamp from 10 cm to 20 cm away from a plant &mdash; doubling the distance &mdash; the light intensity falls to \(\frac{1}{4}\) of its original value (not half). If the distance triples, intensity falls to \(\frac{1}{9}\).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Inverse Square Law Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Problem:</strong> A lamp is placed 15 cm from pondweed and produces a rate of 20 bubbles per minute. The lamp is moved to 30 cm. Predict the new rate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Distance doubles (15 &#8594; 30 cm), so the intensity becomes \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\) of the original.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Step 2:</strong> New rate \(= 20 \times \frac{1}{4} = 5\) bubbles per minute.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Note: this assumes light is the only limiting factor. In practice, the curve may already be at a plateau, in which case the prediction would overestimate the change.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Core Practical: Investigating the Effect of Light Intensity on Photosynthesis</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n24">The standard method uses aquatic pondweed (such as <em>Elodea</em>) or algae beads. Oxygen produced by photosynthesis is collected as bubbles (or measured as percentage O&#8322; saturation with a sensor) as a proxy for photosynthesis rate.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Core Practical Method and Variables</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Independent variable:</strong> Distance of the lamp from the plant (used as a measure of light intensity).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Dependent variable:</strong> Number of oxygen bubbles produced per minute (or O&#8322; sensor reading).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Control variables:</strong> CO&#8322; concentration (maintained by adding sodium hydrogen carbonate solution to the water); temperature (water jacket around the plant, or temperature-controlled water bath); same plant cutting for each measurement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Key precautions:</strong> Allow the plant to equilibrate at each distance for several minutes before counting bubbles. A thermometer measures temperature &mdash; it does <em>not</em> control it; the water bath or jacket controls temperature. Repeat each distance at least three times and calculate a mean to reduce the effect of anomalous results.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Analysing results:</strong> Plot rate of photosynthesis against distance (or against light intensity calculated using the inverse square law). A curved relationship indicates a limiting factor effect. If plotting rate vs. \(\frac{1}{d^2}\) gives a straight line through the origin, light intensity is the sole limiting factor at those distances.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n30" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three control variables for the light intensity core practical, then explain what a thermometer does vs what controls temperature.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the light intensity core practical: the independent variable is lamp distance; the dependent variable is bubble rate. A thermometer <strong>measures</strong> temperature &mdash; a water bath or jacket <strong>controls</strong> it. Adding sodium hydrogen carbonate to the water controls CO&#8322; concentration, ensuring light is the only variable changing.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Photosynthesis is endothermic: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\). It occurs in chloroplasts using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Rate of photosynthesis is limited by light intensity, CO&#8322; concentration and temperature. When one factor is in short supply, increasing others has no effect until that factor is addressed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Light intensity follows the inverse square law (\(I \propto \frac{1}{d^2}\)): doubling distance reduces intensity to a quarter. In the core practical, lamp distance is the independent variable, bubble rate is the dependent variable, and a water bath controls temperature.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">A common error in method questions is writing that a thermometer &lsquo;controls&rsquo; temperature &mdash; it only <em>measures</em> it. State that the temperature is controlled by a water bath. When asked about limiting factors, do not say the rate &lsquo;stops&rsquo; increasing &mdash; say another factor has become limiting. In Higher tier inverse square law questions, show your working clearly: write out \(I \propto \frac{1}{d^2}\), substitute both distances, and state the ratio before giving the final answer with units.</p>
