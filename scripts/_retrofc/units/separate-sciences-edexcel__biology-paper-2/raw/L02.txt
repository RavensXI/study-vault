--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Absorbing Water: The Root Hair Cell</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Highly specialised epidermal cells on plant roots with long, thin extensions that greatly increase the surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions from the soil.">Root hair cells</dfn> are found on the outer layer of plant roots, just behind the growing tip. Each cell has a long, thin extension &mdash; the root hair &mdash; that projects into the soil between soil particles. This extension dramatically increases the surface area in contact with soil water.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Water enters root hair cells by <strong>osmosis</strong>: soil water has a higher water potential than the cell contents, so water moves down the water potential gradient across the partially permeable cell membrane. Mineral ions (such as nitrates and phosphates) enter by <strong>active transport</strong>, using energy from respiration to move ions from the soil (low concentration) into the cell (higher concentration) &mdash; against the concentration gradient.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and distinguish between the process used to absorb water vs the process used to absorb mineral ions. Name the energy source for active transport.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Water enters root hair cells by <strong>osmosis</strong> (high to low water potential). Mineral ions enter by <strong>active transport</strong>, which requires energy from respiration. Root hairs are adapted with a large surface area and thin walls to maximise absorption.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Xylem: Water Transport Upwards</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Plant vascular tissue made of hollow, dead cells with lignified walls, forming continuous tubes that transport water and dissolved mineral ions from roots to leaves in the transpiration stream.">Xylem</dfn> is a vascular tissue made of dead cells whose end walls have broken down, forming long hollow tubes. The cell walls are reinforced with <dfn class="term" data-def="A hard, waterproof polymer deposited in the walls of xylem vessels, making them rigid, impermeable and able to withstand the tension of the transpiration stream.">lignin</dfn>, which makes the tubes rigid and waterproof so they can withstand the tension created by water moving upward. Xylem transports water and dissolved mineral ions from the roots, up through the stem, and to the leaves &mdash; in a continuous column called the transpiration stream.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The water moves because of a continuous pull from above: as water evaporates from the leaves, it creates tension that &lsquo;sucks&rsquo; more water up through the xylem. This cohesion&ndash;tension mechanism works because water molecules are attracted to each other (cohesion) and to the xylem walls (adhesion), maintaining a continuous unbroken column.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Phloem: Translocation of Sugars</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Plant vascular tissue made of living cells with sieve plates, transporting dissolved sugars (mainly sucrose) from leaves to the rest of the plant. The process is called translocation.">Phloem</dfn> is a vascular tissue made of living cells arranged in columns. Sieve plates at the end of each cell allow substances to flow between cells. Phloem transports dissolved sugars &mdash; mainly sucrose &mdash; produced during photosynthesis in the leaves, to all other parts of the plant that need them, including growing roots, developing fruits and storage organs. This transport process is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of dissolved sugars and other organic molecules through phloem from areas where they are made (sources) to areas where they are used or stored (sinks).">translocation</dfn>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Xylem cells are <strong>dead</strong> and hollow, with lignified walls; they transport water and minerals <strong>upward only</strong> (unidirectional). Phloem cells are <strong>living</strong>, with sieve plates; they transport dissolved sugars in <strong>both directions</strong> (bidirectional &mdash; from source to sink, wherever the plant needs them). Both xylem and phloem run through the vascular bundle in the stem, root and leaf veins.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Transpiration</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The evaporation of water vapour from the surfaces of a plant, primarily through the stomata in the leaves. It drives the transpiration stream, pulling water up through the xylem.">Transpiration</dfn> is the loss of water vapour from plant leaves, mainly through <dfn class="term" data-def="Pores on the underside of leaves (and sometimes upper surface) that open and close to control gas exchange and water loss.">stomata</dfn>. When stomata are open (to allow CO&#8322; in for photosynthesis and O&#8322; out), water vapour diffuses out down its concentration gradient. The evaporation at the leaf surface creates a &lsquo;pull&rsquo; that draws water up from the xylem to replace what was lost.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting the Rate of Transpiration</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Light intensity:</strong> Light causes stomata to open (guard cells become turgid in light). More open stomata = more water vapour lost = higher transpiration rate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Temperature:</strong> Higher temperature gives water molecules more kinetic energy, increasing the rate of evaporation and diffusion from the leaf.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Humidity:</strong> In humid air, the concentration gradient of water vapour between the leaf and the air is smaller, so diffusion (and transpiration rate) is lower. In dry air, the gradient is steeper and transpiration is faster.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Air movement (wind):</strong> Wind removes humid air from around the leaf, maintaining a steep concentration gradient and increasing the transpiration rate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Leaf Adaptations for Efficiency</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n18">The leaf is adapted to balance photosynthesis and water conservation. A waxy <dfn class="term" data-def="A waterproof, waxy layer covering the outer surface of the leaf epidermis, reducing water loss by evaporation from the leaf surface.">cuticle</dfn> covers the upper epidermis, reducing evaporation from the flat leaf surface. Stomata (controlled by <dfn class="term" data-def="Pairs of cells surrounding each stoma on a leaf. They change shape when water moves in or out, opening or closing the stoma.">guard cells</dfn>) are mostly on the underside of the leaf (lower epidermis), away from direct sunlight, further limiting unnecessary water loss. The spongy mesophyll layer below contains air spaces that allow CO&#8322; and O&#8322; to diffuse to and from photosynthesising cells efficiently.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three leaf adaptations that reduce water loss, then explain how guard cells control stomatal opening.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Leaves reduce unnecessary water loss through: (1) the waxy cuticle on the upper surface; (2) stomata mostly on the lower (shaded) surface; (3) guard cells closing stomata in the dark or when water is scarce. These adaptations balance gas exchange for photosynthesis against water conservation.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Calculating Transpiration Rate</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n21">A potometer measures water uptake by a plant cutting as a proxy for transpiration rate. Water uptake is recorded as the distance a bubble moves in a capillary tube over a set time. The rate of water uptake (in cm per minute, or cm&#179; per minute if the bore area is known) is calculated as:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n22">$$\text{rate of water uptake} = \frac{\text{distance moved by bubble (cm)}}{\text{time (min)}}$$</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Potometer Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Problem:</strong> A student sets up a potometer and records that the air bubble moves 4.5 cm in 3 minutes under normal conditions. Calculate the rate of water uptake.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Rate = distance &divide; time = 4.5 &divide; 3 = 1.5 cm per minute.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Step 2:</strong> The student then turns a fan on. If the bubble moves 9.0 cm in 3 minutes, rate = 9.0 &divide; 3 = 3.0 cm per minute &mdash; the wind has doubled the transpiration rate by maintaining a steeper water-vapour concentration gradient.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Water enters root hair cells by osmosis (down the water potential gradient); mineral ions enter by active transport (against the gradient, using energy from respiration). Root hairs maximise surface area for absorption.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Xylem (dead, lignified cells) transports water and minerals upward in the transpiration stream; phloem (living, sieve-plated cells) transports dissolved sugars by translocation from sources to sinks in both directions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Transpiration is controlled by stomata and guard cells. Rate increases with higher temperature, higher light, lower humidity and increased wind. Rate of water uptake = distance bubble moves ÷ time.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">Common errors include confusing xylem (dead cells, water and minerals upward) with phloem (living cells, sugars bidirectionally). When asked to &lsquo;explain&rsquo; why transpiration rate increases on a hot day, do not just say &lsquo;water evaporates faster&rsquo; &mdash; explain that higher temperature gives water molecules more kinetic energy, increasing the rate of evaporation and maintaining a steeper concentration gradient between the leaf and the air. For rate of water uptake calculations, always include units (cm min&#8315;&#185; or cm&#179; min&#8315;&#185;).</p>
