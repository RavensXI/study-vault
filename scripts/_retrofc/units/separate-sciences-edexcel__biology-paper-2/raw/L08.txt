<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Levels of Organisation in Ecosystems</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Living things exist in a hierarchy of levels. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A community of living organisms interacting with the non-living (abiotic) components of their environment.">ecosystem</dfn> is the largest level: it includes all the organisms in a habitat together with the non-living factors that affect them. Within the ecosystem, a <dfn class="term" data-def="All the populations of different species living and interacting together in a habitat.">community</dfn> is made up of all the different species. A <dfn class="term" data-def="All the organisms of one species living in a habitat at a particular time.">population</dfn> is all the individuals of a single species.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Non-living factors that affect a community, such as light intensity, temperature, moisture, soil pH and mineral content.">Abiotic factors</dfn> include light intensity, temperature, moisture level, soil pH, mineral content, wind speed and availability of water. <dfn class="term" data-def="Living factors that affect a community, such as food availability, new predators, new pathogens and competition between organisms.">Biotic factors</dfn> include food availability, predators, pathogens and competition. Interactions between abiotic and biotic factors determine which species can live in a habitat and in what numbers.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Organisms in a community can interact in different ways. <dfn class="term" data-def="A relationship between two organisms where one (the parasite) benefits at the expense of the other (the host), which is harmed but usually not killed.">Parasitism</dfn> is where one organism benefits at the direct expense of another (e.g. fleas living on a dog). <dfn class="term" data-def="A relationship between two organisms in which both benefit, for example oxpeckers eating parasites on cattle.">Mutualism</dfn> is where both organisms benefit (e.g. oxpecker birds eating insects on cattle — the bird gets food, the cattle lose parasites).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and describe the difference between a community and a population, then give one example of each from a woodland ecosystem.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ecosystem &rarr; community (all species) &rarr; population (one species). Abiotic factors are non-living; biotic factors are living. Stable communities maintain balanced populations when abiotic and biotic factors are in equilibrium.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Sampling Techniques: Quadrats and Belt Transects</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7">To investigate the distribution and abundance of organisms in a habitat, scientists use systematic sampling methods. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A square frame of known area placed randomly in a habitat to count or estimate the number of organisms of a given species within it.">quadrat</dfn> is a square frame placed at random (using random numbers to generate coordinates) in the habitat. The number or percentage cover of organisms inside is recorded. Multiple quadrats are used to give a representative mean.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A line drawn across a habitat from one edge to another. Quadrats or observations are recorded at regular intervals along the line to show how species change across the habitat.">belt transect</dfn> is used to investigate how species distribution changes along an environmental gradient &mdash; for example, from the edge of a woodland into open grassland, or up a rocky shore. Quadrats are placed at regular intervals along the transect line and species recorded at each position. This reveals zonation: changes in species distribution that correlate with the changing abiotic conditions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Explain to someone else: when would you use a quadrat versus a belt transect? Name one variable that must be controlled in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Quadrats are used to estimate the abundance or percentage cover of species in a habitat, using random sampling. Belt transects are used when species distribution is expected to change along a gradient. Both are core practical techniques &mdash; examiners test apparatus, method, control variables and how data are analysed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Trophic Levels and Energy Transfer</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11">Energy enters ecosystems through <strong>photosynthesis</strong> in producers (plants and algae). Energy then passes through organisms at each <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeding level in a food chain. Producers are at trophic level 1; primary consumers are at trophic level 2, and so on.">trophic level</dfn> as organisms eat each other. At each transfer, most energy is lost as heat from respiration, through waste materials and in parts of organisms that are not eaten. Typically, only about 10% of energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next &mdash; this limits food chains to around four or five levels.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n12">Energy transfer efficiency can be calculated:
$$\text{efficiency (\%)} = \frac{\text{energy transferred to next level}}{\text{energy available at current level}} \times 100$$
For example, if 10,000 kJ is available at trophic level 1 (producers) and only 1,000 kJ reaches trophic level 2 (primary consumers), the transfer efficiency is 10%. This low efficiency is why <strong>fish farming</strong> (raising fish in enclosures and controlling feeding) can be more energy-efficient than wild fishing: energy is not lost through the fish swimming large distances or maintaining body temperature in cold water.</p>
</div>

For example, grey squirrels introduced to the UK outcompete red squirrels for food and carry a disease (squirrelpox) to which red squirrels have no immunity.</p>

This causes rapid algal growth (an algal bloom), which blocks light from underwater plants, causing them to die. Decomposers break down the dead plant matter, consuming dissolved oxygen. The low oxygen levels kill fish and other aquatic organisms, reducing biodiversity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Biodiversity and Food Security</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of all different species of organisms on Earth, or within a particular ecosystem. High biodiversity makes ecosystems more resilient to change.">Biodiversity</dfn> refers to the variety of different species in an ecosystem. High biodiversity means ecosystems are more stable and resilient &mdash; if one species declines, others can take over some of its roles. Human activities &mdash; deforestation, pollution, overexploitation of natural resources &mdash; are driving a significant loss of biodiversity worldwide.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Strategies to maintain biodiversity include habitat protection and restoration, <strong>reforestation</strong> (replanting trees where forests have been cleared), wildlife breeding programmes for endangered species, and seed banks that preserve plant genetic diversity. Maintaining biodiversity also supports food security by preserving the genetic diversity of crop species and the wild species that act as pollinators and pest controllers.</p>

Threats include growing global population, changes in climate affecting crop yields, spread of new crop diseases, and limited freshwater supplies. Solutions include developing drought-resistant crop varieties through selective breeding or genetic technology, reducing food waste and improving the efficiency of food production systems.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The Carbon Cycle and the Water Cycle</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Carbon is constantly cycled through the environment. Producers remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere through photosynthesis, incorporating it into organic molecules. Animals eat producers and use carbon in their own bodies. When organisms respire, carbon is returned to the atmosphere as CO<sub>2</sub>. When organisms die, <dfn class="term" data-def="Microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) that break down dead organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide through respiration and returning mineral ions to the soil.">decomposers</dfn> break down dead organic matter through respiration and decay, releasing CO<sub>2</sub> back into the atmosphere. Combustion of fossil fuels is an additional pathway that returns carbon stored over millions of years to the atmosphere in a short time.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land and living organisms. Water evaporates from oceans, lakes and soil; plants release water vapour through transpiration. Water vapour rises and condenses to form clouds, then falls as precipitation. Run-off and rivers return water to the sea. <dfn class="term" data-def="A technology that removes salt from seawater to produce fresh water, typically by reverse osmosis or evaporation. It is energy-intensive and used in water-scarce regions.">Desalination</dfn> can supplement freshwater supplies in arid regions but requires significant energy input.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Ecosystems are organised into communities (all species) and populations (one species). Abiotic and biotic factors determine which species survive; quadrats and belt transects are used to sample distribution and abundance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Energy is transferred between trophic levels with roughly 10% efficiency; biodiversity is threatened by human activity but can be protected by habitat restoration, reforestation, breeding programmes and seed banks.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The carbon cycle moves carbon through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and combustion. The water cycle moves water through evaporation, condensation and precipitation, with desalination offering an additional freshwater source in arid areas.</li>
</ul>

On eutrophication, trace the full chain of events from fertiliser run-off to fish death &mdash; partial chains score partial credit.</p>
