<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Endocrine System</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The body's system of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream to coordinate body functions. Responses are slower and longer-lasting than nervous responses.">endocrine system</dfn> uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Chemical messengers produced by endocrine glands and transported in the blood to target organs, where they trigger a specific response.">hormones</dfn> as chemical messengers. Hormones are secreted directly into the blood by endocrine glands and are carried to target organs, where they bind to specific receptors and trigger a response. Unlike nervous signals (fast, localised, short-lived), hormonal responses are slower to initiate but longer-lasting and affect a wider area of the body.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A small gland in the brain that produces hormones controlling many other endocrine glands. Often called the master gland.">pituitary gland</dfn> in the brain is the master gland: it detects hormone levels in the blood and secretes hormones that stimulate other glands to produce or stop their own hormones. Key glands and their hormones include:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Pituitary gland</strong> (base of brain) &mdash; produces FSH, LH, TSH, growth hormone and ADH.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Thyroid gland</strong> (neck) &mdash; produces thyroxine, which regulates metabolic rate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Pancreas</strong> (abdomen) &mdash; produces insulin and glucagon to control blood glucose.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Adrenal glands</strong> (above kidneys) &mdash; produce adrenalin for the fight-or-flight response.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Ovaries</strong> (female) &mdash; produce oestrogen and progesterone.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Testes</strong> (male) &mdash; produce testosterone.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the six main endocrine glands with one hormone produced by each. Then state one key difference between hormonal and nervous communication.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The pituitary gland is the &lsquo;master gland&rsquo; &mdash; it controls other endocrine glands. Hormones travel in the blood to target organs. Hormonal responses are slower but longer-lasting and more widespread than nervous responses.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Adrenalin and the Fight-or-Flight Response</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the adrenal glands that prepares the body for immediate physical action in response to perceived threat. It raises heart rate, increases blood glucose and dilates airways.">Adrenalin</dfn> is secreted by the adrenal glands in response to sudden stress, danger or excitement. It prepares the body for immediate action by triggering the <dfn class="term" data-def="The physiological response to perceived threat: adrenalin increases heart rate, diverts blood to muscles, raises blood glucose and heightens alertness to enable fighting or fleeing.">fight-or-flight response</dfn>:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Heart rate increases</strong> &mdash; more blood (and therefore more oxygen and glucose) delivered to muscles per minute.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Blood glucose rises</strong> &mdash; adrenalin stimulates the liver to break down glycogen into glucose, providing fuel for muscle contraction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Blood diverted to muscles</strong> &mdash; arterioles to digestive organs constrict; those to muscles dilate, redirecting blood flow.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Airways dilate</strong> &mdash; bronchioles widen to allow greater air flow, increasing oxygen delivery to the blood.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Pupils dilate</strong> &mdash; to increase the amount of light entering the eye, improving vision for detecting threats.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why These Responses Help Survival</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Every component of the fight-or-flight response increases the body&rsquo;s capacity for explosive physical activity &mdash; either fighting or fleeing. The non-essential processes (digestion, fine motor control) are temporarily suppressed; resources are redirected to the systems needed for survival. The response is rapid because it involves the nervous system triggering adrenalin release, which then acts on multiple organs simultaneously &mdash; demonstrating how the nervous and endocrine systems work together.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Thyroxine and Negative Feedback</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the thyroid gland that regulates the body's metabolic rate, growth and development. Its release is controlled by negative feedback involving the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.">Thyroxine</dfn> is produced by the thyroid gland and regulates the body&rsquo;s basal metabolic rate &mdash; the rate at which cells use energy at rest. It is essential for normal growth, development and cell function.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Thyroxine levels in the blood are regulated by a <dfn class="term" data-def="A control mechanism in which the output of a process inhibits further production, keeping levels within a set range. Used to regulate thyroxine and many other hormones.">negative feedback</dfn> loop involving three organs:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Thyroxine Negative Feedback Loop</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">1. The <strong>hypothalamus</strong> in the brain monitors blood thyroxine levels. When thyroxine is low, it releases <dfn class="term" data-def="Thyrotropin-releasing hormone: produced by the hypothalamus to signal the pituitary gland to release TSH.">TRH</dfn> (thyrotropin-releasing hormone).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">2. TRH signals the <strong>pituitary gland</strong>, which releases <dfn class="term" data-def="Thyroid-stimulating hormone: produced by the pituitary gland in response to TRH. It travels to the thyroid gland and stimulates production of thyroxine.">TSH</dfn> (thyroid-stimulating hormone) into the blood.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">3. TSH reaches the <strong>thyroid gland</strong>, stimulating it to produce and release more thyroxine into the blood. Metabolic rate rises.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">4. As thyroxine levels rise back to normal, the hypothalamus detects this and reduces TRH release &mdash; which reduces TSH &mdash; which reduces thyroxine production. This &lsquo;switching off&rsquo; of the stimulus when the outcome is achieved is negative feedback. It prevents thyroxine from rising indefinitely.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this and trace the negative feedback loop for thyroxine: name all three hormones (TRH, TSH, thyroxine) and the organ that produces each one.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thyroxine regulation: hypothalamus releases TRH &rarr; pituitary releases TSH &rarr; thyroid produces thyroxine &rarr; high thyroxine suppresses TRH and TSH release (negative feedback). This keeps metabolic rate within a normal range.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">The Menstrual Cycle</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n28">The menstrual cycle is approximately 28 days long and is controlled by four hormones interacting through feedback mechanisms. It prepares the uterus for potential pregnancy each month:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Four Hormones of the Menstrual Cycle</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Follicle-stimulating hormone: produced by the pituitary gland. Causes an egg to mature in a follicle in the ovary and stimulates oestrogen production.">FSH</dfn></strong> (follicle-stimulating hormone): released by the pituitary gland in the first half of the cycle. It stimulates a follicle in the ovary to mature (the egg inside begins to develop) and causes the ovary to produce oestrogen.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the ovaries that causes the uterus lining to thicken, inhibits FSH to prevent multiple eggs maturing, and at high levels triggers the LH surge that causes ovulation.">Oestrogen</dfn></strong>: produced by the ovaries as the follicle matures. It causes the uterus lining to thicken and build up with blood vessels, ready for a fertilised egg. Oestrogen inhibits further FSH release (negative feedback &mdash; preventing multiple follicles maturing) and at high levels stimulates a surge in LH from the pituitary.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Luteinising hormone: produced by the pituitary gland. A mid-cycle surge triggers ovulation — the release of the mature egg from the ovary.">LH</dfn></strong> (luteinising hormone): its surge around day 14 triggers <dfn class="term" data-def="The release of a mature egg from the ovary, typically around day 14 of the 28-day menstrual cycle.">ovulation</dfn> &mdash; the mature egg is released from the follicle into the fallopian tube.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the empty follicle (corpus luteum) after ovulation. It maintains the uterus lining and inhibits FSH and LH, preventing further egg maturation.">Progesterone</dfn></strong>: produced by the empty follicle (now called the corpus luteum) after ovulation. It maintains the uterus lining and inhibits FSH and LH. If no fertilisation occurs, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone levels fall, the uterus lining breaks down, and menstruation begins. The cycle then restarts.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the sequence of the four menstrual cycle hormones in order, naming the gland/organ that produces each one and its main action.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Menstrual cycle hormones: FSH (pituitary &rarr; matures follicle, triggers oestrogen) &rarr; Oestrogen (ovaries &rarr; thickens uterus lining, triggers LH surge) &rarr; LH surge (pituitary &rarr; ovulation on day ~14) &rarr; Progesterone (corpus luteum &rarr; maintains lining; drops if no fertilisation &rarr; menstruation).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Fertility Treatments: IVF and Clomifene</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n35">Some women have difficulty conceiving because they do not produce enough FSH to mature eggs. <dfn class="term" data-def="A fertility drug that blocks oestrogen receptors in the hypothalamus, reducing the negative feedback signal. The hypothalamus responds by releasing more FSH and LH, stimulating ovulation.">Clomifene</dfn> is a fertility drug that blocks oestrogen receptors in the hypothalamus. The hypothalamus interprets this as low oestrogen and increases its release of signals that stimulate FSH production. Higher FSH levels stimulate follicle maturation and ovulation, increasing the chance of conception.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n36"><dfn class="term" data-def="In vitro fertilisation: a fertility treatment in which eggs collected from the ovaries (after hormonal stimulation) are fertilised with sperm outside the body and one or two resulting embryos are transferred to the uterus.">IVF</dfn> (in vitro fertilisation) involves: giving the woman FSH and LH injections to stimulate multiple follicles to mature; collecting the eggs by minor surgery; fertilising them with sperm in a laboratory dish; allowing embryos to develop for a few days; and transferring one or two embryos to the uterus. IVF raises ethical questions &mdash; it is emotionally and physically demanding, expensive, has a limited success rate, can result in multiple pregnancies, and raises questions about the status of unused embryos.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n38">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Adrenalin (adrenal glands) triggers the fight-or-flight response: raised heart rate, increased blood glucose, dilated airways and pupils, blood diverted to muscles. It prepares the body for rapid physical action.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40">Thyroxine (thyroid gland) regulates metabolic rate via negative feedback: hypothalamus releases TRH &rarr; pituitary releases TSH &rarr; thyroid releases thyroxine &rarr; high thyroxine suppresses TRH and TSH.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n41">The 28-day menstrual cycle is controlled by FSH, oestrogen, LH and progesterone. FSH matures the follicle; oestrogen thickens the uterus lining and triggers LH surge; LH causes ovulation; progesterone maintains the lining. IVF and clomifene are fertility treatments.</li>
</ul>

Missing any step loses marks.</p>
