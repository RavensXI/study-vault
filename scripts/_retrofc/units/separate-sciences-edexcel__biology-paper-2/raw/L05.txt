--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the body controls temperature, blood glucose, and what goes wrong in diabetes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Homeostasis?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to maintain optimum conditions for function, in response to internal and external changes.">Homeostasis</dfn> is the maintenance of a stable internal environment inside the body. Your cells need precise conditions to carry out chemical reactions &mdash; particularly for enzyme activity. If conditions drift too far from the optimum, enzymes stop working efficiently and cells can be damaged or die. The body therefore constantly monitors and adjusts conditions including body temperature, blood glucose concentration, and water balance (<dfn class="term" data-def="The regulation of water levels and salt concentration in the body, principally by the kidneys.">osmoregulation</dfn>).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the three main conditions controlled by homeostasis, plus the three components shared by all control systems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Homeostasis maintains optimum internal conditions for enzyme activity. All control systems share three components: receptors (detect change), a coordination centre (processes information) and effectors (produce the response).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Most homeostatic systems work through <dfn class="term" data-def="A control mechanism where a change in conditions triggers a response that counteracts or reverses that change, returning conditions to the set point.">negative feedback</dfn>. When a variable rises above its set point, the response brings it back down. When it falls below the set point, the response pushes it back up. This keeps internal conditions within a narrow, safe range.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Thermoregulation &mdash; Controlling Body Temperature</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n6">The core human body temperature is approximately 37&deg;C &mdash; the optimum for most enzymes. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A region of the brain that acts as the body's thermostat, monitoring blood temperature and coordinating the responses that raise or lower it.">hypothalamus</dfn> acts as the body&rsquo;s thermostat. It contains thermoreceptors that detect the temperature of the blood flowing through it and compares this to the set point. The hypothalamus also receives information from thermoreceptors in the skin about the external temperature.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The skin is the main organ of thermoregulation. It has two structural layers: the outer <dfn class="term" data-def="The outermost layer of skin, containing no blood vessels. It forms a protective barrier and contains the sweat pores through which sweat reaches the surface.">epidermis</dfn>, which is avascular and protective, and the deeper <dfn class="term" data-def="The inner layer of skin containing blood capillaries, sweat glands, hair follicles and sensory receptors.">dermis</dfn>, which contains capillaries, sweat glands and hair follicles. Adjustments in the dermis drive most of the body&rsquo;s temperature responses.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">When body temperature rises above the set point, the hypothalamus coordinates four responses. First, blood vessels in the skin undergo <dfn class="term" data-def="The widening of blood vessels near the skin surface, which increases blood flow to the skin and allows more heat to be lost to the surroundings.">vasodilation</dfn> &mdash; the arterioles supplying skin capillaries widen, bringing more warm blood close to the skin surface so heat radiates outward. Second, <strong>sweating</strong> increases &mdash; sweat glands secrete more sweat, and as water evaporates from the skin surface it absorbs latent heat from the body, cooling it. Third, hair follicle muscles relax so hairs lie flat, reducing the insulating layer of trapped air. Fourth, skeletal muscles reduce any involuntary shivering.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Responses When the Body Is Too Cold</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">When temperature drops below the set point, the hypothalamus coordinates opposite responses. Arterioles near the skin surface undergo <dfn class="term" data-def="The narrowing of blood vessels near the skin surface, which reduces blood flow to the skin and conserves body heat.">vasoconstriction</dfn> &mdash; they narrow, diverting blood away from the skin so less heat radiates outward. Sweating decreases. Hair erector muscles contract, pulling hairs upright to trap a thicker layer of insulating air. The body also begins <strong>shivering</strong> &mdash; rapid, involuntary contractions of skeletal muscles generate heat through respiration.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain, in your own words, what vasodilation does and why it cools you down. Mention blood, skin and heat radiation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vasodilation widens skin blood vessels, bringing more blood to the surface and increasing heat loss by radiation. Vasoconstriction narrows them, conserving heat. These are both Triple-only topics and are commonly tested in extended-response questions.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Blood Glucose Control</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Blood glucose concentration must stay within a narrow range. After eating, it rises; during exercise it can fall. The <strong>pancreas</strong> continuously monitors blood glucose and secretes two hormones to correct deviations.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">When blood glucose rises above the set point, the pancreas secretes <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreas that causes cells to take up glucose and the liver to convert glucose to glycogen, lowering blood glucose concentration.">insulin</dfn>. Insulin causes body cells (especially muscle and liver cells) to take up more glucose from the blood. It also stimulates the liver to convert excess glucose into <dfn class="term" data-def="An insoluble storage carbohydrate made from glucose, stored in the liver and muscles. Converted back to glucose when blood glucose is low.">glycogen</dfn> through glycogenesis. Both actions lower blood glucose back toward the set point.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n14">When blood glucose falls below the set point, the pancreas secretes <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the alpha cells of the pancreas that stimulates the liver to convert glycogen back into glucose, raising blood glucose concentration.">glucagon</dfn>. Glucagon triggers glycogenolysis &mdash; the conversion of stored glycogen back into glucose, which is released into the blood. This raises blood glucose back to the set point. Together, insulin and glucagon maintain blood glucose in a classic negative feedback loop: each hormone opposes the effect of the other.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. From memory, draw a simple diagram showing how insulin and glucagon create a negative feedback loop around the blood glucose set point.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Insulin (beta cells) lowers blood glucose: glucose enters cells; liver converts glucose to glycogen. Glucagon (alpha cells) raises blood glucose: liver converts glycogen back to glucose. This is a negative feedback loop. Higher tier only.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Diabetes Mellitus</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Diabetes is a condition in which blood glucose is not properly controlled. There are two main types.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="A form of diabetes in which the immune system destroys the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas, so little or no insulin is produced.">Type 1 diabetes</dfn> is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system destroys the beta cells of the pancreas. Without insulin, blood glucose rises dangerously after meals. It usually develops in childhood or adolescence and is not related to lifestyle. Treatment requires regular <strong>insulin injections</strong> (or an insulin pump), along with careful monitoring of diet and blood glucose levels. Without treatment, blood glucose can reach dangerously high levels.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A form of diabetes in which body cells become resistant to insulin, so blood glucose is not effectively taken up, even though some insulin may still be produced.">Type 2 diabetes</dfn> develops when body cells become resistant to insulin. The pancreas may still produce insulin but the cells no longer respond effectively. It is strongly associated with being overweight or obese. It is typically managed by dietary changes (reducing sugar and refined carbohydrates), increasing physical activity, and sometimes medication. In some cases, weight loss alone can reverse the condition.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20">Body mass index (<dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of body fatness calculated as mass in kg divided by height in metres squared. Values above 30 are classed as obese.">BMI</dfn>) is calculated as mass (kg) divided by height squared (m<sup>2</sup>): 
    $$\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{mass (kg)}}{\text{height}^2 \text{ (m}^2\text{)}}$$
    A BMI above 30 is classed as obese. Waist-to-hip ratio compares the circumference of the waist to the hips and is an indicator of where fat is stored; higher ratios are associated with greater health risk. Both measures can indicate elevated risk of type 2 diabetes.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Homeostasis maintains stable internal conditions through negative feedback; the hypothalamus coordinates temperature control using vasodilation, vasoconstriction, sweating and shivering.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The pancreas monitors blood glucose and secretes insulin (when glucose is high) or glucagon (when glucose is low) to return it to the set point.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Type 1 diabetes results from autoimmune destruction of beta cells; it requires insulin injections. Type 2 diabetes involves insulin resistance and is managed primarily through diet and lifestyle changes.</li>
</ul>
