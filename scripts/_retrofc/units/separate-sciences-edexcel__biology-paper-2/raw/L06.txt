How the kidneys filter blood, reabsorb what the body needs, and how ADH controls water balance.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Urinary System</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of organs that produces, stores and excretes urine. It includes the kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra.">urinary system</dfn> removes metabolic waste from the blood and regulates the water and salt content of the body. It consists of two <strong>kidneys</strong>, each connected to the <strong>bladder</strong> by a <strong>ureter</strong>. Urine passes from the bladder to the outside through the <strong>urethra</strong>. The kidneys receive blood from the renal arteries and return filtered blood to the circulation via the renal veins.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The most important waste product removed by the kidneys is <dfn class="term" data-def="A nitrogen-containing waste product produced in the liver by the breakdown of excess amino acids. It is filtered from the blood by the kidneys and excreted in urine.">urea</dfn>. Urea is produced in the liver when excess amino acids are broken down. It is toxic in high concentrations and must be continuously removed from the blood. The kidneys also remove excess water, excess mineral ions (such as sodium) and other soluble waste products.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the four parts of the urinary system in order, plus the main waste product removed by the kidneys and where that waste is made.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The urinary system: kidneys &rarr; ureters &rarr; bladder &rarr; urethra. The kidneys filter urea (produced in the liver from excess amino acids), excess water and mineral ions from the blood to form urine.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Nephron</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Each kidney contains approximately one million tiny tubules called <dfn class="term" data-def="The functional unit of the kidney. Each nephron filters blood, reabsorbs useful substances and produces a concentrated urine-like filtrate.">nephrons</dfn>. The nephron is where filtration and reabsorption take place. Blood enters each nephron&rsquo;s filtering unit at the <dfn class="term" data-def="A knot of blood capillaries inside the Bowman's capsule where high-pressure filtration of the blood takes place.">glomerulus</dfn>, which is a knot of capillaries enclosed in a cup-shaped structure called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A cup-shaped structure at the start of the nephron that surrounds the glomerulus and collects the filtrate forced out of the blood.">Bowman&rsquo;s capsule</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The high blood pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules out of the blood and into the Bowman&rsquo;s capsule &mdash; this is <strong>ultrafiltration</strong>. The filtrate contains water, glucose, urea, mineral ions and amino acids. Large molecules such as proteins and blood cells are too big to pass through and remain in the blood.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Selective Reabsorption</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which useful substances such as glucose, water and mineral ions are actively transported or reabsorbed from the filtrate back into the blood in the nephron.">Selective reabsorption</dfn> occurs as the filtrate travels along the nephron&rsquo;s tubule. All glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed into the surrounding capillaries by active transport (which requires energy from respiration). Most water is reabsorbed by osmosis. Useful mineral ions are also reabsorbed. By the time the filtrate reaches the collecting duct, it contains mainly urea, excess water and excess mineral ions &mdash; this becomes urine.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The cells lining the nephron tubule have <strong>microvilli</strong> (tiny projections that increase surface area) and many <strong>mitochondria</strong> to supply energy for active transport. These adaptations maximise the efficiency of reabsorption.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain why glucose never appears in healthy urine, naming the process and the part of the nephron responsible.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All glucose is reabsorbed from the filtrate by active transport in the nephron tubule. Healthy urine therefore contains no glucose. The presence of glucose in urine is an indication that blood glucose is too high for full reabsorption &mdash; a sign of uncontrolled diabetes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">ADH and the Control of Water Balance</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">The volume of water reabsorbed by the kidneys is controlled by <dfn class="term" data-def="Anti-diuretic hormone. A hormone produced by the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary gland that increases the permeability of the collecting duct to water, increasing water reabsorption.">ADH</dfn> (anti-diuretic hormone). ADH is produced by the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary gland into the bloodstream.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">When the blood becomes too concentrated (e.g. when you are dehydrated or have eaten a salty meal), the hypothalamus detects the low water potential of the blood. More ADH is released into the blood. ADH travels to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The final segment of the nephron through which filtrate passes before entering the ureter. Its permeability to water is controlled by ADH.">collecting duct</dfn> of the nephron and makes it more permeable to water. More water is reabsorbed by osmosis into the surrounding capillaries, so a smaller volume of <strong>more concentrated</strong> urine is produced.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>When Blood Water Content Is Too High</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">When blood water potential is too high (e.g. after drinking a large volume of water), the hypothalamus detects this and <strong>less ADH</strong> is released. The collecting duct becomes less permeable to water, so less water is reabsorbed. A larger volume of <strong>more dilute</strong> urine is produced. This is a classic negative feedback loop: the response (changing ADH levels) reverses the original change in blood water potential.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Kidney Failure: Dialysis and Transplants</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16">If the kidneys fail, waste products and excess water accumulate in the blood. Without treatment, this is fatal. There are two main treatments.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A medical procedure that artificially filters the blood using a machine when the kidneys are unable to do so. Blood passes through a partially permeable membrane to remove waste products.">Dialysis</dfn> uses a machine to filter the blood artificially. Blood is passed through a machine where it flows on one side of a partially permeable membrane, while dialysis fluid flows on the other side. Urea and excess mineral ions diffuse across the membrane into the dialysis fluid. The dialysis fluid is carefully formulated to maintain the correct concentrations of glucose and mineral ions, so these are not lost from the blood. Patients typically require dialysis sessions several times per week, each lasting several hours.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">A <strong>kidney transplant</strong> replaces the failed kidney with a healthy donor kidney. If the transplant is successful it gives the patient much greater freedom and quality of life than dialysis. However, the immune system may reject the new organ &mdash; to reduce this risk, donors are matched as closely as possible by tissue type and patients take immunosuppressant drugs for life, which increases their vulnerability to infection.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing Dialysis and Kidney Transplants</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Dialysis is widely available and avoids major surgery, but it is time-consuming, requires repeated sessions, restricts diet and quality of life, and does not cure kidney failure. A kidney transplant restores near-normal function and is a long-term solution, but it requires matching a suitable donor, involves major surgery with associated risks, and requires lifelong immunosuppression. Organ availability is limited by the number of donors.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The kidneys filter blood by ultrafiltration in the glomerulus, then selectively reabsorb all glucose, amino acids and most water in the nephron tubule, leaving urea and excess water to become urine.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">ADH controls water reabsorption in the collecting duct: more ADH &rarr; more water reabsorbed &rarr; concentrated urine; less ADH &rarr; less water reabsorbed &rarr; dilute urine.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Kidney failure is treated by dialysis (artificial filtration) or kidney transplant; transplants offer a better quality of life but require immunosuppression and a matched donor.</li>
</ul>
