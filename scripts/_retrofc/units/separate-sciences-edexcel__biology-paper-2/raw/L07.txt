<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Surface Area to Volume Ratio</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Single-celled organisms can exchange substances directly through their cell membrane because their <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of an organism's surface area to its total volume. Smaller organisms have a higher ratio, allowing efficient exchange directly across the body surface.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn> (SA:V ratio) is large. As organisms grow larger, their volume increases faster than their surface area, and the SA:V ratio falls. A small cube has a higher SA:V ratio than a large cube. For a large, active organism, the surface area of the body wall alone cannot exchange gases or nutrients fast enough &mdash; specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems are therefore essential.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain in one sentence why large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces. Use the term SA:V ratio.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>As organisms get larger, the surface area to volume ratio decreases. Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces (e.g. alveoli, villi) and transport systems (e.g. blood) to supply all cells with oxygen and nutrients efficiently.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Alveoli and Gas Exchange</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n5">The lungs contain millions of tiny air sacs called <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny air sacs in the lungs where gas exchange occurs. Their structure maximises the rate of diffusion of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out.">alveoli</dfn> (singular: alveolus). Their structure is adapted to maximise the rate of diffusion:</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Large surface area</strong> &mdash; millions of alveoli create an enormous total surface area for exchange.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Thin walls</strong> &mdash; one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is very short.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Good blood supply</strong> &mdash; a dense capillary network maintains a steep concentration gradient.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Ventilation</strong> &mdash; breathing constantly refreshes air in the alveoli, maintaining a high oxygen concentration.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fick&rsquo;s Law of Diffusion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A law stating that the rate of diffusion is proportional to surface area multiplied by concentration difference, divided by membrane thickness.">Fick&rsquo;s law</dfn> formalises the factors that affect diffusion rate:
    $$\text{Rate of diffusion} \propto \frac{\text{surface area} \times \text{concentration difference}}{\text{membrane thickness}}$$
    This means the rate of diffusion increases when surface area is larger, concentration difference across the membrane is greater, or membrane thickness is smaller. The alveolus maximises all three: large surface area, steep gradient maintained by blood flow and ventilation, and walls only one cell thick.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Blood Components</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Blood is a tissue transported around the body through blood vessels. It has four components:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Biconcave disc-shaped cells containing haemoglobin that carry oxygen from the lungs to body tissues. They have no nucleus, maximising space for haemoglobin.">Red blood cells</dfn> &mdash; contain <strong>haemoglobin</strong>, which binds to oxygen in the lungs (forming oxyhaemoglobin) and releases it in body tissues. Their biconcave disc shape increases surface area for gas exchange, and they have no nucleus to carry more haemoglobin.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>White blood cells</strong> &mdash; part of the immune system. Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens; lymphocytes produce antibodies and provide immunological memory.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Platelets</strong> &mdash; small cell fragments that trigger blood clotting at wound sites, forming a fibrin mesh that seals the injury and prevents blood loss and entry of pathogens.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The liquid component of blood, a straw-coloured fluid that carries dissolved substances including glucose, amino acids, carbon dioxide, urea, hormones and mineral ions.">Plasma</dfn> &mdash; the liquid part of blood, which transports dissolved substances including glucose, amino acids, carbon dioxide, urea and hormones.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Blood Vessels</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">There are three types of blood vessel, each adapted for its function:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Blood vessels that carry blood away from the heart. They have thick, muscular, elastic walls to withstand high pressure.">Arteries</dfn> &mdash; carry blood away from the heart at high pressure. Their walls are thick and muscular with elastic fibres to withstand and smooth out the pressure pulses from the heartbeat.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Blood vessels that carry blood back towards the heart. They have thinner walls than arteries and contain valves to prevent backflow.">Veins</dfn> &mdash; carry blood towards the heart at lower pressure. Their walls are thinner and they contain valves to prevent backflow.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny blood vessels with walls only one cell thick that connect arteries to veins. Gases, nutrients and waste products exchange between blood and tissues here.">Capillaries</dfn> &mdash; walls are only one cell thick, minimising diffusion distance. They form a dense network reaching every cell, allowing exchange of oxygen, glucose, carbon dioxide and waste with body tissues.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and draw a simple table comparing arteries, veins and capillaries: pressure, wall thickness, valves, function.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Arteries: high pressure, thick muscular walls, no valves, carry blood away from heart. Veins: low pressure, thin walls, valves prevent backflow, carry blood to heart. Capillaries: one-cell thick walls, site of exchange with tissues.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">The Heart and Cardiac Output</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n24">The heart is a double pump. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the rest of the body. It has four chambers: two upper <strong>atria</strong> that receive blood and two lower <strong>ventricles</strong> that pump it out. The left ventricle has a much thicker wall than the right because it must generate higher pressure to push blood around the entire body.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n25">The output of the heart is described by the cardiac output equation:
$$\text{cardiac output} = \text{stroke volume} \times \text{heart rate}$$
where cardiac output is measured in cm<sup>3</sup> per minute, <dfn class="term" data-def="The volume of blood pumped out of the left ventricle with each heartbeat, typically around 70 cm³ at rest in an adult.">stroke volume</dfn> is the volume pumped per beat (cm<sup>3</sup>) and heart rate is the number of beats per minute.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n27">Cardiac output = stroke volume &times; heart rate = 70 &times; 72 = <strong>5040 cm<sup>3</sup> per minute</strong> (approximately 5 litres per minute). During vigorous exercise, both stroke volume and heart rate increase, so cardiac output can rise to over 20 litres per minute in a trained athlete.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Respiration</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n29"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which cells release energy from glucose using oxygen. Products are carbon dioxide and water.">Aerobic respiration</dfn> releases energy from glucose in the presence of oxygen:
$$C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O$$
This occurs in the mitochondria and is the main source of energy for all cell processes.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Anaerobic Respiration and Fermentation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n30">When oxygen is limited (e.g. during intense exercise), cells switch to <dfn class="term" data-def="The release of energy from glucose without oxygen. In animals, the product is lactate; in yeast and plant cells, ethanol and carbon dioxide are produced.">anaerobic respiration</dfn>. In human muscle cells, glucose is partially broken down to produce <strong>lactate</strong> (also called lactic acid). This releases much less energy than aerobic respiration and causes muscle fatigue. After exercise, extra oxygen is needed to break down accumulated lactate &mdash; this is the oxygen debt.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">In yeast and plant cells, anaerobic respiration produces <strong>ethanol</strong> and <strong>carbon dioxide</strong> &mdash; a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="Anaerobic respiration in yeast and plant cells, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide from glucose.">fermentation</dfn>. Fermentation is used commercially in baking (CO<sub>2</sub> makes bread rise) and in brewing (ethanol is produced).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Large organisms need specialised exchange surfaces; alveoli maximise diffusion rate by providing large surface area, short diffusion distance and a steep concentration gradient, as described by Fick&rsquo;s law.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Blood carries oxygen (red blood cells), dissolved substances (plasma) and provides immune defence (white blood cells); arteries, veins and capillaries are each structurally adapted to their role.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Cardiac output = stroke volume &times; heart rate. Aerobic respiration produces CO<sub>2</sub> and water from glucose and oxygen; anaerobic respiration produces lactate in animals, or ethanol and CO<sub>2</sub> in yeast.</li>
</ul>
