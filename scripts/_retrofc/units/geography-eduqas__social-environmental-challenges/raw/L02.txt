--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Regions, Big Social Challenges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia together hold over a third of the world’s population and most of its extreme poverty. Both regions have made real progress this century — child mortality has fallen, more children are in school, life expectancy is up — but four challenges still loom large: getting girls through school, ending <dfn class="term" data-def="Work done by children that interferes with their education, health or development; often hazardous and full-time.">child labour</dfn>, supporting <dfn class="term" data-def="A person forced to leave their country to escape war, persecution or natural disaster, who cannot safely return.">refugees</dfn>, and reducing <dfn class="term" data-def="The death of a baby before its first birthday; expressed as deaths per 1,000 live births.">infant mortality</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the four main social challenges facing sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four main social challenges in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia are getting girls through school, ending child labour, supporting refugees, and reducing infant mortality. All four are linked — tackling one helps the others.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Education and Girls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Universal primary school is one of the most reliable ways to break the cycle of poverty. Girls who finish primary and secondary school marry later, have fewer children, earn more and lose fewer babies before age one. South Asia has made huge progress — girls’ primary enrolment is now near boys’ in India, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. Sub-Saharan Africa lags: in countries like Niger and Chad, only around half of girls complete primary school, and far fewer reach secondary.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Reasons why girls drop out are stubborn: school fees, the cost of uniforms and books, long unsafe walks to class, lack of girls’ toilets, early marriage, and the assumption that boys’ education is more valuable. The <strong>BRAC schools</strong> network in Bangladesh built one-room village schools targeted at girls and now educates more than a million children. International targets, especially the UN Sustainable Development Goal 4, push every country to deliver inclusive education.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Child Labour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Roughly 160 million children worldwide work in jobs that should be done by adults — in farms, mines, factories, brick kilns and homes as domestic servants. Many are in South Asia, particularly India and Pakistan, and in sub-Saharan Africa, especially in cocoa farms in Ivory Coast and Ghana. Children work because families need their income; a missed school year often means missed school forever. Hazardous work damages bodies still growing — spinal injuries from hauling bricks, lung disease from textile dust, eye damage from carpet weaving.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Tackling child labour is hard because it is woven into household survival. Successful approaches combine free school meals (so children eat when they study), conditional cash transfers (governments pay families to keep children in class), and adult literacy programmes (so parents earn more and rely less on children’s wages). Brazil’s <em>Bolsa Família</em> is a model now copied in several African and South Asian countries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Refugees and International Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Conflict, persecution and climate change push millions to leave home. Sub-Saharan Africa hosts more than 7 million refugees and asylum seekers, with major flows from South Sudan, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Somalia. Most cross into neighbouring countries — Uganda hosts more than 1.5 million people from neighbouring states. South Asia’s refugee picture is dominated by the Rohingya: nearly a million fled Myanmar to Bangladesh after 2017, where most still live in the world’s largest refugee settlement at Cox’s Bazar.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Refugees face overcrowded camps, limited schooling and irregular work. International responses include the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR), national resettlement programmes, and bilateral agreements between donor and host countries. The challenge is that refugees usually need years of support, not weeks — emergency aid alone cannot rebuild a life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two reasons people become refugees and one country example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Refugees flee war, persecution and disaster — for example, the Rohingya from Myanmar to Bangladesh after 2017, and South Sudanese into Uganda. Most stay in neighbouring LICs, not in HICs, often for years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Infant Mortality and Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In sub-Saharan Africa, around 27 babies in every 1,000 die before their first birthday — ten times the rate in HICs. Causes are linked: malnutrition, unclean water, poor maternal health, weak vaccination coverage, malaria and HIV. South Asia’s rates have fallen sharply (especially in Bangladesh and Sri Lanka) but Pakistan and Afghanistan still struggle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Solutions follow the diagnosis: rolling out vaccines (the GAVI alliance now reaches more than half of the world’s children), training community midwives, distributing insecticide-treated bed nets, and providing oral rehydration salts for diarrhoea, the second-biggest killer of under-fives. UN <strong>Sustainable Development Goal 3</strong> targets a global infant mortality rate of 12 per 1,000 by 2030.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Top-down</strong> projects are large-scale, often funded by the World Bank or UN, like national vaccination drives or new rural roads. They reach millions but can miss local needs. <strong>Bottom-up</strong> projects, run by NGOs and community groups, are smaller and slower — like BRAC’s village schools or local health worker training — but fit the daily realities of the people they serve. The most successful countries combine both.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia face four key social challenges: girls’ education, child labour, refugees and infant mortality — all linked through poverty and weak services.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">South Asia has made faster progress on schooling and infant mortality (e.g. Bangladesh, Sri Lanka) than parts of sub-Saharan Africa where rates remain high (e.g. Niger, Chad).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Successful responses combine top-down programmes (UN goals, GAVI vaccines) with bottom-up projects (BRAC schools, community midwives, conditional cash transfers).</li>
</ul>
