--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why gender and health are sharper measures of progress than national wealth alone.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Beyond GDP — Measuring How Lives Are Lived</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A country’s wealth is one story. A country’s daily life is another. Geographers studying <dfn class="term" data-def="Improvements in people's quality of life, including health, education, gender equality and freedom from poverty.">social development</dfn> ask a different question from those measuring economic development: not ‘how rich is this country?’ but ‘how well are people in this country actually living?’ The answers can surprise you. Two countries with similar GDPs can have very different rates of literacy, life expectancy and women’s rights.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how does social development differ from economic development, and why does it matter?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Economic development measures wealth (GDP, GNI). Social development measures how people actually live — their health, education and equality. Two countries can have similar GDP but very different daily lives.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Health as a Measure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Health indicators reveal the strength of public services, food supply and clean water. <dfn class="term" data-def="The average number of years a newborn baby is expected to live, given current health and mortality rates.">Life expectancy</dfn> is one of the sharpest signals: babies born in Japan today can expect to live to around 84, while those born in Sierra Leone may only reach the early 60s. <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of children who die before their first birthday for every 1,000 live births in a year.">Infant mortality rate</dfn> is even more sensitive — a high rate often signals weak maternal care, poor sanitation or malnutrition. South Asia has cut its infant mortality in half since 2000, but rates in parts of sub-Saharan Africa remain among the world’s highest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Other health measures fill in the picture. The number of doctors per 1,000 people, access to vaccines, and rates of HIV, malaria and tuberculosis all sharpen the comparison. The lower-income world made huge gains on these indicators between 2000 and 2020 — child deaths halved — before the COVID-19 pandemic temporarily reversed some progress.</p>

Yet 50% of its people lack access to safe water, life expectancy sits below 60 and most live in poverty — the wealth flows to a small elite. Costa Rica has a much lower GDP per person but boasts higher literacy, longer life expectancy and stronger gender equality. GDP measures the size of an economy. It does not measure how the money is shared.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Gender as a Measure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="How equally women and men can access education, work, healthcare and political power in a country.">Gender equality</dfn> is now central to social development. Geographers and the United Nations look at the share of girls completing primary and secondary school, the share of women in paid work and parliament, and gendered measures of health like maternal mortality. The United Nations <strong>Gender Inequality Index (GII)</strong> rolls these together into a single score: the lower the score, the closer the country is to equality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The story varies sharply by region. In Rwanda, more than 60% of MPs are women — the highest share in the world. In parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, by contrast, fewer than half of girls complete secondary school, and women still earn less than men for the same work in almost every country. Gender data shows progress as well as gaps: girls’ school enrolment in South Asia has risen sharply since 2000.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three indicators of gender equality and one country example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gender equality is measured through girls’ school enrolment, women in paid work and parliament, and maternal mortality. Together these capture how power, money and survival are shared between the sexes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Development Continuum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The old labels ‘developed’ and ‘developing’ suggest two clear groups, but geographers now picture a <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that countries sit on a sliding scale of social development rather than in two fixed groups.">development continuum</dfn> — a sliding scale on which every country sits somewhere different. Sweden and Sierra Leone are at opposite ends; India, Indonesia and South Africa sit in the middle and are moving fast. The continuum captures change. Vietnam, once near the bottom of the social development scale, has cut child mortality, raised life expectancy and improved girls’ education within a generation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">A Closing — or Widening — Gap?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">For most of the 21st century, the social development gap has narrowed. Life expectancy has risen worldwide. Child deaths have fallen. Girls’ school enrolment has climbed. The Millennium and Sustainable Development Goals helped focus international funding on these targets. But progress is uneven. Sub-Saharan Africa still lags on almost every health indicator. Conflict in places like South Sudan and Yemen reverses gains overnight. Climate change threatens to widen rather than close future gaps.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">A growing economy does not automatically translate into longer, fairer lives. Some oil-rich states have a high GDP but high gender inequality; some lower-GDP states like Sri Lanka and Cuba have life expectancy approaching that of HICs. The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income, education and life expectancy precisely because no single number tells the full story. Geographers use a basket of indicators — health, education, gender, poverty — to build a fuller picture of how a country is really doing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Social development measures how people actually live — their health, education and gender equality — rather than just national wealth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Health indicators (life expectancy, infant mortality) and gender indicators (girls’ schooling, women in parliament, maternal mortality) reveal gaps that GDP hides.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Countries sit on a development continuum that shifts over time; the global gap has narrowed since 2000 but climate change and conflict threaten progress.</li>
</ul>
