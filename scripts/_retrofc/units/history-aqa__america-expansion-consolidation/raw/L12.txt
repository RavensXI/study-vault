--- DESCRIPTION ---
Homesteaders, railroads and how cattle, wire and water reshaped the Great Plains 1862-1890.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Two 1862 Acts That Made the West</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In May and July 1862, with most Southern Democrats absent from Congress, the Republican-dominated Senate and House passed two laws that would remake the West. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1862 federal law offering 160 acres of public land to any settler who lived on and improved it for five years and paid an $18 filing fee.">Homestead Act</dfn> offered 160 acres of public land to any head of household who lived on it for five years and paid a small fee. The Pacific Railroad Act granted federal land and bonds to two companies &mdash; the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific &mdash; to build a transcontinental rail line. Together the two laws aimed to fill the Great Plains with farming families and bind the nation by iron rail.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many acres did a homesteader claim, how long did they have to live on the land, and what year was the Act passed?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under the 1862 Homestead Act, a settler could claim 160 acres (a quarter section) of public land for an $18 filing fee. To gain title they had to live on the land, build a dwelling and cultivate crops for five continuous years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why the Headline Hides the Failure Rate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Homestead Act became a founding myth of the American West &mdash; free land for any family with the courage to claim it. The reality was harsher. Around 1.6 million homestead claims were filed between 1862 and 1900; more than half were abandoned before the five years were up. On the dry High Plains, 160 acres was simply too small to support a family without irrigation. Drought, debt, isolation, plagues of grasshoppers and prairie fires drove most homestead families either to sell up to neighbours or to give up and leave. Engaging with this failure rate is what separates a top-band answer from a textbook recall.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Transcontinental Railroad</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Construction on the transcontinental railroad began in 1863 and finished on 10 May 1869, when the Union Pacific (building west from Omaha, Nebraska) met the Central Pacific (building east from Sacramento, California) at Promontory Point, Utah. A golden spike marked the joining. The line cut a journey across the continent from six months by wagon to about a week by train. Federal land grants to the railroad companies were vast: by 1871 around 175 million acres of public land had been granted to railroads &mdash; an area larger than Texas. Railroads then sold this land at a profit to the very settlers homesteading nearby.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Railroad companies were not just transport providers &mdash; they were marketers of the West. They sent agents to Northern Europe and to the Eastern states with pamphlets and posters promising fertile land and a healthy climate. Northern Pacific brochures advertising the Dakotas appeared in Sweden, Norway, Germany and Britain in five different languages. By 1890 around a quarter of homestead claims came from immigrant families. Railroads ran subsidised emigrant trains carrying settlers and their goods west at low fares &mdash; on the calculation that filled-up land along the line meant freight to ship in both directions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Farming Problems on the Plains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Great Plains punished settlers used to Eastern conditions. Annual rainfall west of the 100th meridian was less than 20 inches &mdash; below the threshold for traditional grain farming. Timber was scarce; settlers built homes from cut-out blocks of prairie turf and were nicknamed <dfn class="term" data-def="A nickname for Plains homesteaders, especially those who built their first homes from blocks of prairie turf cut with a steel plough.">sod-busters</dfn>. The thick grass roots blunted ordinary cast-iron ploughs. Grasshopper plagues, the worst in 1874-75, stripped entire fields in hours. Prairie fires, often started by lightning or sparks from passing trains, swept across hundreds of miles. Winter blizzards killed cattle and isolated families for weeks. Children walked miles to one-room schools when weather allowed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Solutions: New Tools, New Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Settler ingenuity and industrial invention combined to make the Plains farmable. John Deere&rsquo;s steel plough (improved through the 1860s) cut through tough sod that broke iron blades. Wind-powered pumps lifted water from deep aquifers. <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of farming developed for low-rainfall areas, in which the topsoil is broken up after each rain to trap moisture and a portion of land is left fallow each year.">Dry farming</dfn> techniques broke up the soil after each rain to trap moisture, leaving fields fallow in alternate years. Drought-resistant Russian Turkey Red wheat, brought by Mennonite immigrants from the steppe, transformed yields in Kansas and the Dakotas. Joseph Glidden&rsquo;s 1874 patent for cheap, mass-producible <dfn class="term" data-def="Twisted wire with sharp barbs, patented by Joseph Glidden in 1874; cheap fencing that allowed homesteaders to enclose Plains land for the first time.">barbed wire</dfn> finally let homesteaders fence their fields against drifting cattle &mdash; the open range began to close.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Cattle Industry: Open Range to Big Die-Up</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">From the late 1860s, Texas longhorn cattle were driven north along the Chisholm Trail to railhead cowtowns such as Abilene, Dodge City and Wichita. Joseph McCoy turned Abilene into the first cowtown in 1867. The herds grazed for free on the unfenced grassland of the Great Plains &mdash; the &lsquo;open range&rsquo;. Eastern and British investors poured money into ranch syndicates, expecting easy returns. The system collapsed in two stages. First, barbed wire enclosed the range from the mid-1870s. Then, the savage winter of 1886-87 &mdash; the &lsquo;Big Die-Up&rsquo; &mdash; killed up to nine-tenths of cattle in some northern ranges. By 1890 the open range was over; cattle ranching survived on a smaller, fenced and fed scale.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four farming problems and four matching solutions, like a vocab pair test.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four big farming problems on the Plains were lack of timber, lack of water, tough sod and crop failure from drought or pests. The four big solutions were sod homes, windmill-pumped wells, John Deere&rsquo;s steel plough and dry farming with drought-resistant wheat. Barbed wire (1874) closed the open range.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">The 1862 Homestead Act offered 160 acres for a five-year residence, but more than half of claims were abandoned before title was granted &mdash; the Plains were harder than the headline suggested.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">The 1869 transcontinental railroad bound the country together, opened the Plains to settlement and made the railroad companies the largest private landlords in the West.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Steel ploughs, windmills, dry farming, drought-resistant wheat and barbed wire turned the Plains farmable; the same barbed wire and the 1886-87 winter ended the open-range cattle era by 1890.</li></ul>
