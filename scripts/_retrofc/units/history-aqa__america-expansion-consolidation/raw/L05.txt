--- DESCRIPTION ---
Early federal policy towards Native nations: removal, the Indian Frontier and why it broke down.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Removal Before the Plains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the time of our period, US policy towards Native nations already had a hard edge. President Andrew Jackson&rsquo;s 1830 <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1830 Act of Congress that authorised the federal government to remove Native nations from the southeastern United States to lands west of the Mississippi.">Indian Removal Act</dfn> authorised the federal government to move the so-called Five Civilised Tribes &mdash; the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek and Seminole &mdash; off their lands in Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and the Carolinas. Pressure from white cotton planters and the discovery of gold on Cherokee land made the policy bipartisan in the South. Between 1830 and 1839 around 60,000 people were forced to walk to Indian Territory in modern Oklahoma. Roughly 4,000 Cherokee died on the 1838&ndash;39 march that became known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced 1838&ndash;39 removal of the Cherokee Nation to Indian Territory, on which roughly a quarter died of cold, hunger and disease.">Trail of Tears</dfn>. Removal set the legal and cultural template for Plains policy: Native nations could be moved by act of Congress, treaties could be rewritten, and the federal government would back settler demands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in which year did Congress pass the Indian Removal Act, who was president, and what happened to roughly a quarter of Cherokee on the 1838&ndash;39 march?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1830 Indian Removal Act, signed by Andrew Jackson, forced around 60,000 Native people from the Southeast onto Indian Territory in modern Oklahoma. The Trail of Tears (1838&ndash;39) killed roughly 4,000 Cherokee. Removal trained Washington in moving Native peoples by act of Congress, a habit that would shape Plains policy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Permanent Indian Frontier (1834)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Where would the removed peoples go? In 1834 Congress passed the Indian Trade and Intercourse Act, drawing what became known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1834 line west of the Mississippi designated by Congress as &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; permanently closed to white settlement; in practice it lasted barely fifteen years.">Permanent Indian Frontier</dfn>. A line was drawn from the Red River in the south to the Missouri River in the north. Everything west of that line would, in theory, be &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; for ever, with white traders requiring licences and white settlers excluded. The army built a chain of forts (Smith, Gibson, Leavenworth) along the line to enforce it. The vision was simple: removed Eastern peoples and the Plains nations would all live west of the line; white America would be content with the eastern half of the continent.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why Permanent Was Not Permanent</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The line lasted barely fifteen years. The 1840s brought four hammer blows. First, missionary reports began to draw farmers towards Oregon: the first big wagon train left Independence in 1841 and the trail crossed straight through &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo;. Second, Manifest Destiny made it politically easy to argue that the Plains belonged to a wider American future. Third, the 1846 Oregon Treaty with Britain and the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo gave the United States title to vast new lands &mdash; the Pacific Northwest and the Mexican Cession &mdash; lying beyond the supposedly permanent frontier. The Plains were now a corridor between the eastern United States and its new western territory, not a buffer protecting Native land.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Fourth, gold. The 1848 California discovery sent perhaps a quarter of a million migrants overland in five years. The trails carved deep ruts across the Platte River country, killed buffalo for meat, frightened the herds away from traditional hunting grounds, and spread cholera into Plains camps. Trespass on &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; was no longer a rare crime; it was the basis of national life. Federal officials had a choice: enforce the Permanent Frontier and stop the migration, or quietly abandon the policy. They chose the second.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA), set up within the War Department in 1824 and moved to the new Department of the Interior in 1849, was the agency that did the day-to-day managing of relations with Native nations. It hired Indian agents to live near each nation, distribute treaty payments and negotiate complaints. In practice the system was riddled with patronage and corruption: agents inflated supply costs, pocketed annuities meant for Native peoples, and ignored settler abuses. By 1860 even sympathetic officials in Washington were calling the BIA a national disgrace, but the agency remained the front line of US policy on the Plains for decades.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">From Separation to Concentration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">By the early 1850s policy had a new name: <dfn class="term" data-def="The mid-19th-century US policy of pushing Plains nations onto smaller, defined areas to keep migration corridors clear &mdash; the predecessor of the reservation system.">concentration</dfn>. Instead of separating whites and Plains nations entirely (which the Permanent Frontier had tried), Washington would now push the Plains nations onto smaller, defined areas, leaving wide corridors for white travel and settlement. The first big test was the 1851 Fort Laramie Treaty (covered in Lesson 6), which assigned named territories to the Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Crow and Shoshone. In return, the United States promised an annual <dfn class="term" data-def="A yearly payment of money or goods promised by the US Government to a Native nation in return for land or peace.">annuity</dfn> of $50,000 in goods for fifty years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Concentration was, on paper, more honest than the Permanent Frontier had been. It accepted that white migration would not stop. But it was not, in any deep sense, a peace policy: it stripped vast tracts away from people who had not lost a war, and it created the rule that Plains nations should now live in places defined by Washington. Within twenty years concentration would harden into the reservation system, and within forty into the Dawes Act&rsquo;s break-up of communal land. Each step shrank the territory available; none was reversed.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The period study expects students to track change over time. The clearest line in this lesson runs from 1830 (Removal) through 1834 (Permanent Frontier) to the early 1850s (concentration), and on to the post-Civil-War reservation system. At each stage, the available territory shrank and the central role of Washington grew. A useful self-test is to write the four steps in order and explain in one sentence why each one was a response to the failure of the previous one.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four reasons the Permanent Indian Frontier collapsed by the early 1850s, in single phrases.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Permanent Indian Frontier of 1834 was undone by four pressures: pioneer migration along the Oregon and California Trails from 1841; the doctrine of Manifest Destiny; territorial gains from the 1846 Oregon Treaty and the 1848 Mexican Cession; and the 1848 California Gold Rush. The line that was supposed to last for ever lasted about fifteen years.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">The 1830 Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears taught Washington that Native nations could be moved by act of Congress &mdash; a template that shaped Plains policy.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">The 1834 Permanent Indian Frontier promised to set aside &lsquo;Indian country&rsquo; for ever; pioneer migration, Manifest Destiny, the 1848 Cession and the Gold Rush undid it within fifteen years.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">By the early 1850s policy had shifted to concentration: pushing Plains nations onto smaller defined areas, the start of the long road to the reservation system.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">Period-study questions on this lesson often ask students to track change over time, and examiners punish chronologies that just list events. Top answers explain why each new policy was a response to the failure of the one before it. Practise the move: 1830 Removal &rarr; 1834 Permanent Frontier (because removed peoples needed a destination) &rarr; early 1850s concentration (because the Frontier could not hold against migration). Three steps with explicit causal links beats a six-event timeline every time.</p>
