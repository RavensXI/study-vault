--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why slavery, westward expansion and abolitionism pushed North and South toward civil war by 1861.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Economies, One Country</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1850 the United States looked like two countries pretending to be one. The North was industrialising fast: by 1860 it had 1.3 million factory workers, 70% of the country&rsquo;s railway track and a free-labour culture in which workers could (in theory) save, leave a job and start their own business. The South was a plantation economy whose wealth came from cotton, sugar and tobacco grown by enslaved labour. Around 4 million people were held in slavery in the South in 1860, on the eve of war. The wealthiest 1,500 families on the Continent &mdash; the so-called planter class &mdash; held political power in southern state capitals and shaped southern politics in Washington.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what was the population of enslaved people in the South by 1860, and what proportion of US railway track did the North hold?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1860 around 4 million people were held in slavery in the southern states, while the North held roughly 70% of the country&rsquo;s railway track and 1.3 million factory workers. The two regions were not just politically different &mdash; their entire labour systems were incompatible.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Real Quarrel: Slavery&rsquo;s Expansion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A common student answer is that &lsquo;the Civil War was about slavery&rsquo;. The historiographical consensus is sharper. The proximate fight was not over slavery&rsquo;s existence in the South &mdash; the federal government did not have the constitutional power to abolish it there &mdash; but over slavery&rsquo;s <em>expansion</em> into new western territories. The 1820 <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1820 agreement that admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, and barred slavery north of latitude 36&deg;30&prime; in the Louisiana Purchase.">Missouri Compromise</dfn> had managed the issue for thirty years. It admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state to keep the Senate balanced, and drew a line at latitude 36&deg;30&prime; across the Louisiana Purchase: north of the line, no new slave states; south of it, slavery permitted.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Mexican Cession of 1848 dropped a vast new territory into a country whose Compromise had been written for the Louisiana Purchase. The 1850 Compromise tried to patch the system: California entered as a free state, the Fugitive Slave Act required northerners to return runaway enslaved people, and the slave trade was banned in Washington DC. The patch held for four years. In 1854 Stephen Douglas&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1854 Act that organised the Kansas and Nebraska territories on the principle of popular sovereignty, allowing settlers to vote on slavery and effectively repealing the Missouri Compromise.">Kansas-Nebraska Act</dfn> repealed the Missouri Compromise altogether: in any new territory the question of slavery would now be decided by the votes of settlers themselves &mdash; <em>popular sovereignty</em>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Bleeding Kansas and Dred Scott</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The 1854 Act made Kansas the front line. Anti-slavery free-soilers and pro-slavery Missourians both rushed in to swing the vote. By 1856 the territory had two rival governments and open political violence: in May, pro-slavery raiders sacked the free-soil town of Lawrence; days later, the abolitionist John Brown led the killing of five pro-slavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek. Eastern newspapers christened the territory &lsquo;Bleeding Kansas&rsquo;. In 1857 the Supreme Court ruled in <em>Dred Scott v. Sandford</em> that Black people could not be US citizens and that Congress had no power to ban slavery in any territory &mdash; in effect declaring the entire 1820 Compromise unconstitutional. Northern opinion was outraged.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Three currents of <dfn class="term" data-def="The 19th-century movement to end the institution of slavery, especially strong in the northern United States and Britain.">abolitionism</dfn> shaped public opinion. Harriet Beecher Stowe&rsquo;s 1852 novel <em>Uncle Tom&rsquo;s Cabin</em> sold 300,000 copies in its first year and 1.5 million in Britain &mdash; Lincoln reportedly greeted Stowe in 1862 as &ldquo;the little woman who wrote the book that made this great war&rdquo;. The Underground Railroad, a network of safe houses and guides, helped perhaps 100,000 enslaved people escape to free states or Canada between 1810 and 1860; conductors like Harriet Tubman became national figures. And the abolitionist John Brown moved from Kansas to a far more dramatic act: in October 1859 he led a raid on the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, hoping to spark a slave uprising. Brown was captured and hanged within two months. To the South his raid proved that abolitionists meant insurrection; to many in the North he became a martyr.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Lincoln, Davis and the Election of 1860</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">By 1860 the political parties had broken along the same fault line. The Republican Party, founded in 1854, opposed slavery&rsquo;s expansion (though not its existence in the South). Its candidate, <dfn class="term" data-def="The 16th US President; led the Union through the Civil War, signed the Emancipation Proclamation and was assassinated in 1865.">Abraham Lincoln</dfn>, ran on a platform of stopping slavery from spreading any further west. The Democratic Party split into northern and southern wings, fielding two candidates and dividing its vote. Lincoln won the November 1860 election with 40% of the popular vote and a comfortable majority in the Electoral College &mdash; without carrying a single southern state. Within six weeks South Carolina had voted to secede; by February 1861 seven states had formed the Confederate States of America with <dfn class="term" data-def="President of the Confederate States of America from 1861 to 1865; a former US Senator and Secretary of War.">Jefferson Davis</dfn> as president. Four more states joined after war began.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The trigger was Fort Sumter, a federal garrison in Charleston harbour, South Carolina. Lincoln refused to surrender it. Confederate forces opened fire on 12 April 1861. The fort fell within 36 hours and Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers. The Civil War &mdash; the bloodiest in American history &mdash; had begun.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">Examiners regularly flag a recurring student error on this option: writing &lsquo;the war was about slavery&rsquo; and stopping there. The historiographical consensus, set out by James McPherson and others, is more precise: the proximate fight was over slavery&rsquo;s expansion into new territories. Saying so explicitly &mdash; and showing why the Missouri Compromise, Kansas-Nebraska Act, Dred Scott decision and Lincoln&rsquo;s 1860 platform all turned on that issue &mdash; is what separates a top-band answer from a competent one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the four key dates from this lesson in order &mdash; Missouri Compromise, Kansas-Nebraska Act, Dred Scott, Fort Sumter &mdash; with one sentence on each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The path to civil war ran through four key dates: 1820 Missouri Compromise (managed slavery&rsquo;s expansion); 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act (repealed it); 1857 Dred Scott decision (struck the entire Compromise down); 1860 Lincoln election and 1861 Fort Sumter (turned political crisis into shooting war).</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">The North and South had become two incompatible economies &mdash; industrial free labour versus plantation slavery, with around 4 million enslaved people in the South by 1860.</li><li data-narration-id="n18">The 1820 Missouri Compromise managed slavery&rsquo;s expansion for thirty years; the 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act repealed it; the 1857 Dred Scott decision struck the framework down.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">Abolitionism (Stowe, the Underground Railroad, John Brown), Lincoln&rsquo;s 1860 election and the firing on Fort Sumter in April 1861 turned political deadlock into the bloodiest war in American history.</li></ul>
