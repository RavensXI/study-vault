--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the 1861-65 war reshaped life for civilians North and South — economy, society, race.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A War of Civilians, Not Just Soldiers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The American Civil War of 1861&ndash;1865 cost roughly 620,000 soldiers&rsquo; lives, more than every other US war combined until Vietnam. But the spec is not interested in battles for their own sake &mdash; it asks about the social and economic impact on civilian populations. Northerners and Southerners experienced the war so differently that it is best understood as two separate home fronts running in parallel: one industrialising, one collapsing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: roughly how many soldiers died in the Civil War, and which two years bracket the conflict?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The American Civil War ran from April 1861 (Confederate attack on Fort Sumter) to April 1865 (Lee&rsquo;s surrender at Appomattox). Around 620,000 soldiers died, more than 2 per cent of the US population &mdash; equivalent today to roughly 7 million American deaths.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Northern Home Front: Boom Under Pressure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For the Union the war was an industrial accelerant. Northern factories, already growing before 1861, expanded sharply to supply uniforms, weapons, ammunition and railway track. Wages rose, although food prices rose faster. Women moved into factory and clerical work in unprecedented numbers, with around 200,000 entering the federal civil service or wartime nursing. Treasury Secretary Salmon Chase printed paper money &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="Paper currency issued by the US federal government during the Civil War, not backed by gold; nicknamed for its colour.">Greenback</dfn> &mdash; to fund the war effort, and Congress passed the first federal income tax in 1861.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Compulsory military service required by law.">Conscription</dfn> arrived in March 1863 and proved bitterly unpopular. A wealthy man could pay $300 to avoid service or hire a substitute, which working-class men saw as proof of a &lsquo;rich man&rsquo;s war and a poor man&rsquo;s fight&rsquo;. The result was the New York Draft Riots of July 1863: four days of violence in which Irish-American workers attacked recruiting stations, federal buildings and Black New Yorkers. At least 119 people were killed before Union troops, fresh from Gettysburg, restored order.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Southern Home Front: Collapse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Confederacy faced the opposite trajectory. The Union naval blockade, declared by Lincoln in April 1861, choked off Southern cotton exports and the imported goods on which the South depended. Food shortages set in by 1862. Bread riots broke out in Richmond, Virginia in April 1863 when crowds of women looted shops. Confederate currency lost its value; by war&rsquo;s end a barrel of flour cost $1,000 in Confederate dollars. Slave labour kept plantations running, but as Union armies advanced, hundreds of thousands of enslaved people walked off and crossed into Union lines as <dfn class="term" data-def="Enslaved people who escaped to Union lines during the Civil War; treated as confiscated 'property of war' rather than returned.">contraband</dfn>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">From November to December 1864, Union General William Tecumseh Sherman led 60,000 troops on a 285-mile destructive march from Atlanta to Savannah, Georgia. His &lsquo;total war&rsquo; strategy targeted the civilian economy &mdash; railroads were torn up and twisted into &lsquo;Sherman&rsquo;s neckties&rsquo;, plantations burned, livestock taken or killed. Sherman estimated his march did $100 million of damage. The aim was to break Confederate civilian morale by showing the war could no longer be kept at arm&rsquo;s length, and historians widely agree it shortened the war by months.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Slavery in Wartime</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Lincoln did not begin the war as an abolitionist. His public goal in 1861 was to preserve the Union; he believed an immediate emancipation would push the border states (Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri) into the Confederacy. After the bloody Union victory at Antietam (September 1862), he moved. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Lincoln's January 1863 executive order declaring all enslaved people in Confederate-held territory to be free.">Emancipation Proclamation</dfn>, issued on 1 January 1863, declared all enslaved people in Confederate-held territory free. It did not legally free people in the Union border states, but it transformed the war&rsquo;s meaning: the Union army was now an army of liberation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Around 200,000 Black soldiers served in the Union Army and Navy from 1863, organised into the United States Coloured Troops. They were initially paid less than white soldiers and faced execution rather than capture if taken by Confederate forces. The famous 54th Massachusetts Regiment&rsquo;s assault on Fort Wagner in July 1863 helped break Northern doubts that Black soldiers would fight.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Wartime emergency produced a permanent expansion of federal power. The 1861 Revenue Act introduced the first income tax. The 1862 Legal Tender Act made Greenbacks legal currency. The 1863 National Banking Act created federally chartered banks issuing a uniform currency &mdash; ending decades of state-bank chaos. The Homestead Act and Pacific Railroad Act, both 1862, channelled federal land into settlement and rail. The Republicans, in control of Congress while the Southern Democrats were absent, used the war to remake the American state.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Why the Sequence of Battles Matters Briefly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">For civilians, three turning points mattered. Antietam (September 1862) was bloody but inconclusive militarily; politically it gave Lincoln the Union victory he needed to issue emancipation. Gettysburg and Vicksburg (both July 1863) broke the Confederacy&rsquo;s strategic position &mdash; Gettysburg ended Lee&rsquo;s last invasion of the North; Vicksburg gave the Union control of the Mississippi River, splitting the Confederacy in two. By spring 1865, with Atlanta fallen and Sherman in the Carolinas, Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox Court House on 9 April 1865. Lincoln was assassinated five days later by Confederate sympathiser John Wilkes Booth.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three social impacts on the North and three on the South in two short lists.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Northern home front saw industrial growth, women in new jobs, paper currency and draft riots. The Southern home front saw blockade, hyperinflation, food riots and Sherman&rsquo;s scorched-earth march. Both sides experienced massive expansion of federal power and the legal end of slavery in Confederate territory.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The Civil War transformed Northern civilian life through industrial boom, paper currency, conscription riots and an expanding federal state.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The Confederate home front collapsed under blockade, hyperinflation, bread riots and Sherman&rsquo;s March &mdash; the war was won as much against Southern civilians as Southern armies.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The 1863 Emancipation Proclamation and the service of 200,000 Black Union soldiers turned the war into an instrument of liberation, paving the way for the 13th Amendment in 1865.</li></ul>
