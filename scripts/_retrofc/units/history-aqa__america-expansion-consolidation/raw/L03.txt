--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why farmers and miners headed west, the trails they took and the dangers they faced.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Farmers Went West</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Long before gold dominated the headlines, the great force pushing Americans westward was hunger for land. East-coast soils had been farmed for two centuries; in places they were exhausted. The 1837 Panic, a sharp banking crisis, drove thousands of indebted farmers off their plots, while immigration from Ireland and Germany was crowding eastern cities. Beyond the Mississippi, by contrast, lay vast tracts of fertile valley land in Oregon and California &mdash; territory the United States did not yet control but that missionary reports described in glowing terms. The first pioneer wagon trains for Oregon set out in 1841; by 1846, when Britain ceded the Oregon Territory south of the 49th parallel, the trickle had become a flow.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two main pioneer trails, the years they were heavily used, and one push factor and one pull factor that drove farmers west.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Oregon Trail (used heavily from 1841) and the California Trail (especially from 1849) both began at Independence, Missouri and ran roughly 2,000 miles west. Pioneer farmers were pushed by soil exhaustion and the 1837 Panic, and pulled by the fertile Willamette Valley and free or cheap land.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Wagon Train</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most pioneer farmers travelled in <dfn class="term" data-def="A column of covered wagons grouped together for safety on the journey across the Plains and mountains.">wagon trains</dfn> &mdash; columns of fifteen to fifty covered wagons that gathered each spring at Independence, Missouri. Wagons were small (around four feet wide and ten feet long), pulled by oxen rather than horses because oxen ate prairie grass and could pull heavier loads, and packed with tools, seeds, flour, bacon, dried fruit and a little furniture. Families walked beside their wagons rather than rode in them. Each train elected a captain who set the pace, decided when to camp and resolved disputes. The journey took four to six months and had to be completed before the autumn snows closed the mountain passes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Hardships on the Trail</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The dangers were real, though students sometimes overstate the role of Native attacks. Far more migrants died of cholera, typhoid and dysentery picked up at riverside campsites where dirty water mixed with human waste. Accidents &mdash; children run over by wagons, drownings at river crossings, gunshots while loading firearms &mdash; killed many more. Hailstorms, lightning, blizzards in the Rockies and stampedes of oxen all took their toll. Of around 350,000 people who crossed the Plains by 1860, perhaps 20,000 died &mdash; roughly one in seventeen.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">In 1846 a party of 87 California-bound migrants led by George Donner left the Oregon Trail to try a shortcut described in a guidebook by Lansford Hastings &mdash; a route Hastings himself had never travelled. The detour cost three weeks. The group reached the Sierra Nevada in late October, just as the first heavy snows hit the passes. Trapped at a high mountain lake (now called Donner Lake), 41 of the 87 died over the winter; some of the survivors ate the bodies of those who had died. The story horrified the East but did not stop the migration &mdash; if anything, it underlined the importance of leaving Independence early enough in the spring and trusting the established trail.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The 1848 Gold Strike</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">On 24 January 1848 a Connecticut carpenter named James Marshall, building a sawmill on the American River for the Swiss landowner John Sutter, spotted gold flakes in the river bed. The news leaked despite efforts to keep it quiet, and by the autumn it had reached the East. President Polk announced the discovery in his December 1848 message to Congress. In 1849 around 80,000 men &mdash; the original <dfn class="term" data-def="A miner who travelled to California during the 1849 peak of the Gold Rush.">forty-niners</dfn> &mdash; rushed to California by sea around Cape Horn, by the Panama route, or overland on the California Trail. Within two years California&rsquo;s population had jumped from around 14,000 to over 220,000, and it was admitted to the Union as a state in 1850.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Boom Towns and Lawlessness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Mining camps grew into <dfn class="term" data-def="A frontier settlement that grew very rapidly around a mine or other resource and often shrank just as quickly when the resource ran out.">boom towns</dfn> within months. San Francisco, a small port of 800 in 1848, had 25,000 inhabitants by 1850. Tents gave way to clapboard buildings and saloons; prices for food, tools and lodging soared because supplies could not keep up with demand. Most miners worked as individuals with pans and rockers along stream beds &mdash; placer mining &mdash; and few struck it rich. Far more money was made selling shovels, eggs and whisky than digging for gold.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Boom towns had no functioning legal system at first. With courts hundreds of miles away, miners formed <dfn class="term" data-def="A self-appointed group of local citizens who enforced order, often violently, in places without formal courts or police.">vigilance committees</dfn> to deal with theft and murder. The committees sometimes worked &mdash; San Francisco&rsquo;s 1851 committee shut down a violent gang in days &mdash; and sometimes lynched the wrong man. The lawlessness fed wider stereotypes of the West that would last into the 20th century.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Wider Effects of the Rush</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The Gold Rush transformed the West in ways the trickle of pioneer farmers had not. It pulled in roughly 25,000 Chinese immigrants between 1849 and 1854, who faced violence and discriminatory taxes. It tore up rivers and forests for hydraulic mining and timber. It pushed Native Californians off their land, and the state&rsquo;s Native population fell from around 150,000 in 1845 to under 30,000 by 1870. And it accelerated calls for a transcontinental railroad &mdash; eventually built in 1869 &mdash; because the months-long sea route around Cape Horn was no longer fit for purpose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three consequences of the 1848 California Gold Rush in single sentences &mdash; one social, one economic, one political.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1848 strike at Sutter&rsquo;s Mill triggered the California Gold Rush. Around 80,000 forty-niners arrived in 1849 alone; California&rsquo;s population grew fifteenfold by 1850; statehood followed the same year; and the rush sped up displacement of Native peoples and the call for a transcontinental railroad.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Push factors (1837 Panic, soil exhaustion, urban overcrowding) and pull factors (Oregon valleys, California gold, free land) made the West a destination for hundreds of thousands of farmers and miners.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The Oregon and California Trails were dangerous &mdash; cholera, accidents and weather killed roughly one in seventeen migrants &mdash; but the journey was made possible by wagon trains and elected captains.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The 1848 Gold Rush transformed California in months: forty-niners, boom towns, vigilance committees, Chinese immigration, displacement of Native peoples and statehood in 1850.</li></ul>
