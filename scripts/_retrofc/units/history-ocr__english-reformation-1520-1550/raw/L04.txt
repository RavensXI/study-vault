--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Cromwell's surveyors mapped monastic wealth and compiled evidence of monastic sin in 1535.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Building the Case Against the Monasteries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1535 Thomas Cromwell organised two distinct operations that together would provide the justification for dissolving England&rsquo;s monasteries. The first was a comprehensive survey of all Church wealth. The second was a targeted investigation of monastic conduct. Both fed directly into the legislation that followed in 1536.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Valor Ecclesiasticus 1535</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A national survey of all Church income and property ordered by Henry VIII in 1535, used to assess what the monasteries were worth before dissolution.">Valor Ecclesiasticus</dfn> was a survey ordered by Henry in January 1535. Royal commissioners fanned out across England and Wales to record the income of every church institution &mdash; monasteries, convents, friaries, chantries, and parish churches. Within a year they had compiled a detailed record running to six volumes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The survey found that the monasteries collectively had an annual income of around &pound;165,000 &mdash; an enormous sum. Smaller houses with income below &pound;200 per year were identified as candidates for immediate closure. The Valor gave the Crown a precise financial map of what was available to seize. It is also a uniquely detailed historical source: no other pre-modern European country produced anything comparable in the 1530s.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what was the Valor Ecclesiasticus, when was it completed, and what annual income did it reveal the monasteries held collectively?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Valor Ecclesiasticus was completed in 1535 and revealed collective monastic annual income of around &pound;165,000. Monasteries earning under &pound;200 per year were targeted first. It is one of the most detailed administrative surveys produced in Tudor England.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Cromwell&rsquo;s Visitors and the Comperta</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Alongside the Valor, Cromwell dispatched a team of royal visitors to inspect monastic discipline. The principal visitors were <dfn class="term" data-def="One of Cromwell's chief monastic visitors in 1535-36, known for aggressive and colourful reporting of monastic misconduct.">Richard Layton</dfn> and Thomas Legh. Their task was to identify evidence of immorality, incompetence or financial mismanagement &mdash; or, critics argue, to manufacture it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The visitors moved at speed: Layton boasted he could inspect a house in a single day. Their reports, compiled in a document known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The summarised findings of Cromwell's monastic visitors, presented to Parliament in 1536 as evidence justifying the dissolution of smaller monasteries.">Comperta</dfn> (Latin: &lsquo;things discovered&rsquo;), alleged widespread sodomy, keeping of concubines, superstitious relics and financial corruption. The Comperta was presented to Parliament in 1536 to justify the First Suppression Act.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Historians debate the reliability of the Comperta fiercely. The visitors were appointed by Cromwell and had a clear brief to find fault. Layton and Legh were known for bullying abbots and friars into signing confessions. Some allegations &mdash; including highly specific sexual charges &mdash; appear in the reports for almost every house visited, which has led historians such as G.W. Bernard and J.J. Scarisbrick to question whether they reflect a template rather than genuine investigation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">On the other hand, some monastic houses were genuinely small, poor and poorly run. Visitation records from church authorities before 1535 (bishops&rsquo; registers) do sometimes report discipline problems. The debate is not &lsquo;was everything fine&rsquo; vs &lsquo;was everything corrupt&rsquo; &mdash; it is about the degree to which Cromwell&rsquo;s visitors exaggerated what they found to serve a political purpose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">What the Crown Stood to Gain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The Valor made clear that dissolution would transfer enormous wealth to the Crown. Beyond income, the monasteries held <dfn class="term" data-def="The income-generating land and property holdings of a monastery, which would transfer to the Crown upon dissolution.">temporalities</dfn> &mdash; vast landed estates that produced rents. The sale or leasing of former monastic land to local gentry created a new class of landowner with a financial stake in the Reformation continuing &mdash; they would resist any Catholic restoration.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words why the Valor Ecclesiasticus and the Comperta together made it easier for Cromwell to push dissolution through Parliament.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Valor provided financial justification (monasteries were wealthy and could enrich the Crown); the Comperta provided moral justification (monasteries were corrupt and should be closed). Together they gave Parliament both an economic and an ethical reason to support the 1536 Suppression Act.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Richard Layton was a lawyer and churchman who had helped Cromwell in earlier administrative tasks. Thomas Legh was a civilian lawyer known for his harsh treatment of abbots. John ap Rice was a more careful observer whose detailed notes survive and are considered more reliable by historians. Together they covered hundreds of houses in less than a year &mdash; a rate that, critics argue, made genuine investigation impossible.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">The Human Cost: Monks, Nuns and Lay Workers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Before turning to the dissolution process itself, it is worth understanding what monastic communities looked like in human terms. The approximately 825 houses in England housed around 10,000 monks, nuns, friars and canons, served by a much larger number of lay brothers and paid servants &mdash; perhaps 30,000&ndash;40,000 people in total depending on how dependants are counted. For many of these people, the monastery was not just their workplace but their entire social world from childhood.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Monks and nuns who had taken lifetime vows faced a particular dilemma when dissolution arrived. Those who accepted the dissolution and surrendered voluntarily typically received a pension, though pensions were often small and irregularly paid. Those who refused &mdash; like Abbot Whiting of Glastonbury &mdash; risked execution. Former monks and nuns who had lived in enclosed communities had little preparation for life in the secular world. Evidence from post-dissolution wills and court records suggests that many former religious slipped into poverty within a generation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The Valor Ecclesiasticus recorded not just buildings and revenues but also the obligations the monasteries owed: charitable distributions, almsgiving, hospitality, and the maintenance of bridges and roads in return for holding their lands. When the Crown took over monastic lands, it rarely maintained these obligations at the same level. This gap between what the monasteries provided and what replacement provision was made is a central issue for assessing the dissolution&rsquo;s social impact.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Valor Ecclesiasticus (1535) mapped all Church income; it revealed collective monastic income of ~&pound;165,000 and identified smaller monasteries (under &pound;200/year) as first targets.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Cromwell&rsquo;s visitors (Layton, Legh, ap Rice) produced the Comperta in 1535&ndash;36, alleging widespread monastic misconduct; its reliability is disputed by historians.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The two documents together gave Parliament financial and moral justification for the First Suppression Act of 1536 &mdash; wealth would enrich the Crown; alleged corruption justified closure.</li>
</ul>
