--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Defeat in the East</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1904 Nicholas II went to war with Japan over influence in Manchuria and Korea. Senior ministers expected a quick victory that would, in Interior Minister Plehve&rsquo;s phrase, give Russia &lsquo;a small victorious war&rsquo; to distract a restless population. Instead the Russian Pacific squadron was destroyed at Port Arthur, the Baltic fleet sailed halfway round the world only to be annihilated at Tsushima in May 1905, and the army lost the land battle of Mukden. By spring 1905 Russia had lost a war to an Asian power for the first time in the modern era. The political shockwave at home was immediate: defeat exposed the autocracy as incompetent as well as repressive.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three Russian defeats of 1904&ndash;05 and one consequence of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Russo-Japanese War of 1904&ndash;05 ended in three humiliating defeats &mdash; Port Arthur, Mukden and Tsushima &mdash; and discredited the autocracy at the moment that domestic discontent was spilling onto the streets.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Bloody Sunday</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">On a freezing Sunday morning in January 1905, around 150,000 striking workers and their families marched through St Petersburg to the Winter Palace. They were led by Father Georgi Gapon, an Orthodox priest who ran a police-approved workers&rsquo; assembly. They carried icons and portraits of the Tsar, sang hymns, and bore a respectful petition asking for an eight-hour day, fairer wages and a constituent assembly. Nicholas was not in the city. The Imperial Guard, drawn up around the Palace Square, opened fire on the procession.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Official figures admitted to about 100 dead; eyewitnesses and later historians put the toll at over 200, possibly up to 1,000 including those who died of wounds. The day became known as <dfn class="term" data-def="22 January 1905, when imperial troops fired on a peaceful workers&rsquo; procession to the Winter Palace led by Father Gapon, killing several hundred.">Bloody Sunday</dfn>. The political effect was incalculable. Workers who had marched believing in the &lsquo;Little Father&rsquo; left convinced he had personally ordered the massacre. Lenin, in exile, called it &lsquo;the awakening of Russia&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Revolution Spreads</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By summer 1905 strikes had paralysed industrial Russia. Peasants burned manor houses across the central provinces. Mutinies broke out in the armed forces, most famously aboard the Black Sea battleship <em>Potemkin</em> in June, where sailors killed officers in protest at maggoty meat. National minorities &mdash; Poles, Finns, Georgians &mdash; demanded autonomy. Liberal middle-class professionals organised the &lsquo;Union of Unions&rsquo; in May, demanding a constituent assembly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In October a general strike paralysed the railways and printing presses. Workers in St Petersburg formed the city&rsquo;s first <dfn class="term" data-def="An elected workers&rsquo; council. The St Petersburg Soviet of 1905 was the first; the model would return decisively in 1917.">soviet</dfn>, a council of factory delegates that ran the strike. The young Leon Trotsky, then a Menshevik, became its de facto chair. The state&rsquo;s authority in the capital had effectively collapsed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Witte, recalled to government, told Nicholas he had two choices: military dictatorship or a constitution. On 17 October 1905 the Tsar reluctantly issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="The decree of 17 October 1905 in which Nicholas II conceded civil rights, legal political parties and an elected parliament (the Duma).">October Manifesto</dfn>, conceding three things: civil rights (freedom of speech, assembly, conscience), legal political parties, and an elected parliament called the Duma. He told his mother he had signed it &lsquo;with sickness in my soul&rsquo; and described those who had pushed it on him as traitors. The Manifesto was a strategic retreat, not a conversion to constitutional government.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Why the Autocracy Survived</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">By October the regime looked finished. Within two months it had won. Three things saved it. First, the October Manifesto split the opposition. Liberals and Octobrists welcomed the concessions; revolutionaries dismissed them. The middle-class moderate base, which had marched alongside workers all summer, now had something to lose by joining a Bolshevik-led uprising.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Second, the Tsar had ended the war. The Treaty of Portsmouth in September brought home the Manchurian field army, who were used to crush the Moscow Uprising in December 1905 with house-to-house artillery. Third, the army itself had stayed loyal. Although there were mutinies in the navy and a few army units, the bulk of the conscript infantry obeyed orders. By March 1906 the St Petersburg Soviet had been arrested, Trotsky among them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">In December 1905, after the moderate liberals had accepted the Manifesto, Bolshevik and Menshevik agitators in Moscow tried to push the revolution further. They built barricades in the working-class Presnya district and called for a general strike. The Tsar sent the elite Semyonovsky Guards Regiment from St Petersburg with field artillery. For nine days, troops shelled the district building by building, killing perhaps 1,000 civilians. The uprising&rsquo;s defeat broke the back of the 1905 Revolution: it showed that the Manifesto had separated the moderate middle class from any further street fighting, and that the regime would now use overwhelming military force without hesitation. Lenin took two lessons. First, soviets were a powerful institution. Second, an unarmed urban population could not defeat a disciplined army. Both lessons would shape Bolshevik thinking through 1917.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the three reasons Tsarism survived 1905, ranking them from most to least important.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Tsarist regime survived 1905 because the October Manifesto split moderate opposition from radical revolutionaries, the army stayed loyal, and the war with Japan ended in time to free troops to crush the Moscow Uprising.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Aftermath</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Lenin would later call 1905 &lsquo;the dress rehearsal&rsquo; for 1917. The phrase captures both the lessons learned and the lessons missed. Workers had discovered the soviet as an institution of mass democracy. Revolutionaries had seen that mutinies in the armed forces were the regime&rsquo;s nightmare. But the regime had also learned: rapid concession, moral discrediting of the opposition through religious imagery, and a willingness to use overwhelming force once the political moment had passed. The chapter was not closed. The Manifesto&rsquo;s promise of a Duma still had to be tested, and the next four years &mdash; the era of Stolypin &mdash; would show how Nicholas planned to honour it.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (1904&ndash;05) discredited the autocracy and turned domestic protest into open revolution after Bloody Sunday in January 1905.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The October Manifesto conceded civil rights, legal parties and a Duma. Nicholas signed it under duress and resented it for the rest of his reign.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">The autocracy survived because the Manifesto split moderate liberals from revolutionaries, the army stayed loyal, and the end of the war freed troops to crush the December uprising in Moscow.</li></ul>

For chain-of-consequence questions on Bloody Sunday or the Manifesto, drop the listing habit &mdash; explain how military defeat produced strikes, which produced the October Manifesto, which split the opposition, which let the regime use force in December.</p>
