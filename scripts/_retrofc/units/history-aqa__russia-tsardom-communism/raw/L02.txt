--- DESCRIPTION ---
How autocracy, the Okhrana and the Orthodox Church faced liberal and revolutionary challenge.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Sacred Duty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Nicholas II did not become a tyrant by choice. He became one by inheritance and conviction. His father Alexander III had reversed liberal reforms after the assassination of Alexander II in 1881 and trained Nicholas to see <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government in which a single ruler holds total power, accountable to no parliament or constitution.">autocracy</dfn> as a religious duty owed to God and ancestor. The official slogan of the regime &mdash; &lsquo;Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality&rsquo; &mdash; treated the Tsar as the divinely appointed Father of his people. Nicholas wrote in his diary that constitutional government would be &lsquo;senseless dreams&rsquo;. The problem was that the empire he inherited was no longer a society in which such an idea could survive.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three words of the Tsarist motto and what each meant for ordinary Russians.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Tsarist regime rested on three pillars summarised in the slogan &lsquo;Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality&rsquo;. The Orthodox Church taught obedience to the Tsar; the Tsar ruled without parliament or written constitution; and Russification subordinated minority peoples to Russian identity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Court and the Tsarina</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Nicholas&rsquo;s court was small, traditional and increasingly cut off from real political life. Senior posts went to relatives and trusted nobles rather than expert administrators. His wife Tsarina Alexandra, a German-born princess who converted to Orthodoxy, was devoutly religious and politically inflexible. Together they preferred quiet family life at Tsarskoe Selo to the public ceremonies of capital politics. As the years went on, real decision-making shrank to a tiny circle of intimates &mdash; a vulnerability that the war years would expose disastrously.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Okhrana and the Police State</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Behind the spiritual rhetoric stood a police state. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Tsarist secret police, founded in 1881, responsible for hunting revolutionaries and infiltrating opposition groups.">Okhrana</dfn> was the secret police arm of the Ministry of the Interior. Created after the assassination of Alexander II in 1881, it ran agents inside the universities, the factories and even the revolutionary parties themselves. By 1900 the Okhrana had infiltrated the Bolsheviks so deeply that one of Lenin&rsquo;s closest collaborators, Roman Malinovsky, was a paid agent passing names to the police.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Punishment was harsh but selective. The regime preferred to send agitators to Siberian exile rather than create martyrs. Lenin himself spent three years in Shushenskoe between 1897 and 1900, free to read and write but cut off from political action. The Okhrana also relied on censorship of newspapers, the banning of trade unions and laws against public meetings of more than three people without permission.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The Russian Orthodox Church was effectively a department of state, run by the Tsar&rsquo;s Holy Synod and headed by a layman, the Procurator. Priests were paid by the government and were expected to denounce political agitators in their parishes. Russification &mdash; banning the Polish language in schools, restricting Jewish residence to the Pale of Settlement, suppressing Finnish autonomy &mdash; was meant to bind the empire together. In practice it created angry educated minorities, especially Poles and Jews, who would supply many of the empire&rsquo;s most determined revolutionaries. Pogroms against Jewish communities, often tolerated by local police, drove waves of emigration to Britain and America.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Liberal Opposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Not all opposition was revolutionary. Russia&rsquo;s small middle class &mdash; doctors, lawyers, university professors, factory owners &mdash; mostly wanted what western liberals wanted: a parliament, a constitution, civil rights. They expressed themselves through the <em>zemstva</em> (rural councils) and through professional associations. Their political home from 1905 onwards would be the Constitutional Democrats or <dfn class="term" data-def="The Constitutional Democratic Party founded in 1905, led by Pavel Milyukov, calling for a Western-style parliamentary monarchy.">Kadets</dfn>, led by the historian Pavel Milyukov, who wanted a British-style parliamentary monarchy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A more conservative liberal current, the Octobrists, was content to accept the limited reforms Nicholas would offer in 1905 and stop there. Both groups posed a problem for Nicholas: they were patriotic and respectable, so persecution looked unjust, but their demands meant the end of pure autocracy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Revolutionary Opposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">More dangerous, in the regime&rsquo;s eyes, were the revolutionary parties. The Socialist Revolutionaries, formed in 1901, drew on the older Russian populist tradition of going &lsquo;to the people&rsquo;. They believed the peasantry, not the proletariat, would lead the revolution and used political assassination as a tactic &mdash; murdering ministers and grand dukes through their &lsquo;Combat Organisation&rsquo;. Two interior ministers, Sipyagin in 1902 and Plehve in 1904, were among their victims.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, founded in 1898, was Marxist and looked to the urban working class. At its Second Congress in London and Brussels in 1903 it split. Lenin demanded a small, disciplined party of full-time professional revolutionaries; Julius Martov wanted a mass party along German socialist lines. Lenin&rsquo;s faction won a procedural vote &mdash; hence the name <dfn class="term" data-def="&lsquo;Majoritarians&rsquo; &mdash; the faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party led by Lenin from 1903, demanding a small disciplined party of professional revolutionaries.">Bolsheviks</dfn>, &lsquo;majoritarians&rsquo; &mdash; while Martov&rsquo;s group became the Mensheviks. The split would shape the revolutions to come.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The 1903 Split &mdash; A Misleading Name</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The label &lsquo;Bolshevik&rsquo; (majority) referred only to a single vote at the 1903 Congress, on which faction would control the editorial board of the party newspaper. Until 1917 the Mensheviks were generally larger than the Bolsheviks inside Russia. But the Bolshevik label stuck, giving Lenin&rsquo;s faction a useful claim to legitimacy. Until 1912 the two groups were technically still one party; the formal break came at the Prague Conference. This is why students should not assume a sharp Bolshevik&ndash;Menshevik divide before 1914 &mdash; many local activists moved between the factions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four main opposition groups by 1903 with one defining feature each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1903 there were four main opposition currents: the Kadets (liberal, parliamentary), the Octobrists (conservative liberal), the Socialist Revolutionaries (peasant-focused, used assassination), and the Social Democrats &mdash; split into Bolsheviks under Lenin and Mensheviks under Martov.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why It Mattered</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">In 1894 the regime believed it could ride out criticism through the Okhrana, the Church and the army. By 1904 Russia had assassins inside the cabinet, Marxist printing presses inside its factories and a Tsar still convinced that constitutional government was a &lsquo;senseless dream&rsquo;. The system that looked unshakeable to Nicholas in his Tsarskoe Selo study was already cracking on the streets of St Petersburg. The next blow &mdash; Bloody Sunday &mdash; would come from outside the political parties altogether, but the parties would be ready to exploit it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Nicholas II saw autocracy as a religious duty inherited from his father; the regime&rsquo;s ideology was &lsquo;Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality&rsquo; and rested on the Church and the Okhrana.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Liberal opposition (Kadets, Octobrists) wanted a parliamentary monarchy; revolutionary opposition was split between the peasant-focused Socialist Revolutionaries and the Marxist Social Democrats.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">The 1903 split between Bolsheviks and Mensheviks divided the Marxist movement over how to organise &mdash; a small disciplined cadre versus a mass party &mdash; and would shape the revolutions of 1917.</li></ul>

For interpretation questions, recognise that a totalitarian-school historian (like Conquest) will read 1903 as the moment Lenin built the dictatorial party that would later become Stalin&rsquo;s; a revisionist will stress how small and divided the Bolsheviks really were before 1917. Use precise contextual knowledge &mdash; the 1903 vote in London, Malinovsky as an Okhrana agent &mdash; to test the interpretation.</p>
