=== L11 Collectivisation and the War on the Kulaks ===

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Forced grain seizures, dekulakisation and the catastrophic Ukrainian famine of 1932-33.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In 1928 Stalin abandoned the NEP. The Five Year Plans needed grain to feed the cities and to export for foreign currency, and the peasants were not delivering enough. Stalin&rsquo;s solution was to take farming out of peasant hands altogether. The result, between 1929 and 1933, was the most violent transformation of rural Europe in modern history: an estimated five to seven million dead, a famine that ravaged Ukraine and Kazakhstan, and the destruction of an entire way of life.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The &lsquo;Great Turn&rsquo; of 1928</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">By 1927 a &lsquo;grain procurement crisis&rsquo; meant the state was buying about 25 per cent less grain than the year before. Bukharin argued for higher prices and continued NEP. Stalin instead drove a hard line: peasants were hoarding grain, and the Five Year Plans could not wait. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Stalin&rsquo;s 1928 abandonment of the NEP and turn to forced collectivisation, rapid industrialisation and centralised planning.">Great Turn</dfn> of 1928 ended the NEP and committed the state to forced industrialisation paid for by squeezing the countryside.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">How Collectivisation Worked</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The forced merging of peasant farms into state-controlled collective farms (kolkhozes) and state farms (sovkhozes) from 1929 onwards.">Collectivisation</dfn> meant peasants surrendered their land, livestock and tools to a collective. Two structures dominated:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>The kolkhoz</strong>: a notionally cooperative collective farm. Peasants worked the land together; the state took a fixed quota first, then peasants were paid in kind from what remained. They kept tiny private plots.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>The sovkhoz</strong>: a state farm where peasants were paid wages like factory workers; less common but more directly state-run.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Tractors, ploughs and other machinery were concentrated in <dfn class="term" data-def="State-run depots that hired out tractors and other machinery to nearby collective farms and provided political control through their commissars.">Machine Tractor Stations</dfn> (MTS), which hired equipment to the kolkhozes for a fee paid in grain. Each MTS had a political department that watched the surrounding farms. The state could reach into rural Russia in a way the Tsars never had.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: define kolkhoz, sovkhoz and MTS in one sentence each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Collectivisation merged peasant farms into kolkhozes (cooperative collectives) and sovkhozes (state farms). Machine Tractor Stations supplied equipment and political surveillance, giving the state direct control of grain extraction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Dekulakisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In December 1929 Stalin called for &lsquo;the liquidation of the <dfn class="term" data-def="Better-off peasants in late Tsarist and Soviet Russia; the label was applied loosely during dekulakisation to anyone who resisted collectivisation.">kulaks</dfn> as a class&rsquo;. The kulak label, which had once meant a richer peasant employing labour, was now applied to almost anyone resisting collectivisation. A peasant with one extra cow, two horses, or a metal roof could be denounced. Roughly a fifth of dekulakised families were executed or sent to forced-labour colonies; another two thirds were deported to Siberia, the Urals or Kazakhstan. Over five million people were affected.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The label was politically constructed. Local activists faced quotas: each district had to find its kulaks. As one historian put it, the dekulakisation campaign was less a war on rich peasants than a quota-driven assault on the most successful tenth of the village.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Peasants resisted in ways open to the powerless. Many slaughtered their livestock rather than hand it over: between 1929 and 1933, USSR cattle stocks fell from about 70 million head to 38 million, sheep and goats from 147 million to 50 million, horses from 33 million to 16 million. Peasants buried grain, broke tools, or fled to the cities. Stalin briefly retreated in March 1930 with his article &lsquo;Dizzy with Success&rsquo;, blaming over-zealous local officials for &lsquo;excesses&rsquo; &mdash; but the policy resumed within months.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Famine of 1932-1933</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1932 sown area had collapsed and grain output was below 1928 levels &mdash; yet state procurement quotas were raised, partly to fund industrial imports. The collision produced famine. The hardest-hit areas were Ukraine, the North Caucasus and Kazakhstan. In Ukraine, where the famine is now called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Ukrainian famine of 1932-33, in which roughly four million Ukrainians died after Soviet grain quotas stripped the countryside.">Holodomor</dfn> (&lsquo;death by hunger&rsquo;), about four million people died. In Kazakhstan, around 1.5 million Kazakhs &mdash; nearly a quarter of the population &mdash; died as nomadic herding was destroyed and forced settlement disrupted the seasonal grazing on which Kazakh life depended.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Soviet authorities denied the famine externally and prevented peasants from leaving the affected regions through internal passport controls (introduced December 1932). The Soviet press carried no reports; foreign journalists in Moscow who tried to investigate, such as the Welshman Gareth Jones, were dismissed and discredited by colleagues. Survivors described eating bark, weeds, leather and, in extreme cases, human corpses. Whether Stalin deliberately engineered the Ukrainian famine to crush Ukrainian national identity, or whether it was a product of brutal policy without specific genocidal intent, remains debated. Many historians, the Ukrainian state and several governments now classify the Holodomor as genocide; Russian and some Western historians question the deliberate-targeting argument while accepting Stalin&rsquo;s responsibility for mass death.</p>

    <span>Was Collectivisation Successful?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">In political terms, yes &mdash; the state could now extract grain at will, fund industrial imports, and control the countryside. By 1936, around 90 per cent of peasants worked on collective farms. In agricultural terms, no &mdash; Soviet grain output did not regain 1928 levels for decades. Livestock numbers took even longer to recover. Examiner reports flag students who write &lsquo;collectivisation succeeded&rsquo; without distinguishing between political control (the regime won) and productivity (the regime lost).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the death tolls for the Holodomor and Kazakh famine, with one cause for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1932-33 famine killed roughly four million Ukrainians (the Holodomor) and around 1.5 million Kazakhs. Causes included raised grain quotas, peasant resistance through livestock slaughter, and internal passports introduced in December 1932 that prevented escape from famine zones.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Stalin&rsquo;s &lsquo;Great Turn&rsquo; of 1928 ended the NEP and committed the regime to forced collectivisation: kolkhozes, sovkhozes and Machine Tractor Stations replaced the peasant village.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Dekulakisation affected over five million people; the kulak label was applied loosely to anyone resisting, and a fifth of those targeted were executed or worked to death.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">The 1932-33 famine killed roughly four million Ukrainians (Holodomor) and 1.5 million Kazakhs; the regime won political control of the countryside but Soviet grain output did not regain 1928 levels for decades.</li></ul>
