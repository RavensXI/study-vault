--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Empire Built on Contradictions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Nicholas II became Tsar in 1894, he inherited the largest land empire on Earth &mdash; over 22 million square kilometres stretching from Poland to the Pacific. Roughly 125 million people lived under his rule, but only one in seven were ethnic Russians. The rest were Poles, Finns, Ukrainians, Jews, Muslims of Central Asia and dozens of other groups, often resentful of <dfn class="term" data-def="The forced imposition of Russian language, religion and culture on non-Russian peoples of the empire.">Russification</dfn> imposed from St Petersburg. Russia was not one society &mdash; it was several, held together by the army and the Orthodox Church.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how big was Russia's population in 1894, and what fraction were ethnic Russians?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1894 the Russian Empire covered roughly one-sixth of the world's land surface and held about 125 million people. Around 80% were peasants, most of them illiterate and tied to their village. Less than 4% lived in cities of over 100,000.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Witte and the Industrial Surge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Russia in 1894 was racing to catch up with the industrial powers of Western Europe. The man driving that race was Sergei Witte, Finance Minister from 1892 to 1903. Witte borrowed heavily from French banks, introduced the gold standard in 1897, and protected Russian industry behind high tariffs. His flagship project was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 9,000 km railway begun in 1891 linking Moscow to Vladivostok on the Pacific, designed to open Siberia to industry and trade.">Trans-Siberian Railway</dfn>, started in 1891 and largely complete by 1904.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The results were striking. Coal output in the Donbas tripled in the 1890s; oil production in Baku briefly overtook the United States; new steel mills rose in Yekaterinoslav. By 1914 Russia was the world&rsquo;s fourth largest industrial economy. But the surge was lopsided. Foreign investors owned almost half of Russian heavy industry, and growth concentrated in a handful of cities &mdash; St Petersburg, Moscow, Warsaw, Riga &mdash; leaving most of the empire untouched.</p>

Railway track doubled from about 30,000 km in 1890 to over 60,000 km by 1905. Witte funded this expansion partly through high indirect taxes on vodka, sugar and matches, which fell hardest on the peasantry. Critics inside the court called him &lsquo;the German&rsquo; for borrowing capitalist methods; supporters argued he was the only person preventing Russia from slipping behind Britain, France and Germany permanently.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Life in the New Cities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The factories of St Petersburg and Moscow drew hundreds of thousands of peasants off the land. By 1900 the Russian working class &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The industrial working class, especially factory workers without land or capital of their own.">proletariat</dfn> &mdash; numbered around three million. Their lives were brutal. A typical Putilov metalworker in St Petersburg worked an 11-hour day, six days a week, for wages well below those of a German or British equivalent. Workers slept in dormitories called <em>artel</em> houses, often eight or ten to a room, with shared bunks taken in shifts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Trade unions and strikes were illegal. The Okhrana watched factory floors for agitators. When workers did organise, employers responded with mass dismissals and the army with rifles &mdash; as students would soon see at Bloody Sunday in 1905. The conditions made urban Russia the natural recruiting ground for the revolutionary parties that would dominate the next twenty years.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Peasant Majority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Eighty per cent of Russians lived in villages, mostly in European Russia between the Volga and the western frontier. Although serfdom had been abolished in 1861, freedom came at a price. Peasants had to pay <dfn class="term" data-def="Annual instalments peasants had to pay the state for forty-nine years to buy back the land they had been freed onto in 1861.">redemption payments</dfn> for the land they had been freed onto, often for forty-nine years. Most land was held collectively by the village commune, the <em>mir</em>, which decided each year which strips each family farmed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The mir was deeply conservative. Strips were narrow and scattered to share good soil fairly, which made modern farming almost impossible. Population growth meant strips shrank with every generation. Famine in 1891&ndash;92 killed between 400,000 and half a million peasants. Many viewed the Tsar as the &lsquo;Little Father&rsquo; who would one day give them more land &mdash; a hope that the state cynically encouraged.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">At the top sat about 1% of the population: the nobility, who owned around a quarter of the land and dominated the army officer corps and the higher civil service. Below them, a small middle class &mdash; perhaps 1.5% &mdash; of factory owners, merchants, lawyers and doctors, often resentful that political power was closed to them. The urban proletariat was around 4% but growing fast. Beneath everyone else, the peasants &mdash; over 80% &mdash; whose labour fed the cities and whose grain exports paid for Witte's loans.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two reasons Russian peasants in 1894 were poorer than peasants in Germany or France.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Russian agriculture was uniquely backward in 1894 because of redemption payments, the mir&rsquo;s strip-farming system, and rapid population growth that shrank each family&rsquo;s share. Famines in 1891 and 1901 showed how thin the margin of survival was.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A Society Primed for Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Witte&rsquo;s industrial drive built a modern economy on top of a medieval social order. The same railway that carried Donbas coal to St Petersburg also brought Marxist pamphlets to factory dormitories. The same peasants whose grain paid for the loans starved when the harvest failed. By the time Nicholas II had been on the throne for ten years, Russia was producing both steel and revolutionaries faster than it could absorb either. Historians often describe pre-1905 Russia as a country with an early-twentieth-century economy and a seventeenth-century political system &mdash; a contradiction the next chapter would test to destruction.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Russia in 1894 was the largest land empire on Earth but extremely uneven &mdash; a small modern industrial sector in a few cities, surrounded by an enormous, impoverished peasant majority.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Witte&rsquo;s industrialisation made Russia the world&rsquo;s fourth largest industrial economy by 1914, but it relied on foreign loans, peasant taxes and brutal urban working conditions.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Redemption payments, the mir and famines kept the peasantry poor and resentful, while the new urban proletariat became fertile ground for revolutionary politics.</li></ul>
