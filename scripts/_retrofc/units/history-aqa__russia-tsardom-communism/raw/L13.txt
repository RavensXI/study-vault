--- DESCRIPTION ---
Show trials, the NKVD, the gulag, censorship and the propaganda image of Stalin as the great leader.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Between 1936 and 1938 Stalin systematically destroyed his own party, his army officer corps, and millions of ordinary citizens. The peak years of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1936-38 wave of mass arrests, executions and labour camp sentences in the USSR; also called the Great Purge or the Yezhovshchina.">Great Terror</dfn> &mdash; sometimes called the &lsquo;Yezhovshchina&rsquo; &mdash; saw roughly 750,000 people shot and over a million sent to forced-labour camps. Why this happened, and how much was Stalin&rsquo;s personal will, is one of the most contested questions in Soviet history.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Trigger: the Murder of Kirov</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">On 1 December 1934, Sergei Kirov, the popular Leningrad party chief, was shot dead at the Smolny Institute by a young dissident, Leonid Nikolaev. Stalin used the murder as proof that hidden enemies were active inside the party. The same day, a decree authorised summary trials and execution of suspected terrorists. Whether Stalin himself ordered the killing remains debated &mdash; the totalitarian school argues yes, revisionists say a deranged loner is sufficient. Either way, Kirov&rsquo;s death triggered the wave of arrests that culminated in the Great Terror.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Show Trials of the Old Bolsheviks, 1936-1938</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Between August 1936 and March 1938, three great Moscow <dfn class="term" data-def="Public trials of leading Bolsheviks in Moscow 1936-38, with rehearsed confessions used to legitimise the Great Terror.">show trials</dfn> destroyed the surviving leadership of the October Revolution.</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>August 1936</strong> &mdash; the &lsquo;Trial of the Sixteen&rsquo;: Zinoviev, Kamenev and 14 others confessed to plotting with Trotsky to murder Stalin and Kirov. All were shot.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>January 1937</strong> &mdash; the &lsquo;Trial of the Seventeen&rsquo;: Karl Radek and Yuri Pyatakov among them. Most shot.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>March 1938</strong> &mdash; the &lsquo;Trial of the Twenty-One&rsquo;: Bukharin, the leading Bolshevik intellectual and former defender of the NEP, confessed and was executed. So was Yagoda, the former NKVD chief.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Confessions were extracted by sleep deprivation, threats to families and prolonged interrogation. The trials were a public theatre: foreign journalists were allowed in to watch defendants admit to fantastic crimes. Western observers split &mdash; some accepted the confessions, others (including the philosopher John Dewey&rsquo;s commission) denounced them as fabrications. Modern historians treat the confessions as forced.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three Moscow show trials, who was the lead defendant in each, and what each trial signalled.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three Moscow show trials destroyed the Old Bolshevik leadership: the Trial of the Sixteen (Aug 1936, Zinoviev and Kamenev shot), the Trial of the Seventeen (Jan 1937), and the Trial of the Twenty-One (Mar 1938, Bukharin shot). Confessions were extracted by torture and threats to families.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Purge of the Red Army</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In June 1937, Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky and seven other senior generals were tried in secret and shot, accused of plotting with Nazi Germany. The purge then ran through the officer corps. Of the Red Army&rsquo;s eight marshals, five were shot. Of 80 senior army commanders, 75 were executed or imprisoned. Around 35,000 officers in total &mdash; perhaps a third of the corps &mdash; were removed. The damage was visible four years later: the early disasters of Operation Barbarossa in 1941 partly reflected an army shorn of its experienced leadership.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The NKVD, Yezhov and the Gulag</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The institutional driver was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Soviet secret police of the 1930s and 1940s, which ran the gulag system and carried out the Great Terror under Yagoda, Yezhov and Beria.">NKVD</dfn> (the People&rsquo;s Commissariat for Internal Affairs), heir to the Cheka. From September 1936 it was led by Nikolai Yezhov &mdash; the &lsquo;Yezhovshchina&rsquo; takes its name from him. Yezhov set quotas for arrests by region: each NKVD office had a target number of &lsquo;enemies&rsquo; to find. Quotas drove denunciations as ordinary Soviets accused colleagues, neighbours and rivals to deflect attention from themselves.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Most arrested were sent to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Soviet network of forced-labour camps, run by the NKVD; expanded enormously during the Great Terror.">gulag</dfn>: a network of forced-labour camps stretching from Solovki to Kolyma in the Soviet Far East. Conditions were brutal &mdash; mining gold or felling timber on starvation rations in Arctic temperatures. Estimates of camp deaths in the late 1930s vary; perhaps a million died of cold, hunger and overwork. By 1939 the gulag held around 1.6 million prisoners and was a significant economic asset, building canals, railways and mines.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Yezhov himself was arrested in November 1938 and shot in 1940. Lavrenti Beria replaced him and wound the Terror down somewhat, though arrests and shootings continued. The pattern &mdash; Stalin discarding his own enforcers when they became too associated with the violence &mdash; would repeat.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">The Terror was not limited to old revolutionaries. Mass operations targeted entire categories: ex-kulaks deported in 1929-30, Polish residents of the USSR (around 110,000 shot in the &lsquo;Polish Operation&rsquo; of 1937-38), Germans, Latvians and other minorities, plus engineers, priests and teachers. Roughly 750,000 people were executed in 1936-38, with another million-plus sent to camps. Examiner reports flag students who write &lsquo;only old Bolsheviks were purged&rsquo; &mdash; this is wrong and loses marks.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Censorship, Socialist Realism and the Cult of Personality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A controlled culture sat alongside the violence. <dfn class="term" data-def="The official Soviet artistic style from the 1930s, requiring art to depict heroic workers, leaders and a glorious socialist future in accessible realist form.">Socialist Realism</dfn> became the only permitted style in 1934: novels, paintings, films and music had to portray heroic workers, wise leaders and a glorious socialist future. Modernist and experimental art was condemned as &lsquo;formalist&rsquo;. Photographs were retouched to remove disgraced figures &mdash; Trotsky was airbrushed out of pictures of Lenin&rsquo;s funeral; Yezhov was airbrushed out alongside Stalin once he too was purged. School textbooks rewrote the history of the revolution to put Stalin alongside Lenin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The cult of personality reached its peak. Stalin&rsquo;s portraits hung in every classroom, factory and kolkhoz. Streets, cities and a tank model bore his name. The 1936 Constitution proclaimed the most democratic political system in the world &mdash; a paper document, since real power lay with Stalin and the Politburo. The cult was not just imposed: ordinary Soviets, including many who lost relatives in the Terror, often genuinely believed Stalin was being deceived by NKVD officials acting on their own authority.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Why? The Historiography</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Why did the Terror happen? The two main schools clash sharply.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Conquest vs Fitzpatrick and Getty</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">The <strong>totalitarian school</strong>, led by Robert Conquest in *The Great Terror* (1968), argues the Purges were driven top-down by Stalin&rsquo;s personal will to remove rivals and consolidate power. The Terror was deliberate, planned, and centrally directed. <strong>Revisionists</strong>, led by J. Arch Getty (*Origins of the Great Purges*, 1985) and Sheila Fitzpatrick, argue the Terror grew partly out of bureaucratic chaos, denunciations from below, and conflict between Moscow and the regions. Stalin set the tone, but quotas and local activists shaped the scale. Both schools agree Stalin was responsible &mdash; they disagree on whether the violence was a centrally controlled machine or a chain reaction Stalin lost control of and only later contained. Top-band 8-mark Evaluate an Interpretation answers can name these schools and use them to test which view a given source is drawing on.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and write a one-sentence summary of the totalitarian view and the revisionist view of the Terror.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The totalitarian school (Conquest) argues the Terror was driven top-down by Stalin&rsquo;s personal will. Revisionists (Fitzpatrick, Getty) argue it grew partly from quota culture, denunciations from below and bureaucratic chaos &mdash; though Stalin set the political climate that allowed it.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">The murder of Kirov in December 1934 triggered the Great Terror; three Moscow show trials (1936, 1937, 1938) destroyed the Old Bolshevik leadership including Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">The Red Army was decapitated in 1937 (Tukhachevsky and the senior officer corps), with consequences for Soviet performance in 1941; the NKVD under Yezhov ran the gulag and shot roughly 750,000 people in 1936-38.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Socialist Realism, censorship and the cult of personality controlled culture; historiography splits between Conquest&rsquo;s totalitarian reading and the revisionist account from Fitzpatrick and Getty.</li></ul>
