--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sole traders, partnerships, Ltds, Plcs and not-for-profits — and what limited liability really means.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Legal Structure Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before a business trades, its owners have to decide what kind of organisation it will be. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal form a business takes, such as sole trader, partnership, private limited company or public limited company.">legal structure</dfn> chosen affects how much control the owners keep, how profits are shared, where the business can raise money, and crucially, what happens if things go wrong. A self-employed dog walker and Tesco PLC are both businesses, but they operate under very different rules. Choosing the wrong structure can cripple a good idea — so this decision is one of the first serious tests for any entrepreneur.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Sole Traders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A business owned and run by one person who keeps all the profit and has unlimited liability.">sole trader</dfn> is a business with one owner. Think of a local plumber, a market-stall holder or a personal trainer. Setting up is simple: register with HMRC and start trading. The owner keeps all the profit and makes every decision. The downside is the same detail that makes it attractive — the owner is the business in the eyes of the law. They face <dfn class="term" data-def="The owner of a business is personally responsible for all the business's debts — their own assets can be taken to pay them.">unlimited liability</dfn>, meaning if the business owes money it cannot repay, creditors can come after the owner’s house, car and savings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write two advantages and two disadvantages of being a sole trader from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sole traders keep all the profit and have full control, but they have unlimited liability and limited ways to raise finance. Most UK businesses are sole traders — simple to set up, but risky if the business fails.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Partnerships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A business owned by 2–20 partners who share responsibility, profit and unlimited liability.">partnership</dfn> has between 2 and 20 owners who share the workload, combine their skills and pool capital. A high-street solicitor, a local accountancy firm and most GP surgeries run as partnerships. Decisions are shared, profits are split according to a partnership agreement, and more finance can be raised than by one individual. The drawback is that partners still have unlimited liability — and one partner’s mistake can land the others with the bill. Disagreements between partners also slow decisions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Private Limited Companies (Ltd)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A company whose shares are sold privately — usually to family, friends or invited investors — and are not traded on the stock exchange.">private limited company</dfn> (Ltd) is a separate legal entity owned by shareholders. Shares can be sold to family, friends or invited investors but not to the general public. Most UK companies with ‘Ltd’ at the end — Greggs was one before it floated, Dyson still is — fall into this category. The crucial advantage is <dfn class="term" data-def="Shareholders can only lose the amount they invested in the company — their personal assets are protected.">limited liability</dfn>. If the company goes bust, shareholders can only lose the money they put in; their personal assets are safe. In return, Ltds must register at Companies House and publish annual accounts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Public Limited Companies (Plc)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A company whose shares are traded on the stock exchange and can be bought by any member of the public.">public limited company</dfn> (Plc) can sell shares to the general public through the stock exchange. Tesco PLC, BT Group PLC and JD Sports Fashion PLC are household examples. Going public opens up huge sources of finance — millions of pounds can be raised in a single share issue — and raises brand profile. The catch: ownership is diluted, takeovers become possible, and every result is scrutinised by shareholders and the press. Plcs also face strict reporting and must publish detailed annual reports.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Both Ltds and Plcs have limited liability and are separate legal entities. The key difference is how they raise money. An Ltd can only sell shares to people it invites; a Plc can sell to anyone through the London Stock Exchange. That changes the scale of finance available — and the amount of outside control the owners must accept. When Aston Martin floated as a Plc in 2018, the founders accepted public scrutiny of every quarterly result in return for the cash to expand globally.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The decision to move from Ltd to Plc is called a <strong>flotation</strong> or Initial Public Offering (IPO). Many UK family businesses stay as Ltds precisely because they want to keep control inside the family. Going public is almost impossible to undo cheaply.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Not-for-Profit Organisations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Some organisations exist to serve a social or charitable purpose rather than to make profit for owners. Charities like Oxfam and the British Heart Foundation are <dfn class="term" data-def="An organisation that trades with a social aim — any surplus is reinvested rather than paid to owners.">not-for-profit organisations</dfn>. They still have to cover costs, pay staff and manage cash — but any surplus is reinvested into the cause rather than paid out to shareholders. Social enterprises like The Big Issue operate the same way: they sell a product to fund a mission.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence what limited liability actually limits — and what it does not.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Limited liability protects the <em>owners’</em> personal assets — it does not protect the business itself. A company can still go bust; limited liability simply caps how much each shareholder can personally lose at the amount they invested.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">A lone builder just starting out will usually begin as a sole trader — cheap, simple, no reporting. Two dental graduates opening a practice together are suited to a partnership — shared skills, shared finance. A growing family catering business taking on larger contracts will often become a private limited company to protect the owners’ homes from supplier claims. A national retailer needing hundreds of millions to open stores will eventually list as a Plc. The right structure depends on the size of the business, the risk it faces, and how much outside finance it needs.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Sole traders and partnerships are simple to set up but carry unlimited liability — owners’ personal assets are at risk if the business fails.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Private limited companies (Ltd) and public limited companies (Plc) give shareholders limited liability — they can only lose what they invested.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">The right legal structure depends on the size of the business, how much finance is needed and how much risk the owners are willing to personally take.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">Students lose marks on this topic by saying ‘the business has limited liability’. It doesn’t — the <em>shareholders</em> do. Write ‘the owners are only liable for what they invested’ instead. For 9-mark questions asking you to recommend a structure, always tie your choice to the specific business: a street-food van does not need Plc-level finance, and a multinational cannot run as a sole trader. Name the feature of the business (size, finance need, risk) that drives your choice.</p>
