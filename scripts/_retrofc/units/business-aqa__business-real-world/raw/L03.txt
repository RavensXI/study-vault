--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why businesses set objectives, how they change over time, and why success isn't just about profit.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Aims vs Objectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The long-term, general goal a business is trying to achieve, such as 'becoming the UK's most ethical coffee chain'.">aim</dfn> is the big-picture destination — where the business wants to end up. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific, measurable target that helps a business achieve its aim — often given a time limit.">objective</dfn> is a specific, measurable target that moves the business closer to the aim. If Costa Coffee’s aim is to be the UK’s leading coffee chain, one of its objectives might be to open 50 new stores this year. Aims tell you the destination; objectives tell you the route. Good objectives are usually <strong>SMART</strong> — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Main Objectives Businesses Set</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The specification lists seven common objectives. The first, and often the only one for a brand-new business, is <dfn class="term" data-def="The objective of a business to keep trading and avoid going bust, often the main aim during a business's first year.">survival</dfn> — just keeping the lights on through the fragile early months. Once past that, most businesses aim for profit maximisation, making the largest possible profit from their activities. Growth, measured by sales or by opening new sites, is another common goal — Greggs has grown from a Newcastle bakery to over 2,500 UK shops.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of total sales in a market held by one business, usually expressed as a percentage.">Market share</dfn> targets focus on taking sales from rivals. Customer satisfaction targets — measured through surveys, reviews and repeat purchase rates — help businesses like John Lewis justify higher prices. Social and ethical objectives focus on doing the right thing: Tony’s Chocolonely exists to make chocolate slave-free. Shareholder value — the return owners receive through dividends and a rising share price — matters most for Plcs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list all seven business objectives from memory, with a UK business example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The main objectives businesses set are: survival, profit maximisation, growth, market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives, and shareholder value. Different objectives suit different stages and different types of business.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Why Set Objectives at All?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Objectives give a business direction. Without them, staff pull in different directions and decisions are made reactively rather than strategically. Clear objectives <strong>motivate</strong> workers — a target to open five new stores by December gives everyone something concrete to aim at. They provide a <strong>yardstick</strong> for measuring success: did the business hit a 15% market share or not? They support <strong>planning</strong> because without a target, you cannot decide how much money, staff or stock you need. Finally, they help a business <strong>secure finance</strong> — banks and investors want evidence the business knows where it is going before they lend. Without clear objectives, a business also struggles to prioritise: when cash is tight, which project gets cut first? Objectives answer that question before the pressure hits.</p>

A not-for-profit like Oxfam drops profit altogether and sets social objectives (e.g. number of clean-water projects delivered). The level of competition also matters — in a crowded market like streaming, Netflix must fight to hold market share; in a niche market, customer satisfaction may be enough.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Why Objectives Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Objectives evolve with the business. In its first year, a street-food start-up like a new burger van focuses on survival — cover costs, build a regular customer base. As it settles, it may target profit maximisation. If a second van follows, the focus shifts to growth. An established business like Tesco now talks heavily about sustainability and reducing plastic — ethical objectives rise up the agenda once the basics are secure. Objectives can also change in response to external events: during the 2023 cost-of-living squeeze, many retailers quietly dropped ambitious growth targets and returned to survival and customer retention.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Measuring Success Beyond Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Profit is the easiest way to judge a business, but it is not the only one. Rising customer satisfaction scores, a growing workforce, improved environmental performance, or bigger community donations can all mark success. Lush cosmetics is famously profitable but also measures itself on animal-welfare standards and packaging reduction. Tony’s Chocolonely measures success partly by traceable cocoa beans. For a charity like the British Heart Foundation, success is the number of research grants funded, not profit. Even shareholder-driven firms increasingly publish <strong>non-financial</strong> metrics — Unilever reports on its Sustainable Living brands and John Lewis on its staff-partner share of profit. These measures matter because customers, investors and potential employees now look beyond the bottom line when choosing who to buy from, invest in, or work for. A business that scores highly on ethical and customer metrics often delivers stronger long-term profit too — satisfied customers return, and motivated staff stay.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why a one-year-old start-up will usually prioritise survival over profit maximisation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A business’s objectives typically change as it grows — from survival in year one, to profit and growth as it stabilises, and to ethical, shareholder or international objectives once it is established. The same business may hold several objectives at once.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Yes — and this is where strong evaluation answers come from. Profit maximisation pushes businesses to cut costs; social objectives push them to pay fair wages or use ethical suppliers. Growth can damage customer satisfaction if service quality drops. Shareholder value (short-term dividends) can undermine long-term investment. A good answer names the tension and weighs it — e.g. ‘aggressive growth might hit short-term profit because of the cost of new stores, but in the long term it locks in market share that protects future profit.’</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Objectives are SMART targets that turn a business’s aim into a measurable plan — they motivate staff, guide planning and help secure finance.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The main objectives are survival, profit maximisation, growth, market share, customer satisfaction, social and ethical objectives, and shareholder value.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Objectives change over a business’s life — and success can be measured in more ways than profit, especially for established firms and not-for-profit organisations.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When questions ask you to recommend objectives, match the objective to the stage and type of business in the case study. A brand-new cafe does not need shareholder value as its main objective; a Plc does not need survival. Watch for the word ‘most important’ in 12-mark statements — your conclusion must introduce a new weighting, not just repeat points already made. And avoid the common trap of writing about objectives in the abstract — always anchor your answer to the named business.</p>
