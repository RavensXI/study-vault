Why people start businesses, factors of production, opportunity cost and what drives an entrepreneur.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Business For?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An organisation that produces goods or provides services to satisfy customer needs and wants, usually to make a profit.">business</dfn> is any organisation that produces goods or provides services for customers. Businesses exist to satisfy needs and wants — the things people must have (food, shelter, clothing) and the things people choose to buy (a Greggs sausage roll, a Spotify subscription, a BrewDog beer). Some businesses aim to make a profit for their owners; others, like the British Heart Foundation, exist to serve a cause. In every case the core idea is the same: spot a gap, supply something people will pay for, and do it better or cheaper than the alternatives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">It helps to separate <dfn class="term" data-def="Physical products that can be touched, such as a phone or a sandwich.">goods</dfn> from <dfn class="term" data-def="Actions done for customers, such as a haircut or a bus journey.">services</dfn>. Goods are physical — a Tesco ready meal, a pair of Nike trainers. Services are actions performed for customers — a Deliveroo drop-off, a Vets4Pets consultation. Most modern businesses sell a blend of both: a Wetherspoons pub sells burgers (goods) alongside table service and an atmosphere (service).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write a Tesco example of a good, a service, a need and a want from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Businesses supply goods and services to meet needs (essentials) and wants (preferences). The more clearly a business identifies a gap between what customers need or want and what is currently available, the stronger its chance of survival.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Four Factors of Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">To produce anything, a business combines four <dfn class="term" data-def="The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and enterprise.">factors of production</dfn>. <strong>Land</strong> covers all natural resources — the site of a Greggs bakery, the wheat in its flour, the water in its drinks. <strong>Labour</strong> is the human effort involved, from shop floor staff to the head baker. <strong>Capital</strong> means the man-made resources used to produce other goods — ovens, delivery vans, tills. <strong>Enterprise</strong> is the risk-taking skill that pulls the other three together — the entrepreneur who decides to open a branch in a new town and raises the money to do it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Opportunity Cost</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.">Opportunity cost</dfn> is the value of what you give up whenever you make a decision. If Innocent Drinks spends £500,000 on a new factory oven, that same £500,000 cannot be spent on a marketing campaign. The opportunity cost of the oven is the marketing that was sacrificed. Every business decision has one — even your own time, because revising one subject means not revising another.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Three Sectors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">UK businesses sit in one of three sectors. The <strong>primary</strong> sector extracts raw materials — think farming, fishing, mining. The <strong>secondary</strong> sector turns those materials into finished products — a Jaguar Land Rover factory assembling cars. The <strong>tertiary</strong> sector provides services — Lloyds Bank, Vodafone, the NHS. Most jobs in the UK are in the tertiary sector because our economy is service-led.</p>

Understanding the shift matters because where a business sits affects how it competes: a primary-sector dairy farmer faces supermarket price pressure, while a tertiary tech firm competes on ideas and speed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">What Makes an Entrepreneur?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who takes the financial risk of starting and running a business.">entrepreneur</dfn> is the person who takes the financial risk of starting a business. They spot a gap in the market, gather the factors of production and take the personal hit if the idea fails. Characteristics that recur in successful entrepreneurs include being hard working, innovative, well organised and willing to take a calculated risk. James Watt-Smith (Gousto), Sophie Trelawney (Trinny London) and James Watt (BrewDog) all left comfortable jobs because they backed their idea.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Their motives vary. Many want to be their own boss and control their own hours. Others are chasing a passion — turning a hobby into a living. Some spot a gap in the market that no-one else has filled. Some are simply dissatisfied with their current job and want to earn more. Most combine several of these reasons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four factors of production with a real UK business example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every business combines land, labour, capital and enterprise. Remove any one and production stops — a bakery without ovens (capital) or without bakers (labour) cannot make bread, no matter how good the recipe.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Dynamic Business Environment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Businesses face a constantly shifting environment. <strong>Technology</strong> creates new opportunities (contactless payments) and kills old ones (HMV lost out to streaming). <strong>The economy</strong> expands and contracts — rising interest rates squeezed customer spending in 2023. <strong>Legislation</strong> changes what firms can do, from minimum wage rises to plastic-bag charges. <strong>Environmental expectations</strong> now push businesses to cut waste and emissions. Firms that adapt quickly survive; those that don’t, like Thomas Cook or Debenhams, disappear.</p>

When an entrepreneur takes a redundancy payout and invests it in stock rather than putting it in a savings account, the opportunity cost is the interest they would have earned. Strong answers in this topic always name the specific alternative that was given up — ‘the money could have been used for X’ — rather than saying ‘there was an opportunity cost’.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Businesses exist to supply goods and services that meet customer needs and wants, combining land, labour, capital and enterprise to do so.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative given up — every business decision has one, and strong answers name the alternative clearly.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Entrepreneurs take the financial risk of turning an idea into a business, motivated by independence, passion, market gaps and money — and they operate in a dynamic environment shaped by technology, the economy, legislation and environmental expectations.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">For ‘Analyse’ questions on why someone starts a business, use a chain of reasoning: state the motive (e.g. wanting flexible hours), explain the consequence for the owner (control over childcare), then link it to a business outcome (higher commitment, stronger brand). For calculation questions involving opportunity cost, always name the alternative that was sacrificed — a vague ‘they lose out’ will not score. Do not confuse the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors with the size of a business — a tiny artisan bakery is still in the secondary sector.</p>
