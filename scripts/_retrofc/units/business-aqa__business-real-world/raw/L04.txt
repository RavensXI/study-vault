--- DESCRIPTION ---
Who has a stake in a business, what each group wants, and how stakeholder conflicts are resolved.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Are Stakeholders?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Any individual or group that is affected by, or has an interest in, the activities of a business.">stakeholder</dfn> is anyone who is affected by or has an interest in what a business does. When a new Greggs opens in a town, the impact spreads far wider than the bakery itself — owners expect profit, staff gain jobs, customers get sausage rolls, rival coffee shops lose trade, suppliers win a new contract, and the local community gets a bit more footfall. Each of these groups has a different ‘stake’ in the decision. Understanding stakeholders matters because every big business choice helps some and hurts others — and those who are hurt will push back.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Five Main Stakeholder Groups</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The specification focuses on five groups. <strong>Owners</strong> (sole traders, partners or shareholders) invest their money and time. They want profits, dividends and a rising share value. <strong>Employees</strong> depend on the business for their pay and security. <strong>Customers</strong> want good value products and reliable service. <strong>Suppliers</strong> want to be paid on time and receive repeat orders. The <strong>local community</strong> wants jobs and investment, but objects to noise, pollution or traffic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name all five stakeholder groups from memory, writing one objective each group typically has.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five main stakeholder groups are owners, employees, customers, suppliers and the local community. Each has different, often competing, objectives — the business must balance them to succeed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">What Each Stakeholder Wants</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Owners want <dfn class="term" data-def="A share of profit paid to shareholders, usually paid twice a year.">dividends</dfn> and a rising share price. Employees want high wages, job security, safe working conditions and the chance to develop. Customers want quality products at fair prices, honest advertising and good post-sales service. Suppliers want prompt payment and long-term contracts so they can plan their own business. The local community wants employment opportunities and minimal environmental impact — if Amazon opens a new warehouse, locals want the jobs but not the lorries clogging the road at 4am.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Some revision texts add <strong>the government</strong> (which wants tax revenue and employment) and <strong>pressure groups</strong> (which want specific changes, such as plastic-free packaging). The assessed five remain the core.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Impact of Business Activity on Stakeholders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Every business decision radiates out to different stakeholders. When Morrisons cuts 150 middle-management jobs, shareholders may cheer the cost savings while the affected employees lose their income and morale drops across the remaining workforce. When a new Aldi opens in a town, customers gain lower prices, but independent grocers nearby may lose enough trade to close. When Tesla opens a Gigafactory, the local community gets thousands of jobs, but also extra traffic and pressure on local housing. Even an apparently positive decision — raising staff wages, for example — can be divisive: employees welcome the rise, owners see profits dip, and customers face higher prices as costs are passed on. Good managers ask, before every major choice, <em>‘who benefits, who pays, and how loudly will the losers object?’</em> A decision that ignores this question rarely survives contact with reality.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Imagine a delivery firm decides to switch to 24-hour operations. Owners benefit — higher revenue and better use of vans. Customers benefit — overnight deliveries. Employees split: some welcome the night-shift premium, others hate the hours. Suppliers near the depot must also work later. The local community loses — sleep-disturbing lorry noise at 3am. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where different stakeholder groups want opposing things from a business decision.">stakeholder conflict</dfn>: one decision, multiple opposing reactions. The business cannot please everyone, so it must judge which group matters most to its success.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">How Stakeholders Influence Businesses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Stakeholders have levers they can pull. <strong>Owners and shareholders</strong> can vote at annual general meetings, replace directors or refuse further investment. <strong>Employees</strong> can work harder, strike, quit, or go slow — poor treatment kills productivity. <strong>Customers</strong> vote with their wallets: when Boohoo was linked to underpaid factory workers in Leicester, sales fell and the share price halved. <strong>Suppliers</strong> can demand upfront payment or refuse to extend credit when a firm becomes risky. The <strong>local community</strong> can block planning applications or run a campaign through the local media.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Different stakeholders carry different weight. A Tesco Plc decision is shaped heavily by big shareholders, while a village cafe lives or dies on the goodwill of its local community. This is why good 9-mark and 12-mark answers always ask: <em>which</em> stakeholder has the most influence in <em>this</em> context? The answer shifts over time too: a start-up depends heavily on its first customers and early investors; a mature Plc answers to thousands of shareholders and City analysts. The shift from private to public ownership often means managers now spend more time managing investor expectations than they do serving customers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why stakeholder objectives often conflict — using a real decision such as opening a 24-hour supermarket.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stakeholder objectives often conflict: owners want dividends, employees want higher pay, customers want lower prices, and the local community wants minimal disruption. A business cannot maximise all at once — managing the trade-offs is a core leadership skill.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Businesses resolve conflicts through trade-offs and communication. A factory can invest in quieter machinery to reduce community complaints while preserving production. A supermarket can introduce self-checkouts (cuts costs, helps shareholders) but redeploy staff into personal shopping roles rather than making them redundant. Clear communication — explaining a decision honestly — often softens the blow even when the decision itself is unpopular. The businesses that survive conflict best are those that treat stakeholders as partners rather than obstacles.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Stakeholders are individuals or groups affected by a business — the five main groups are owners, employees, customers, suppliers and the local community.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Stakeholders have different and often conflicting objectives — strong answers identify the conflict and explain the trade-off the business must make.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Different stakeholders have different amounts of influence depending on the type and size of the business — customers dominate for small firms, shareholders dominate for Plcs.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For ‘Analyse’ questions on stakeholders, pick one group and develop the chain of reasoning rather than listing all five superficially. A good answer reads ‘Employees are stakeholders because their pay depends on the business — if the business cuts jobs, employees lose income, which reduces their spending power and damages staff morale among those who remain.’ On evaluation questions about whose interests come first, avoid saying ‘the owners always matter most’ — context decides. For a co-operative like John Lewis, employees are the owners.</p>
