Business plans, fixed and variable costs, growth methods, and economies and diseconomies of scale.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Plan?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A written document setting out a business's aims, target market, products, finances and how it will operate.">business plan</dfn> is a written document that sets out what a business will do, who its customers are, and how it expects to make money. It forces entrepreneurs to think before they spend — to answer hard questions about whether an idea actually makes financial sense. Banks, investors and landlords want to see a plan before they commit. Even an established firm updates its plan when it launches a new product. The discipline of writing the plan often matters more than the document itself: it exposes flawed assumptions early, while they are cheap to fix.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What Goes in a Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Typical sections include the business idea and aims, the target market, a marketing plan, an operations plan, details of management and staff, and financial forecasts — including costs, revenue and a cash-flow forecast. Plans aren’t just about raising finance. They help set objectives, guide decisions and track progress. The drawbacks: plans take time to write, are based on predictions that may be wrong, and can give false confidence if markets move unexpectedly (as many did during Covid).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three benefits and two drawbacks of writing a business plan from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Business plans help secure finance, set objectives, guide decisions and reduce risk — but they cost time to write, rely on forecasts that can go wrong, and risk going out of date quickly. Note you are not expected to write a plan in the exam — only to explain its purpose.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Financial Terms You Must Know</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that do not change with the level of output — rent, salaries, insurance.">Fixed costs</dfn> do not change when output changes. Rent on a bakery unit is the same whether you bake 100 loaves or 1,000. <dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that change directly with the level of output — raw materials, packaging.">Variable costs</dfn> change with output — double the loaves and you double the flour. <dfn class="term" data-def="Fixed costs plus variable costs — the total expense of running a business.">Total costs</dfn> equal fixed plus variable costs. <dfn class="term" data-def="The money a business earns from selling its goods or services — price multiplied by quantity sold.">Revenue</dfn> is price multiplied by quantity sold. Profit is revenue minus total costs; if costs exceed revenue, the business makes a loss.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A quick worked example: a bakery sells 1,000 loaves at £2 each. Revenue = £2,000. Variable costs are 60p per loaf (= £600). Fixed costs are £900 per month. Total costs = £1,500. Profit = £500. Increase output to 1,500 loaves and variable costs rise to £900, but fixed costs stay at £900 — spreading the fixed cost over more loaves lowers the average cost per loaf.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Methods of Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Once a business is stable, owners often want to grow. There are two broad paths. <dfn class="term" data-def="Growth funded internally — opening new stores, expanding e-commerce or hiring more staff — without merging with another firm.">Organic growth</dfn> means growing under your own steam — opening new stores, expanding online, outsourcing non-core tasks, or signing up franchisees. Greggs has grown organically from a single Newcastle shop to over 2,500 UK sites. <dfn class="term" data-def="External growth — combining with or buying another business. Mergers join two firms as equals; takeovers buy the target outright.">External growth</dfn> means combining with another business — a <strong>merger</strong> joins two firms as equals, while a <strong>takeover</strong> sees one firm buy another outright. Morrisons’ 2005 takeover of Safeway instantly doubled its store network.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Franchising is a fast route to organic growth: the franchisor (e.g. Subway) lets a franchisee open a branded outlet in return for set-up fees and a share of revenue. The franchisee puts in the capital, the franchisor keeps tight control over the brand. Many McDonald’s in the UK are franchised. E-commerce has opened up another route — ASOS grew globally without opening any shops at all, using the internet to reach customers in more than 200 countries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Economies of Scale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The cost advantages a larger business gains, lowering the average cost of producing each unit.">Economies of scale</dfn> happen when growing output reduces the average cost per unit. Two main types appear in the specification. <strong>Purchasing economies</strong> — a large supermarket like Tesco buys milk in such huge volumes that suppliers give it a discount. <strong>Technical economies</strong> — a large factory can afford specialist machinery that a small workshop cannot. Both push down the average cost of making each product, which allows the business to cut prices, raise profit margins, or invest the difference back into the firm.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Diseconomies of Scale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The cost disadvantages a business faces when it grows too large — poor communication, coordination problems and reduced staff motivation drive up average unit costs.">Diseconomies of scale</dfn> happen when growing further starts to raise average costs rather than lower them. The usual causes: <strong>poor communication</strong> (head office and shop floor lose touch), <strong>coordination issues</strong> (too many layers of management, slow decisions) and <strong>reduced staff motivation</strong> (workers feel like a number in a huge workforce). Many retailers — including parts of Marks & Spencer in the 2010s — hit diseconomies once they expanded too quickly. A strong answer recognises that growth and efficiency are not automatic: scale can hurt as well as help.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the average unit cost formula from memory, plus one example of a purchasing economy and one example of a diseconomy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Average unit cost = total costs ÷ units produced. Economies of scale push this figure down; diseconomies push it up. Growth is only valuable if the average unit cost keeps falling — otherwise the extra sales cost too much to make.</p>
</div>

    <span>Worked Example: Average Unit Cost</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A small bakery has total costs of £1,500 and produces 1,000 loaves. Average unit cost = 1,500 ÷ 1,000 = £1.50 per loaf. If output rises to 2,000 loaves and total costs rise only to £2,400 (thanks to bulk flour discounts), average unit cost falls to £2,400 ÷ 2,000 = £1.20 per loaf. That 30p saving per loaf is the economy of scale. If the bakery grows further and the owner can no longer oversee quality, wastage rises, total costs climb to £4,500 for 3,000 loaves, and average unit cost rises to £1.50 again — a diseconomy of scale.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Business plans help secure finance, set objectives and reduce risk — their weakness is that they rely on forecasts that can quickly go out of date.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Organic growth (opening new sites, franchising, e-commerce) is slower but safer; external growth through mergers and takeovers is faster but riskier.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Economies of scale drive down average unit costs as output grows; diseconomies push them up when communication, coordination and motivation break down at large size.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Calculation questions on this topic (average unit cost, total cost, profit) are frequent — always show working, include units (£, %, per loaf) and double-check that the numbers make sense. Students often forget diseconomies of scale exist — don’t assume growth is always good. In 12-mark ‘Evaluate’ questions on expansion, a strong conclusion weighs the cost advantage of economies of scale against the risk of diseconomies and the communication problems that come with size.</p>
