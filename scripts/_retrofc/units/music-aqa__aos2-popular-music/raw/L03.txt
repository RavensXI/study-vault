Questions can ask about any of them, so know all three equally. The band: Freddie Mercury (lead vocals, piano), Brian May (electric guitar, vocals), John Deacon (bass guitar) and Roger Taylor (drum kit, vocals).</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="The set recordings"><h2>The set recordings</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="q2" data-revision-tip="Cover the box, then write from memory all three set recordings: Bohemian Rhapsody, Seven Seas of Rhye, Love of My Life."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact &mdash; The Set Recordings</div><p><em>Bohemian Rhapsody</em> and <em>Love of My Life</em> come from <em>A Night at the Opera</em> (1975). <em>Seven Seas of Rhye</em> comes from <em>Queen II</em> (1974). The set versions are the 2011 remastered recordings.</p></div></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Six sections" data-track="t1" data-chapters="t1c1,t1c2,t1c3,t1c4,t1c5,t1c6"><h2>Bohemian Rhapsody: six sections, no chorus</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q3">There is no repeating chorus at all &mdash; the song is <dfn class="term" data-def="Music that keeps moving forward with no repeated sections.">through-composed</dfn>, which was very unusual for a 1970s rock single. There are four contrasting sections plus a coda. It opens <dfn class="term" data-def="Voices alone, without instruments.">a cappella</dfn><button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t1">1</button>, in <dfn class="term" data-def="Every voice moving in the same rhythm at the same time.">homorhythmic</dfn> block chords. A piano ballad follows<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="49" data-track="t1">2</button>, with <dfn class="term" data-def="Chords played one note at a time, in a rippling pattern.">arpeggiated</dfn> accompaniment.</p><p data-narration-id="q4">Then come a <dfn class="term" data-def="Deliberately difficult, showing off the performer&rsquo;s skill.">virtuosic</dfn> guitar solo<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="156" data-track="t1">3</button>, an opera section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="183" data-track="t1">4</button>, a hard rock section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="247" data-track="t1">5</button>, and a quiet, reflective outro<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="295" data-track="t1">6</button>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Keys and metre" data-track="t1"><h2>Bohemian Rhapsody: keys and metre</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q5">The a cappella opening and the ballad are both in B flat major &mdash; the very first chord is B flat 6, an unusual added-sixth chord<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t1">1</button><button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="49" data-track="t1">2</button>. The rock section that follows the opera is in E flat major<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="247" data-track="t1">5</button>. The opera section jumps to A major, an unrelated key<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="183" data-track="t1">4</button> &mdash; that jump is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A change of key.">modulation</dfn>. The song then ends on an F major chord<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="295" data-track="t1">6</button>. That is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The fifth note or chord of a key &mdash; it wants to resolve home, not finish.">dominant</dfn> of the opening key of B flat major, which is why the ending feels unfinished.</p><p data-narration-id="q6">The hard rock section shifts into 12/8<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="247" data-track="t1">5</button>, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A metre where each beat divides into three, giving a rolling feel.">compound time</dfn> signature. The rest is almost all 4/4 &mdash; except single bars of 5/4 and 9/8 in the introduction<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t1">1</button>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Voices" data-track="t1"><h2>Bohemian Rhapsody: the voices</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q7">Mercury had a four-octave range and uses all of it: soaring falsetto in some places, belted chest voice in others. The word-setting is almost entirely <dfn class="term" data-def="One note for each syllable of text.">syllabic</dfn>. Listen for <dfn class="term" data-def="A smooth slide between two pitches.">portamento</dfn> slides on words like &lsquo;reality&rsquo;<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t1">1</button>, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short accented note on the beat followed by a longer note off it.">Scotch snap</dfn> on &lsquo;sympathy&rsquo;, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The music acting out the meaning of the words.">word painting</dfn> as the melody rises at the line about looking up.</p><p data-narration-id="q8">In the opera section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="183" data-track="t1">4</button> the &lsquo;Galileo&rsquo; motif uses repeated notes and a rising semitone. &lsquo;Bismillah!&rsquo; works as question and answer: the refusal line lands in <dfn class="term" data-def="The same note doubled eight notes apart.">octaves</dfn>, and the group replies in <dfn class="term" data-def="All parts moving together in chords.">homophonic</dfn> chords.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="The studio" data-track="t1"><h2>Bohemian Rhapsody: built in the studio</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="q8" data-revision-tip="Cover the box and recall the recording details (artist, year, album) for Bohemian Rhapsody, then check accuracy."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact &mdash; The Recording</div><p><em>Bohemian Rhapsody</em> was built in the studio. It uses roughly 180 vocal <dfn class="term" data-def="Extra parts recorded on top of earlier ones, layer by layer.">overdubs</dfn>, bounced across tape generations. The song could not be performed live as recorded.</p></div><p data-narration-id="q9">Brian May&rsquo;s guitar solo at the end of the ballad is virtuosic, with fast <dfn class="term" data-def="Moving up or down the notes of a scale.">scalic</dfn> passages, slides, bends, vibrato and distortion<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="156" data-track="t1">3</button>. In the opera section the drums play the same rhythms as the voices rather than a steady beat, and the staccato crotchet opening imitates the comic characters of real opera<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="183" data-track="t1">4</button>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-track="t1" data-title="The studio sound"><h2>Bohemian Rhapsody: the studio sound</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs30">Production is part of the analysis. There is heavy electric guitar overdubbing. The vocals are panned across the stereo field, most clearly in the opera section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="183" data-track="t1">4</button>. Dynamics swing from alternating quiet and loud phrases to fortissimo interjections, before the final fall away to piano<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="295" data-track="t1">6</button>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Seven Seas of Rhye" data-track="t2" data-chapters="t2c1,t2c2,t2c3,t2c4,t2c5"><h2>Seven Seas of Rhye: the first hit</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q10">A hard rock song in D major. Released in February 1974, it was the single that made Queen&rsquo;s name. It opens with a ten-bar piano introduction<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="5" data-track="t2">1</button>: an <dfn class="term" data-def="Chords played one note at a time.">arpeggiated</dfn> riff in octaves, in semiquavers. The verse<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="25" data-track="t2">2</button> hits with <dfn class="term" data-def="Bare two-note guitar chords &mdash; root and fifth, no third.">power chords</dfn> underneath.</p><p data-narration-id="q11">After the bridge<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="63" data-track="t2">3</button> and a guitar solo<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="84" data-track="t2">4</button>, the song dissolves into a music-hall singalong &mdash; &lsquo;I Do Like to Be Beside the Seaside&rsquo;<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="157" data-track="t2">5</button> &mdash; played on a <dfn class="term" data-def="A pocket electronic keyboard played with a stylus.">stylophone</dfn>, a completely different style to end on. There is no chorus anywhere: instead a vocal hook closes each verse.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Rhye: sounds to name" data-track="t2"><h2>Seven Seas of Rhye: sounds to name</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q12">The guitar solo moves to B flat major before returning to D, and the second bridge modulates to G major<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="84" data-track="t2">4</button>. Three more details to name: power chords in the introduction<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="5" data-track="t2">1</button>; <dfn class="term" data-def="Expressively lowered notes &mdash; usually the flattened third or seventh.">blues notes</dfn> colouring the vocal line<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="25" data-track="t2">2</button>; palm-muting in the first bridge<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="63" data-track="t2">3</button>; and a delay effect at the end of the guitar solo<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="84" data-track="t2">4</button>.</p><p data-narration-id="q13">The bridge is the contrast section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="63" data-track="t2">3</button>. The mode shifts, a new melody breaks the verse pattern, and it ends with a long held vocal cry that launches the guitar solo<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="84" data-track="t2">4</button>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Love of My Life" data-track="t3" data-chapters="t3c1,t3c2,t3c3"><h2>Love of My Life: the ballad</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="q16">The ballad of the three. Home is F major, but the harmony travels constantly &mdash; naming where it goes is where the marks are. The short introduction implies F major, then slips to B flat major in its second phrase<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t3">1</button>.</p><p data-narration-id="qs20">The two verses start in C major and <dfn class="term" data-def="Change key.">modulate</dfn> to F major<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="23" data-track="t3">2</button>. One multi-sectional epic that breaks the rules of song form. One compact hard rock single. One ballad.</p><p data-narration-id="q20">Comparisons almost write themselves: no chorus in two of the three; piano-led against guitar-led; studio construction against near-live simplicity. <h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Bohemian Rhapsody: six sections, no chorus — a cappella B flat opening, ballad to E flat, abrupt A major opera section, 12/8 rock section, quiet F major finish; 180 vocal overdubs and three weeks of recording.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Seven Seas of Rhye: hard rock in D major with an octave piano riff, ten-bar intro, no chorus, a solo that modulates to the flattened submediant B flat, and a seaside-song fade-out; the February 1974 single that became Queen's first hit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Love of My Life: a travelling tonality around F major, sus-chord patterns, harp arpeggios, tenor-and-falsetto lead and a plagal cadence to close.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Section B formula every time: name the element, give the term, locate it in the track.</li>
</ul>
