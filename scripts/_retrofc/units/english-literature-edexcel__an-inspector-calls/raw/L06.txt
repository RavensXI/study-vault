--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Inspector Goole: More Than a Police Officer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Inspector Goole is the most enigmatic character in <em>An Inspector Calls</em>. He arrives at the Birlings’ home, conducts his investigation, delivers one of the most powerful speeches in modern drama, and then leaves. After his departure, the family discover he is not a real police inspector. So who — or what — is he? Priestley deliberately leaves this question unanswered, and the ambiguity is central to the play’s power. The Inspector can be read as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character, object, or figure that represents an abstract idea or moral principle, rather than functioning as a realistic person.">symbolic figure</dfn> — the voice of social conscience, a supernatural being, a time traveller, or even a divine messenger. His name, “Goole,” suggests “ghoul” — a phantom or spirit — hinting that he exists outside the normal rules of reality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">What matters most, however, is his function. Inspector Goole is Priestley’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who expresses the views, beliefs, and political message of the writer within the text.">mouthpiece</dfn>. Every word the Inspector speaks reflects Priestley’s own socialist beliefs: that we are all responsible for each other, that the class system is unjust, and that society must change. The Inspector does not just investigate a crime — he exposes a moral failure. He forces each Birling to confront the consequences of their selfishness and challenges them to change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three possible identities for the Inspector, then explain why his function matters more than his identity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Inspector’s true identity is deliberately ambiguous. He could be supernatural, a time traveller, or a symbolic construct. What matters is his function: he is Priestley’s mouthpiece, delivering the message that “We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other.” Focus your exam answers on what the Inspector <em>represents</em>, not on solving the mystery of who he “really” is.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Inspector’s Method</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Inspector interrogates one character at a time, maintaining strict control over the investigation. He refuses to be distracted by Birling’s attempts to intimidate him (“I was an alderman for years — and Lord Mayor two years ago”) and is unmoved by social status. He speaks with calm authority: “Public men, Mr Birling, have responsibilities as well as privileges.” This line is a direct challenge to Birling’s belief that wealth entitles him to special treatment. The Inspector insists that power comes with moral obligation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">His method is deliberately linear — he deals with one person before moving to the next. This creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="In drama, a pattern in which tension gradually increases through a series of revelations, each more shocking than the last, building towards a climax.">rising pattern of tension</dfn> as each revelation is worse than the one before. It also prevents the family from collaborating to protect themselves. Birling tries to take control several times, but the Inspector always reasserts his authority. Priestley uses this to show that moral authority trumps social authority — the Inspector’s power comes from truth, not from money or title.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Several details suggest the Inspector is not entirely human. His name (“Goole”/ghoul) hints at the supernatural. He seems to already know everything before he asks his questions. He appears just as Birling declares that people should look after themselves — as if summoned by the statement. He controls time within the play, dictating the pace of revelations. And his final prophecy (“fire and blood and anguish”) comes true through the World Wars. Priestley was influenced by the <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval dramatic tradition of presenting abstract moral qualities (like conscience, greed, or death) as characters in a play. Priestley draws on this tradition with Inspector Goole.">morality play</dfn> tradition, in which abstract concepts like conscience, death, or virtue appear as characters to judge the living. The Inspector can be read in this tradition as the embodiment of collective conscience — a figure who exists to force moral reckoning.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Inspector’s Key Quotations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">“Public men, Mr Birling, have responsibilities as well as privileges.” This quotation directly counters Birling’s belief that wealth exempts him from accountability. The balanced structure — “responsibilities” set against “privileges” — suggests they must be equal. Priestley argues that those with power have a greater duty to society, not a lesser one.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">“We don’t live alone. We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other.” This is the play’s thesis statement. The short, declarative sentences give it the force of absolute truth. The repetition of “we” is inclusive — it addresses the Birlings, the audience, and all of society. “One body” carries a Biblical allusion (1 Corinthians 12), giving the message spiritual authority beyond its political content.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Gerald Croft: The Uncomfortable Middle Ground</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Gerald Croft occupies a unique position in the play. He is not a Birling by blood, but he is deeply embedded in the same class system. His family, the Crofts, are “an old country family — landed people,” making them socially superior to the self-made Birlings. Gerald is charming, confident, and knows how to navigate social situations. He is the character most likely to succeed in Edwardian society precisely because he understands its rules.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Gerald’s treatment of Eva/Daisy Renton is the most ambiguous in the play. He genuinely cared for her — he gave her food, shelter, and affection. He even admits he was “sorry for her.” But the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imbalance of social, economic, or personal power between two people that makes any relationship between them inherently unequal.">power imbalance</dfn> makes his “rescue” problematic. Eva was destitute; Gerald was wealthy. She had no choice but to be grateful. When Gerald ended the affair, he gave her money and moved on. For Eva, the loss was devastating. Priestley forces the audience to question whether Gerald’s kindness was genuine compassion or a more palatable form of exploitation.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Gerald’s behaviour after the Inspector’s departure is revealing. He is the one who discovers that “Inspector Goole” is not on the police force. He telephones the Infirmary to confirm no girl has died. He presents these findings as if they exonerate everyone. Gerald aligns himself with Birling’s desire to return to normal, effectively choosing <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach that focuses on practical consequences (such as avoiding a scandal) rather than on moral principles.">pragmatism</dfn> over principle. He even offers Sheila the engagement ring again, hoping to restore the status quo. Priestley uses Gerald to show that emotional responses during the investigation (Gerald did cry when describing his affair) do not necessarily lead to lasting moral change. Gerald felt something in the moment, but when the pressure is removed, he reverts to protecting his position and reputation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: does Gerald ultimately side with the younger or older generation, and what does Priestley argue through this?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gerald occupies the middle ground between the older and younger Birlings. He shows genuine emotion during his confession but ultimately aligns with Birling after the Inspector leaves. Priestley uses him to argue that feeling sorry is not enough — genuine moral change requires action and commitment, not just a moment of regret.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Eva Smith: The Invisible Centre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Eva Smith never appears on stage, yet she is the most important character in the play. She exists only through the descriptions of others, which means the audience must construct her from fragments — just as the upper classes only ever see the working class in fragments: as workers, servants, or statistics. Priestley uses Eva’s absence to make a powerful point: the people who suffer most from inequality are invisible to those who cause their suffering. Eva represents every working-class person exploited by the system the Birlings uphold. Her changing names (Eva Smith, Daisy Renton, “Mrs Birling”) suggest she could be any woman — or every woman — failed by Edwardian society.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Inspector Goole is Priestley’s mouthpiece: his true identity matters less than his function as the voice of collective responsibility and social conscience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Gerald Croft is the moral middle ground — he shows genuine emotion but ultimately sides with Birling, proving that temporary guilt without lasting change is worthless.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Eva Smith never appears on stage, yet she is the play’s moral centre. Her invisibility represents how the powerful render the working class invisible in real life.</li></ul>
