--- DESCRIPTION ---
Act 1: the engagement dinner, Birling’s speech, the Inspector’s arrival, and Sheila’s confession.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Engagement Dinner</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 1 opens on a scene of celebration. The Birling family are gathered around the dining table in their comfortable home in the fictional industrial city of Brumley. Sheila Birling and Gerald Croft have just announced their engagement, and Arthur Birling is in an expansive, self-congratulatory mood. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Instructions in a script that describe the setting, lighting, characters’ movements, and how lines should be delivered.">stage directions</dfn> tell us the lighting is “pink and intimate” — a warm, rosy glow that symbolises the family’s comfortable self-deception. They are celebrating in a bubble of wealth and privilege, completely unaware of the reckoning that is about to arrive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Priestley establishes the family dynamics quickly. Arthur Birling is pompous and self-important, dominating the conversation. His wife Sybil is cold and class-conscious. Sheila appears light-hearted but shows flashes of intelligence. Eric is nervous and drinks too much. Gerald is polished and well-connected — his family are socially superior to the Birlings. Priestley reveals that the engagement is as much a <dfn class="term" data-def="A business relationship or agreement made for mutual financial or social benefit, rather than for love or personal connection.">business alliance</dfn> as a love match: Birling tells Gerald he hopes the marriage will bring their two companies closer together, “for lower costs and higher prices.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Birling hope to gain from the engagement, and what does this reveal about his values?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling sees the engagement as a business opportunity, hoping for “lower costs and higher prices” from the Birling-Croft partnership. Priestley uses this to show that Birling reduces every relationship to profit — even his daughter’s marriage. This establishes the capitalist values that the Inspector will systematically destroy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Birling’s Speech: A Philosophy of Selfishness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">After Gerald and Sheila’s engagement is toasted, Birling delivers his notorious speech. He tells Eric and Gerald that “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” He dismisses the idea of community, calling it “nonsense” and mocking anyone who talks about “community and all that.” He also sneers at the possibility of war and boasts about the Titanic being “unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This speech is structurally crucial. Priestley places it immediately before the Inspector’s arrival so that the doorbell literally interrupts Birling’s capitalist manifesto. The Inspector becomes the physical embodiment of everything Birling denies — collective responsibility, social conscience, and the idea that our actions have consequences for others. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An object, person, or event in a text that represents a larger idea or concept beyond its literal meaning.">symbolism</dfn> is unmistakable: the voice of social justice silences the voice of greed.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>“If We Were All Responsible for Everything…”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Birling dismisses responsibility with the line: “If we were all responsible for everything that happened to everybody … it would be very awkward.” The word “awkward” is deliberately weak and trivial. Birling reduces a profound moral question — should we care about others? — to a matter of minor inconvenience. The understatement reveals his inability to think beyond his own comfort. Priestley wants the 1945 audience, who have just survived a war that required extraordinary collective sacrifice, to recognise this selfishness and reject it completely.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Inspector Arrives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The doorbell rings “sharply” — Priestley’s stage direction emphasising its harshness compared to the cosy atmosphere. <dfn class="term" data-def="A police inspector in the play whose name suggests ‘ghoul’ (a spirit or phantom), hinting at his supernatural or otherworldly nature.">Inspector Goole</dfn> enters and immediately changes the dynamic. The lighting shifts from “pink and intimate” to “brighter and harder,” symbolising scrutiny and truth. The Inspector is described as creating “an impression of massiveness, solidity and purposefulness” — he is an immovable force of moral authority.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Inspector announces that a young woman called Eva Smith has died — she swallowed disinfectant and died in agony in the Infirmary. He has come to ask questions. From this moment, the celebration is over. The Inspector methodically works through the family, revealing how each of them contributed to Eva’s suffering and death. Priestley structures the play so that each revelation builds on the last, creating a chain of responsibility that connects every member of the Birling family to one working-class woman’s destruction.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Birling and Eva Smith</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The Inspector reveals that Eva Smith worked in Birling’s factory and was one of several workers who asked for a pay rise from twenty-two shillings and sixpence to twenty-five shillings a week. Birling refused and sacked the ringleaders, including Eva. He shows no remorse: “If you don’t come down sharply on some of these people, they’d soon be asking for the earth.” Birling sees workers as commodities to be managed, not people with rights or dignity. The phrase “these people” is dehumanising — it creates distance between the Birlings and the working class, treating them as a separate, inferior species.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how much was Eva's requested pay rise, why was she sacked, and what language does Birling use about workers?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling sacked Eva Smith for asking for a pay rise of just two shillings and sixpence a week. His language — “these people” and “come down sharply” — reveals that he views workers as a problem to be controlled, not as human beings deserving of fair treatment. This is the first link in the chain of events that leads to Eva’s death.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Sheila’s Confession</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Sheila is the next to be confronted. After being sacked by Birling, Eva found work at Milwards, a fashionable department store. Sheila went shopping there, tried on a dress that didn’t suit her, and caught Eva holding the dress against herself and smiling. Jealous and humiliated, Sheila complained to the manager and had Eva sacked. Sheila is horrified when she realises the consequences of her actions: “If I could help her now, I would.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Sheila’s response is crucially different from her father’s. While Birling remains defensive and refuses to accept blame, Sheila immediately feels genuine <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of deep regret and guilt for a wrongdoing, often accompanied by a desire to make amends.">remorse</dfn>. She recognises that she used her social power to punish someone who had done nothing wrong. Her line “But these girls aren’t cheap labour — they’re people” is a direct challenge to her father’s worldview and shows that she is already beginning to accept the Inspector’s message of responsibility.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sheila as Priestley’s Hope for Change</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Sheila represents the younger generation who are capable of learning and changing. Priestley uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="A contrast or conflict between older and younger characters, used by Priestley to show that the younger generation can learn from mistakes while the older generation refuses to change.">generational divide</dfn> to suggest that while people like Birling will never accept responsibility, the young can be transformed by confronting the truth. By the end of Act 1, Sheila has already begun to question her family’s values. She becomes increasingly critical of her parents and supportive of the Inspector, foreshadowing her full moral awakening in Acts 2 and 3.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Priestley is speaking to his 1945 audience: the older generation (represented by Birling and Sybil) created the problems that led to two world wars. It is the younger generation who must build a better, fairer society. Sheila and Eric represent this possibility.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Gerald’s Recognition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Act 1 ends with Gerald recognising the name “Daisy Renton” — a name the Inspector mentions as Eva’s later alias. Gerald’s visible reaction tells the audience that he, too, is involved. Priestley uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="A moment of suspense at the end of an act or chapter, designed to keep the audience engaged and eager to find out what happens next.">cliffhanger</dfn> to maintain tension. The act closes with Sheila warning Gerald: “You gave yourself away as soon as he mentioned her name.” The celebration is already in ruins, and the Inspector has only just begun.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Act 1 establishes the Birlings’ wealth and privilege before systematically dismantling it. The lighting change from “pink and intimate” to “brighter and harder” symbolises the exposure of hidden truths.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Birling’s speech about minding your own business is immediately interrupted by the Inspector’s arrival — Priestley uses structure to show that selfish individualism cannot go unchallenged.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Sheila’s immediate remorse contrasts sharply with Birling’s defensiveness, establishing the generational divide that runs through the entire play.</li></ul>
