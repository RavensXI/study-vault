Act 2: Gerald’s affair with Daisy Renton and Mrs Birling’s cold cruelty at the charity committee.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Gerald’s Confession</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 2 picks up where Act 1 left off, with Gerald forced to reveal his relationship with the woman he knew as <dfn class="term" data-def="The false name Eva Smith used after being sacked from Milwards, under which she met Gerald Croft.">Daisy Renton</dfn>. Gerald met her at the Palace Bar — a place where prostitutes were known to find wealthy clients. He claims he “rescued” her from the unwanted attentions of Alderman Meggarty, set her up in a friend’s flat, and began an affair that lasted throughout the summer. Gerald insists his feelings were genuine: “I didn’t feel about her as she felt about me.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Priestley presents Gerald’s involvement with Eva/Daisy as deeply ambiguous. On one hand, Gerald showed her genuine kindness at a time when no one else did — he gave her food, shelter, and affection. Eva “was very grateful” and “intensely grateful”: Gerald admits “I became at once the most important person in her life.” On the other hand, the power imbalance is impossible to ignore. Gerald is a wealthy, upper-class man; Daisy is a destitute, vulnerable woman with no other options. He ultimately ends the affair when it suits him, leaving her with a small amount of money and nothing else. His “rescue” is temporary and self-serving.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: in what way was Gerald both compassionate and exploitative towards Daisy Renton?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gerald’s affair with Daisy Renton shows both genuine compassion and exploitation. He rescued her from poverty but also used his wealth and power to keep her dependent on him. When the affair ends, Gerald moves on easily while Eva is left with nothing. Priestley shows that even “kind” treatment can be a form of control when there is such an extreme power imbalance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sheila’s Growing Authority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">As Gerald confesses, Sheila’s reaction is crucial. Rather than simply being hurt by the revelation of Gerald’s affair, she shows maturity and moral intelligence. She tells Gerald: “I respect you more than I’ve ever done before — because at least you’re being honest.” But she also returns the engagement ring, recognising that the relationship cannot simply continue as before. Sheila has grown beyond the spoiled girl of Act 1. She is now an active participant in the investigation, even warning her mother: “You mustn’t try to build up a kind of wall between us and that girl.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Priestley uses Sheila as the audience’s moral compass. She understands what the Inspector is doing before anyone else and tries to warn her parents. When Mrs Birling begins to speak, Sheila sees the trap that her mother is walking into and desperately tries to stop her. Sheila’s <dfn class="term" data-def="When a character changes and develops over the course of a text, in contrast to a static character who remains the same.">character arc</dfn> from privileged young woman to social conscience is one of Priestley’s most important dramatic achievements.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Engagement Ring: Symbol of Change</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Sheila returning the ring is symbolically significant. The ring represents the Birling-Croft alliance — a union based on class, money, and social status rather than genuine love or shared values. By returning it, Sheila rejects the values the ring represents. She refuses to pretend that nothing has happened. This contrasts sharply with her parents, who will later try to return to “normal” once the Inspector leaves. The ring also symbolises the breaking apart of the old social order — the comfortable pre-war world is shattered, and Sheila has accepted that it cannot be put back together.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Mrs Birling: The Cold Heart of Privilege</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Mrs Sybil Birling is the most unyielding character in the play. She is a prominent member of the Brumley Women’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A private organisation that provided assistance to people in need, run by upper-class women. In the play, Mrs Birling uses her influence to deny Eva help.">Charity Organisation</dfn>, yet when Eva came to her for help — pregnant, penniless, and desperate — Mrs Birling used her influence to have the case rejected. Her reason? Eva had given her name as “Mrs Birling,” and Sybil was offended by what she saw as impudence: “She was claiming elaborate fine feelings and scruples that were simply absurd in a girl in her position.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This line is devastating. Mrs Birling cannot believe that a working-class woman could have genuine feelings. The phrase “a girl in her position” reveals her absolute conviction that class determines a person’s worth and emotional capacity. She denies Eva’s humanity based entirely on her social status. Priestley uses Mrs Birling to represent the most dangerous form of prejudice: one that masquerades as respectability and charity while actually destroying the people it claims to help.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: why did Mrs Birling refuse to help Eva, and what does her phrase 'a girl in her position' reveal?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mrs Birling turned away a pregnant, desperate woman because she was offended by the use of the Birling name. Her line “a girl in her position” reveals she believes working-class people cannot have genuine emotions. Priestley uses her to expose the hypocrisy of upper-class charity — it serves the giver’s sense of superiority, not the needs of the vulnerable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Mrs Birling’s Trap</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In one of the play’s most powerful moments of <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation in which a character’s words or actions have the opposite effect to what they intend, often because the audience knows more than the character.">dramatic irony</dfn>, Mrs Birling unknowingly condemns her own son. The Inspector asks who should bear the most responsibility for Eva’s situation, and Mrs Birling insists it should be “the young man … the father of the child.” She declares: “He should be made an example of. If the girl’s death is due to anybody, then it’s due to him.” She is confident she is pointing the blame away from herself — but the audience, and Sheila, can see what is coming. The father of Eva’s child is Eric.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Priestley constructs this scene with precision. Mrs Birling’s self-righteous insistence that the father must take full responsibility creates a trap she cannot escape from. When Eric’s involvement is revealed in Act 3, her own words will be used against her. Sheila’s desperate warning — “Mother — stop — stop!” — goes unheeded. Priestley shows that Mrs Birling’s arrogance and class prejudice have made her blind to the truth, even when it is staring her in the face.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Inspector’s Method: One Line at a Time</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The Inspector interrogates one character at a time, never allowing them to see the full picture until the trap is sprung. This is a deliberate dramatic technique. Priestley uses it to build tension and to prevent the characters from coordinating their stories or deflecting blame. It also mirrors the structure of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A tightly structured play, popular in the late 19th and early 20th century, in which past secrets are gradually revealed through present-day events. An Inspector Calls follows this pattern.">well-made play</dfn> — past secrets are gradually unearthed, and each revelation makes the situation more desperate. By the time Mrs Birling finishes speaking, the audience knows the full horror is about to be revealed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Gender and Power in Act 2</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Act 2 exposes how men and women are treated differently in Edwardian society. Gerald’s affair would have been considered socially acceptable for a man of his class — keeping a mistress was common among the wealthy. Eva, by contrast, is judged and punished at every turn: sacked for asking for fair wages, sacked again for being pretty, used and abandoned by Gerald, and finally turned away by Mrs Birling’s charity when she is pregnant and alone. Priestley shows that the class system and the gender system work together to destroy women like Eva, who have no power, no voice, and no escape.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Gerald’s relationship with Eva/Daisy is deliberately ambiguous — he showed genuine kindness but also exploited a massive power imbalance. Priestley makes the audience question whether ‘rescue’ by the powerful is truly benevolent.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Mrs Birling is the play’s most morally rigid character. She denies Eva help because of class prejudice, then unknowingly condemns her own son — a devastating example of dramatic irony.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Sheila’s growth continues: she returns the engagement ring and tries to warn her mother. She represents Priestley’s hope that the younger generation will reject the old, class-bound values.</li></ul>
