<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Time Periods, One Play</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>An Inspector Calls</em> was written by J.B. Priestley in 1945 but is set in 1912. Understanding this <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate gap between when a text was written and when it is set, allowing the writer to create dramatic irony and comment on how attitudes have or haven’t changed.">time gap</dfn> is absolutely essential for the Edexcel exam. The 1945 audience had just lived through two devastating World Wars, the Great Depression, and the horrors of the Blitz. They knew exactly how wrong Arthur Birling’s confident predictions about the future would turn out to be — and Priestley uses this knowledge to undermine everything Birling represents.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">By setting the play in 1912 — just two years before the First World War — Priestley creates <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something that a character does not, creating tension, humour, or a sense of the character’s foolishness.">dramatic irony</dfn> on almost every page. When Birling declares that the Titanic is “unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable” and that war is impossible, the audience knows he is catastrophically wrong. This is not just comedy — it is Priestley’s deliberate strategy to destroy Birling’s credibility so that the audience will reject his selfish, capitalist worldview.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list Birling's three false predictions from Act 1, then explain what they prove about his worldview.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birling makes three dramatically ironic predictions in Act 1: the Titanic is “unsinkable,” there will be no war, and the future holds nothing but prosperity. Every single one is proven wrong by history. Priestley uses this to show that Birling’s confident, self-serving view of the world is dangerously deluded.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Edwardian Britain: Class and Capitalism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1912, Britain was a deeply unequal society. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of King Edward VII’s reign (1901–1910) and its aftermath, characterised by rigid class divisions, industrial wealth for the few, and poverty for the many.">Edwardian era</dfn> was defined by a rigid class system in which wealthy industrialists like Arthur Birling held enormous power over their workers. Factory owners could hire and fire at will, wages were desperately low, and there was no welfare state, no NHS, and no unemployment benefit. If you lost your job, you faced poverty, the workhouse, or starvation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Women were particularly vulnerable. In 1912, women could not vote, had limited employment options, and were entirely dependent on men for financial security. A working-class woman like Eva Smith had almost no power — she could be exploited by employers, used by wealthy men, and turned away by charities with no legal protection. Priestley makes Eva a symbol of everyone failed by a society that values profit over people.</p>

The Birlings exist in a comfortable, insulated bubble — they eat well, drink champagne, and celebrate Sheila’s engagement to Gerald Croft while people like Eva Smith have nothing. Priestley uses setting to show that the upper classes deliberately shut out the suffering of others.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Priestley’s Socialism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">J.B. Priestley was a committed <dfn class="term" data-def="A political philosophy that advocates for collective responsibility, public ownership of key industries, and a fairer distribution of wealth — the opposite of the individualist capitalism Arthur Birling represents.">socialist</dfn>. He believed passionately that society should look after all its members, not just the wealthy. During the Second World War, Priestley presented a hugely popular BBC radio programme called <em>Postscripts</em>, in which he argued that Britain should build a fairer society after the war. His broadcasts reached millions and helped shift public opinion towards the idea of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A system in which the government provides healthcare, housing, education, and financial support for citizens, funded by taxation. Created in Britain after 1945.">Welfare State</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In 1945 — the year Priestley wrote <em>An Inspector Calls</em> — Britain held a general election. The Labour Party won a landslide victory on a platform of social reform, and went on to create the NHS, the modern benefits system, and free secondary education. Priestley wrote the play as a rallying cry for this new, fairer Britain. The Inspector’s final speech — “We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other” — is Priestley speaking directly to the 1945 audience, urging them not to return to the selfish, divided society of 1912.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: when was An Inspector Calls written, what political event happened that year, and what quotation is Priestley's core message?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Priestley wrote <em>An Inspector Calls</em> in 1945, the same year Labour won the general election. The play is a political argument for collective responsibility and the Welfare State. The Inspector’s message — “We are members of one body” — directly echoes Priestley’s own socialist beliefs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Dramatic Irony as a Weapon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Priestley weaponises dramatic irony to discredit Arthur Birling. When Birling confidently tells Gerald that “the Titanic … unsinkable, absolutely unsinkable” and predicts that there will be no war, the audience knows the Titanic sank in April 1912 and that the First World War began in 1914. This makes Birling look absurdly out of touch. But Priestley’s purpose goes deeper than comedy. By making Birling’s capitalist, individualist philosophy sound ridiculous, Priestley ensures the audience will side with the Inspector’s socialist alternative.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Birling’s Speech: “A Man Has to Mind His Own Business”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In Act 1, Birling delivers a long speech about how “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” This is a direct statement of capitalist <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that individuals should look after themselves rather than relying on or being responsible for the wider community.">individualism</dfn>. Birling rejects the idea that people have any responsibility towards strangers. The timing is crucial — his speech is immediately interrupted by the Inspector’s arrival, as if Priestley is physically cutting off Birling’s selfish philosophy. The doorbell becomes a dramatic device: the voice of social conscience interrupting the voice of greed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Priestley places Birling’s speech just before the Inspector arrives deliberately. The audience has barely heard Birling’s self-centred worldview before it is challenged — structurally showing that this kind of thinking cannot go unchallenged. This is a clear example of how Priestley uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a writer organises and sequences events, speeches, and scenes to shape the audience’s understanding and emotional response.">dramatic structure</dfn> to reinforce his message.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why Context Matters for Edexcel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the Edexcel English Literature exam (Component 1, Section B), context is assessed through AO3. You are expected to show understanding of the relationship between the text and the contexts in which it was written and set. For <em>An Inspector Calls</em>, this means you must demonstrate awareness of the 1912 setting, the 1945 writing context, Priestley’s political beliefs, and how the time gap creates dramatic irony. The strongest responses embed context naturally within analysis rather than bolting it on as a separate paragraph.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The play is set in 1912 but written in 1945 — this time gap creates dramatic irony that undermines Birling’s capitalist worldview and strengthens the Inspector’s socialist message.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Priestley was a committed socialist who used the play to argue for collective responsibility, the Welfare State, and a fairer society after the Second World War.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Birling’s false predictions (Titanic, no war) are not just funny — they are Priestley’s deliberate strategy to destroy the credibility of selfish individualism.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">For Edexcel Section B, you will be given a short quotation as a stimulus and must write an essay response. AO3 (context) is crucial. Always link context to Priestley’s purpose — don’t just state historical facts. For example, instead of “In 1912, there was no Welfare State,” write “Priestley highlights Eva’s lack of support to show a 1945 audience exactly why the Welfare State is needed.” This turns context into analysis.</p>
