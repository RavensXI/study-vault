Key themes: responsibility, class, generational conflict, gender, and morality in An Inspector Calls.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Responsibility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Responsibility is the central theme of <em>An Inspector Calls</em>. The entire play asks a single question: are we responsible for other people? Priestley presents two opposing answers. Arthur Birling argues for <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that individuals should focus on their own success and wellbeing without obligation to help others or contribute to the wider community.">individualism</dfn>: “a man has to mind his own business and look after himself and his own.” Inspector Goole argues for <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that all members of society share a duty to care for one another, especially the most vulnerable. This is the Inspector’s core message and Priestley’s political position.">collective responsibility</dfn>: “We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other.” The play is structured as a debate between these two positions, with the Inspector systematically proving that Birling’s philosophy leads to suffering and death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">What makes the theme powerful is that Priestley shows responsibility operating in a chain. No single Birling killed Eva Smith. Each contributed one link — sacking her, getting her sacked, using her, rejecting her — and the cumulative effect was fatal. Priestley argues that irresponsibility is not just about individual cruelty but about a system that allows the powerful to harm the vulnerable without consequences. The chain of responsibility is also a chain of complicity: everyone is involved, and claiming ignorance is no defence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name all five links in the chain of responsibility for Eva's death, in order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Priestley presents responsibility as a chain: Birling sacked Eva, Sheila got her sacked again, Gerald used and abandoned her, Mrs Birling denied her charity, and Eric got her pregnant. Each link in the chain is a separate act of selfishness, but together they destroy a life. The play argues that in a connected society, there is no such thing as an isolated action.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme 2: Class and Social Inequality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Class is the engine that drives every event in the play. The Birlings occupy a position of wealth and power that insulates them from the consequences of their actions. Eva Smith, by contrast, has no power, no voice, and no protection. When she asks for a modest pay rise, she is sacked. When she finds new work, she is sacked again because a wealthy customer is jealous. When she seeks help from a charity, she is refused because she has the wrong social status. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The rigid social hierarchy of early twentieth-century Britain, dividing society into upper, middle, and working classes, with little opportunity for movement between them.">class system</dfn> is not just a backdrop — it is the mechanism through which every injustice occurs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Priestley exposes how the upper classes use language to dehumanise the working class. Birling calls his workers “these people” and talks about “coming down sharply” on them. Mrs Birling claims Eva’s “fine feelings” are “absurd in a girl in her position.” This language reveals a fundamental belief that working-class people are less human, less capable of genuine emotion, and less deserving of respect. Priestley shows that class prejudice is not just unpleasant — it is lethal.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The 1945 audience had just lived through the Second World War, during which class barriers had been significantly weakened. People from all backgrounds fought, worked, and suffered together. The shared experience of war created a powerful sense of national solidarity. Priestley capitalises on this: he sets the play in 1912 to remind the audience of the old, rigid class system and to argue that Britain must never return to it. The Labour election victory of 1945 and the creation of the Welfare State were direct results of this shift in attitudes. Priestley wrote <em>An Inspector Calls</em> to ensure the audience understood why the old system was unjust and why the new one was necessary.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Theme 3: Age and Generational Conflict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The conflict between older characters (who resist change) and younger characters (who embrace it), used by Priestley to argue that social progress depends on the younger generation.">generational divide</dfn> is Priestley’s structural mechanism for showing who can change and who cannot. Arthur and Sybil Birling are so deeply embedded in Edwardian values that they cannot accept the Inspector’s message. When the Inspector is revealed as possibly fake, they are immediately relieved: “The whole thing’s different now.” For them, the absence of punishment means the absence of guilt.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Sheila and Eric react completely differently. Sheila insists: “It doesn’t make any difference … You began to learn something. And now you’ve stopped.” The young Birlings understand that moral responsibility does not depend on whether you get caught. Priestley uses this divide to speak directly to his audience: the older generation created the conditions for two World Wars. It is the younger generation who must ensure it never happens again.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Theme 4: Gender</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The play exposes how women are treated differently in Edwardian society. Eva Smith is exploited at every stage by both men and women. She is sacked by Birling (a male employer), sacked by Sheila (a wealthy female customer), used by Gerald (a wealthy male lover), and refused help by Mrs Birling (a female gatekeeper of charity). Priestley shows that <dfn class="term" data-def="A social system in which men hold primary power and women are largely excluded from political, economic, and social authority.">patriarchal</dfn> values harm women across all classes, though working-class women like Eva are the most vulnerable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Sheila’s transformation also has a gender dimension. At the start of the play, she is the dutiful daughter, excited about her engagement ring and easily managed by her father. By Act 3, she has become an independent moral agent who challenges both her parents and her fiancé. Priestley suggests that the liberation of women — their ability to think, speak, and act independently — is part of the broader social transformation he is calling for.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: is Eva exploited only by men or also by women? Name one male and one female character who harm her.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eva Smith is exploited by both men (Birling, Gerald, Eric) and women (Sheila, Mrs Birling), showing that the class system and patriarchal values work together to destroy working-class women. Sheila’s rejection of her passive role represents Priestley’s argument that a fairer society must also be one where women have genuine agency.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Theme 5: Morality and Conscience</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The play asks: what does it mean to be a good person? Priestley rejects the Edwardian definition of morality, which was based on <dfn class="term" data-def="A concern with outward behaviour and social norms, particularly sexual behaviour and public decency, rather than genuine care for others.">respectability</dfn> — appearing decent, attending church, running charities, and maintaining social status. The Birlings tick all these boxes and yet are morally bankrupt. Mrs Birling runs a charity but turns away a desperate woman. Birling is a magistrate and former Lord Mayor but exploits his workers. Gerald is charming and well-mannered but uses a vulnerable woman for his own pleasure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Priestley replaces this shallow definition with a deeper one: true morality is about how you treat the most vulnerable people in society. The Inspector embodies this moral standard. He is not interested in social status, reputation, or appearances — he is interested in actions and their consequences. The play argues that conscience is not something imposed from outside (by law, religion, or social convention) but something that must come from within. Those who have it (Sheila, Eric) are transformed. Those who don’t (Birling, Sybil) are condemned to repeat their mistakes.</p>

Gender and class overlap: Eva’s vulnerability is the product of being both working-class and female. The generational divide connects to morality: the older generation defines morality as respectability, while the younger generation discovers that true morality means accepting responsibility for others. --- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Responsibility operates as a chain in the play: each Birling contributes one link, and the cumulative effect is Eva’s death. Priestley argues that there is no such thing as an isolated action in a connected society.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Class, gender, and age intersect: Eva is destroyed because she is working-class, female, and powerless. Priestley shows these systems of inequality working together to oppress the most vulnerable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">True morality, for Priestley, is not about appearances or respectability but about how you treat the most vulnerable. The play replaces Edwardian shallow morality with a deeper, socialist definition based on collective care.</li></ul>

The best responses treat themes as arguments Priestley is making, not just topics the play happens to include.</p>
