--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Stevenson’s language choices, narrative structure and Gothic techniques create meaning and suspense.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Language: Key Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Stevenson’s language is precise and purposeful. Every word choice contributes to the novella’s atmosphere of mystery, horror and moral unease.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Animalistic Imagery</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n3">Hyde is consistently described using <dfn class="term" data-def="Language that compares a person or thing to an animal, suggesting primitive or uncivilised qualities.">animalistic imagery</dfn>. He moves with “ape-like fury,” is called “troglodytic” (cave-dwelling), and hisses “like a cornered snake.” This language links Hyde to degeneration theory — the Victorian fear that humans could reverse-evolve into primitive creatures. By making Hyde animal-like, Stevenson suggests that evil is not a moral failing but a biological reality lurking inside everyone.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Language of the Unspeakable</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n4">Characters repeatedly struggle to describe Hyde. Enfield says he is “not easy to describe” and gives “a strong feeling of deformity.” Utterson notes “deformity without any nameable malformation.” This technique of describing something by saying it <em>cannot</em> be described is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Describing something as indescribable or beyond words, often to heighten its impact on the reader.">apophasis</dfn>. It makes Hyde more terrifying because the reader must imagine the horror for themselves.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n5">This linguistic vagueness also reflects Victorian society’s inability to confront evil directly. The characters can <em>sense</em> that Hyde is wrong, but their respectable vocabulary has no words for what they are feeling.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which literary technique Stevenson uses extensively, what three specific weather/lighting elements mirror moral confusion...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson uses <strong>pathetic fallacy</strong> extensively. London’s fog, darkness and gaslight mirror the moral confusion and hidden evil at the heart of the story. The city itself becomes a character — concealing, distorting and threatening.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Structure: Three Narratives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The novella’s structure is one of its most distinctive features. It uses three different <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a text is organised, including its sequence of events, shifts in perspective and use of time.">structural</dfn> layers:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Chapters 1–8:</strong> Third-person narrative focused on Utterson. The reader shares his limited perspective, piecing together clues alongside him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Chapter 9:</strong> Lanyon’s first-person letter. A shift to direct testimony from someone who witnessed the transformation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Chapter 10:</strong> Jekyll’s first-person “full statement.” The final revelation, told by the man at the centre of the mystery.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why This Structure Matters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Each narrative layer reveals more of the truth. Utterson sees the outside; Lanyon witnesses the transformation; Jekyll explains everything. This structure mirrors the peeling away of a disguise — the deeper we go, the closer we get to the horrifying reality beneath the respectable surface.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The use of <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative told through documents such as letters, diaries or reports rather than a single continuous narration.">epistolary elements</dfn> (letters and written statements) is also significant. The truth can only be told in writing, after death. In Victorian society, some truths were literally unspeakable — they could only be committed to paper and sealed away.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall whose statement ends the novella, whose reaction we never see, and what type of ending forces readers to draw their own c...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The novella ends with Jekyll’s statement, not with Utterson’s reaction. The reader never learns what happens after Jekyll’s death. This open ending forces the reader to draw their own conclusions about the meaning of the story.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Narrative Perspective and Reliability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Stevenson carefully controls what the reader knows and when. Utterson is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator whose account the reader can trust because they have no reason to lie or distort.">reliable narrator</dfn> in the sense that he reports honestly what he sees. But his perspective is limited — he cannot understand what is happening because it is beyond rational explanation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Jekyll, by contrast, is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator whose account may be biased, incomplete or deliberately misleading.">unreliable narrator</dfn>. Even in his “full statement,” he distances himself from Hyde (“He, I say — I cannot say I”) and minimises his own responsibility. The reader must decide how much to trust Jekyll’s version of events.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Gothic Conventions in the Novella</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Stevenson draws on several <dfn class="term" data-def="Established patterns or features that are typical of a particular literary genre.">Gothic conventions</dfn>:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>The doppelgänger</strong> — Hyde is Jekyll’s dark double, a common Gothic motif representing the hidden self.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>The mad scientist</strong> — Jekyll, like Frankenstein, conducts an experiment that creates a monster beyond his control.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>The discovered manuscript</strong> — The truth is revealed through found documents (Lanyon’s letter, Jekyll’s statement), a technique used by Gothic writers to create a sense of authenticity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Atmosphere of dread</strong> — Fog, darkness, locked doors and screaming winds create a persistent sense of menace.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Animalistic imagery links Hyde to degeneration:</strong> Words like “ape-like” and “troglodytic” suggest evil is a biological reality, not just a moral failing.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>The three-layer structure mirrors the theme of hidden identity:</strong> Each narrator reveals more, moving from outside observation to direct confession.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Jekyll is an unreliable narrator:</strong> Even his “full statement” contains self-deception, reminding the reader that truth is never simple in the novella.</li></ul>

But always explain <em>why</em> Stevenson uses these techniques — what effect they create and what ideas they convey. Terminology without analysis will not reach the highest levels.</p>
