--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Victorian fears about science, class and hidden identity shaped Stevenson's novella.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Context Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">To understand <em>The Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde</em>, you need to understand the world Robert Louis Stevenson was writing in. The novella was published in <strong>1886</strong>, during the height of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of British history during Queen Victoria’s reign (1837–1901), characterised by rapid industrialisation, strict moral codes and imperial expansion.">Victorian era</dfn>. Victorian society shaped every character, theme and plot twist in the story.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Victorian Respectability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Victorians placed enormous value on <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of being socially acceptable and morally correct, particularly important for middle- and upper-class Victorians.">respectability</dfn>. For middle- and upper-class men like Jekyll, Utterson and Enfield, reputation was everything. A gentleman was expected to be moral, restrained and rational at all times. Any sign of improper behaviour — gambling, drinking, sexual misconduct — could destroy a man’s standing in society.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">This obsession with appearances created a culture of <strong>hypocrisy</strong>. Many Victorians lived double lives: respectable in public, indulgent in private. Stevenson’s novella directly explores this idea through Jekyll’s transformation into Hyde.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which two contrasting areas of Victorian London Stevenson used to represent society's divide and moral duality.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Victorian London had a stark divide between the wealthy West End and the poverty-stricken East End. Stevenson uses settings like Soho to represent the hidden, darker side of respectable society.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Science and Darwin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Victorian period saw extraordinary scientific progress. Charles Darwin published <em>On the Origin of Species</em> in <strong>1859</strong>, arguing that humans had evolved from animals. This was deeply shocking to a society built on Christian beliefs. If humans were just advanced animals, perhaps the “beast” was still inside them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Stevenson taps into this fear. Hyde is described in animalistic terms — “like a monkey,” “troglodytic.” Jekyll’s experiment suggests that the primitive, violent side of human nature can never be fully suppressed, only hidden behind a mask of civilisation.</p>

Scientists feared that immoral behaviour, poverty and disease could cause people to regress into primitive creatures. Hyde embodies this fear — he is smaller, younger and more ape-like than Jekyll, as though he represents an earlier, less evolved version of humanity.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Religion and Morality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Victorian Britain was deeply religious. The Church taught that good and evil were separate forces, and that moral people could resist sin through willpower and faith. Jekyll’s experiment challenges this: it suggests that <strong>evil is not external but lives inside every person</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">This would have horrified Victorian readers. The idea that a respectable doctor could harbour murderous impulses struck at the heart of their moral framework.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the specific religious doctrine from Stevenson's Edinburgh upbringing that taught humans are born sinful.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stevenson was raised in a strict Calvinist household in Edinburgh. Calvinism taught that humans are born sinful, which directly influenced the novella’s exploration of innate evil.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A literary genre featuring dark settings, supernatural elements and themes of fear, death and the unknown.">Gothic literature</dfn> was hugely popular in the Victorian era. Writers like Mary Shelley (<em>Frankenstein</em>) and Bram Stoker (<em>Dracula</em>) explored fears about science, morality and the unknown. Stevenson’s novella fits squarely in the Gothic tradition: it features a mad scientist, a monstrous double, dark London streets and an atmosphere of creeping dread.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The Gothic genre allowed Victorian writers to explore taboo subjects — violence, desire, madness — in a way that “respectable” fiction could not.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Stevenson’s Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Robert Louis Stevenson was born in <strong>Edinburgh</strong> in 1850. Edinburgh itself was a city of contrasts: the elegant, respectable New Town and the dark, crowded Old Town existed side by side. This real-life duality influenced the novella’s exploration of hidden identities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Stevenson reportedly wrote the first draft of the novella in just <strong>three days</strong> after a vivid nightmare. His wife Fanny criticised the draft for being too much of a straightforward horror story, so he burned it and rewrote it as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A story with a deeper symbolic meaning beneath the surface narrative.">allegory</dfn> about the duality of human nature.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Stevenson was fascinated by the real-life story of <strong>Deacon Brodie</strong>, an 18th-century Edinburgh cabinet-maker who was a respected citizen by day and a burglar by night. Brodie was eventually caught and executed. Stevenson even co-wrote a play about him. The parallels with Jekyll and Hyde are obvious — Brodie was a real-life example of Victorian duality.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Victorian respectability created hypocrisy:</strong> The pressure to appear moral drove people to hide their true natures — exactly what Jekyll does.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Science versus religion:</strong> Darwin’s theory of evolution and degeneration theory created fears that humans were still part-animal. Hyde represents this primitive side.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Stevenson drew on real life:</strong> Edinburgh’s divided geography, Deacon Brodie’s double life and strict Calvinist upbringing all shaped the novella’s central theme of duality.</li></ul>
