<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 4: The Carew Murder Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Nearly a year after the events of Chapter 2, Hyde commits a far more serious crime. A maid witnesses him brutally beating an elderly gentleman — Sir Danvers Carew, a respected MP — to death with a heavy walking stick. The attack is <dfn class="term" data-def="Lacking any clear reason or motive; done without provocation.">unprovoked</dfn>: Carew had simply asked Hyde for directions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The violence is extreme. The maid describes how Hyde “broke out in a great flame of anger” and “clubbed him to the earth” with “ape-like fury.” The cane snaps in half under the force of the blows. Stevenson uses this scene to show that Hyde’s evil is <strong>escalating</strong> — from trampling a child to committing murder.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: Who originally owned the broken walking stick found at the murder scene, and what was its significance?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The broken walking stick had originally been Utterson’s gift to Jekyll, and was recognised by Utterson when it was presented to him as evidence. This detail physically connects Jekyll to the murder and foreshadows the eventual revelation that they are the same person.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Utterson takes the police to Hyde’s rooms in <strong>Soho</strong>. The setting is significant: Soho was one of Victorian London’s most disreputable areas, associated with poverty, crime and vice. Hyde’s rooms are furnished with luxury items but show signs of a hasty departure — ashes in the fireplace, a burnt cheque book. Hyde has vanished.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Stevenson describes Soho in fog and darkness: “a district of some city in a nightmare.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="Representing one thing through another; using concrete images to convey abstract ideas.">symbolism</dfn> is clear — Soho represents the hidden, immoral side of London that respectable Victorians preferred to ignore. The fog that blankets the streets mirrors the moral confusion surrounding Jekyll’s double life.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Chapter 5: The Letter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Utterson visits Jekyll, who is visibly shaken. Jekyll claims he has received a letter from Hyde promising to disappear forever. Jekyll asks Utterson to judge whether the letter should be given to the police. Utterson agrees to keep it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Later, Utterson’s clerk Mr Guest notices that the handwriting in Hyde’s letter is remarkably similar to Jekyll’s own handwriting — just slanted differently. Utterson is horrified: “Henry Jekyll forge for a murderer!” He assumes Jekyll is covering for Hyde, not yet suspecting the truth.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What crucial evidence about Jekyll and Hyde's handwriting does Stevenson reveal, and why doesn't Utterson act on it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The matching handwriting is a crucial clue that Jekyll and Hyde are the same person. Stevenson plants this evidence for the reader, but Utterson — bound by Victorian propriety — refuses to follow it to its logical conclusion.</p>
</div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chapter 6: Remarkable Incident of Dr Lanyon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Time passes. Hyde has disappeared, and Jekyll seems renewed — sociable, charitable and “at peace.” For two months, he appears to have conquered his demons. But then Jekyll suddenly withdraws from society, refusing all visitors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">When Utterson visits <strong>Dr Lanyon</strong>, he finds him shockingly changed — pale, thin and visibly dying. Lanyon says he has received “a shock” from which he will never recover. He refuses to discuss Jekyll, saying only: “I wish to see or hear no more of Dr Jekyll … I am quite done with that person.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Lanyon’s Horror</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Lanyon’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A fundamental change in attitude or character resulting from a traumatic experience.">transformation</dfn> is one of the novella’s most powerful moments. A once-confident, robust man has been reduced to a dying shell. Stevenson withholds the cause of his horror — we only learn what Lanyon witnessed in Chapter 9. This delayed revelation builds suspense and suggests that the truth is so terrible it literally kills the man who sees it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Lanyon dies shortly afterwards. He leaves Utterson a sealed letter, only to be opened after Jekyll’s death or disappearance. Utterson — ever the <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who acts with integrity and follows moral principles strictly.">man of principle</dfn> — respects Lanyon’s wishes and does not open it.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Structural Technique: Delayed Revelation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Stevenson uses sealed letters as a structural device throughout the novella. Jekyll’s will, Lanyon’s letter and Jekyll’s own “full statement” are all documents that contain the truth but are withheld from the reader. This creates a pattern of secrets and revelations that mirrors the theme of hidden identity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Hyde’s violence escalates:</strong> The Carew murder shows that evil, once unleashed, grows more extreme. The “ape-like fury” links to degeneration theory.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Clues are planted but ignored:</strong> The matching handwriting, the broken cane and Hyde’s connection to Jekyll are all present, but Utterson’s Victorian propriety prevents him from confronting the truth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Lanyon’s death foreshadows the terrible truth:</strong> Whatever he witnessed was so horrifying it destroyed him. Stevenson withholds this to maximise suspense.</li></ul>
