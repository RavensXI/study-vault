--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Stevenson uses Jekyll and Hyde to explore the duality of human nature and Victorian repression.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dr Henry Jekyll</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jekyll is a wealthy, respected doctor and scientist. He is “a large, well-made, smooth-faced man of fifty” who hosts dinner parties and donates to charity. He represents the ideal Victorian gentleman: moral, successful and generous.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">But Jekyll harbours a secret. In his “full statement,” he confesses that he always felt a <dfn class="term" data-def="The division of something into two opposed or contrasting parts.">duality</dfn> within himself. He describes “those provinces of good and ill which divide and compound man’s dual nature.” He was not a hypocrite in the usual sense — he genuinely wanted to be good. But the pressure of Victorian respectability meant he had to suppress his desires entirely, rather than finding a healthy balance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What was Jekyll's original motivation for creating Hyde, and what tragedy resulted from separating good from evil?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jekyll does not create Hyde to be evil. He creates Hyde to experience freedom — to indulge his “undignified” pleasures without risking his reputation. The tragedy is that separating good from evil makes both sides more extreme.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jekyll’s Tragic Arc</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Jekyll’s story follows a classic tragic pattern. His <dfn class="term" data-def="A fatal flaw in a character that leads to their downfall, from Greek tragedy.">hamartia</dfn> (tragic flaw) is his belief that he can control the experiment. At first, he transforms voluntarily and enjoys the freedom. Then Hyde grows stronger. The transformations become involuntary. Finally, the potion stops working, and Jekyll is trapped as Hyde permanently. His suicide is the only escape.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Stevenson suggests that <strong>repression</strong> is the true cause of Jekyll’s downfall. If Victorian society had allowed Jekyll to express all sides of himself, he would never have needed the experiment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Mr Edward Hyde</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Hyde is Jekyll’s alter ego — the physical embodiment of everything Jekyll represses. He is described as small, pale, and giving an impression of <dfn class="term" data-def="Physical irregularity or abnormality that deviates from normal appearance.">deformity</dfn>. Characters who encounter him feel instinctive disgust but cannot explain why.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Hyde’s appearance is deliberately vague. Stevenson uses words like “troglodytic,” “ape-like” and “hardly human” to suggest he is <strong>less evolved</strong> than a normal person. This connects to Victorian fears about <dfn class="term" data-def="The reversal of evolutionary progress; the idea that humans could regress to a more primitive state.">degeneration</dfn> — the idea that humanity could evolve backwards.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: Why is Hyde physically smaller than Jekyll, and what does this size difference symbolize about repression?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hyde is smaller than Jekyll because, as Jekyll explains, his evil side had “less exercise” — it had been suppressed for years. This physical detail reinforces the idea that repression stunts part of the self rather than eliminating it.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Hyde’s Escalating Violence</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Hyde’s violence escalates throughout the novella. He tramples a child (Chapter 1), murders Sir Danvers Carew with “ape-like fury” (Chapter 4), and becomes increasingly uncontrollable. Each act is more extreme than the last. Stevenson uses this escalation to show that evil, once released from restraint, does not stay moderate — it grows.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Crucially, Hyde feels no guilt. Jekyll describes him as “pure evil.” This distinguishes Hyde from Jekyll, who does feel remorse but uses the separation of identity to avoid taking full responsibility.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Relationship Between Jekyll and Hyde</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Jekyll and Hyde are not simply “good” and “evil.” Jekyll himself is not entirely good — he created Hyde out of selfish desire for freedom. And Hyde is not a separate person but a part of Jekyll that has always existed. The novella’s central argument is that <strong>everyone contains both good and evil</strong>, and that trying to deny this makes things worse.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jekyll’s Self-Deception</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Even in his final confession, Jekyll refers to Hyde in the third person: “He, I say — I cannot say I.” He tries to maintain the fiction that Hyde is a separate being. But this is <dfn class="term" data-def="The act of misleading oneself about the true nature of a situation or one’s own motives.">self-deception</dfn>. Jekyll created Hyde, enjoyed being Hyde, and is ultimately destroyed by Hyde. They are the same person.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Stevenson uses this self-deception to criticise Victorian society more broadly. Just as Jekyll refuses to acknowledge his darker self, Victorian society refused to acknowledge the poverty, vice and cruelty that existed beneath its respectable surface.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Jekyll is not simply ‘good’:</strong> He creates Hyde out of selfish desire and refuses to take full responsibility. His tragic flaw is believing he can control the experiment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Hyde embodies degeneration:</strong> His small stature, ape-like features and escalating violence connect to Victorian fears about evolutionary regression.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Repression causes destruction:</strong> Stevenson argues that suppressing one side of human nature does not eliminate it — it makes it more dangerous when it finally emerges.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When writing about Jekyll and Hyde, avoid the common mistake of treating them as completely separate characters. Examiners want to see that you understand they are <strong>two sides of the same person</strong>. Use Jekyll’s own quotation — “man is not truly one, but truly two” — to anchor your argument.</p>
