--- DESCRIPTION ---
Rules for current, voltage and resistance in series and parallel circuits, plus I-V characteristics.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Series Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit where components are connected one after another in a single loop.">series circuit</dfn>, all components are connected in a single loop. There is only one path for current to flow, so the current is the same at every point in the circuit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the three rules for series circuits including the equation for total resistance using R₁, R₂ and R₃."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In a series circuit: current is the same everywhere, potential differences add up to equal the supply voltage, and total resistance equals the sum of all individual resistances: \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The voltage across a component, measured in volts.">potential difference</dfn> is shared between components in series. The larger the resistance of a component, the greater its share of the total voltage. If two identical resistors are in series, each gets half the supply voltage.</p>

<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Series Circuit</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">A 12 V battery is connected in series with a 4 Ω resistor and an 8 Ω resistor. Calculate the current and the voltage across each resistor.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Step 1: Total resistance \(R_{\text{total}} = 4 + 8 = 12 \; \Omega\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Step 2: Current \(I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{12}{12} = 1\) A (same throughout)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Step 3: \(V_1 = IR_1 = 1 \times 4 = 4\) V across the 4 Ω resistor</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Step 4: \(V_2 = IR_2 = 1 \times 8 = 8\) V across the 8 Ω resistor</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Check: \(4 + 8 = 12\) V, which equals the supply voltage.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Parallel Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit where components are connected across each other, providing multiple paths for current.">parallel circuit</dfn>, components are connected on separate branches. Each branch provides a different path for the current to flow through.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State what's the same across parallel branches, how currents combine, and how total resistance compares to individual values."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In a parallel circuit: potential difference is the same across each branch, currents in the branches add up to give the total current from the supply, and total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance.</p>
</div>



<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n14">The total resistance of resistors in parallel is found using:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">$$\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Adding more resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance, because there are more paths for the current. This means the total current from the battery increases.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Parallel Circuit</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17">Two resistors (6 Ω and 3 Ω) are connected in parallel across a 12 V supply. Calculate the current in each branch and the total current.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Branch 1: \(I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\) A</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Branch 2: \(I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2} = \frac{12}{3} = 4\) A</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Total current: \(I_{\text{total}} = 2 + 4 = 6\) A</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Investigating Components</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">You need to know the I-V characteristics of three components: <dfn class="term" data-def="A component with a resistance that stays constant as current changes, giving a straight-line I-V graph.">fixed resistors</dfn> (ohmic conductors), <dfn class="term" data-def="A thin coiled wire whose resistance increases as it heats up.">filament lamps</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A semiconductor device that only allows current to flow in one direction.">diodes</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">A fixed resistor gives a straight line through the origin on an I-V graph — current is directly proportional to voltage (Ohm’s law). A filament lamp gives a curve: as current increases, the wire heats up and resistance increases. A diode only conducts in one direction — it has very high resistance in reverse bias.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n24">To investigate the I-V characteristics of a component, connect it in series with a variable resistor, ammeter, and power supply. Connect a voltmeter in parallel across the component. Vary the resistance using the variable resistor to change the current and voltage. Record pairs of I and V values, then swap the power supply connections to get negative values. Plot V on the x-axis and I on the y-axis.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n25">Two other important components are the <dfn class="term" data-def="A resistor whose resistance decreases as temperature increases.">thermistor</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A resistor whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases.">LDR</dfn> (light-dependent resistor). A thermistor’s resistance drops as temperature rises, making it useful in temperature sensors. An LDR’s resistance drops as light intensity increases, making it useful in automatic lighting circuits.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Series: same current, voltages add up, \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2\); Parallel: same voltage, currents add up, \(\frac{1}{R_T} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\)</li><li data-narration-id="n29">I-V graphs: resistors give straight lines, filament lamps curve, diodes only conduct in one direction</li><li data-narration-id="n30">Thermistor resistance decreases with temperature; LDR resistance decreases with light intensity</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">A common exam mistake is mixing up series and parallel rules. Remember: in series, current is the same everywhere and voltages add up; in parallel, voltage is the same across branches and currents add up. When asked to “describe” I-V characteristics, refer to the shape of the graph and what happens to resistance.</p>
