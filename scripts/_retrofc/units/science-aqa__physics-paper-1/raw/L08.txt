--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the atomic model developed, types of nuclear radiation, and calculating half-life.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Model of the Atom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Our understanding of atomic structure has developed over time. The first model was the <dfn class="term" data-def="An early atomic model suggesting atoms are solid spheres that cannot be divided.">solid sphere model</dfn> proposed by Dalton in the early 1800s. He imagined atoms as tiny, indivisible balls.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">In 1897, J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the <dfn class="term" data-def="A model of the atom as a ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it, like plums in a pudding.">plum pudding model</dfn> — a sphere of positive charge with negative electrons scattered throughout, like plums in a pudding.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which three scientists conducted the 1909 gold foil experiment and what it proved about atomic structure."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In 1909, Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but some were deflected and a few bounced back. This proved the atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with electrons orbiting at a distance — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The model of the atom with a small, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by orbiting electrons.">nuclear model</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Bohr refined the nuclear model by proposing that electrons orbit the nucleus at specific distances in fixed energy levels (shells). Niels Bohr’s model explained why atoms emit and absorb specific frequencies of electromagnetic radiation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Later, James Chadwick discovered the <dfn class="term" data-def="A subatomic particle found in the nucleus with no charge and a relative mass of 1.">neutron</dfn> in 1932, completing our picture of the atom: a nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in energy levels.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Radioactive Decay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Some atomic nuclei are <dfn class="term" data-def="An unstable nucleus that emits radiation to become more stable.">radioactive</dfn> — they are unstable and emit radiation to become more stable. This process is called radioactive decay and is random. You cannot predict when a particular nucleus will decay.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the three types of nuclear radiation using their Greek letter symbols and state they have different properties."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There are three types of nuclear radiation: alpha (\(\alpha\)) particles, beta (\(\beta\)) particles, and gamma (\(\gamma\)) rays. Each has different properties, penetrating power, and ionising ability.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Alpha (\(\alpha\))</strong> — two protons and two neutrons (a helium nucleus). Strongly ionising, stopped by paper or a few centimetres of air. The atomic number decreases by 2 and the mass number decreases by 4.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Beta (\(\beta\))</strong> — a high-speed electron emitted when a neutron turns into a proton. Moderately ionising, stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. The atomic number increases by 1, mass number stays the same.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Gamma (\(\gamma\))</strong> — an electromagnetic wave with no mass or charge. Weakly ionising, only reduced significantly by several centimetres of lead or thick concrete. The atomic number and mass number are unchanged.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Half-Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay, or for the activity to halve.">half-life</dfn> of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half the unstable nuclei to decay, or for the count rate (activity) to fall to half its original value.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">After one half-life, half the original nuclei remain. After two half-lives, a quarter remain. After three half-lives, one-eighth remain, and so on.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Half-Life Calculation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">A sample has an activity of 800 Bq. After 30 minutes, the activity is 100 Bq. Calculate the half-life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">800 → 400 → 200 → 100 = three half-lives</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Three half-lives = 30 minutes, so one half-life = \(\frac{30}{3} = 10\) minutes.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Uses and Hazards of Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Nuclear radiation has useful applications: gamma rays are used in medical tracers and to sterilise equipment; beta radiation is used in thickness gauges; alpha sources are used in smoke detectors.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Nuclear Contamination and Irradiation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The unwanted presence of radioactive material on or inside an object or person.">Contamination</dfn> occurs when radioactive material gets onto or inside the body. This is more dangerous because the source stays close to cells. <dfn class="term" data-def="Exposure to radiation from an external source, without the radioactive material being on the object.">Irradiation</dfn> is exposure to radiation from an outside source — once the source is removed, the exposure stops.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Precautions include: keeping radioactive sources in lead-lined containers, using tongs to handle sources, wearing protective clothing, and minimising exposure time.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n25">The atomic model evolved from solid spheres through plum pudding to the nuclear model after Rutherford’s gold foil experiment</li><li data-narration-id="n26">Three types of radiation: alpha (helium nucleus, most ionising), beta (electron, moderate), gamma (EM wave, least ionising)</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Half-life is the time for half the unstable nuclei to decay; contamination is more hazardous than irradiation</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">Half-life questions often ask you to read values from a graph. Find the starting activity, halve it, and read across to the time axis. If asked for the fraction remaining after several half-lives, remember: each half-life halves what’s left. Know the difference between contamination (material on you) and irradiation (radiation from outside).</p>
