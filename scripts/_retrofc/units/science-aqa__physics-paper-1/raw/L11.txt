--- DESCRIPTION ---
How nuclear fission and fusion release energy and how radiation is used.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Nuclear Fission</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and neutrons.">Nuclear fission</dfn> is the splitting of a large, unstable nucleus into two smaller daughter nuclei. This process releases a large amount of energy, along with two or three neutrons and gamma radiation. Fission can occur spontaneously in very unstable isotopes, but in nuclear reactors it is induced by bombarding a fissile nucleus (such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239) with a slow-moving neutron.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">When a uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron, it becomes unstable and splits. The two daughter nuclei and the released neutrons carry away kinetic energy. The total mass of the products is slightly less than the mass of the original nucleus plus the neutron — this “missing” mass has been converted into energy according to Einstein’s equation \(E = mc^2\).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall what happens when at least one neutron from each fission causes another fission event.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In nuclear fission, the neutrons released can go on to cause further fission events. If at least one neutron from each fission causes another fission, a self-sustaining <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of nuclear fission reactions where neutrons released by one fission event trigger further fission events.">chain reaction</dfn> occurs.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How a Nuclear Reactor Works</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">In a nuclear power station, the chain reaction must be controlled so that exactly one neutron from each fission event causes another fission. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rods made of a neutron-absorbing material (such as boron) that are raised or lowered into a reactor core to control the rate of fission.">Control rods</dfn> (made of boron or cadmium) absorb excess neutrons. Lowering the rods into the reactor core slows the reaction; raising them speeds it up.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A material in a nuclear reactor that slows down fast neutrons so they can be absorbed by fuel nuclei.">moderator</dfn> (usually water or graphite) slows down fast-moving neutrons. Slow neutrons are more likely to be absorbed by uranium-235 nuclei, making fission more likely. The energy from fission heats a coolant (often water), which produces steam to drive turbines connected to generators, producing electricity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Nuclear Fusion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The joining of two small, light nuclei to form a larger nucleus, releasing energy.">Nuclear fusion</dfn> is the process where two light nuclei join together to form a larger nucleus. This releases a huge amount of energy — even more per kilogram than fission. Fusion is the process that powers stars, including our Sun. In the Sun’s core, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium at temperatures of around 15 million °C.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Fusion is difficult to achieve on Earth because the nuclei are positively charged and repel each other. To overcome this electrostatic repulsion, extremely high temperatures and pressures are needed to force the nuclei close enough together for the strong nuclear force to take over and bind them. Maintaining these conditions long enough to sustain a fusion reaction is a major engineering challenge.</p>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why both fission and fusion release energy using the terms binding energy per nucleon and E = mc².">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both fission and fusion release energy because the products have a higher binding energy per nucleon than the reactants. The “missing” mass is converted to energy via \(E = mc^2\).</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fusion vs Fission: A Comparison</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Fission splits large nuclei (like uranium-235) and is used in current nuclear power stations. It produces radioactive waste that must be stored safely for thousands of years. Fusion joins small nuclei (like hydrogen isotopes) and produces far less radioactive waste. However, no commercially viable fusion reactor exists yet because of the extreme conditions required.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Both processes convert a small amount of mass into a large amount of energy. Fusion releases more energy per kilogram of fuel and uses hydrogen (abundant in seawater) as fuel, making it a potentially clean and almost unlimited energy source for the future.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Uses of Nuclear Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Nuclear radiation has many practical applications beyond power generation. In medicine, gamma rays are used in radiotherapy to destroy cancer cells. A focused beam of gamma radiation is aimed at a tumour from multiple angles, concentrating the dose on the cancer while minimising damage to healthy tissue. Radioactive <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance containing a radioactive isotope that is introduced into the body so its movement or concentration can be tracked using a detector.">tracers</dfn> (such as technetium-99m) are injected into the body and tracked using a gamma camera to diagnose conditions like blocked blood vessels or organ function problems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In industry, beta or gamma sources are used to monitor the thickness of materials during manufacturing. If the material gets too thick, less radiation passes through to the detector, triggering an adjustment. Gamma radiation is also used to sterilise medical equipment and food, killing bacteria without chemicals or heat.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Choosing the Right Type of Radiation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The choice of radiation type depends on its penetrating power and ionising ability. Alpha particles are highly ionising but have very low penetration — they are stopped by a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper. They are used in smoke detectors, where alpha particles ionise air between two electrodes. When smoke enters, it absorbs the alpha particles, the current drops, and the alarm sounds.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Beta particles penetrate further (stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium) and are used for thickness monitoring of thin materials like paper or aluminium foil. Gamma rays are the most penetrating (only significantly reduced by thick lead or concrete) and are chosen for sterilisation and medical imaging because they can pass through the body.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Nuclear Waste and Safety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Nuclear fission produces radioactive waste, which is categorised as low-level, intermediate-level or high-level depending on its activity and half-life. Low-level waste (contaminated clothing, tools) is compacted and stored in lined, shallow trenches. Intermediate-level waste is mixed with concrete and stored in steel containers. High-level waste (spent fuel rods) generates significant heat and must be cooled before being vitrified (turned into glass) and stored deep underground in geological repositories.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Safety precautions when handling radioactive materials include: keeping exposure time as short as possible, maintaining maximum distance from the source, using appropriate shielding (lead-lined containers, thick concrete walls), and monitoring exposure with film badges or electronic dosimeters.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Nuclear fission splits large unstable nuclei, releasing energy and neutrons that sustain a chain reaction; reactors use control rods and moderators to manage this.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Nuclear fusion joins small nuclei at extreme temperatures and pressures, releasing even more energy per kilogram than fission; it powers stars but is not yet viable on Earth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Nuclear radiation is used in medicine (radiotherapy, tracers), industry (thickness monitoring, sterilisation) and the home (smoke detectors), with the type chosen based on penetrating power.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n21">When comparing fission and fusion, make sure you clearly state which involves splitting and which involves joining. A common mistake is confusing the two. For questions about nuclear reactors, name the function of each component (control rods, moderator, coolant) separately — do not merge their roles.</p>
