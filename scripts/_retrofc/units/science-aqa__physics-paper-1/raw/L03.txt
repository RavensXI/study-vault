--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Electric Current and Charge</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Electric current is the flow of <dfn class="term" data-def="Particles that carry electric charge, such as electrons.">charge carriers</dfn> through a conductor. In metals, these charge carriers are electrons that move through the wire when a voltage is applied.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the equation for current and state what I, Q and t represent including their units."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Current is measured in amperes (A) and represents the amount of charge flowing past a point per second. The equation is \(I = \frac{Q}{t}\) where I is current, Q is charge in coulombs, and t is time in seconds.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n4">Current is measured using an <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument that measures electric current, connected in series with the component.">ammeter</dfn>, which must be connected in series with the component you want to measure. This ensures all the current flows through the ammeter.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n5">Potential Difference</h2><p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred per unit charge between two points in a circuit.">Potential difference</dfn> (also called voltage) is the driving force that pushes current around a circuit. It represents the energy transferred per coulomb of charge.</p><p data-narration-id="n7">The equation for potential difference is:</p><p data-narration-id="n8">$$V = \frac{W}{Q}$$</p><p data-narration-id="n9">Where V is potential difference in volts, W is work done (energy transferred) in joules, and Q is charge in coulombs.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Measuring Potential Difference</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n10">Potential difference is measured using a <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument that measures potential difference, connected in parallel with the component.">voltmeter</dfn>. Unlike an ammeter, a voltmeter must be connected in parallel with the component. This allows it to measure the energy difference between the two points without affecting the current flow.</p><p data-narration-id="n11">Think of potential difference like the height difference in a waterfall — the greater the height difference, the more energy each drop of water has when it reaches the bottom.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n12">Resistance and Ohm's Law</h2><p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The opposition to current flow in a circuit, measured in ohms.">Resistance</dfn> opposes the flow of current in a circuit. Different materials and components have different resistances — metals have low resistance while insulators have very high resistance.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write Ohm's Law equation, define V, I and R with units, and state when this relationship applies."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Ohm's Law states that \(V = I \times R\) where V is potential difference in volts, I is current in amperes, and R is resistance in ohms (Ω). This relationship only applies to ohmic conductors at constant temperature.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n15">You can rearrange Ohm's law to find any of the three quantities:</p><p data-narration-id="n16">$$I = \frac{V}{R} \text{ and } R = \frac{V}{I}$$</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Factors Affecting Resistance</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n17">The resistance of a wire depends on four factors: its length (longer = higher resistance), cross-sectional area (thicker = lower resistance), material (different metals have different resistivity), and temperature (higher temperature usually = higher resistance for metals).</p><p data-narration-id="n18">Some components like filament bulbs are non-ohmic — their resistance changes with temperature, so they don't follow Ohm's law exactly.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n19">Series Circuits</h2><p data-narration-id="n20">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit where components are connected end-to-end in a single loop.">series circuit</dfn>, components are connected end-to-end in a single loop. Current has only one path to follow.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the three key equations for series circuits: current relationship, voltage relationship, and resistance relationship."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>In series circuits: current is the same everywhere (\(I_1 = I_2 = I_3\)), potential difference is shared between components (\(V_{total} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\)), and total resistance equals the sum of individual resistances (\(R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\)).</p></div><p data-narration-id="n22">If one component fails in a series circuit, the whole circuit stops working because the current path is broken. This is why old Christmas lights used to all go out when one bulb failed.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n23">Parallel Circuits</h2><p data-narration-id="n24">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit where components are connected on separate branches, each with its own path for current.">parallel circuit</dfn>, components are connected on separate branches. Current can follow multiple paths.</p><p data-narration-id="n25">In parallel circuits, each component receives the full supply voltage. This is why household appliances are connected in parallel — each device gets the full 230V regardless of what other appliances are switched on.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Parallel Circuit Rules</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n26">In parallel circuits: potential difference is the same across each branch (\(V_1 = V_2 = V_3\)), total current equals the sum of branch currents (\(I_{total} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3\)), and total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance.</p><div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n27">The formula for total resistance in parallel is: \(\frac{1}{R_{total}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}\)</p>
</div></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n28">Electrical Power</h2><p data-narration-id="n29">Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer in a circuit. It tells us how quickly electrical energy is being converted to other forms like heat, light or motion.</p><p data-narration-id="n30">The basic power equation is:</p><p data-narration-id="n31">$$P = V \times I$$</p><p data-narration-id="n32">Where P is power in watts, V is potential difference in volts, and I is current in amperes. By combining this with Ohm's law, we get two more useful equations: \(P = I^2R\) and \(P = \frac{V^2}{R}\).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n35">Current is the flow of charge (\(I = Q/t\)), potential difference is energy per charge (\(V = W/Q\)), and they're related by Ohm's law (\(V = IR\))</li><li data-narration-id="n36">In series circuits: same current everywhere, voltages add up, resistances add up</li><li data-narration-id="n37">In parallel circuits: same voltage on each branch, currents add up, total resistance is less than any individual resistance</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">When drawing circuit diagrams, always use the correct symbols and show ammeters in series, voltmeters in parallel. For calculations, identify what you're given and what you need to find, then choose the appropriate equation. Remember that in series circuits everything adds up (V and R), but in parallel circuits only current adds up.</p>
