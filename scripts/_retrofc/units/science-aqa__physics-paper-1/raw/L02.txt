--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why energy is always conserved, how to calculate efficiency, and the pros and cons of energy resources.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Conservation of Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred between stores.">law of conservation of energy</dfn> states that energy can never be created or destroyed. In any energy transfer, the total energy before and after is always the same.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">In a closed system, where nothing can enter or leave, the total energy stays constant. However, in most real-world situations energy is <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy that spreads out to the surroundings and becomes less useful, often as thermal energy.">dissipated</dfn> — it spreads out to the surroundings as thermal energy and becomes increasingly difficult to use.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what we really mean when we say energy is 'wasted' and what happens to the total energy in the system."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Energy is never “lost” — when we say energy is wasted, we mean it has been transferred to less useful stores (usually thermal energy in the surroundings). The total energy in the system has not changed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The fraction of total input energy that is transferred to useful output energy.">Efficiency</dfn> tells you how good a device is at transferring energy to useful stores. It is calculated using:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy}}{\text{total input energy}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Efficiency can also be calculated using power:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output power}}{\text{total input power}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Efficiency is a ratio and has no units. It is always between 0 and 1. To express it as a percentage, multiply by 100. No device can ever be more than 100% efficient.</p>

<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11">A motor uses 500 J of electrical energy and transfers 350 J to the kinetic store. Calculate the efficiency.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">\(\text{efficiency} = \frac{350}{500} = 0.70\) or 70%</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">This means 70% of the input energy is usefully transferred. The remaining 30% (150 J) is dissipated as thermal energy due to friction and sound.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list three specific ways to increase efficiency, including lubrication, insulation, and design improvements."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Ways to increase efficiency include reducing friction (lubrication, streamlining), using thermal insulation to reduce unwanted heating, and improving design to minimise energy dissipation.</p>
</div>
</div>



<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Reducing Unwanted Energy Transfers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A material that reduces the rate of thermal energy transfer.">Thermal insulation</dfn> reduces the rate of energy transfer by heating. In a house, loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, and double glazing all reduce the amount of thermal energy lost to the surroundings.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Lubrication reduces friction between moving parts. This means less energy is wasted as heat in the thermal store, so more energy goes to the useful output.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Insulation in Buildings</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">The thicker the walls of a house, the slower the rate of thermal energy transfer. Cavity wall insulation traps air between the inner and outer walls. Since air is a poor conductor, this reduces energy transfer by conduction. Double glazing works on the same principle — a layer of gas between two panes of glass reduces conduction and convection.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div><h2 data-narration-id="n19">Energy Resources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Energy resources can be classified as <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that will run out and cannot be replaced on a human timescale, such as fossil fuels and nuclear.">non-renewable</dfn> or <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources that are replenished naturally and will not run out, such as solar, wind, and tidal.">renewable</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Non-renewable resources include fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear fuel. These are finite — once used up, they cannot be replaced within a human timescale.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n22">Renewable resources include solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, geothermal, wave power, and biofuels. These are replenished naturally and will not run out.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Comparing Energy Resources</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n23">Fossil fuels are reliable and produce large amounts of energy, but release carbon dioxide (contributing to climate change) and are running out. Nuclear power is reliable and produces no carbon dioxide during operation, but produces radioactive waste that must be stored safely for thousands of years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Renewable resources produce no greenhouse gases during operation and will not run out. However, many are weather-dependent (solar, wind) or location-dependent (tidal, geothermal), and some have high initial costs or low power output compared to fossil fuels.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n25">The UK is working towards generating more electricity from renewable sources to reduce carbon emissions and meet climate targets. However, a mix of energy resources is needed to ensure a reliable supply.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Energy is conserved in all transfers — wasted energy is dissipated to the surroundings, usually as thermal energy</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Efficiency = useful output / total input (always between 0 and 1); improved by reducing friction and adding insulation</li><li data-narration-id="n30">Energy resources are either renewable (replenished naturally) or non-renewable (finite); the UK needs a mix for reliable supply</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">In efficiency questions, always check whether the answer should be a decimal or a percentage. If the question says “calculate the efficiency,” give a decimal unless it specifically asks for a percentage. When comparing energy resources, use specific advantages and disadvantages — “it is bad for the environment” is too vague. Say “it releases carbon dioxide which contributes to climate change.”</p>
