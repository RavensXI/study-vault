<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How the Atomic Model Has Developed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Our understanding of atomic structure has been revised several times as new experimental evidence emerged. The earliest scientific model, proposed by Dalton in the early 1800s, described atoms as tiny, indivisible solid spheres &mdash; a <dfn class="term" data-def="The earliest scientific model of the atom, proposed by John Dalton, describing atoms as tiny, solid, indivisible spheres.">solid sphere model</dfn> that could not explain electrical phenomena.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">In 1897, J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the <dfn class="term" data-def="A model of the atom proposed by J.J. Thomson in which the atom consists of a diffuse sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded within it, resembling plums in a pudding.">plum pudding model</dfn>. The atom was described as a ball of positive charge with negative electrons scattered throughout it. This model was overturned by Ernest Rutherford&rsquo;s gold foil experiment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: who conducted the gold foil experiment, when, and what three observations led to the nuclear model?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1909, Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden fired alpha particles at a thin gold foil. Most passed straight through (atom is mostly empty space); some deflected at large angles; a very few bounced straight back (dense positive nucleus exists). This led to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The model of the atom in which a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus is surrounded by negatively charged electrons at relatively large distances.">nuclear model</dfn> of the atom.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Niels Bohr (1913) refined the nuclear model by proposing that electrons orbit the nucleus only in specific, fixed energy levels or shells. This explained why atoms emit and absorb specific frequencies of electromagnetic radiation. James Chadwick completed the picture in 1932 by discovering the <dfn class="term" data-def="A subatomic particle found in the nucleus with no electric charge and a relative mass of 1.">neutron</dfn>, confirming that nuclei contain both protons and neutrons.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The structure of an atom is described using two numbers in the standard notation \(^A_Z\text{X}\): the <strong>mass (nucleon) number</strong> \(A\) gives the total number of protons plus neutrons; the <strong>atomic (proton) number</strong> \(Z\) gives the number of protons. Electrons orbit the nucleus at set distances, and atoms are electrically neutral because the number of electrons equals the number of protons.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Radioactive Decay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Some nuclei are unstable and emit radiation to become more stable. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="The spontaneous, random emission of radiation from an unstable atomic nucleus as it loses energy to reach a more stable state.">radioactive decay</dfn>. It is a <strong>random</strong> process &mdash; it is impossible to predict when a specific nucleus will decay, but for a large sample of nuclei the statistical behaviour is predictable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Radioactive decay can be detected using a <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that detects ionising radiation by counting pulses produced when radiation enters a gas-filled tube and ionises the gas.">Geiger-M&uuml;ller tube</dfn> (GM tube) or photographic film. Any measurement of radioactivity must subtract background radiation &mdash; the low-level ionising radiation always present from natural and artificial sources.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the three types of nuclear radiation, their composition, and their relative ionising and penetrating powers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three types of ionising radiation are emitted from unstable nuclei: alpha (\(\alpha\)), beta-minus (\(\beta^-\)), beta-plus (\(\beta^+\)) and gamma (\(\gamma\)). Also, neutron emission is possible. All four are ionising. Alpha is most ionising but least penetrating; gamma is least ionising but most penetrating.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Properties of Alpha, Beta and Gamma Radiation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Alpha (\(\alpha\)) particle:</strong> two protons and two neutrons &mdash; equivalent to a helium-4 nucleus (\(^4_2\text{He}\)). Alpha particles are strongly ionising but have very low penetrating power: they are stopped by a few centimetres of air or a sheet of paper. In alpha decay, the parent nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons: mass number decreases by 4, proton number decreases by 2.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Beta-minus (\(\beta^-\)) decay:</strong> a neutron transforms into a proton, emitting a high-speed electron. The proton number increases by 1; the mass number stays the same. Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Moderately ionising.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Beta-plus (\(\beta^+\)) / positron emission:</strong> a proton transforms into a neutron, emitting a positron (the antimatter equivalent of an electron). The proton number decreases by 1; the mass number stays the same. Used in PET scanning (positron emission tomography).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Gamma (\(\gamma\)) radiation:</strong> high-frequency electromagnetic radiation emitted from the nucleus after alpha or beta decay, as the nucleus loses excess energy. No change to proton or mass number. Only significantly reduced by several centimetres of lead or thick concrete. Weakly ionising but highly penetrating.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Balanced Nuclear Equations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In a nuclear equation, both the total mass number and the total proton number must be conserved (balanced) on both sides of the arrow. The general rule: \(\sum A\) and \(\sum Z\) are each equal before and after the decay.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Examples: Balancing Nuclear Equations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Alpha decay of radium-226:</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">$$^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow ^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Check: top (226 = 222 + 4 ✓); bottom (88 = 86 + 2 ✓).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Beta-minus decay of carbon-14:</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Check: top (14 = 14 + 0 ✓); bottom (6 = 7 + (&minus;1) ✓).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Beta-plus decay:</strong> proton number decreases by 1, mass number unchanged, positron emitted (\(^0_{+1}\text{e}\)).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write a balanced nuclear equation for alpha decay of an arbitrary nucleus, checking that both mass number and proton number balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In any nuclear equation: mass numbers (top) must balance on both sides; proton numbers (bottom) must balance on both sides. Alpha: &minus;4 mass, &minus;2 proton. Beta-minus: 0 mass, +1 proton. Beta-plus: 0 mass, &minus;1 proton. Gamma: no change to either number.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Half-Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The time for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay, or equivalently the time for the activity (count rate) of a radioactive source to halve.">half-life</dfn> of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for half the undecayed nuclei to decay, or for the activity to fall to half its original value. Because decay is random, it cannot be predicted when a specific nucleus will decay &mdash; but half-life allows us to predict the statistical behaviour of a very large number of nuclei.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n28">After one half-life: \(\frac{1}{2}\) of the original nuclei remain. After two half-lives: \(\frac{1}{4}\) remain. After three half-lives: \(\frac{1}{8}\) remain. The activity decreases by the same ratio each half-life.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Half-Life Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29">A radioactive sample has an activity of 1,600 Bq. After 40 minutes the activity is 200 Bq. Calculate the half-life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Number of halvings: \(1600 \rightarrow 800 \rightarrow 400 \rightarrow 200\) = three half-lives.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Three half-lives = 40 minutes, so one half-life = \(40 \div 3 \approx 13.3\) minutes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">Common error: do not confuse &lsquo;fraction remaining&rsquo; with &lsquo;fraction decayed&rsquo;. After 3 half-lives, \(\frac{1}{8}\) remains and \(\frac{7}{8}\) has decayed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Background Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34"><dfn class="term" data-def="The low-level ionising radiation always present in the environment from natural and artificial sources, including radon gas, cosmic rays, medical procedures and nuclear industry.">Background radiation</dfn> comes from natural sources (radon gas from the ground, cosmic rays, food and drink containing trace radioactive isotopes, building materials) and artificial sources (medical procedures such as X-rays, emissions from the nuclear industry). It must always be subtracted from measured count rates to obtain the count rate from a source alone.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">The atomic model evolved from Dalton&rsquo;s solid sphere through Thomson&rsquo;s plum pudding to Rutherford&rsquo;s nuclear model (1909 gold foil experiment) and Bohr&rsquo;s shell model; Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Alpha (\(^4_2\text{He}\): &minus;4 mass, &minus;2 proton), beta-minus (0 mass, +1 proton), beta-plus (0 mass, &minus;1 proton) and gamma (no change) each have distinct penetrating and ionising properties; nuclear equations must balance mass number and proton number on both sides.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Half-life is the time for activity to halve; after \(n\) half-lives, \((\frac{1}{2})^n\) of the original nuclei remain; always subtract background radiation before calculating.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">The most common errors in nuclear equation questions are: writing the wrong sign on the beta particle (beta-minus is \(^0_{-1}\text{e}\), not \(^0_{+1}\text{e}\)); forgetting that gamma emission does not change either number; and mixing up &lsquo;fraction remaining&rsquo; with &lsquo;fraction decayed&rsquo; in half-life problems. When reading a half-life from a graph, read down from the y-axis to where the activity halves, then across to the x-axis &mdash; always show your construction lines on graph questions. Remember to subtract background radiation before plotting if a background count is given.</p>
