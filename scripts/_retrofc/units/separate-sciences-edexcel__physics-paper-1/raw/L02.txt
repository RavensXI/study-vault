<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Resultant Forces and Newton&rsquo;s First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The single force that has the same effect as all the individual forces acting on an object combined; found by adding forces as vectors.">resultant force</dfn> is the overall force on an object once all individual forces are combined. Forces are vectors, so direction matters: two forces of 10 N in opposite directions give a resultant of zero; two forces of 10 N in the same direction give a resultant of 20 N.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in uniform motion remains in uniform motion, unless acted on by a resultant force.">Newton&rsquo;s first law</dfn> (Sir Isaac Newton, 1643&ndash;1727, published in <em>Principia Mathematica</em>, 1687) states that an object will remain at rest or continue to move at a constant velocity unless a <em>non-zero</em> resultant force acts on it. In other words: no resultant force means no change in motion. This is also why <dfn class="term" data-def="The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion; greater for objects with more mass.">inertia</dfn> &mdash; an object&rsquo;s resistance to change in motion &mdash; increases with mass.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State Newton's first law in full and explain what happens to velocity when the resultant force on a moving object becomes zero.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Newton&rsquo;s first law: a resultant force of zero means no change in motion. An object at rest stays at rest; a moving object keeps moving at constant velocity. Inertia resists changes &mdash; heavier objects have more inertia and are harder to accelerate or stop.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Newton&rsquo;s Second Law and F = ma</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The resultant force acting on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration: F = ma.">Newton&rsquo;s second law</dfn> states that the resultant force on an object is directly proportional to its acceleration and inversely proportional to its mass:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$F = m a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Where \(F\) is resultant force in newtons (N), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(a\) is acceleration in m/s<sup>2</sup>. Rearranging: \(a = F/m\) and \(m = F/a\).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">In this core practical, a trolley on a smooth runway is accelerated by hanging masses on a string over a pulley. Light gates measure the time for the trolley to pass through, allowing calculation of velocity and acceleration. The independent variable is the force applied (changed by moving masses from the trolley to the hanger, keeping total mass constant); the dependent variable is acceleration; controlled variables include total system mass, surface friction, and runway angle.</p>

Keeping total mass constant is essential &mdash; if you add mass to the hanger without removing it from the trolley, both force and mass change, so results are meaningless.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Newton&rsquo;s Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Whenever two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other; the forces act on different objects and are of the same type.">Newton&rsquo;s third law</dfn> states that for every force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force. The key points: the two forces are equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, act on <em>different</em> objects, and are the same type of force. A swimmer pushes water backwards (action); the water pushes the swimmer forwards (reaction).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Students often confuse Newton&rsquo;s third law with balanced forces. Balanced forces act on the <em>same</em> object (e.g. weight and normal reaction on a resting book). Newton&rsquo;s third law pairs always act on <em>different</em> objects: the book pushes down on the table (gravity transmitted), the table pushes up on the book.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Weight and Terminal Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The gravitational force acting on an object due to its mass; W = mg, measured in newtons.">weight</dfn> of an object is the gravitational force on it:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">$$W = m g$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Where \(W\) is weight in newtons, \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(g\) is gravitational field strength (10 N/kg near Earth&rsquo;s surface). Mass is the amount of matter in an object (kg); weight changes with gravitational field strength; mass does not.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">When an object falls through a fluid (air or liquid), <dfn class="term" data-def="A force opposing an object&rsquo;s motion through a fluid, increasing with speed; also called air resistance in air.">drag</dfn> increases as speed increases. Eventually drag equals weight and the resultant force is zero &mdash; the object reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="The constant maximum speed reached when drag force equals weight during freefall through a fluid.">terminal velocity</dfn>. A skydiver opening a parachute dramatically increases drag, decelerating to a lower terminal velocity (suitable for landing).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe the forces and motion of a skydiver from jumping to landing, using the terms weight, drag, terminal velocity, and resultant force.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At terminal velocity: weight = drag, resultant force = 0, velocity is constant. Before terminal velocity: weight &gt; drag, resultant force downward, object accelerates. When parachute opens: drag &gt; weight, resultant force upward, object decelerates to a new lower terminal velocity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Stopping Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The total stopping distance of a vehicle has two components. <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance a vehicle travels during the driver&rsquo;s reaction time before brakes are applied.">Thinking distance</dfn> is the distance covered while the driver reacts; it increases with speed and is worsened by tiredness, alcohol, drugs, or distraction. <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance a vehicle travels after the brakes are applied until it comes to rest.">Braking distance</dfn> is the distance covered while the brakes slow the vehicle; it increases with speed, wet/icy roads, worn tyres, and worn brakes.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n25">Total stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. At higher speeds, braking distance increases much more than linearly (because kinetic energy is proportional to \(v^2\)), so doubling speed more than doubles braking distance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Momentum and Its Conservation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The product of an object&rsquo;s mass and velocity; a vector quantity measured in kg m/s.">Momentum</dfn> (\(p\)) is calculated as:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">$$p = m v$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Where \(p\) is momentum in kg&nbsp;m/s, \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(v\) is velocity in m/s. Momentum is a vector &mdash; direction matters.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The <dfn class="term" data-def="In the absence of external forces, the total momentum of a system before and after a collision remains constant.">law of conservation of momentum</dfn> states that in a closed system (no external forces), total momentum before a collision equals total momentum after. This principle underpins how engineers design car crumple zones, safety barriers, and space probes.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n31">A 1000 kg car moving at 12 m/s collides with and joins to a stationary 500 kg car. Calculate their combined velocity after the collision.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">Momentum before = \(1000 \times 12 + 500 \times 0 = 12\,000\) kg&nbsp;m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33">Momentum after = \((1000 + 500) \times v = 1500v\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n34">Conservation: \(1500v = 12\,000\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35">\(v = 12\,000 / 1500 = 8\) m/s</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Newton&rsquo;s first law: zero resultant force = constant velocity (or rest). Newton&rsquo;s second law: \(F = ma\), where F is <em>resultant</em> force. Newton&rsquo;s third law: forces on interacting objects are equal, opposite, and of the same type, acting on different objects.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Terminal velocity: drag = weight, resultant force = 0. Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Momentum \(p = mv\); in a closed system, total momentum is conserved in any collision.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n36">Momentum calculation questions: always include direction (or use sign convention). If two objects move in opposite directions, one momentum is negative. For stopping-distance questions, state <em>both</em> thinking and braking distance factors to access full marks &mdash; the examiner expects both listed. For F = ma questions, always calculate the <em>resultant</em> force first (driving force minus friction) before dividing by mass. Rearranging F = ma to find mass when force and acceleration are known (m = F / a) is a common source of errors &mdash; practise all three forms.</p>
