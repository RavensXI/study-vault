Renewable and non-renewable energy resources, and calculating power using P = E/t.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Renewable and Non-Renewable Resources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every device that uses energy needs a resource to supply it. Energy resources fall into two broad categories. <dfn class="term" data-def="An energy source that is finite and cannot be replenished on a human timescale, such as coal, oil, natural gas and nuclear fuel.">Non-renewable</dfn> resources will eventually run out: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear fuel are the main examples. <dfn class="term" data-def="An energy source that is replenished naturally and will not run out on a human timescale, such as solar, wind, tidal, hydroelectric, geothermal, wave power and biofuels.">Renewable</dfn> resources are replenished continuously by natural processes and will not run out.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list every non-renewable resource, then every renewable resource, from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear fuel. Renewable: solar, wind, hydroelectric, tidal, wave, geothermal and biofuels. The key difference is whether the resource can be replaced within a human lifetime.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Fossil Fuels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Fossil fuels were formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient organisms. When burned, they release chemical energy stored in their bonds. <dfn class="term" data-def="Fuels formed from the compressed remains of prehistoric organisms over millions of years; includes coal, oil and natural gas.">Fossil fuels</dfn> are reliable — they can generate electricity whenever needed — but burning them releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change. They also release sulfur dioxide (especially coal), which causes acid rain, and particulate pollution that harms air quality.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Once a fossil fuel reserve is used up, it cannot be replaced within any realistic human timeframe. This makes them <strong>finite</strong> resources and a long-term supply risk.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Nuclear Fuel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A non-renewable fuel, typically uranium-235, whose nuclei release energy when they undergo fission in a nuclear reactor.">Nuclear fuel</dfn> (commonly uranium-235) releases energy through nuclear fission rather than combustion. Nuclear power stations generate large amounts of electricity with no carbon dioxide emissions during operation — a significant advantage for reducing greenhouse gas output. The main disadvantages are: radioactive waste that must be safely stored for thousands of years, high construction and decommissioning costs, and the public perception of safety risk.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain two advantages and two disadvantages of nuclear power compared to fossil fuels.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nuclear power: advantage — no CO&#x2082; during operation, high energy output; disadvantage — radioactive waste, expensive decommissioning. Fossil fuels: advantage — reliable, cheap infrastructure; disadvantage — CO&#x2082; emissions, finite supply.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Renewable Resources in Detail</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Renewable sources produce electricity without releasing carbon dioxide during operation, but each has specific drawbacks.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Wind, Solar and Hydroelectric Power</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Wind:</strong> turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into electrical energy. Reliable where wind speeds are consistently high (offshore sites are favoured in the UK). Disadvantage: output is variable and weather-dependent; turbines can generate noise and are considered visually intrusive by some communities.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Solar (photovoltaic):</strong> solar panels convert light energy directly into electrical energy. No moving parts, very low maintenance. Disadvantage: output depends on daylight and cloud cover; less effective at high latitudes or in winter.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hydroelectric:</strong> water stored behind a dam is released to drive turbines. Highly reliable and can respond quickly to peaks in demand. Disadvantage: requires flooding large valleys, which destroys habitats and displaces communities; limited suitable sites.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Tidal, Wave, Geothermal and Biofuels</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Tidal:</strong> the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun drives predictable tidal flows. Barrages or tidal stream generators harness this movement. Highly predictable, but limited to coastal locations with significant tidal range.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Wave power:</strong> devices on the ocean surface capture the kinetic and potential energy of waves. Potential near coastlines, but reliable technology at scale remains under development.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Geothermal:</strong> heat energy from within the Earth is used to produce steam, driving turbines. Very reliable and continuous. Limited to regions with accessible geothermal activity (e.g. Iceland, parts of the USA).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Organic material (plant or animal waste) burned or processed to release chemical energy; considered renewable because new plants can be grown to replace those burned.">Biofuels</dfn> are derived from organic matter (crops, wood, waste). They are considered renewable because new biomass can be grown. However, burning biofuels does release carbon dioxide — the argument is that this is offset by the carbon absorbed by growing plants. Land use for biofuel crops can compete with food production.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Comparing Resources: A Summary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">When evaluating energy resources, you should consider: reliability (can the source generate power on demand?), environmental impact (CO&#x2082;, habitat loss, pollution), cost (construction, fuel, decommissioning), and scalability (can it supply enough power for an entire country?).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Most countries use a <strong>mix</strong> of energy resources to balance reliability, cost and environmental impact. No single renewable resource is yet able to replace fossil fuels entirely without large-scale energy storage solutions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">The Power Equation: P = E/t</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of energy transfer, measured in watts (W). One watt equals one joule per second.">Power</dfn> tells us how quickly energy is transferred. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">$$P = \frac{E}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Where \(P\) is power in watts (W), \(E\) is energy transferred in joules (J), and \(t\) is time in seconds (s). One watt means one joule of energy is transferred every second.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write the power equation from memory, including the unit for each quantity and its symbol.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>\(P = E / t\) where \(P\) is power in watts, \(E\) is energy in joules, and \(t\) is time in seconds. Rearranged: \(E = Pt\) and \(t = E / P\).</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Power and Energy Calculations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Example 1:</strong> A wind turbine transfers 900,000 J of energy in 600 s. Calculate its power output.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">\(P = \frac{E}{t} = \frac{900{,}000}{600} = 1{,}500\) W (1.5 kW)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Example 2:</strong> A 2.5 kW solar panel operates for 4 hours. Calculate the energy transferred in joules.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Convert time: \(4 \times 3600 = 14{,}400\) s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">\(E = Pt = 2500 \times 14{,}400 = 36{,}000{,}000\) J = 36 MJ</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">Always convert hours to seconds (multiply by 3600) before substituting into the equation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Domestic and Industrial Energy Use</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Households use energy for heating, lighting, cooking, refrigeration, and powering appliances. Industrial users include manufacturing, transport and agriculture. The total demand on the National Grid must be met by the combined output of all generating sources at every moment.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n35"><dfn class="term" data-def="The national network of cables and transformers that transmits electricity from power stations to homes and businesses.">The National Grid</dfn> balances supply and demand in real time. Renewable sources with variable output (wind, solar) make this balancing task more complex, which is why energy storage — such as pumped-storage hydroelectric schemes — plays an important role alongside variable renewables.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Non-renewable resources (fossil fuels, nuclear) are finite and reliable; renewable resources (solar, wind, tidal, hydroelectric, geothermal, wave, biofuels) are replenished naturally but many are weather- or location-dependent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Fossil fuels release CO&#x2082; and contribute to climate change; nuclear produces no CO&#x2082; during operation but generates radioactive waste.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40">Power \(P = E/t\) in watts; energy \(E = Pt\) in joules; always convert time to seconds before calculating.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n36">When evaluating energy resources in a 6-mark question, structure your answer around at least two resources and compare them on clear criteria: reliability, environmental impact and cost. Avoid vague statements like &#x201c;it is bad for the environment&#x201d; &mdash; name the specific impact (releases CO&#x2082; contributing to climate change; flooding valleys destroys habitats). For power calculations, the most common error is forgetting to convert minutes or hours into seconds before using \(P = E/t\). Always show your unit conversion as a separate step.</p>
