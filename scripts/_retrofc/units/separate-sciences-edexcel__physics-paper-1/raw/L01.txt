--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Scalars and Vectors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every physical quantity is either a <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has magnitude (size) only, with no direction; e.g. speed, distance, mass, temperature.">scalar</dfn> or a <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has both magnitude and direction; e.g. velocity, displacement, force, acceleration.">vector</dfn>. Scalars have size only. Vectors have both size and direction. This distinction matters in physics: a car travelling at 20 m/s northwards and another travelling at 20 m/s southwards have the same speed but very different velocities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total length of path taken by an object, regardless of direction; a scalar quantity measured in metres.">Distance</dfn> is a scalar &mdash; it measures the total length of path covered, with no regard for direction. <dfn class="term" data-def="The straight-line distance from start to end point, including direction; a vector quantity measured in metres.">Displacement</dfn> is a vector &mdash; it measures how far an object is from its starting point in a straight line, and in which direction. A runner completing one lap of a 400 m track has covered 400 m distance but zero displacement.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Speed and Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of distance; a scalar quantity measured in m/s.">Speed</dfn> is a scalar &mdash; it tells you how fast an object is moving but not the direction. The equation for speed is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$v = \frac{s}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Where \(v\) is speed in m/s, \(s\) is distance in metres, and \(t\) is time in seconds. Rearranging gives \(s = vt\) and \(t = s / v\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write down typical speeds for: walking, running, cycling, a car in a town, a car on a motorway, and sound in air.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Typical speeds: walking ~1.5 m/s; running ~3 m/s; cycling ~6 m/s; car in a town ~13 m/s; car on a motorway ~30 m/s; sound in air ~330 m/s. These values are given as context &mdash; know the order of magnitude.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of displacement; a vector quantity measured in m/s, specifying both speed and direction.">Velocity</dfn> is a vector. It has both magnitude (speed) and direction. An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a <em>changing velocity</em> because its direction changes continuously &mdash; this means it is accelerating even though its speed is constant.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s²; a vector quantity that can be positive (speeding up) or negative (slowing down).">Acceleration</dfn> measures how quickly velocity changes. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Where \(a\) is acceleration in m/s<sup>2</sup>, \(v\) is final velocity in m/s, \(u\) is initial velocity in m/s, and \(t\) is time in seconds. A negative value for \(a\) means the object is decelerating (slowing down).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the equation v² = u² + 2as and state when you would use it instead of the basic acceleration equation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For uniform (constant) acceleration when time is not given: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), where \(s\) is the distance covered. This equation is provided on the equation sheet &mdash; but you must be able to rearrange and apply it correctly.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Uniform vs Non-Uniform Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">When an object experiences a constant resultant force, its acceleration is uniform &mdash; the same value throughout. A car on a straight motorway with constant engine force and negligible air resistance would have uniform acceleration. In contrast, real vehicles and falling objects experience non-uniform acceleration because resistive forces (air resistance, drag) increase with speed, gradually reducing the resultant force and therefore the acceleration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">On a velocity&ndash;time graph, uniform acceleration appears as a straight line (constant gradient). Non-uniform acceleration appears as a curve where the gradient decreases over time &mdash; such as the velocity of a sky diver before reaching terminal velocity, where the line levels off as drag builds up.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Distance&ndash;Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph plotting distance on the y-axis against time on the x-axis; the gradient of the line equals the speed.">distance&ndash;time graph</dfn> shows how far an object has moved from a reference point over time. The gradient (slope) of the line gives the speed.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">A <strong>horizontal line</strong> means the object is stationary (gradient = 0, speed = 0).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">A <strong>straight diagonal line</strong> means constant speed (constant gradient).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">A <strong>curve getting steeper</strong> means increasing speed (acceleration). The instantaneous speed at any point on a curve is found by drawing a tangent at that point and calculating its gradient.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Velocity&ndash;Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph plotting velocity on the y-axis against time on the x-axis; the gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives displacement.">velocity&ndash;time graph</dfn> shows how velocity changes over time. Two key rules:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Gradient = acceleration.</strong> A steeply rising line means large acceleration; a falling line means deceleration.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Area under the graph = displacement (distance).</strong> Split the area into rectangles and triangles to calculate the total distance travelled.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n36">Investigating Motion: Core Practical</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n37">A core practical for this topic involves investigating the relationship between acceleration and force or mass, often using a dynamics trolley and light gates on a measured track. Students measure the time for the trolley to pass through each light gate, calculate the change in velocity, and determine acceleration. By varying the applied force (using hanging masses on a pulley string) while keeping the trolley mass constant, the effect of force on acceleration can be plotted as a graph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n38">The key practical skills assessed include: correctly using light gates to measure velocity, identifying the independent variable (force or mass), controlling the dependent variable (acceleration), and recognising sources of error such as friction on the runway. Compensating for friction by tilting the runway slightly is a common technique &mdash; the trolley should move at constant speed without the hanging mass before adding a force.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n41">Scalars have magnitude only (speed, distance, mass); vectors have magnitude and direction (velocity, displacement, acceleration). Speed \(v = s/t\); acceleration \(a = (v - u)/t\). For uniform acceleration without time: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n42">Distance&ndash;time graph: gradient = speed; horizontal line = stationary; curve steepening = acceleration. Velocity&ndash;time graph: gradient = acceleration; area under line = displacement.</li>
</ul>
