<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Electromagnetic Spectrum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous range of electromagnetic waves, ordered by wavelength or frequency, from radio waves to gamma rays. All travel at the same speed in a vacuum.">electromagnetic (EM) spectrum</dfn> is a continuous family of transverse waves. All EM waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum &mdash; the speed of light, \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s &mdash; and can travel through empty space without needing a medium. All EM waves are transverse: their oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of travel.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The seven main groupings in order of decreasing wavelength (and increasing frequency) are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">radio waves &rarr; microwaves &rarr; infrared &rarr; visible light &rarr; ultraviolet &rarr; X-rays &rarr; gamma rays.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A useful mnemonic: <strong>R</strong>ed <strong>M</strong>onkeys <strong>I</strong>n <strong>V</strong>ests <strong>U</strong>sually <strong>X</strong>cel <strong>G</strong>loriously. Because all EM waves travel at the same speed, waves with higher frequency must have shorter wavelength (from \(v = f\lambda\) with constant \(v\)).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recite the seven EM wave types in order of decreasing wavelength, then state what they all have in common.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All seven EM wave types are transverse, travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum, transfer energy from source to observer, and obey \(v = f\lambda\). Order (decreasing wavelength): radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Uses and Dangers of EM Waves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Each region of the spectrum has properties that make it suited to specific applications, but higher-frequency waves are generally more dangerous because they carry more energy and are more ionising.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic waves with the longest wavelengths in the spectrum, used for broadcasting, satellite communications and long-distance signalling.">Radio waves</dfn> are used for broadcasting (TV and radio), satellite transmission and long-distance communication. They can diffract around hills and buildings. Radio waves can be produced by oscillating electrical circuits and can also induce oscillations in aerial circuits when they are received.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic waves between radio and infrared; used in satellite communication, mobile phones and microwave ovens.">Microwaves</dfn> are used in satellite communication, mobile phone networks and cooking. In microwave ovens, the waves are absorbed by water molecules, causing them to vibrate and heat the food. Danger: internal heating of body cells &mdash; avoid prolonged direct exposure.</p>

</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths just longer than red visible light; emitted by all warm objects and used in remote controls, thermal imaging and optical fibres.">Infrared</dfn> (IR) radiation is emitted by all warm objects. Uses include thermal imaging cameras, television remote controls, short-range optical fibre communications, cooking (grills, toasters) and security systems. Danger: skin burns from excessive exposure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Visible light is the narrow band our eyes detect, from red (longest wavelength in this range) to violet (shortest). Uses: photography, illumination, optical fibre communication. The different colours of visible light correspond to different frequencies.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic radiation with frequencies just above violet visible light; causes tanning and fluorescence, used in security marking and detecting forged banknotes.">Ultraviolet</dfn> (UV) radiation causes skin to tan and can damage surface cells, leading to sunburn and increasing the risk of skin cancer. UV is used in fluorescent lamps, security marking and detecting forged banknotes (which do not fluoresce like genuine notes). Danger: damage to surface cells and eyes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>X-rays and Gamma Rays</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="High-energy electromagnetic waves that pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by bone; used in medical imaging and airport security scanners.">X-rays</dfn> pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by denser materials such as bone and metal. Uses: medical imaging (radiographs), airport security scanners, detecting internal flaws in metal structures. Danger: cell damage and increased mutation risk &mdash; medical staff use lead shielding and minimise exposure time.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The highest-frequency electromagnetic waves, emitted by radioactive nuclei; used in cancer treatment, sterilisation and medical tracers.">Gamma rays</dfn> have the shortest wavelength and highest frequency. They are emitted by radioactive nuclei and are highly penetrating. Uses: radiotherapy (killing cancer tumours), sterilising food and medical equipment, and as tracers in medical imaging. Danger: mutation or damage to cells in the body &mdash; lead or thick concrete shielding required.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list one use and one danger for each of the seven EM wave types in order from radio to gamma.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Danger increases with frequency: radio and microwaves pose low risk at typical exposure levels; UV damages surface cells and eyes; X-rays and gamma rays can cause cell mutation, damage to DNA and cancer. Higher frequency = more energy per photon = greater ionising effect.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Refraction and Ray Diagrams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">When light crosses a boundary between materials of different optical densities, it refracts (changes direction). The ray is described relative to the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to a surface at the point where a ray meets it; used as the reference line for measuring angles of incidence and refraction.">normal</dfn> &mdash; a line perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The angle between an incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence.">angle of incidence</dfn> is measured between the incoming ray and the normal. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The angle between the refracted ray and the normal at the point of refraction.">angle of refraction</dfn> is measured between the refracted ray and the normal. When light enters a denser medium (e.g. air into glass), it slows down and bends towards the normal. When it exits into a less dense medium, it speeds up and bends away from the normal.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Core Practical: Refraction in Rectangular Glass Blocks</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Place a rectangular glass block on plain paper. Shine a narrow ray of light at the flat face at an angle. Draw around the block and mark where the ray enters and exits. Remove the block and use a ruler to draw the complete ray path, including the refracted ray inside the glass. Measure the angle of incidence and angle of refraction at both surfaces using a protractor. Record how the ray bends towards the normal on entering the glass and away from the normal on leaving.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">This experiment demonstrates: (a) refraction changes direction at a boundary; (b) the ray inside the glass travels in a straight line; (c) the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but displaced sideways. Control variables include: distance of the light source, use of the same glass block throughout.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="A law relating the angles of incidence and refraction at a boundary between two media: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n is the refractive index of each medium.">Snell&rsquo;s law</dfn> gives the quantitative relationship between the angles of incidence and refraction: \(n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2\), where \(n\) is the refractive index of each medium and \(\theta\) is the angle measured from the normal. For light travelling from air (\(n \approx 1\)) into glass (\(n \approx 1.5\)), the refracted ray is bent towards the normal.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Infrared Emission and Absorption</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">All bodies emit infrared radiation. The hotter an object, the more infrared it emits and the shorter its peak emission wavelength. For a body to remain at a constant temperature, the average power it radiates must equal the average power it absorbs. If it absorbs more than it emits, it warms up; if it radiates more than it absorbs, it cools down.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n25">The intensity and wavelength distribution of radiation emitted by a body depend on its surface temperature. This principle underpins our understanding of how the Earth&rsquo;s temperature is regulated: the planet absorbs solar radiation and re-emits infrared. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of this outgoing infrared and re-emit it in all directions, reducing net heat loss &mdash; the greenhouse effect.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n26">Fill a Leslie cube (a hollow metal cube with four different surface finishes: matt black, shiny black, matt white, shiny white) with hot water. Place an infrared detector at an equal distance from each face in turn and record the radiation emitted. Result: matt black emits the most infrared; shiny silver (or white) emits the least. This confirms that dark, matt surfaces are the best emitters and absorbers of infrared, while shiny surfaces are the poorest emitters (best reflectors).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">The EM spectrum runs from radio waves (longest wavelength) to gamma rays (shortest wavelength, highest frequency); all types are transverse, travel at \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum, and transfer energy from source to observer.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Danger increases with frequency: microwaves heat body cells; UV damages surface cells and eyes; X-rays and gamma rays cause cell damage and mutation. Each type has specific, examinable uses.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Refraction occurs when light changes speed at a boundary &mdash; it bends towards the normal when slowing down, and away when speeding up; frequency stays constant. Angles are always measured from the normal.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">For EM spectrum questions, learn the order of waves and one use and one danger for each type &mdash; these appear in both multiple choice and extended-response questions. When drawing ray diagrams for refraction, always draw the normal first as a dashed line, then measure angles from it, not from the surface. A common error is measuring the angle of incidence from the surface instead of the normal, giving the complement of the correct angle. For higher-tier Snell&rsquo;s law questions, always quote the formula, substitute in the known values, and give your answer to 2&ndash;3 significant figures with the correct unit (refractive index has no unit).</p>
