<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Waves?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Oscillations that transfer energy and information from one place to another without transferring matter.">Waves</dfn> transfer energy and information without transferring matter. The particles of the medium oscillate about their rest positions — they do not travel with the wave. Evidence for this can be seen with water waves: a floating object bobs up and down but does not travel along with the wave front.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">There are two fundamental types. In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the oscillations are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave travel, such as light waves, water waves and all electromagnetic waves.">transverse wave</dfn>, the oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. All electromagnetic waves and water waves are transverse. In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the oscillations are parallel to the direction of wave travel, such as sound waves and primary (P) seismic waves.">longitudinal wave</dfn>, the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound and P-waves (primary seismic waves) are longitudinal.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Longitudinal waves create alternating regions of <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where the particles are pushed close together, creating a region of higher pressure.">compression</dfn> (particles squeezed close) and <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where the particles are spread further apart, creating a region of lower pressure.">rarefaction</dfn> (particles spread out). You can model this with a Slinky spring: push it lengthways to see compressions and rarefactions travel along it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: which wave type has oscillations perpendicular to travel, which has them parallel, and give an example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Transverse waves: oscillations perpendicular to direction of travel (e.g. light, water waves). Longitudinal waves: oscillations parallel to direction of travel (e.g. sound, P-waves). All waves transfer energy without transferring matter.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Wave Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Every wave can be described using four properties:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum displacement of a particle from its rest (equilibrium) position, measured in metres.">Amplitude</dfn> is the maximum displacement from the rest position. A larger amplitude means the wave carries more energy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The distance between two successive points that are in phase, such as crest to crest or trough to trough, measured in metres.">Wavelength</dfn> (symbol \(\lambda\)) is the distance from one point on a wave to the identical point on the next wave, e.g. crest to crest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The number of complete waves passing a fixed point per second, measured in hertz (Hz).">Frequency</dfn> (symbol \(f\)) is the number of complete waves passing a point per second, measured in hertz (Hz).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for one complete wave to pass a fixed point, or for one complete oscillation, measured in seconds.">period</dfn> (symbol \(T\)) is the time for one complete wave and is linked to frequency by:
$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$
If \(f = 50\) Hz, then \(T = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02\) s.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Wave Equation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Wave speed, frequency and wavelength are related by two equivalent forms of the wave equation that you must be able to recall and apply:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$v = f \lambda$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">$$v = \frac{x}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Where \(v\) is wave speed in metres per second (m/s), \(f\) is frequency in hertz (Hz), \(\lambda\) is wavelength in metres (m), \(x\) is distance in metres and \(t\) is time in seconds.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write both forms of the wave equation, including all symbols and their units, from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both wave equations must be recalled: \(v = f\lambda\) (speed = frequency × wavelength) and \(v = x/t\) (speed = distance ÷ time). Common rearrangement: \(f = v / \lambda\) and \(\lambda = v / f\).</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Example 1:</strong> A sound wave has a frequency of 440 Hz and a wavelength of 0.75 m. Calculate the wave speed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">\(v = f\lambda = 440 \times 0.75 = 330\) m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Example 2:</strong> A water wave travels 12 m in 4 s. Calculate the wave speed, then find the wavelength if the frequency is 1.5 Hz.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Speed: \(v = x/t = 12/4 = 3\) m/s. Wavelength: \(\lambda = v/f = 3/1.5 = 2\) m.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Always check: speed in m/s, frequency in Hz, wavelength in m. Do not mix units.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Reflection and Refraction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">When waves reach a boundary between two different materials, they may be reflected, refracted, transmitted or absorbed &mdash; often a mixture of all four.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="The bouncing back of a wave when it hits a surface; the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal.">Reflection</dfn> occurs when a wave bounces off a surface. The law of reflection states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal (a line perpendicular to the surface at the point of contact).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="The change in direction of a wave as it passes from one medium to another, caused by a change in speed.">Refraction</dfn> occurs when a wave changes speed as it crosses a boundary. If the wave slows down, it bends towards the normal; if it speeds up, it bends away. Frequency does not change during refraction &mdash; only speed and wavelength change.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain in your own words: what changes and what stays the same when a wave is refracted at a boundary.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>During refraction: speed changes, wavelength changes, frequency stays the same. Direction changes if the wave crosses the boundary at an angle (no direction change if it arrives along the normal).</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Core Practical: Wave Speed in a Solid and a Fluid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The core practical requires you to investigate the suitability of equipment to measure wave speed, frequency and wavelength in both a solid and a fluid.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Core Practical Method: Fluid Waves (Ripple Tank) and Solid Waves (Metal Rod)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Fluid (ripple tank):</strong> A vibrating bar or dipper creates water waves on the surface. A stroboscope can be used to &lsquo;freeze&rsquo; the wave pattern. Measure the wavelength directly from the wave pattern projected onto a screen. Measure frequency using the stroboscope setting or a timer. Calculate speed using \(v = f\lambda\). Alternatively, time how long it takes waves to travel a measured distance: \(v = x/t\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Solid (metal rod):</strong> Strike one end of a metal rod with a hammer; the vibration travels as a compression wave along the rod. A microphone at the far end detects when the pulse arrives. With two microphones at a known separation, a data logger records the time between signals. Calculate speed from \(v = x/t\). Evaluate suitability: the rod method is more precise because water waves are affected by surface tension and depth variations.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Seismic Waves, Ultrasound and Infrasound</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n33">Sound is only audible to humans between about 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound waves with a frequency greater than 20,000 Hz, above the upper limit of human hearing.">Ultrasound</dfn> (above 20,000 Hz) has uses in foetal scanning, sonar depth-finding and industrial flaw detection. <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound waves with a frequency less than 20 Hz, below the lower limit of human hearing.">Infrasound</dfn> (below 20 Hz) is used to study seismic activity and can travel vast distances.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Seismic P-waves (longitudinal) travel through both solids and liquids. S-waves (transverse) can only travel through solids. The detection of S-waves and P-waves by seismographs provides evidence for the internal structure of the Earth.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel (e.g. light); longitudinal waves have oscillations parallel to travel (e.g. sound), with compressions and rarefactions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">The wave equation \(v = f\lambda\) links speed, frequency and wavelength; period \(T = 1/f\). During refraction, frequency stays the same while speed and wavelength change.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">The core practical measures wave speed in both a fluid (ripple tank) and a solid (metal rod), evaluating the suitability of each method; ultrasound exceeds 20,000 Hz and infrasound is below 20 Hz.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">In wave calculations the most common error is confusing frequency and period &mdash; remember \(T = 1/f\), not the other way around. If you calculate a speed faster than the speed of light (\(3 \times 10^8\) m/s) for a sound wave, you have almost certainly used Hz where you needed the period, or multiplied instead of dividing. For the core practical question, the mark scheme typically wants: independent variable, dependent variable, how each is measured, control variables, and how speed is calculated. Listing five or six bullet points in order usually secures the top band.</p>
