--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Technology Shapes What Media Products Are</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Media products are not created in a vacuum &mdash; every product is shaped by the technologies available to make it, distribute it and consume it. A newspaper from the 1890s looks nothing like a digital news feed, not just because the content has changed, but because the technologies of production, distribution and consumption are completely different. When new technologies arrive, they change what it is possible to create, who can create it and how audiences experience it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The technologies and processes used to create a media product, from camera equipment to editing software.">Production technology</dfn> determines what images and sounds a producer can capture and craft. <dfn class="term" data-def="The technologies and platforms through which a finished media product is delivered to audiences.">Distribution technology</dfn> determines how finished products reach audiences &mdash; whether by broadcast signal, physical disc, streaming server or social media algorithm. <dfn class="term" data-def="The technologies through which audiences access and experience media products, from cinema screens to smartphones.">Consumption technology</dfn> determines the conditions under which audiences experience the product &mdash; a shared public cinema is a very different context from a private smartphone screen. All three dimensions matter for media analysis.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three technology dimensions that shape a media product? Give one example of how each has changed in the digital age.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three dimensions of technology: production (how it is made), distribution (how it reaches audiences) and consumption (how audiences experience it). New technologies transform all three simultaneously.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Computer-Generated Imagery (CGI)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Visually realistic imagery created using computer software rather than physical sets, costumes or practical effects.">Computer-generated imagery</dfn> (CGI) has fundamentally transformed what is possible in screen media. Before digital production, visual effects relied on miniature models, matte paintings, puppetry and practical in-camera techniques. CGI made it possible to create photorealistic environments, creatures, crowds and physical effects that would be impossible, prohibitively expensive or dangerous to achieve practically. The scale and texture of environments visible in contemporary action films, fantasy series and video games are products of this technology.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">CGI does more than add spectacle &mdash; it changes the connotations and verisimilitude of media products. A historical drama can digitally recreate a city as it appeared centuries ago. A superhero film can place a character against a photorealistic alien landscape. These capabilities alter audience expectations: consumers of high-budget screen media now expect a level of visual credibility that was technically impossible a generation ago, and the absence of it reads as low-budget or amateurish. CGI has also made it possible to alter the appearance of real people &mdash; ageing actors digitally, smoothing skin, changing body proportions &mdash; which raises significant questions about representation and authenticity.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A digital image standard with significantly greater resolution and clarity than standard definition, providing sharper, more detailed images.">High definition</dfn> (HD) and its successors (4K, 8K) changed the sensory quality of moving-image media. Where standard-definition television had a grainy, slightly distanced quality, HD delivers images of extreme clarity that can make a drama feel more documentary-like in its immediacy. This hyper-realism has aesthetic consequences: make-up, prosthetics and physical sets that were convincing in standard definition can appear artificial in HD close-ups, requiring significant investment to maintain believability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">HD also changed shooting styles. The increased sensitivity of HD cameras means producers can use lower light levels, which has enabled a more naturalistic, less artificially lit aesthetic in prestige drama. Conversely, the extreme clarity of HD has encouraged some music video and advertising directors to use stylised, deliberately lo-fi aesthetics &mdash; grainy film stock looks, VHS textures &mdash; as a countercultural signal against the corporate smoothness of high-definition commercial media.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Image Manipulation and Authenticity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The alteration of a digital image using software to change colours, remove or add elements, or adjust the appearance of people or objects.">Image manipulation</dfn> has made the photograph an increasingly unreliable document of reality. In advertising, beauty, fashion and celebrity media, digital retouching is standard practice: skin is smoothed, bodies are reshaped, backgrounds are replaced, blemishes removed. The result is an image that presents an idealised version of a person or product that may never have existed in front of the camera.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">This matters for Media Studies because it is central to questions of representation: the body types, skin tones and facial features presented as &ldquo;normal&rdquo; or &ldquo;aspirational&rdquo; in manipulated images are constructions. Audiences who internalise these as realistic standards are responding to a constructed reality rather than the diversity of human appearance. Debates about the regulation of image manipulation in advertising &mdash; particularly in relation to body image and the audiences these products target &mdash; are ongoing. Some media organisations have introduced labelling requirements for digitally altered images; others resist this as an interference with creative freedom.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, give one example of image manipulation and explain one consequence it has for how audiences understand representation in that media form.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Image manipulation allows producers to alter photographs digitally, shaping what audiences perceive as &lsquo;normal&rsquo; or &lsquo;aspirational&rsquo; appearance. It is central to debates about representation, authenticity and the responsibility of media producers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Mobile Communication Technology and User-Generated Content</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of smartphones and tablets to create, share and consume media content at any time and in any place.">Mobile communication technology</dfn> has transformed both who can create media and where media can be consumed. A smartphone in a pocket is simultaneously a high-definition camera, a video editor, a broadcasting studio and a media consumption device. The barrier to media production has fallen dramatically: the equipment required to make a video that could reach millions of viewers is now available to almost anyone with a mid-range phone and a social media account.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The content produced by ordinary people rather than professional media organisations is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Media content created and published by members of the public, rather than by professional media organisations, typically distributed through social media platforms.">user-generated content</dfn> (UGC). UGC has complicated the linear model of communication: audiences are now also producers. A spectator at a news event can film it and publish it before the professional news crews arrive. A consumer of a music video can create a response or parody and distribute it globally. This blurring of the producer/consumer distinction &mdash; sometimes captured in the term &ldquo;prosumer&rdquo; &mdash; has created new forms of media language and new questions about the quality, authenticity and reliability of media content.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The convergence of previously separate media functions (broadcast, internet, communication) into single devices or platforms.">Digital convergence</dfn> describes the merging of previously distinct media technologies into single platforms and devices. A smart TV is simultaneously a broadcast receiver, a streaming client, a games console and a social media portal. This convergence has changed patterns of media consumption fundamentally: audiences no longer need to choose between television, radio, print and online media &mdash; they access all of these through the same device at the same time.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Streaming platforms have particularly transformed the relationship between production technology and audience behaviour. When audiences can access an entire series immediately rather than waiting for weekly broadcasts, producers can structure narratives differently, creating long-form story arcs that require consistent viewing across many hours. This &ldquo;streaming-era&rdquo; narrative form &mdash; with its complex characters, slow-burn development and cliff-hanger episode endings &mdash; is a direct product of the consumption technology available to contemporary audiences.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Technology shapes media products in three dimensions: production (how it is made), distribution (how it reaches audiences) and consumption (how audiences experience it). New technologies transform all three at once.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">CGI, HD and image manipulation have transformed the visual language of screen media, raising the bar for realism and credibility while creating new questions about authenticity and representation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Mobile communication technology and user-generated content have blurred the line between producers and audiences, while digital convergence and streaming have changed how, where and when audiences consume media products.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Questions about technology are often phrased as &lsquo;how has technology shaped the form of a media product&rsquo; or &lsquo;how do developments in technology affect the content or meaning of media products&rsquo;. The key is to be specific about which technology (CGI, HD, image manipulation, mobile technology, streaming) and to explain a precise consequence for the product&rsquo;s form, content or meaning &mdash; not just to say technology &lsquo;makes things better&rsquo;. Always consider all three dimensions: production, distribution and consumption, as the highest-band answers tend to connect at least two of these.</p>
