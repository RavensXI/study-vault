<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed and Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled per unit time, measured in metres per second (m/s). Speed is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude but no direction.">Speed</dfn> tells you how fast an object is moving. It is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude but no direction. The equation linking distance, speed and time is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$s = v \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Where \(s\) is distance (m), \(v\) is speed (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). You can rearrange this to find speed (\(v = \frac{s}{t}\)) or time (\(t = \frac{s}{v}\)). Typical speeds to know: walking (~1.5 m/s), running (~3 m/s), cycling (~6 m/s), a car in town (~13 m/s), and the speed of sound in air (~340 m/s).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a given direction. Velocity is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.">Velocity</dfn> is speed in a stated direction. It is a vector quantity. An object travelling in a circle at constant speed has a <strong>changing velocity</strong> because its direction constantly changes &mdash; meaning it is always accelerating, even though its speed stays the same.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Similarly, <strong>distance</strong> (scalar) measures total ground covered, while <dfn class="term" data-def="The shortest straight-line distance from start to finish, measured in a specific direction. Displacement is a vector.">displacement</dfn> (vector) measures how far and in what direction from the start. A runner completing a 400 m lap of a track covers a distance of 400 m but has a displacement of zero &mdash; they end up back where they started.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four scalar quantities and four vector quantities from memory. Scalars: speed, distance, mass, energy. Vectors: velocity, displacement, force, acceleration.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Speed and distance are scalars (magnitude only). Velocity and displacement are vectors (magnitude + direction). An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a changing velocity because direction constantly changes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s². Acceleration is a vector quantity.">Acceleration</dfn> is the rate at which velocity changes. An object accelerates when it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Where \(a\) is acceleration (m/s<sup>2</sup>), \(v\) is final velocity (m/s), \(u\) is initial velocity (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). A negative value means the object is decelerating. For example, a car braking from 20 m/s to rest in 5 s has an acceleration of \(a = \frac{0 - 20}{5} = -4 \text{ m/s}^2\).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Question:</strong> A cyclist accelerates from 2 m/s to 14 m/s in 6 seconds. Calculate her acceleration.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Identify values: \(u = 2 \text{ m/s}\), \(v = 14 \text{ m/s}\), \(t = 6 \text{ s}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Apply the equation: \(a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{14 - 2}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2 \text{ m/s}^2\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Note:</strong> Always include units in your final answer. Forgetting units is one of the most common reasons for losing marks on calculation questions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Distance-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. The gradient of the line gives the speed of the object.">distance-time graph</dfn>, <strong>time is always plotted on the x-axis</strong> and distance on the y-axis. The gradient (slope) of the line gives the speed. You can read the shape of the line as follows:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">&bull; A <strong>horizontal line</strong> means the object is stationary (speed = 0).<br>&bull; A <strong>straight diagonal line</strong> means constant speed &mdash; steeper gradient means greater speed.<br>&bull; A <strong>curve getting steeper</strong> means the object is accelerating.<br>&bull; A <strong>curve getting shallower</strong> means the object is decelerating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">To find the speed at a specific instant on a curved distance-time graph, draw a <dfn class="term" data-def="A straight line that touches a curve at exactly one point and has the same gradient as the curve at that point.">tangent</dfn> to the curve at that point and calculate its gradient.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what does the gradient represent on a distance-time graph? What does it represent on a velocity-time graph? These are the two most commonly tested graph-reading skills.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a distance-time graph: gradient = speed. On a velocity-time graph: gradient = acceleration. <strong>Time is always on the x-axis for motion graphs.</strong> Plotting them the wrong way round is a common exam error.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Velocity-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and velocity on the y-axis. The gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives distance travelled.">velocity-time graph</dfn>, two key pieces of information can be extracted:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">&bull; The <strong>gradient</strong> equals the acceleration. A steeper slope means greater acceleration. A horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration). A downward slope means deceleration.<br>&bull; A line <strong>below the x-axis</strong> means the object is moving in the opposite direction.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
  <p data-narration-id="n24">The <strong>area under the line</strong> on a velocity-time graph equals the distance travelled. For a triangle, the area is \(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\). For a rectangle, it is base &times; height. For irregular shapes, split into triangles and rectangles and add them together.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Terminal Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">When an object falls through a fluid (such as air or water), two forces act on it: its <strong>weight</strong> (downwards) and <strong>drag</strong> (upwards, opposing motion). Initially, weight is much greater than drag, so the object accelerates downwards. As speed increases, drag increases. Eventually, drag equals weight. At this point the resultant force is zero and the object reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="The constant speed reached by a falling object when the upward drag force equals the downward weight, giving zero resultant force and zero acceleration.">terminal velocity</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">A skydiver provides a clear example. After jumping, they accelerate rapidly. As speed builds, air resistance increases until it equals their weight &mdash; this is their first terminal velocity (around 55 m/s for a spread-eagled position). When the parachute opens, surface area dramatically increases, causing air resistance to suddenly far exceed weight. The skydiver decelerates until a new, much lower terminal velocity is reached (around 5&ndash;7 m/s), safe for landing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">On a velocity-time graph, terminal velocity appears as a curve that starts steep (rapid acceleration) and gradually flattens out to a horizontal line (constant velocity). The curve of a parachute opening would show a sharp decrease in velocity before levelling off again at the lower terminal speed.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Speed and distance are scalars; velocity and displacement are vectors. Acceleration = rate of change of velocity, calculated using \(a = \frac{v-u}{t}\). Always include units in your answer.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">On a distance-time graph, gradient = speed (time on x-axis always). On a velocity-time graph, gradient = acceleration; area under line = distance. Horizontal line = constant velocity (not necessarily stationary).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Terminal velocity is reached when drag equals weight (resultant force = zero, acceleration = zero). A parachute increases drag, producing a lower second terminal velocity safe for landing.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">For motion graph questions, always label your axes with both the quantity <em>and</em> the unit (e.g. &ldquo;time / s&rdquo;, &ldquo;velocity / m/s&rdquo;) &mdash; missing units on axes costs marks. Time must always go on the x-axis for motion graphs. When asked to &ldquo;describe the motion&rdquo;, refer to the gradient explicitly: &ldquo;the gradient is decreasing, so the object is decelerating.&rdquo; For extended responses on terminal velocity, make sure you state that the <em>resultant force</em> is zero, not just that &ldquo;forces are equal&rdquo; &mdash; the command word &ldquo;explain&rdquo; requires the link between zero resultant force and zero acceleration.</p>
