Newton's three laws, F = ma calculations, inertial mass, weight versus mass, and stopping distances.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Newton&rsquo;s First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues at the same speed and direction, unless acted on by an unbalanced (resultant) force.">Newton&rsquo;s First Law</dfn> states that an object remains at rest or continues to move at constant velocity unless acted upon by a <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall force on an object when all individual forces are combined, taking into account their direction.">resultant force</dfn>. This means:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">&bull; If the resultant force is <strong>zero</strong>, a stationary object stays still and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction.<br>&bull; If there is a <strong>resultant force</strong>, the object accelerates &mdash; which means it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A spacecraft drifting in deep space continues at the same velocity indefinitely because there is no friction or air resistance to produce a resultant force. A book on a table remains stationary because the normal contact force from the table (upward) exactly balances its weight (downward).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why a moving object does NOT need a force to keep moving. Include what Newton's First Law tells us.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Objects do not need a force to keep moving at constant velocity. A resultant force is only needed to <strong>change</strong> the speed or direction of an object. Zero resultant force &rarr; no change in motion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Newton&rsquo;s Second Law: F = ma</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The resultant force on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration: F = ma.">Newton&rsquo;s Second Law</dfn> states that the resultant force on an object is equal to its mass multiplied by its acceleration:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$F = m \times a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where \(F\) is resultant force (N), \(m\) is mass (kg), and \(a\) is acceleration (m/s<sup>2</sup>). The equation shows that a larger force produces a larger acceleration for the same mass, and a larger mass requires a larger force for the same acceleration. Remember: \(F\) is always the <strong>resultant</strong> force, not just any single force acting on the object.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Weight, Mass and Gravitational Field Strength</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). Mass does not change with location.">Mass</dfn> is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kg and does not change with location. <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of gravity acting on an object, measured in newtons (N). Weight depends on mass and gravitational field strength.">Weight</dfn> is the force of gravity acting on that mass, measured in newtons. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$W = m \times g$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(W\) is weight (N), \(m\) is mass (kg) and \(g\) is gravitational field strength (N/kg). At the Earth&rsquo;s surface, \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\). A 70 kg person weighs 700 N on Earth but would weigh only about 112 N on the Moon, where \(g \approx 1.6 \text{ N/kg}\). Their mass stays at 70 kg in both locations. The numerical value of \(g\) at the Earth&rsquo;s surface also equals the acceleration due to gravity in free fall (\(10 \text{ m/s}^2\)).</p>

<div class="higher-only">
  <h2 data-narration-id="n18">Inertial Mass</h2>
  <p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of an object, defined as the ratio of resultant force to acceleration: m = F ÷ a.">Inertial mass</dfn> is defined as the ratio of the resultant force applied to an object to the acceleration it produces:</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n20">$$m = \frac{F}{a}$$</p>
  <p data-narration-id="n21">This is Newton&rsquo;s Second Law rearranged. An object with greater inertial mass is harder to accelerate for the same applied force. Inertial mass and gravitational mass (the mass that determines how strongly gravity pulls an object) have the same value &mdash; this equivalence is a fundamental feature of physics and was central to Einstein&rsquo;s thinking, though it is not required beyond GCSE level.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Newton&rsquo;s Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force, acting on a different object.">Newton&rsquo;s Third Law</dfn> states: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. These action-reaction pairs always act on <strong>different objects</strong> and are always the <strong>same type of force</strong>. For example:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">&bull; When you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you with an equal force in the opposite direction (both are normal contact forces).<br>&bull; When the Earth pulls you downwards by gravity (your weight), you pull the Earth upwards with an equal gravitational force.<br>&bull; A swimmer pushes water backwards &mdash; the water pushes the swimmer forwards.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">A common mistake is to treat weight and the normal contact force as a Third Law pair. They are not &mdash; both act on the <em>same</em> object. A true Third Law pair involves forces on <em>different</em> objects.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the four features of a Newton's Third Law pair. They must be: equal in size, opposite in direction, the same type of force, and acting on different objects.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Newton&rsquo;s Third Law pairs: equal in size, opposite in direction, same type of force, acting on <strong>different</strong> objects. If both forces act on the same object, they are NOT a Third Law pair.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Stopping Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The total stopping distance of a vehicle is the sum of the <strong>thinking distance</strong> and the <strong>braking distance</strong>. Thinking distance is the distance covered from when the driver sees a hazard to when the brakes are applied. Braking distance is the distance covered while the brakes are applied and the vehicle slows to a stop.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">&bull; <strong>Factors increasing thinking distance:</strong> higher speed, tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions such as a mobile phone.<br>&bull; <strong>Factors increasing braking distance:</strong> higher speed (braking distance is proportional to speed squared, so doubling speed quadruples braking distance), wet or icy roads, worn tyres, poorly maintained brakes, larger mass of vehicle.<br>&bull; <strong>Speed affects both</strong>: at higher speed the vehicle covers more ground during the reaction time and also needs more braking distance to decelerate from a higher initial velocity.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Newton&rsquo;s First Law: objects keep the same velocity unless a resultant force acts. Newton&rsquo;s Second Law: \(F = ma\) where F is the resultant force. Newton&rsquo;s Third Law: action-reaction forces are equal, opposite, same type, and act on different objects.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Weight = mass &times; gravitational field strength (\(W = mg\)). Mass is constant regardless of location; weight changes with gravitational field strength. On Earth, \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Speed increases both. Reaction time affects thinking distance; road and vehicle conditions affect braking distance.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">For questions on stopping distance, always distinguish clearly between thinking distance and braking distance &mdash; examiners penalise conflating the two. For F = ma calculations, always work out the <em>resultant</em> force first before substituting; a common error is using the engine force alone when resistive forces are given. For Newton&rsquo;s Third Law questions, check that your pair of forces acts on two <strong>different</strong> objects and is the same type of force &mdash; if both forces act on the same object, they are simply a balanced pair, not a Third Law pair.</p>
