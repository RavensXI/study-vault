--- DESCRIPTION ---
Charge transfer, electric fields, current, potential difference, resistance, Ohm's law, and I-V characteristics.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Static Electricity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A property of all matter. There are two types of charge — positive and negative. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.">Charge</dfn> is a fundamental property of all matter. In most objects, the number of positive charges equals the number of negative charges, so the object is electrically neutral. When two insulating materials are rubbed together, friction causes electrons to transfer from one surface to the other, creating <dfn class="term" data-def="A build-up of charge on the surface of an insulating material, caused by the transfer of electrons through friction.">static electricity</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The material that <strong>gains</strong> electrons becomes negatively charged. The material that <strong>loses</strong> electrons becomes positively charged. It is always the electrons that move &mdash; protons are locked inside the nucleus and cannot transfer between objects. For example, when a polythene rod is rubbed with a cloth, electrons move from the cloth to the rod: the rod becomes negative and the cloth becomes positive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Static charge builds up only on <strong>insulators</strong> because electrons cannot flow away through the material. On a conductor, any excess charge spreads out immediately. If enough charge accumulates, it can discharge as a spark &mdash; this is what causes lightning.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: why do positive charges never transfer between objects? What actually moves, and in which direction does the charged object end up?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Static electricity is caused by the transfer of <strong>electrons</strong> between insulating materials. An object becomes positive by <em>losing</em> electrons &mdash; not by gaining positive charges. Protons never move between objects.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Electric Fields</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A charged object creates an <dfn class="term" data-def="A region of space around a charged object where another charged object experiences a force.">electric field</dfn> in the space around it. Electric field lines show the direction a positive charge would move if placed in the field &mdash; they point away from positive charges and towards negative charges. Where field lines are closer together, the field is stronger.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Two charged objects exert forces on each other through their electric fields &mdash; like charges repel and unlike charges attract. This force acts even without the objects touching, which is why a charged balloon can attract small pieces of paper without contact.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Current, Charge and Potential Difference</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of flow of charge (electrons) around a circuit, measured in amperes (A).">Current</dfn> is the rate of flow of charge through a conductor. For current to flow, there must be a source of <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred per unit charge across a component, measured in volts (V). Also called voltage.">potential difference</dfn> and a complete (closed) circuit. The equation linking charge, current and time is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$Q = I \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Where \(Q\) is charge (coulombs, C), \(I\) is current (amperes, A), and \(t\) is time (seconds, s). In a series circuit, current is the same at every point because charge is not used up. Current is measured with an <strong>ammeter</strong> in series; potential difference is measured with a <strong>voltmeter</strong> in parallel across the component.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Resistance and Ohm&rsquo;s Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how much a component opposes the flow of current, measured in ohms (Ω).">Resistance</dfn> opposes the flow of current. The greater the resistance, the smaller the current for a given potential difference. The relationship is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">$$V = I \times R$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Where \(V\) is potential difference (V), \(I\) is current (A), and \(R\) is resistance (&#x03A9;). <dfn class="term" data-def="The current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided the temperature remains constant. This applies to ohmic conductors only.">Ohm&rsquo;s Law</dfn> states that current is directly proportional to potential difference, provided temperature remains constant. This applies only to <strong>ohmic conductors</strong> &mdash; components whose resistance stays constant.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Practise rearranging V = IR. Write the triangle: V at top, I × R at bottom. Cover V to get V = IR; cover I to get I = V/R; cover R to get R = V/I.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>\(V = IR\) links potential difference, current and resistance. Ohm&rsquo;s Law: current is proportional to PD only if temperature is constant (ohmic conductor). Current measured by ammeter in series; PD by voltmeter in parallel.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">I-V Characteristics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph of current (I) against potential difference (V) for a component. The shape shows whether the component obeys Ohm's law and how its resistance changes.">I-V characteristic</dfn> graph shows how current varies with potential difference for a component. You need to know the shape for three key components (PAG P7):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">&bull; <strong>Fixed resistor (ohmic conductor):</strong> a straight line through the origin. Gradient is constant, so resistance is constant. Obeys Ohm&rsquo;s Law.<br>&bull; <strong>Filament lamp:</strong> an S-shaped curve that starts steep (low resistance when cool) and flattens as PD increases (higher resistance as the filament heats up). As current increases, the filament temperature rises, so resistance increases. The curve is symmetrical in forward and reverse directions.<br>&bull; <strong>Diode:</strong> current only flows in one direction (the forward direction). In the reverse direction, resistance is very high and virtually no current flows. There is a threshold voltage (roughly 0.6 V for a silicon diode) below which even forward current is negligible.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Some components have resistance that changes with environmental conditions:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">&bull; A <strong>light-dependent resistor (LDR)</strong> has high resistance in the dark and low resistance in bright light. Used in automatic streetlights and burglar alarms.<br>&bull; An <strong>NTC thermistor</strong> (negative temperature coefficient) has high resistance when cold and low resistance when hot. Used in thermostats, fire alarms, and engine management systems.<br>Both are used in potential divider circuits where their changing resistance produces a varying output voltage that depends on light level or temperature.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Sketch the I-V graphs for a fixed resistor, a filament lamp, and a diode from memory. Label the key features of each. The exam often asks you to identify a component from its I-V graph or explain why the graph has a particular shape.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>I-V graph shapes: resistor = straight line (constant resistance, obeys Ohm&rsquo;s Law); filament lamp = curve (resistance increases with temperature); diode = conducts in one direction only.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Static electricity is caused by electron transfer between insulators. Only electrons move &mdash; positive charges never transfer. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge creates an electric field in the surrounding space.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Current (A) = charge (C) ÷ time (s). Resistance opposes current. Ohm&rsquo;s Law: \(V = IR\) applies when temperature is constant. Ammeter in series; voltmeter in parallel.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">I-V graph shapes: fixed resistor = straight line; filament lamp = S-curve (resistance increases with temperature); diode = one-way conduction only. LDR and thermistor resistance changes with light and temperature.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">When answering questions about static electricity, always state that electrons transfer &mdash; never protons or positive charges. For I-V graph questions, explain the shape by linking it to resistance and temperature (for the lamp) or the one-way nature of current flow (for the diode). For calculation questions using \(V = IR\), always identify which value you are solving for and rearrange the equation before substituting numbers. A common error is using the wrong value for current when the ammeter is in parallel &mdash; remember, the ammeter must always be in series.</p>
