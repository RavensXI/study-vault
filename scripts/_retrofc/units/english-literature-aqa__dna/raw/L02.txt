<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Act 1: The Crime Is Revealed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>DNA</em> opens with Mark and Jan on a street, speaking in short, panicked bursts. Through their fragmented conversation, the audience learns that something terrible has happened to a boy called Adam. The group were bullying him — making him walk across a grille, steal vodka, eat leaves — and things escalated. Someone threw him into a shaft, and he fell. The group believes Adam is dead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Kelly deliberately withholds the full details of what happened. The audience never sees the incident directly. Instead, it is reconstructed through unreliable, overlapping accounts from Mark and Jan. This technique creates <dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic effect where the audience does not have access to all the facts, creating suspense and forcing them to piece together the truth from partial information.">dramatic uncertainty</dfn> — the audience is unsettled because they cannot be sure exactly what took place.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What specific acts of bullying did the group subject Adam to before the incident?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The group bullied Adam by making him walk across a grille, steal vodka, and eat leaves. The bullying escalated until someone threw Adam into a shaft. The group believes he is dead. Kelly never shows the incident on stage — all information comes from fragmented second-hand accounts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Group’s Panic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When the group gathers in the wood, the atmosphere is one of collective terror. John Tate, who was the group’s leader before the incident, tries to maintain control but is clearly out of his depth. He threatens anyone who uses the word “dead” and insists they need to “bond” together. His leadership is based on <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of fear, threats, or aggression to control others, rather than earning genuine loyalty or respect.">intimidation</dfn> rather than competence, and it is already crumbling.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The group’s panic reveals their immaturity. They argue, blame each other, and talk over one another. Nobody takes responsibility. Kelly uses the fragmented, overlapping dialogue to show a group falling apart under pressure — exactly the kind of situation where someone calm and decisive can seize control.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">John Tate bans the word “dead” and threatens: “I’m in charge. Everyone is happy. What am I?” His desperate repetition and forced assertions of control show a leader who has lost authority. The group humours him but does not genuinely respect him. When Phil finally speaks and offers a plan, the power shift is instant — John Tate is sidelined without a word of protest. Kelly uses John Tate to show that leadership based purely on aggression collapses the moment a real crisis arrives.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">By Act 3, John Tate has left the group entirely and “found God.” This could be read as genuine remorse, or as another form of avoidance — he finds a new authority figure (God) to tell him what to do, just as the group found Phil.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Phil’s Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Phil, who has been sitting in silence eating an ice cream while Leah talks at him, suddenly speaks. His plan is chillingly detailed. He tells the group to take a sample of Adam’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The unique genetic material found in every cell of a person’s body, which can be used in forensic science to identify individuals.">DNA</dfn> from a jumper Adam was wearing, plant it on a fictional “postman” suspect, and create a fake witness account using a man they found living rough in the woods. The plan involves framing an innocent person for a crime that may or may not have been committed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">What makes Phil’s intervention so striking is its calm precision. While everyone else is panicking, Phil thinks logically and strategically. He does not shout or threaten — he simply presents the plan as though it were obvious. The group immediately accepts his authority because he offers them what they desperately want: a way out. Kelly shows that in a crisis, people will follow anyone who appears confident, regardless of the morality of the solution.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the three key steps of Phil’s cover-up plan from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Phil’s cover-up plan has three components: (1) take Adam’s DNA from clothing, (2) plant it on a fictional suspect by smearing it on a postman, and (3) use a man living rough as a fake witness. The group frames an innocent person. Phil gains leadership not through intimidation but through calm, strategic thinking.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Leah’s Monologues</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Interwoven with the plot are Leah’s long, uninterrupted monologues, delivered to Phil in the field. Phil never responds. Leah talks about bonobos (peaceful apes), the nature of happiness, and what it means to be alive. These speeches seem random, but they carry deep thematic weight.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Leah’s monologues function as the play’s moral <dfn class="term" data-def="The underlying philosophy or moral lesson conveyed through a narrative, often expressed indirectly through character or situation rather than stated outright.">conscience</dfn>. She is the only character who genuinely grapples with the ethics of what the group has done. Her references to bonobos — apes that resolve conflict through affection rather than violence — offer an alternative to the group’s behaviour. But Leah is trapped: she knows the cover-up is wrong, yet she cannot bring herself to defy Phil or leave the group.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Bonobos and What They Represent</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Leah describes bonobos as creatures that use empathy and physical affection to resolve disputes, in contrast to chimpanzees (and, by implication, the group) who use violence and dominance. She says bonobos are “our nearest relative,” suggesting that humans have the capacity for peaceful resolution but choose aggression instead. This speech directly comments on the group’s choice to cover up their crime rather than confess. Kelly uses Leah to ask: if even apes can choose empathy over violence, why can’t we?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Phil’s silence during these monologues is itself significant. He offers no response, no comfort, and no moral engagement. His refusal to speak reduces Leah to a voice crying in the wilderness — heard by the audience but ignored by the one person she wants to reach.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Act 2: The Plan in Action</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">In Act 2, the group executes Phil’s plan. They find the man living rough in the woods and use him as their fake witness. The police believe the story. An innocent postman is arrested. The cover-up appears to work perfectly, and the group begins to feel a grim sense of relief. But Kelly signals that this relief is temporary — Mark and Jan’s street scene at the start of Act 2 reveals growing unease. The foundation for the play’s escalation in Acts 3 and 4 is already being laid.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Cathy, meanwhile, is thriving. She is excited about the cover-up and relishes her role in it. Her enthusiasm for the deception — and later for violence — makes her one of the play’s most unsettling characters. While others feel guilt or anxiety, Cathy feels empowered. Kelly uses her to show that group pressure does not affect everyone equally: some people discover that they enjoy cruelty.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Acts 1 and 2 establish the central crisis: the group bullied Adam, he fell into a shaft, and they believe he is dead. Phil devises a cover-up plan that frames an innocent person.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Phil replaces John Tate as leader not through intimidation but through calm strategic thinking — the group follows him because he offers certainty in a crisis.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Leah’s monologues function as the play’s moral conscience, exploring alternatives to violence through her references to bonobos — but Phil’s silence renders her powerless.</li></ul>
