<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Modern Text Essay: What Examiners Reward</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Modern text essay questions on <em>DNA</em> ask you to write about how Kelly presents a theme, character, or idea across the whole play. You will usually be offered a choice of two questions and must answer one in full.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Crucially, modern text papers are typically <strong>closed book</strong> and do not provide an extract. You must write about the whole play from memory. This makes the modern text essay one of the most demanding parts of your literature course, because everything depends on what you can remember. On AQA Paper 2 Section A the 30 marks are split AO1 12, AO2 12 and AO3 6, with a further 4 marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What four things does a strong modern text essay deliver?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A strong modern text essay on <em>DNA</em> delivers four things: a sustained, personal response to the play supported by references from memory; analysis of Kelly’s methods — the four-act structure, the staging, the silences — and the effects they create; context (Milgram, 2008 youth violence, moral philosophy, Connections programme) embedded in your analysis; and clear, fluent academic writing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">What Each Strand of Your Essay Needs to Do</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Argument and response:</strong> Have a clear thesis and support it with <dfn class="term" data-def="A statement or proposition that is supported by evidence and reasoning, forming the backbone of an analytical essay.">textual evidence</dfn>. Maintain an analytical style throughout — every paragraph should develop your reading of the play. Kelly’s methods are part of that argument: AO2 carries 12 marks, so say what his choices — the four-act structure, the silences, the field they keep returning to — actually do to an audience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Context:</strong> Show understanding of the relationships between the text and the contexts in which it was written. For <em>DNA</em>, this means: Kelly’s response to 2008 youth violence, Milgram’s obedience experiment, gang culture, moral philosophy, and the National Theatre Connections programme. Context must be <strong>embedded</strong> in your analysis, not bolted on as a separate paragraph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Writing quality:</strong> Use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures for clarity, purpose, and effect, with accurate spelling and punctuation. This rewards clear, fluent academic writing.</p>

AO2 rewards analysis of the <strong>effect</strong> a choice creates, so discuss Kelly’s structural and dramatic choices — staging, silence, the four-act structure, the field as a recurring space — and explain what each one does to an audience. Treat every observation about method as an opportunity to say more about meaning; a technique checklist with no meaning attached still earns nothing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Quotation Strategies for a Closed-Book Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Since you do not have the text in front of you, you need practical strategies for incorporating evidence. You do <strong>not</strong> need to memorise long quotations. Examiners reward relevant references to the play, which can take several forms:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Short quotations</strong> (1–4 words): These are the most effective for a closed-book exam. Examples: Phil’s “silence,” Leah’s “bonobos,” John Tate banning the word “dead,” Cathy becoming “more and more extreme” after Phil retreats</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Paraphrased references</strong>: Describe a specific moment accurately. Example: “When Phil calmly outlines his plan while eating an ice cream, Kelly contrasts his detachment with the group’s panic.”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Structural references</strong>: Refer to the play’s form and design. Example: “The replacement of Leah with Richard in the final field scene shows that the play’s moral conscience has been silenced.”</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down five short quotations or keywords from DNA that you could use in an exam essay.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For a closed-book exam, memorise <strong>short quotations</strong> (1–4 words) and <strong>specific moments</strong>. You do not need long quotations. Examiners reward accurate paraphrased references to the play just as much as direct quotation. Aim for 2–3 key references per paragraph.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Essay Planning: The 5-Minute Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Assume you have around 45 minutes for the modern text question. Spend the first 5 minutes planning. A strong plan should include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Thesis</strong>: One sentence stating your overall argument. Example: “Kelly presents guilt as widespread but ultimately meaningless, because no character translates their guilt into action.”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>3–4 paragraph topics</strong>: Each covering a different character, moment, or aspect of the play. Aim for chronological coverage (early play, middle, end) to show whole-text knowledge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Key references</strong>: 2–3 short quotations or specific moments for each paragraph.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Context links</strong>: One context point per paragraph (e.g., Milgram, 2008 youth culture, Connections programme).</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Model Paragraph: Guilt and Responsibility</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><em>Model paragraph (for the question: “How does Kelly present ideas about guilt in DNA?”):</em></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">“Kelly presents guilt as something that is felt but never acted upon, suggesting that moral awareness without courage is ultimately meaningless. Leah is the character most consumed by guilt, delivering long monologues in the field about <dfn class="term" data-def="A brief, memorable phrase or concept used as evidence in an essay, particularly useful when a full quotation cannot be recalled.">bonobos</dfn> — apes who resolve conflict through empathy rather than violence — as an alternative to the group’s behaviour. However, Phil’s refusal to respond renders her moral questioning powerless. Leah knows the cover-up is wrong, yet she never defies Phil or leaves the group. Kelly may be drawing on Milgram’s 1963 obedience experiment, which showed that most people will follow immoral instructions when an authority figure takes charge: Leah, like Milgram’s distressed participants, continues to comply despite her visible anguish. Her disappearance in Act 4 confirms that in Kelly’s world, the voice of conscience is silenced, not because it is wrong, but because it lacks the power to be heard.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>What this paragraph does well:</strong> It opens with a clear point about the theme. It uses a short keyword reference (“bonobos”) and a paraphrased moment (Phil’s silence, Leah’s disappearance) rather than long quotations. Context (Milgram) is embedded naturally, not bolted on. It ends with analysis of Kelly’s wider message.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The most common mistakes in the modern text essay are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Retelling the story</strong>: The examiner knows the plot. Every sentence should analyse, not describe. Use “Kelly presents…” or “Kelly suggests…” rather than “Then Phil says…”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Bolting on context</strong>: Do not write a separate paragraph about Milgram or 2008. Weave context into your analysis: “Kelly, writing in 2008 during a period of intense public anxiety about youth violence, uses Phil’s calm detachment to show how…”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Technique-spotting</strong>: You can mention dramatic choices (structure, staging, dialogue style) to support your argument, but do not write paragraphs about “Kelly uses a metaphor to…”</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Only writing about one part of the play</strong>: You must show knowledge of the whole text. Reference events or characters from at least three different parts of the play.</li>
</ul>

<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">Paper 2, Section A: one essay question on DNA, 30+4 marks, no extract. AO1 (12 marks) and AO2 (12 marks — Kelly’s methods) carry equal weight, with AO3 (6 marks) for context and AO4 (4 marks) for SPaG.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Memorise short quotations (1–4 words) and specific moments. Paraphrased references are just as effective as direct quotation in a closed-book exam.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">Plan for 5 minutes, write 3–4 analytical paragraphs, embed context within your analysis, and avoid retelling the plot or bolting on separate context paragraphs.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">Time yourself: 5 minutes planning, 35 minutes writing, 5 minutes checking SPaG. Write <strong>3–4 developed paragraphs</strong>, each making a clear point, supporting it with evidence, and linking to context. Quality matters more than quantity — a focused, well-argued essay of 3 paragraphs will outscore a rambling essay of 5. Use the opening sentence of each paragraph to signal your argument to the examiner.</p>
