<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Enid's World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The play's opening scenes introduce Enid Matthews, a Jamaican-born mother who has built a life in London through hard work and <dfn class="term" data-def="Giving up something valued for the sake of others.">sacrifice</dfn>. She works long hours — as a hospital cleaner, sometimes at two jobs — to provide for her daughters, Del and Viv, and is determined that they will succeed through education and respectability. Enid has deliberately distanced herself from her Caribbean roots: she rarely speaks about Jamaica and dismisses traditional practices as backward. Pinnock presents Enid as a woman whose survival strategy is <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of adopting the cultural norms of the dominant society.">assimilation</dfn> — she believes that fitting into English society is the best way to protect her daughters.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is Enid's survival strategy and why has she chosen it?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Enid's rejection of her Caribbean heritage is born from experience, not indifference. She believes that <dfn class="term" data-def="Conforming to social expectations of decency and manners.">respectability</dfn> and English education will protect her daughters from the racism she has experienced. But this strategy creates a painful gap between Enid and her cultural identity — and between Enid and her daughters, who sense a heritage they cannot name.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Del and Viv</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Del (Delores) is the <strong>elder</strong> daughter, aged 18. She is rebellious and instinctive — she stays out late, loses her job after arguing with her manager, and is drawn to Mai and the world of Obeah. Viv (Vivienne) is the <strong>younger</strong> daughter, aged 17, and is academically focused: she is preparing for her A-level exams and plans eventually to study at university. The sisters represent two responses to the immigrant experience — Del's resistance to assimilation and Viv's apparent conformity — but Pinnock complicates both positions as the play develops.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">Scene 1 takes place at Mai's Deptford bedsit on Easter Monday. Enid brings both daughters to Mai for a reading — her stated concern is about a letter she received from her sister Cynthia in Jamaica saying that their mother is seriously ill and asking for money. Enid suspects her sister may be exaggerating to extract funds, but Mai advises her to send the money anyway. Enid's other worry — which she shares cautiously — is about Del's behaviour. Del stays behind at Mai's bedsit when Enid and Viv leave. This opening scene establishes the play's two settings and the pull that Mai's Caribbean world exerts on Del.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Mai: The Obeah Woman</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Mai is a practitioner of <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of folk magic, spiritual healing and divination from Caribbean and West African tradition.">Obeah</dfn> — Caribbean folk magic rooted in West African spiritual traditions. She lives in a Deptford bedsit, physically declining but spiritually authoritative. She represents everything Enid has tried to leave behind: Jamaica, the old ways, spiritual belief systems that do not fit into English respectability. Mai's presence connects the family to their cultural roots, offering an alternative to Enid's strategy of assimilation. She recognises Del's potential as her spiritual successor from the start.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Brod: The Male Voice</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">Brod (Broderick) is Enid's long-standing friend, a fellow Windrush-generation Jamaican who is present throughout key scenes — most significantly Scenes 2, 3 and 7. He is the play's only significant male character and provides a counterpoint to Enid's resilience. Brod has a serious drinking problem, has lost contact with his children, and is deeply disenchanted with life in England. He embodies a Windrush experience that Enid has refused to allow herself: the disillusionment of a man who gave up everything for a country that never fully accepted him.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Scene 3: Enid's Mother Dies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The emotional turning point of the play's first half comes in Scene 3, when Enid learns that her mother has died in Jamaica. The news arrives via another letter — confirming what Enid had feared since Scene 1. This is not a simple bereavement: Enid's relationship with her mother was strained. Her mother called her 'Miss English', mocking Enid's assimilation. Enid sent money home but kept her distance — both geographically and emotionally. The death therefore carries grief compounded by guilt, estrangement and the knowledge that the relationship can never now be repaired.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does Enid's mother's death in Scene 3 function dramatically in the play?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Enid's emotional collapse after her mother's death in Scene 3 is the play's first major crisis. It cracks open the composure she has maintained through assimilation. Her grief is not simply about her mother's death — it is about everything she left behind in Jamaica: her roots, her language, the life she might have had. Pinnock uses this bereavement to humanise Enid and to begin the process by which she is gradually forced to reconnect with what she suppressed.</p>
</div>
