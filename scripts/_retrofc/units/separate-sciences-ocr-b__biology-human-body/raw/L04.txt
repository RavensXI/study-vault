--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Structure of the Nervous System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The human <dfn class="term" data-def="The organ system that detects stimuli and coordinates rapid responses, consisting of the brain, spinal cord and a network of nerves.">nervous system</dfn> allows the body to detect changes in the environment and produce coordinated responses. It has two main divisions:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Central nervous system (CNS)</strong> &mdash; the brain and spinal cord. This is the processing centre: it receives information from receptors, interprets it and sends out instructions to effectors.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Peripheral nervous system</strong> &mdash; the nerves that extend from the CNS to the rest of the body. These include sensory neurones (carrying signals to the CNS) and motor neurones (carrying signals from the CNS to muscles and glands).</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the full sequence of a nervous response from stimulus to response, naming each component in order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The nervous response pathway: <strong>stimulus &rarr; receptor &rarr; sensory neurone &rarr; relay neurone (CNS) &rarr; motor neurone &rarr; effector &rarr; response</strong>. The CNS (brain and spinal cord) is the coordination centre in the middle of every voluntary response.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Neurones and Their Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">There are three types of <dfn class="term" data-def="A specialised cell that transmits electrical impulses. There are three types: sensory, relay and motor neurones, each with a distinct structure and function.">neurone</dfn>:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sensory neurones</strong> carry impulses from <dfn class="term" data-def="Specialised cells that detect a specific type of stimulus &mdash; such as light, pressure, temperature, sound or chemicals &mdash; and convert it into an electrical signal.">receptors</dfn> to the CNS.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Relay neurones</strong> are found within the CNS and connect sensory neurones to motor neurones.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Motor neurones</strong> carry impulses from the CNS to <dfn class="term" data-def="An organ (muscle or gland) that carries out a response when stimulated by a motor neurone. Muscles contract; glands secrete substances.">effectors</dfn> (muscles, which contract, or glands, which secrete).</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n11">All neurones have a <strong>cell body</strong> (containing the nucleus), <strong>dendrites</strong> (short extensions that receive impulses) and a long <strong>axon</strong> that transmits impulses away from the cell body. Many axons are surrounded by a <strong>myelin sheath</strong> &mdash; an insulating fatty layer that speeds up the transmission of electrical impulses.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Synapses: Communication Between Neurones</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Neurones do not physically touch each other. The tiny gap between two neurones is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="The microscopic gap between two neurones where chemical neurotransmitters carry signals from one neurone to the next.">synapse</dfn>. When an electrical impulse reaches the end of one neurone, it triggers the release of chemical messengers called <strong>neurotransmitters</strong> into the synaptic gap. These molecules diffuse across the gap and bind to receptor proteins on the next neurone, triggering a new electrical impulse there.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Synapses are important because they allow signals to pass in only one direction (neurotransmitters are released from one side only), and because they allow the nervous system to integrate information from many inputs before deciding whether to generate a response. Many drugs and toxins work by interfering with synaptic transmission &mdash; for example, some drugs mimic neurotransmitters, while others block their receptors.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Reflex Actions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that does not involve a conscious decision. Reflexes protect the body from harm and are faster than voluntary responses.">reflex action</dfn> is an automatic, rapid response to a stimulus that bypasses conscious thought. Reflexes protect the body from harm &mdash; for example, pulling your hand away from a hot surface, blinking when something approaches your eye, or the pupil reflex in bright light.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The pathway for a reflex is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The pathway through which a reflex response travels: receptor &rarr; sensory neurone &rarr; relay neurone (spinal cord) &rarr; motor neurone &rarr; effector.">reflex arc</dfn>. In a spinal reflex, the relay neurone is in the spinal cord rather than the brain, which is why reflexes are so fast &mdash; the signal does not need to travel all the way to the brain for a decision to be made.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain in one sentence why reflexes are faster than voluntary responses. Name two examples of protective reflex actions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reflex arcs bypass the brain &mdash; the relay neurone is in the spinal cord &mdash; making reflexes faster than voluntary actions. This speed is crucial: reflexes protect us from harm before we have time to think. Examples include withdrawing from pain and the pupil light reflex.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Reflex Arc: Step by Step</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Using the example of pulling your hand away from a hot surface:</p>
<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">A <strong>receptor</strong> in the skin (a thermoreceptor) detects the intense heat stimulus.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">An impulse travels along a <strong>sensory neurone</strong> to the spinal cord.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">In the spinal cord, the impulse crosses a synapse and passes along a <strong>relay neurone</strong>.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The relay neurone crosses another synapse and triggers a <strong>motor neurone</strong>.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The impulse reaches the <strong>effector</strong> (the bicep muscle in the arm), which contracts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The <strong>response</strong>: the hand is pulled away. Simultaneously, a signal is sent to the brain so you become conscious of the event &mdash; but this happens after the response has already occurred.</li>
</ol>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Pupil Light Reflex</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">When bright light shines into the eye, the pupil automatically constricts (gets smaller) to protect the sensitive retina. This is the pupil light reflex. Light-sensitive receptors in the retina send signals along sensory neurones to the brain stem; motor signals return along motor neurones to the circular muscles of the iris, causing them to contract and the pupil to narrow. In dim light, the opposite occurs: radial muscles contract and the pupil dilates to allow more light in.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">The pupil reflex is coordinated in the brain stem rather than the spinal cord &mdash; it is still a reflex (automatic) but its relay station is higher up the nervous system. Doctors use this reflex to assess brain function: an absence of the pupil reflex after a head injury may indicate serious brain damage.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">The Brain: Regions and Functions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The brain is the master coordination centre of the nervous system. It is made up of billions of interconnected neurones and is divided into distinct regions, each associated with particular functions. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The largest region of the brain, responsible for conscious thought, memory, learning, language, voluntary movement and the interpretation of sensory information.">cerebrum</dfn> is the largest region and controls conscious thought, memory, language and voluntary movement. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The region of the brain at the back that coordinates balance and fine motor control, ensuring smooth and precise movement.">cerebellum</dfn> coordinates balance and the precision of movement. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The part of the brain that connects the brain to the spinal cord and controls unconscious vital functions such as heart rate and breathing rate.">medulla oblongata</dfn> controls automatic (unconscious) functions such as heart rate and breathing rate.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n30">Understanding the brain remains one of the biggest challenges in science. Techniques such as MRI scanning allow scientists to observe which brain regions are active during different tasks, but the complexity of billions of neurones interacting makes a full understanding difficult. Investigations into brain function are constrained by ethical limits on what experiments can be performed on living human subjects, by individual variation between brains, and by the fact that damage to one brain region may produce different effects in different people. This is an ongoing area of scientific investigation where models are continually refined as new evidence emerges.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">The nervous system consists of the CNS (brain and spinal cord) and peripheral nerves. The response pathway is: stimulus &rarr; receptor &rarr; sensory neurone &rarr; relay neurone &rarr; motor neurone &rarr; effector &rarr; response.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Synapses are gaps between neurones where neurotransmitters diffuse across to carry a signal. They ensure one-directional transmission and allow the nervous system to integrate multiple signals.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Reflex actions are rapid, automatic responses that travel through the reflex arc, bypassing the brain. The relay neurone in the spinal cord coordinates the response, making reflexes faster than voluntary actions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">For reflex arc questions, examiners want the components in the correct order &mdash; write the full sequence: receptor &rarr; sensory neurone &rarr; relay neurone &rarr; motor neurone &rarr; effector. A very common error is omitting the relay neurone. When explaining why reflexes are faster than voluntary actions, always state that the relay neurone is in the spinal cord, so the signal does not need to travel all the way to the brain before a response is sent. For synapse questions, describe three steps: impulse arrives &rarr; neurotransmitters released into the gap &rarr; bind to receptors on next neurone &rarr; new impulse generated.</p>
