--- DESCRIPTION ---
Aerobic and anaerobic respiration and the organelles that make it possible.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Cellular Respiration?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A universal chemical process occurring continuously in all living cells that breaks down glucose to transfer energy, supplying ATP for biological processes.">Cellular respiration</dfn> is a chemical process that takes place continuously in every living cell. It is not the same as breathing &mdash; respiration is a series of chemical reactions inside cells that transfer energy from glucose to a molecule called <dfn class="term" data-def="Adenosine triphosphate: the molecule that stores and transfers energy within cells for all biological processes.">ATP</dfn> (adenosine triphosphate). This energy powers everything cells do: muscle contraction, nerve impulse transmission, protein synthesis and active transport.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Respiration is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, usually as heat, making the surroundings warmer.">exothermic</dfn> reaction &mdash; it releases energy to the surroundings. This is why your body generates heat, and why your temperature rises during exercise.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the difference between respiration and breathing in one sentence, then name the energy-carrying molecule that respiration produces.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cellular respiration is a chemical reaction inside cells &mdash; it is NOT breathing. It breaks down glucose and transfers the energy to ATP. It is exothermic, and occurs continuously in all living cells &mdash; animals, plants, fungi and bacteria alike.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Aerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Respiration that uses oxygen, occurring in mitochondria. It fully breaks down glucose to carbon dioxide and water, releasing a large yield of ATP.">Aerobic respiration</dfn> uses oxygen and takes place mainly in the <strong>mitochondria</strong>. Because glucose is completely broken down, aerobic respiration yields a large amount of ATP &mdash; far more than anaerobic respiration. This is why organisms with high energy demands (such as muscles during sustained exercise) rely primarily on aerobic respiration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The word equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} + \text{energy (ATP)}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The balanced symbol equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O$$</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Anaerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Respiration that occurs without oxygen, producing a smaller yield of ATP. In animals it produces lactic acid; in yeast it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.">Anaerobic respiration</dfn> occurs when oxygen supply cannot keep up with demand &mdash; for example, during intense exercise. Because glucose is not fully broken down, far less ATP is produced. In animals and humans, the product is <dfn class="term" data-def="A compound produced by animal cells during anaerobic respiration that accumulates in muscles and causes fatigue and pain.">lactic acid</dfn>, which builds up in muscles causing the burning sensation of fatigue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The word equation for anaerobic respiration in animals is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{lactic acid} + \text{energy (ATP)}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In yeast (and other microorganisms), anaerobic respiration produces ethanol and carbon dioxide &mdash; this process is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The anaerobic breakdown of glucose by yeast to produce ethanol and carbon dioxide, used in bread-making and brewing.">fermentation</dfn>. The equations are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{energy (ATP)}$$</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. From memory, write the products of anaerobic respiration in (a) animals and (b) yeast. Which produces more ATP &mdash; aerobic or anaerobic?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aerobic respiration produces a much larger ATP yield than anaerobic respiration because glucose is fully broken down. Anaerobic respiration in animals produces lactic acid; in yeast it produces ethanol + carbon dioxide (fermentation). Both types occur in the absence of sufficient oxygen.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">After intense exercise, you continue to breathe heavily even though you have stopped moving. This is because your body needs extra oxygen to break down the lactic acid that accumulated during anaerobic respiration &mdash; this is called the <strong>oxygen debt</strong>. The liver converts lactic acid back to glucose using this extra oxygen. Heart rate and breathing rate remain elevated until all the lactic acid has been removed and the oxygen debt is repaid.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Mitochondria: The Cellular Power Stations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="A double-membraned organelle found in eukaryotic cells where aerobic respiration occurs, producing ATP. Also known as the powerhouse of the cell.">Mitochondria</dfn> (singular: mitochondrion) are the site of aerobic respiration. They have two membranes: a smooth outer membrane and a highly folded inner membrane. The folds, called <strong>cristae</strong>, increase the surface area for the reactions of aerobic respiration to take place. Cells with high energy demands &mdash; muscle cells, liver cells, sperm cells &mdash; contain hundreds or thousands of mitochondria.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Mitochondria Were Identified: A Science History Angle</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Mitochondria were first observed under light microscopes in the nineteenth century, but their function was unknown. Researchers working through the mid-twentieth century gradually worked out that these structures were the site of aerobic respiration by isolating them from cell homogenates and testing which reactions they could carry out. This is a good example of how scientific knowledge builds incrementally: first observation (structure), then experimental isolation (function), then detailed biochemical analysis of the reaction steps. Modern electron microscopy later revealed the cristae in detail, explaining why the surface area of the inner membrane is so large.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">This progression from observation to mechanism illustrates a core principle of science: each new technique reveals a new level of detail, and earlier models are refined rather than discarded entirely.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Comparing Aerobic and Anaerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">It is important to be able to compare the two types of respiration clearly:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Oxygen required?</strong> Aerobic: yes. Anaerobic: no.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>ATP yield:</strong> Aerobic: high. Anaerobic: low.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Products:</strong> Aerobic: CO₂ and water. Anaerobic: lactic acid (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Location:</strong> Aerobic: mainly mitochondria. Anaerobic: cytoplasm.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Applications: Fermentation in Industry and Food</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The process of <dfn class="term" data-def="The anaerobic breakdown of organic molecules by microorganisms. In yeast, glucose is converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide &mdash; the basis of bread-making and brewing.">fermentation</dfn> by yeast is exploited in several industries. In bread-making, yeast ferments sugars in dough, producing carbon dioxide that causes the bread to rise (the ethanol evaporates during baking). In brewing and wine-making, yeast converts sugars in grains or grapes into ethanol. The fermentation must be carefully controlled: too little oxygen at the start limits yeast growth; too much will switch yeast to aerobic respiration, reducing ethanol yield.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Bacteria also carry out anaerobic respiration. <em>Lactobacillus</em> bacteria ferment lactose in milk to produce lactic acid &mdash; which lowers the pH and causes the milk proteins to coagulate, forming yoghurt and cheese. Understanding the biochemistry of respiration has allowed scientists to optimise these processes and, more recently, to develop biofuels such as bioethanol as more sustainable alternatives to fossil fuels.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Cellular respiration is an exothermic chemical reaction in all living cells that transfers energy from glucose to ATP. It is not breathing.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and occurs in mitochondria, producing CO₂, water and a high ATP yield: \(C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Anaerobic respiration occurs without oxygen, producing lactic acid in animals (or ethanol + CO₂ in yeast via fermentation). It produces far less ATP, and lactic acid accumulates as an oxygen debt that must be repaid after exercise.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">A common error is writing that respiration &ldquo;produces energy&rdquo; &mdash; energy is transferred, not produced; use &ldquo;transfers energy to ATP.&rdquo; Always specify the type of respiration in equations: aerobic uses oxygen, anaerobic does not. For &lsquo;Compare&rsquo; questions on aerobic vs anaerobic, cover oxygen requirement, ATP yield, products and location &mdash; examiners look for contrasting pairs, not just a list of features.</p>
