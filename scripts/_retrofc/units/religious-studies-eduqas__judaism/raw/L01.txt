<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jewish Belief in One G-d</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">At the heart of Judaism is <dfn class="term" data-def="The Jewish belief in one, unique, all-powerful God, expressed in the Shema.">monotheism</dfn> &mdash; the belief in one God and one God alone. The Torah opens with the declaration that G-d created the heavens and the earth, establishing from the first verse that all existence depends on this one creator. Genesis 1:3&ndash;5 describes G-d bringing light into being through speech alone, conveying an unlimited creative power that requires no materials or assistance. Exodus 20:1&ndash;17, the Ten Commandments, begins with G-d identifying himself as the God who brought Israel out of Egypt &mdash; linking belief in one God to the history of a real community.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jewish belief in G-d is not abstract. G-d is understood as <dfn class="term" data-def="All-powerful; able to do anything that is not a logical contradiction.">omnipotent</dfn> (all-powerful), omnipresent (present everywhere), and <dfn class="term" data-def="Wholly good and loving in character, from the Latin for all-good.">omnibenevolent</dfn> (all-loving and good). G-d is also eternal, existing outside time and not subject to change or decay. These qualities distinguish the Jewish God from the gods of surrounding ancient peoples, who were often depicted as limited, rivalrous, or morally flawed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write out the Shema from memory, then explain in one sentence what it declares about G-d.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="The central declaration of Jewish faith, from Deuteronomy 6:4: 'Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one.'">Shema</dfn> is the most important prayer in Judaism, drawn from Deuteronomy 6:4. It declares that G-d is one &mdash; a statement of absolute monotheism. Observant Jews recite it in the morning and the evening.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">G-d as Transcendent, Merciful, and Judge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Judaism teaches that G-d is <dfn class="term" data-def="Beyond and above the physical world, not limited by or contained within creation.">transcendent</dfn> &mdash; wholly beyond the created order, not contained within it. Isaiah 55:8&ndash;9 expresses this powerfully, declaring that G-d&rsquo;s thoughts and ways are higher than human thoughts and ways, as the heavens are higher than the earth. This means G-d cannot be fully comprehended by human reason alone, and visual representations of G-d are forbidden.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Yet G-d is also personal and relational. Jewish tradition emphasises G-d&rsquo;s mercy (<em>rachamim</em>) alongside justice. G-d forgives those who genuinely repent, and the annual cycle of festivals includes Rosh Hashanah (the Day of Judgement) and Yom Kippur (the Day of Atonement), when Jews seek forgiveness. G-d as <dfn class="term" data-def="One who evaluates human conduct and assigns reward or punishment accordingly; a core attribute of the Jewish understanding of G-d.">eternal judge</dfn> means that human moral choices have ultimate significance &mdash; G-d rewards the righteous and punishes wrongdoing, though divine mercy always accompanies divine justice in Jewish theology.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Because G-d is so far beyond human understanding, Jewish tradition exercises great care with the divine name. Many observant Jews write &ldquo;G-d&rdquo; instead of the full word, and the four Hebrew letters of the divine name (the Tetragrammaton, YHWH) are not pronounced as written. When reading the Torah aloud, Jews substitute &ldquo;Adonai&rdquo; (Lord) for the written name.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Jewish place of prayer, study, assembly, and community life.">synagogue</dfn> serves four functions in Jewish life: prayer, study, celebration, and assembly. It is not considered sacred in the way a Temple was sacred; rather, it is the community that makes the place holy. This means synagogues vary enormously in design, from ornate Victorian buildings to converted terraced houses.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Key features of the synagogue include: the <strong>aron hakodesh</strong> (the ark), a cabinet at the front wall containing the Torah scrolls; the <strong>ner tamid</strong> (the eternal light), a lamp that burns continuously above or in front of the ark, representing G-d&rsquo;s constant presence; the <strong>bimah</strong>, a raised platform from which the Torah is read; and displayed text of the Ten Commandments, often above the ark. Seating arrangements differ between traditions: in Orthodox synagogues, men and women sit separately; in Reform and Liberal synagogues, they sit together.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Orthodox, Reform, and Liberal Judaism differ significantly in synagogue practice. In <dfn class="term" data-def="The branch of Judaism that follows traditional law and practice, including full gender separation in worship.">Orthodox</dfn> synagogues, services are conducted primarily in Hebrew, led by a male rabbi, and women do not read from the Torah in the main service. <dfn class="term" data-def="A branch of Judaism that adapts traditional law in light of modern knowledge and circumstances, allowing greater gender equality and use of vernacular languages.">Reform</dfn> and Liberal synagogues use more English in services, ordain female rabbis, and allow mixed seating. These differences reflect wider debates about how Jewish law (<em>halakha</em>) should be interpreted in the modern world.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Home as a Place of Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In Jewish life, the home is as important as the synagogue. The philosopher Abraham Joshua Heschel described the Sabbath as a &ldquo;cathedral in time&rdquo; &mdash; and much of Jewish religious life is practised within the family home rather than in a communal building. This emphasis on domestic faith means that Jewish identity is transmitted across generations primarily through family practice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A visible sign of this is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A small case fixed to the doorpost of a Jewish home, containing a scroll with the Shema and other passages from Deuteronomy.">mezuzah</dfn>, a small case attached to the doorpost of Jewish homes (and often of every room), containing a handwritten parchment with verses from Deuteronomy including the Shema. Touching the mezuzah and kissing one&rsquo;s fingers upon entering and leaving reminds the family of G-d&rsquo;s presence in everyday life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The home is especially central to Shabbat observance. Shabbat begins at sunset on Friday and ends at nightfall on Saturday, following the commandment in Exodus 20:8&ndash;10 to remember the Sabbath and keep it holy. The family lights candles, says blessings over wine (<em>kiddush</em>) and bread (<em>challah</em>), and abstains from creative work. Shabbat creates a weekly rhythm of rest that re-centres Jewish life on G-d. The challenges of observing Shabbat fully &mdash; not driving, not using electronic devices, not cooking &mdash; are real in modern Britain, and different families and communities navigate them differently.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain two ways the Jewish home functions as a place of worship. Include one specific object or practice in your answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The mezuzah (doorpost case) and Shabbat candles are two key ways Jewish domestic space expresses faith. Observing Shabbat at home is commanded in Exodus 20:8&ndash;10: &ldquo;Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.&rdquo;</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n8a">Because G-d is transcendent &mdash; entirely beyond the material world &mdash; Judaism forbids any artistic representation of G-d. Images or statues would reduce the infinite to something finite and manageable, which would misrepresent G-d&rsquo;s true nature. This shapes synagogue decoration: Hebrew calligraphy, geometric patterns, and symbolic imagery replace figurative art. The Shema displayed in beautiful script makes language the medium for divine encounter.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8b">The kippah (skullcap) worn by many Jewish men is a visible reminder that G-d is above, watching over the person&rsquo;s actions. The tallit (prayer shawl) is worn during morning prayer; its fringes (<em>tzitzit</em>) remind the wearer of all 613 commandments. These practices embed awareness of G-d&rsquo;s presence into daily bodily routine.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Judaism is strictly monotheistic. The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4) declares G-d&rsquo;s absolute oneness. G-d is understood as omnipotent, omnipresent, omnibenevolent, eternal, transcendent (Isaiah 55:8&ndash;9), merciful, and judge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The synagogue serves as a place for prayer, study, celebration, and assembly. Key features include the aron hakodesh (Torah ark), ner tamid (eternal light), and bimah (reading platform). Orthodox, Reform, and Liberal synagogues differ in language, gender roles, and interpretation of law.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The home is central to Jewish religious life. The mezuzah marks it as a sacred space, and Shabbat observance &mdash; commanded in Exodus 20:8&ndash;10 &mdash; transforms the domestic environment into a weekly act of worship and identity.</li>
</ul>
