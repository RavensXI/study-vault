<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Centrality of Practice in Jewish Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Judaism is a religion of practice as much as belief. The 613 commandments (<em>mitzvot</em>) of the Torah cover every aspect of daily life &mdash; from prayer and worship to what a Jew eats and how they rest. These practices are not arbitrary rules but expressions of the covenant between God and the Jewish people: each act of observance is a way of honouring and remembering that relationship. This lesson explores three of the most important areas of Jewish practice: worship (including the Shema, the kippah and the tallith), the synagogue, and the laws of Shabbat and kashrut.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Shema: The Heart of Jewish Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The most fundamental Jewish prayer is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The central declaration of Jewish faith, beginning 'Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one', recited morning and evening by observant Jews.">Shema</dfn> (from the Hebrew word meaning &lsquo;hear&rsquo;). It begins: <em>Shema Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai Echad</em> &mdash; &lsquo;Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one.&rsquo; This is the Jewish affirmation of monotheism: God is absolutely one, undivided, unique. Observant Jews recite the Shema in the morning and evening as commanded, and many recite it as the last words before sleep or at the point of death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Shema is not merely a doctrinal statement but an act of devotion. The tradition teaches that when reciting it, one should concentrate fully on the meaning of each word, particularly the word <em>echad</em> (one), drawing out its sound as a sign of intention. The Shema comes from the book of Deuteronomy and is followed by two other Torah passages that together make up the full Shema liturgy. Because Judaism holds that God&rsquo;s name is supremely holy, many Jews write &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="The way some observant Jews write the word 'God' in English, replacing the vowel with a hyphen as a sign of reverence for the divine name.">G-d</dfn>&rsquo; in English correspondence &mdash; deliberately incomplete as a sign of reverence.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Kippah and the Tallith</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A small head covering worn by Jewish men (and some Jewish women) as a sign of humility before God and respect for the divine presence above.">kippah</dfn> (also called a yarmulke) is a small head covering worn by Jewish men. Its purpose is to signify that there is always something &lsquo;above&rsquo; the wearer &mdash; a constant, physical reminder of God&rsquo;s presence and authority. Orthodox Jewish men wear the kippah at all times; for others it is worn during prayer and religious occasions. The wearing of the kippah is a matter of custom and commitment rather than a Torah command, but it has become a universal symbol of Jewish identity and reverence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A prayer shawl with fringes (tzitzit) at its corners, worn by Jewish men during morning prayers as commanded in Numbers 15:38-40, as a reminder of the commandments.">tallith</dfn> (or tallit) is a prayer shawl worn by Jewish men during morning prayers. Its corners are fitted with fringes called <em>tzitzit</em>, which are a physical reminder of the 613 commandments. The Torah commands Jews to attach such fringes to their garments so that, when they see them, they will remember all God&rsquo;s commands. Some Liberal Jewish women also wear the tallith. Like the kippah, the tallith is a &lsquo;wearable theology&rsquo; &mdash; a religious teaching made tangible in fabric.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why Jews wear the kippah and the tallith — what does each symbolise?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The kippah symbolises humility before God &mdash; a reminder that God is always above and present. The tallith, with its <em>tzitzit</em> fringes, is a physical reminder of the 613 commandments. Both are examples of Jewish practice embedding theology in everyday life.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Synagogue: Interior Features</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The synagogue is the central building of Jewish communal life &mdash; a place of prayer, study and community gathering. Several features of the synagogue interior are of religious significance:</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Features of the Synagogue Interior</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Holy Ark; a cabinet or alcove at the front of a synagogue that houses the Torah scrolls.">aron hakodesh</dfn> (Holy Ark) is a cabinet or alcove set into the wall facing Jerusalem, which holds the Torah scrolls. Opening the ark is a moment of great reverence; the congregation stands when it is opened. The ark is often ornately decorated and may be flanked by carved lions, symbols of the tribe of Judah.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The eternal light; a lamp that burns continuously above the aron hakodesh in a synagogue, symbolising the eternal presence of God.">ner tamid</dfn> (eternal light) is a lamp that hangs above the ark and burns continuously. It symbolises the eternal nature of God and may also recall the Menorah &mdash; the seven-branched lamp that burned in the Temple in Jerusalem. The ner tamid has never been extinguished since it was first lit.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A raised platform in the synagogue from which the Torah is read aloud to the congregation.">bimah</dfn> is the raised platform from which the Torah is read. In Orthodox synagogues it is often placed in the centre of the congregation, so that the words of Torah radiate outward to all present. In Reform synagogues it is commonly at the front, closer to where the ark stands. The Torah scroll is lifted, carried and read from the bimah with great ceremony. Many synagogues also display the words of the Ten Commandments near the ark, signifying the centrality of God&rsquo;s law to Jewish life.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Shabbat: The Jewish Sabbath</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Jewish weekly day of rest, observed from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall, commanded in the Torah and commemorating both creation and the Exodus from Egypt.">Shabbat</dfn> (the Sabbath) is the weekly day of rest, observed from Friday evening at sunset to Saturday night when three stars are visible. Exodus 20:8&ndash;10 commands: &lsquo;Remember the Sabbath day by keeping it holy. Six days you shall labour and do all your work, but the seventh day is a sabbath to the Lord your God.&rsquo; Shabbat commemorates two things: God&rsquo;s rest on the seventh day after creation (Genesis 1&ndash;2), and the Israelites&rsquo; liberation from slavery in Egypt, where there had been no rest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Shabbat observance varies considerably between Orthodox and Reform Jews. In Orthodox practice, the prohibitions are extensive: no work, no lighting fires or electricity (which means no cooking, no driving, no using a phone), no writing. The Shabbat is ushered in by the woman of the household lighting two candles, reciting a blessing, and covering her eyes while saying the Hebrew prayer. Kiddush (sanctification) is made over a cup of wine. A double portion of challah bread (recalling the double portion of manna in the wilderness) is shared. Synagogue attendance, family meals and rest define the day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In Reform and Liberal Judaism, the spirit of Shabbat is honoured while some of the detailed prohibitions are relaxed. A Reform family might drive to synagogue, but still light candles, say Kiddush and observe the day as distinctive and holy. Both approaches see Shabbat as one of Judaism&rsquo;s greatest gifts &mdash; a regular rhythm of rest built into the structure of the week.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Exodus 20:8-10 command about Shabbat, and when does Shabbat begin and end?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shabbat is commanded in Exodus 20:8&ndash;10 and runs from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall. It commemorates both creation and the Exodus. Observance involves candle lighting, Kiddush, challah bread and rest from work &mdash; though the extent of prohibitions varies between Orthodox and Reform practice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Kashrut: The Jewish Dietary Laws</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Jewish dietary laws derived from the Torah, governing which foods may be eaten and how they must be prepared.">Kashrut</dfn> (from the root meaning &lsquo;fit&rsquo; or &lsquo;proper&rsquo;) is the system of Jewish food laws. Food that meets these laws is <dfn class="term" data-def="Food that is permitted under Jewish dietary law; the adjective form of kashrut.">kosher</dfn>; food that does not is <dfn class="term" data-def="Food that is not permitted under Jewish law, meaning 'torn' or 'forbidden'.">treifa</dfn> (forbidden). A third category, <dfn class="term" data-def="Food that is neither meat nor dairy and may be eaten with either, such as fish, eggs, fruit and vegetables.">parev</dfn>, refers to foods that are neither meat nor dairy and may be eaten with either.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The main rules of kashrut are drawn from Leviticus 11:1&ndash;24 and other Torah passages. Land animals must chew the cud and have split hooves (so beef and lamb are kosher; pork is not). Fish must have fins and scales (so shellfish are treifa). Birds of prey are forbidden. Animals must be slaughtered in a specific way called <dfn class="term" data-def="The Jewish method of ritual slaughter, in which a trained slaughterer makes a swift, single cut to the animal's throat to minimise suffering and drain the blood, as required by Torah law.">shechitah</dfn>, performed by a trained <em>shochet</em> (ritual slaughterer) who makes a single swift cut to minimise suffering. The blood must be fully drained before the meat can be eaten, since the Torah forbids the consumption of blood.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">One of the most distinctive kashrut rules is the complete separation of milk and meat. This is derived from the Torah&rsquo;s threefold repetition of the phrase &lsquo;you shall not boil a kid in its mother&rsquo;s milk&rsquo; (Exodus 23:19; Exodus 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21). In practice this means observant Jewish households maintain two separate sets of crockery, cutlery and utensils &mdash; one for meat and one for dairy &mdash; and a waiting period between eating meat and dairy meals.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Orthodox Jews observe kashrut because it is commanded in the Torah &mdash; the word of God &mdash; and requires no further justification beyond the command itself. It is also understood to sanctify the act of eating, making a mundane activity into an act of religious obedience. It distinguishes the Jewish community from others and provides a constant, daily practice of self-discipline in obedience to God.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Reform Jews approach kashrut more selectively. Some observe it fully, others partially, and some not at all. The Reform movement generally holds that Jewish ethical principles &mdash; such as justice and compassion &mdash; are more binding than ritual laws, and that Jews must make their own informed choices about observance. Some Reform Jews apply the ethical spirit of kashrut in modern ways, such as choosing ethically farmed meat, even where traditional rules are relaxed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The Shema (&lsquo;Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one&rsquo;) is Judaism&rsquo;s central declaration of faith, recited daily. Physical symbols of devotion include the kippah (humility before God) and the tallith with its tzitzit fringes (reminders of the 613 commandments). The synagogue interior features the aron hakodesh (Torah ark), ner tamid (eternal light) and bimah (reading platform).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Shabbat (from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall) is commanded in Exodus 20:8&ndash;10. It involves candle lighting, Kiddush and rest from work. Orthodox and Reform Jews differ in how strictly they apply the prohibitions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Kashrut (Jewish dietary law) draws on Leviticus 11:1&ndash;24. Food must be kosher (permitted), avoiding treifa foods. Shechitah is the required method of slaughter. Milk and meat must be completely separated in an observant kitchen.</li>
</ul>
