<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sherlock Holmes: The Flawed Genius</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Holmes is not a conventional hero. He is brilliant, arrogant, emotionally detached, and addicted to cocaine. Doyle presents him as a man whose extraordinary intellect is both his greatest strength and his deepest flaw. When he has a case, Holmes is electric: focused, energetic, capable of astonishing deductions. Without a case, he collapses into boredom and self-destruction. His opening line about his mind rebelling “at stagnation” defines his entire character arc.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Holmes’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Holmes’s method of reaching conclusions through systematic observation and logical elimination, inspired by Doyle’s medical professor Dr Joseph Bell.">deductive method</dfn> is his defining quality. He reads Watson’s watch like a scientist reads data. At Pondicherry Lodge, he measures footprints, identifies creosote, and reconstructs the crime while Jones blunders. His famous maxim — “eliminate the impossible” — encapsulates the Victorian ideal of rational inquiry. Yet his rationality has limits: he cannot sustain emotional relationships, he dismisses Watson’s romantic feelings, and he returns to cocaine the moment the intellectual challenge ends.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What are Holmes’s three main character traits and one quotation for each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Holmes is characterised by three tensions: intellectual brilliance (“eliminate the impossible”), emotional detachment (he dismisses Watson’s engagement with the cocaine syringe), and self-destructive restlessness (“my mind rebels at stagnation”). For the exam, always analyse Holmes through these contrasts rather than treating him as simply ‘clever.’</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Dr John Watson: Narrator and Moral Centre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Watson is far more than Holmes’s sidekick. As the <dfn class="term" data-def="The character through whose perspective the story is told, shaping what the reader sees and feels.">first-person narrator</dfn>, he controls everything the reader sees. He is honest, warm, brave, and morally grounded — qualities that contrast with Holmes’s cold brilliance. Watson served in Afghanistan and was wounded by a Jezail bullet (as established in A Study in Scarlet; The Sign of Four only mentions a old wound without naming the Battle of Maiwand specifically); he embodies Victorian ideals of duty, honour and <dfn class="term" data-def="The Victorian ideal of a man who was brave, morally upright, emotionally restrained and protective of women.">masculine stoicism</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Watson’s role as narrator is crucial for the mystery genre. Because Watson is intelligent but not as brilliant as Holmes, the reader discovers clues at Watson’s pace — always one step behind Holmes. This creates suspense. Watson’s romantic subplot with Mary Morstan adds emotional depth. His awareness that the treasure would make Mary rich and therefore ‘above’ him reveals the class anxieties of Victorian courtship. When the treasure is lost, Watson is relieved rather than disappointed — he values love over wealth.</p>

    <span>Mary Morstan: Brave, Composed and Underestimated</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mary Morstan challenges some Victorian <dfn class="term" data-def="The expectation that women should be passive, domestic, and dependent on men for protection and financial support.">gender expectations</dfn> while conforming to others. She is brave: she comes to Baker Street alone, accompanies the men into danger at Pondicherry Lodge, and does not faint or become hysterical at the sight of Bartholomew’s body. Watson describes her as having “a sweet and amiable expression” but also “a firm set of her lips” — Doyle gives her both warmth and determination.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">However, Mary is also defined by her relationship to men. Her story begins with her father’s disappearance and ends with Watson’s proposal. She is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A woman employed to teach and care for children in a wealthy household, paid but occupying an awkward position between the family and the servants.">governess</dfn> — respectable but dependent. The treasure would have given her independence, but its loss is presented as a blessing because it allows Watson to propose. Doyle does not question whether Mary might have wanted the independence that wealth would bring. For the exam, you can analyse Mary as both stronger than the typical Victorian heroine and still constrained by the era’s gender norms.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Jonathan Small: Villain or Victim?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jonathan Small is the novel’s most complex character. He is both the antagonist — responsible for Bartholomew’s murder and the chase — and a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who could be seen as either a wrongdoer or someone treated unfairly, making the reader question simple judgments of good and evil.">sympathetic villain</dfn>. His confession in Chapters 11–12 reveals a man shaped by empire: he lost his leg serving in India, was drawn into the treasure plot by circumstance, and was then betrayed by Major Sholto. He has a genuine grievance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">However, Small is not innocent. He participated in the murder of the merchant without apparent remorse, and he brought Tonga to London knowing Tonga would kill. His throwing of the treasure into the Thames is an act of spite as much as principle. Doyle complicates the reader’s response: you can sympathise with Small’s sense of injustice while recognising his violence. This moral ambiguity is excellent exam material — it shows Doyle questioning simple categories of ‘hero’ and ‘villain.’</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: What three arguments support calling Small a villain, and what three arguments support calling him a victim?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Small is both villain and victim. Villain: he murdered the merchant, he brought Tonga to kill Bartholomew, he destroyed the treasure out of spite. Victim: he lost his leg in imperial service, he was betrayed by Sholto, he spent decades imprisoned. Doyle deliberately complicates moral judgment — the exam rewards students who explore this ambiguity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Tonga: The Silenced Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Tonga is the novel’s most troubling figure. He is an Andaman Islander whom Small befriended in prison, and he is fiercely loyal — Small calls him “staunch and true.” Yet Doyle never gives Tonga a voice. He does not speak a single line of dialogue. He is described using dehumanising language drawn from colonial gazetteers: “savage,” “unhallowed dwarf,” “naturally hideous.” He kills Bartholomew with a blowpipe dart and is himself killed without mourning. In the exam, Tonga is essential for AO3: his treatment reveals the racial prejudice embedded in Victorian literature and colonial attitudes.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Thaddeus Sholto: Comic Relief with a Conscience</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Thaddeus Sholto is one of Doyle’s most vivid comic creations. He is anxious, theatrical, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who is excessively worried about their health, imagining illnesses they do not have.">hypochondriac</dfn>, and surrounded by expensive Eastern art. His nervousness provides comic contrast to the growing danger of the plot. Yet he also has a conscience: he has been sending Mary the pearls out of guilt for his father’s theft. Thaddeus represents the possibility of moral redemption within a corrupt family. His wrongful arrest by Jones adds dramatic irony — the most morally honest of the Sholtos is the one punished.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Thaddeus also functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose qualities contrast with another character’s, highlighting their differences.">foil</dfn> to his twin brother Bartholomew. While Bartholomew hoards the treasure, Thaddeus wants to share it. Their different responses to inherited wealth show that individuals can choose differently even within the same circumstances.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">In the exam, you will score highly by showing how characters function in relation to each other. Holmes and Watson are complementary opposites. Small and Sholto mirror each other as men corrupted by the treasure. Mary and Holmes represent emotion versus intellect. Tonga and the Irregulars are both marginalised figures used by more powerful characters. Character analysis in <em>The Sign of Four</em> is always about relationships, not individuals in isolation.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Holmes is a flawed genius — brilliant but emotionally detached and self-destructive. His deductive method reflects Victorian faith in science, but his cocaine habit shows the limits of pure intellect.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Jonathan Small is both villain and victim, and Tonga is the novel’s most silenced character — both are essential for exploring Doyle’s presentation of empire and racial prejudice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Characters work best when analysed in relation to each other: Holmes/Watson, Small/Sholto, Mary/Holmes, Tonga/the Irregulars.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When writing about character in the exam, avoid simply describing what a character does. Instead, analyse <em>how</em> Doyle presents them (AO2) and <em>why</em> (AO3). For Holmes, discuss the tension between genius and self-destruction. For Mary, explore how she both challenges and conforms to Victorian gender norms. For Small, argue whether he is a villain or a victim. The examiner rewards nuanced analysis, not plot retelling.</p>
