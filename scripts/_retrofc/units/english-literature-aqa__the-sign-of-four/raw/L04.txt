<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 9: The Thames Chase</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The climactic boat pursuit along the River Thames, where Holmes and Watson chase Jonathan Small and Tonga aboard the Aurora.">Thames chase</dfn> is the novel’s most dramatic set piece. Holmes hires a police launch and, with Watson and Athelney Jones aboard, pursues Jonathan Small and Tonga down the river at night. Doyle fills the scene with vivid sensory detail: the dark water, the throbbing engines, the distant lights of London’s docklands. Watson describes “the barges and the riverside buildings racing past us” as the boats cut through the water at high speed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The chase serves multiple purposes. It provides thrilling action after the intellectual puzzle of the earlier chapters. It moves the story from the drawing rooms of Upper Norwood to the industrial Thames — from the respectable face of Victorian London to its working waterfront. And it ends in violence: Tonga fires a <dfn class="term" data-def="A long, hollow tube through which a dart is propelled by blowing, used by Tonga as a lethal weapon.">blowpipe</dfn> dart at the pursuers, narrowly missing Watson. Holmes and Watson fire back, and Tonga is shot and falls into the river, where his body is never recovered.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What happens to Tonga during the Thames chase, and what does Small do with the treasure?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>During the chase, Tonga fires a poison dart at the police launch. Holmes and Watson return fire, and Tonga falls dead into the Thames. Jonathan Small, realising he cannot escape, throws the Agra treasure overboard piece by piece into the river. The treasure that drove the entire plot is lost forever.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Tonga’s Death and Victorian Racial Attitudes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Tonga’s death is troubling from a modern perspective. He is shot without hesitation and his body sinks unrecovered into the Thames — no character mourns him. Doyle describes Tonga using language that reflects deeply racist Victorian attitudes: he is called a “savage,” “unhallowed dwarf,” and compared to an animal. Holmes earlier deduced his origins from a colonial <dfn class="term" data-def="A book about the geography and peoples of a place, here referring to Holmes’s reference work on the Andaman Islands that describes its indigenous people in dehumanising terms.">gazetteer</dfn> that describes the Andaman Islanders as “naturally hideous” and “fierce, morose, and intractable.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In the exam, you should acknowledge that Doyle’s portrayal of Tonga reflects the <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief, widespread in Victorian Britain, that European peoples were superior to non-European peoples, used to justify imperial conquest.">racial prejudice</dfn> of his era. Victorian Britain widely believed in a racial hierarchy that placed white Europeans at the top. Tonga is not given a voice, a backstory, or any humanity — he exists in the novel only as a weapon and a threat. Jonathan Small, by contrast, is given a full confession and the chance to explain himself. This disparity reveals whose stories Victorian society valued and whose it erased.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Chapter 10: Jonathan Small’s Confession — The Backstory</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Chapters 11 and 12 are Jonathan Small’s extended confession, narrated in his own voice. Small was a young British soldier serving in India when he lost his leg to a crocodile. During the <dfn class="term" data-def="A major uprising against British rule in India in 1857, also called the Sepoy Mutiny, during which the Agra treasure was originally seized.">Indian Rebellion of 1857</dfn>, he was posted to guard the Agra fort. A Sikh servant named Abdullah Khan offered Small a share of a vast treasure in exchange for his help in ambushing a merchant. Small agreed. They murdered the merchant and swore a pact — the “sign of the four” — with three Sikh companions, each signing with their names.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Small was caught and imprisoned in the Andaman Islands, where he befriended Tonga. He revealed the treasure’s location to Major Sholto and Captain Morstan in exchange for help escaping, but Sholto betrayed him and took the treasure. Small spent years plotting revenge. His confession is sympathetic in places — he feels genuinely wronged by Sholto — but he also participated in murder and shows no remorse for the merchant’s death.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Agra Treasure: Greed and Its Consequences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A hoard of jewels and precious metals stolen during the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the central object of desire that drives the novel’s plot.">Agra treasure</dfn> is the engine of the entire plot. Every crime in the novel flows from it: the original murder of the merchant, Small’s imprisonment, Sholto’s betrayal, Morstan’s death, Bartholomew’s murder, and the Thames chase. Yet the treasure itself is ultimately worthless — Small throws it into the river rather than let anyone else have it. “I would rather that our old cronies at Doolan had it than that a soul of us should benefit from it,” he says.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Doyle uses the treasure to explore the theme of greed. Everyone who touches the treasure suffers: the original merchant is murdered, the four conspirators are imprisoned, Sholto dies tormented by guilt, Morstan dies in an argument over it, Bartholomew is murdered for it, and Small loses both the treasure and his freedom. The moral is clear: the treasure brings nothing but suffering.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down all the characters who suffer because of the Agra treasure.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Agra treasure destroys everyone who pursues it. The merchant is murdered for it, the four conspirators are imprisoned, Sholto and Morstan both die because of it, Bartholomew is killed, and Small throws it into the Thames. Doyle’s message is that greed corrupts and that stolen wealth brings only suffering.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Chapter 12: The Ending — Love and Cocaine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">With the treasure lost, Watson realises Mary Morstan will remain poor — which removes the class barrier to their relationship. He proposes and she accepts. Watson’s joy contrasts sharply with Holmes’s reaction. Holmes shows no interest in the romance and, with the case solved, returns immediately to his cocaine. The novel ends as it began: Holmes reaching for the syringe.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Cyclical Structure: From Cocaine to Cocaine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The novel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where the ending returns to the same situation as the beginning, suggesting repetition rather than resolution.">cyclical structure</dfn> is significant. It opens with cocaine and closes with cocaine, suggesting that Holmes’s restlessness cannot be permanently satisfied. Each case is only a temporary reprieve. The structure also contrasts Holmes and Watson: Watson moves forward (marriage, emotional fulfilment) while Holmes remains trapped in a cycle (case, cocaine, case). This ending raises questions about whether Holmes’s genius is a gift or a burden.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The contrast between the two endings — Watson’s romantic happiness and Holmes’s solitary cocaine use — mirrors the novel’s dual structure throughout. Watson represents emotion, warmth and human connection; Holmes represents intellect, isolation and obsessive focus. Doyle suggests that neither approach alone is complete.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">The resolution of <em>The Sign of Four</em> is deliberately bittersweet. Justice is served (Small is captured), the mystery is solved, and Watson finds love. But the treasure is lost, Tonga is dead, Holmes is back on cocaine, and the underlying causes — imperial greed, social inequality — remain unchanged. Doyle does not offer a neat, triumphant ending.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The Thames chase is the novel’s dramatic climax — Tonga is killed, Small is captured, and the treasure is thrown into the river, making the object everyone fought over worthless.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Jonathan Small’s confession reveals the full history of the Agra treasure, connecting every crime in the novel back to imperial India and the original act of greed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The cyclical structure (cocaine to cocaine) suggests Holmes’s brilliance is both a gift and a curse, and contrasts with Watson’s emotional fulfilment through marriage to Mary.</li></ul>

If asked about character, contrast Watson’s emotional growth with Holmes’s stasis. If asked about themes, explore how the lost treasure symbolises the futility of greed. Always connect your analysis to Doyle’s methods (AO2) and Victorian context (AO3).</p>
