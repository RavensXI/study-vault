<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 1: Holmes, Watson and Cocaine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens not with a crime, but with a character study. Sherlock Holmes reaches for his <dfn class="term" data-def="A syringe used for injecting substances beneath the skin, here used by Holmes for his cocaine habit.">hypodermic syringe</dfn> and injects a “seven-per-cent solution” of cocaine. Watson watches with open disapproval, calling it a “pathological and morbid process.” This opening immediately establishes two things: Holmes’s extraordinary mind needs constant stimulation, and Watson serves as both narrator and moral conscience.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Holmes explains: “My mind rebels at stagnation. Give me problems, give me work.” Without a case, his brilliance turns inward and becomes destructive. Doyle uses this opening to show that Holmes is not a conventional hero — he is flawed, restless and potentially self-destructive. The cocaine is not glamorised; Watson’s medical perspective ensures the reader understands the danger.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does Holmes say about why he uses cocaine, and what does Watson call the habit?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Holmes tells Watson: “My mind rebels at stagnation.” He uses cocaine to escape boredom when no case occupies his intellect. Watson, as a doctor, calls it a “pathological and morbid process” — Doyle uses Watson’s disapproval to show the habit is harmful, not heroic.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5">Holmes also demonstrates his <dfn class="term" data-def="Holmes’s method of drawing conclusions about a person’s history and habits from small physical details.">deductive method</dfn> by analysing Watson’s pocket watch. From scratches, dents and a pawnbroker’s mark, Holmes deduces that Watson’s late brother was an alcoholic who squandered his inheritance. Watson is hurt and angry, revealing tension beneath their friendship. This scene establishes that Holmes’s deductions, while brilliant, can be cold and invasive.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Chapter 2: Mary Morstan’s Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A young woman who works as a paid companion or tutor in a wealthy household, occupying an ambiguous class position.">Governess</dfn> Mary Morstan arrives at Baker Street with a compelling mystery. Her father, Captain Arthur Morstan, vanished ten years ago after returning from India. Since then, she has received an anonymous pearl every year, and now an unsigned letter invites her to a meeting. Holmes is immediately energised — he has a problem to solve.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Mary is presented as composed, brave and practical. Watson is immediately attracted to her, noting her “sweet and amiable” expression. Doyle uses Watson’s romantic interest to create a secondary plot thread alongside the mystery. Importantly, Watson notes that if the treasure is found, Mary will be wealthy — and as a man of modest means, he feels he cannot honourably declare his feelings to a rich woman. This reflects Victorian attitudes to courtship and class.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">The trio travel through foggy London to meet Thaddeus Sholto, one of the sons of Major John Sholto. Thaddeus is a memorable comic creation — nervous, theatrical, obsessed with his own health, and surrounded by Eastern art and luxury. He reveals the backstory: his father, Major Sholto, had been Captain Morstan’s fellow officer in India. They both knew of the Agra treasure. Major Sholto stole the treasure for himself, and Captain Morstan died of a heart attack during an argument about it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Thaddeus has been secretly sending Mary the pearls out of guilt. He explains that his twin brother, Bartholomew, has now found the treasure hidden in the attic of their family home, <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sholto family estate in Upper Norwood, named to evoke India and the colonial connection to the Agra treasure.">Pondicherry Lodge</dfn>. He invites them to claim Mary’s share.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chapter 4: The Horror at Pondicherry Lodge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When they arrive at Pondicherry Lodge, the housekeeper is frantic — Bartholomew Sholto’s room is locked from inside. Holmes and Watson break in to find Bartholomew dead, his face frozen in a terrible grin. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A poisoned projectile fired from a blowpipe, used by Tonga to kill Bartholomew Sholto.">poison dart</dfn> is embedded in his scalp, and the treasure chest is empty. On the table is a card reading “The sign of the four.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">This is the novel’s most Gothic moment. Doyle uses horror conventions — the locked room, the distorted corpse, the mysterious calling card — to heighten tension. The locked-room mystery was a popular device in detective fiction, creating an apparently impossible crime that only Holmes’s logic can solve. Watson describes Bartholomew’s face as wearing a “horrible smile, a fixed and unnatural grin” — the poison causes involuntary muscle contraction, but the effect is deeply unsettling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and name: What three clues does Holmes find at the scene of Bartholomew’s murder?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Pondicherry Lodge, Holmes finds three crucial clues: a small footprint (Tonga), the marks of a wooden leg (Jonathan Small), and creosote on the rope used to enter through the roof. The card reading “The sign of the four” connects the murder to the original pact over the Agra treasure in India.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A mystery in which a crime appears to have been committed in a sealed room with no way in or out, challenging the detective to find a logical explanation.">locked-room mystery</dfn> was a staple of Victorian detective fiction. Poe used it in ‘The Murders in the Rue Morgue’ (1841). In <em>The Sign of Four</em>, Bartholomew’s room is bolted from inside, yet someone has entered, murdered him, and stolen the treasure. Holmes solves it by looking upward — the killer entered through a trapdoor in the roof. This demonstrates Holmes’s ability to see what others miss, and Doyle’s skill in using genre conventions to create suspense while ultimately providing a rational explanation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">How Doyle Builds Suspense in Chapters 1–4</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Doyle structures these opening chapters as a crescendo. Chapter 1 is slow and domestic — two friends in a sitting room. Chapter 2 introduces the mystery. Chapter 3 raises the stakes with Thaddeus’s revelation. Chapter 4 delivers the shock of murder. Each chapter increases the tension, drawing the reader deeper into the plot. Watson’s first-person narration limits what the reader knows, creating suspense because we discover clues at the same pace as Watson — always one step behind Holmes.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The opening chapter establishes Holmes as a flawed genius whose brilliant mind becomes self-destructive without intellectual stimulation — his cocaine use is a character study, not just a plot detail.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Mary Morstan’s arrival provides both the mystery (her father’s disappearance, the pearls) and a romantic subplot that reflects Victorian class anxieties.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Bartholomew Sholto’s murder at Pondicherry Lodge uses Gothic horror and the locked-room mystery convention to create the novel’s central crime scene.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When writing about Chapters 1–4 in the exam, focus on how Doyle establishes character through action and dialogue rather than description. Holmes is characterised by what he <em>does</em> (injecting cocaine, analysing the watch) more than what he says about himself. Similarly, the setting of foggy London is not just atmosphere — it reflects the mystery’s moral confusion. Always link technique to effect on the reader.</p>
