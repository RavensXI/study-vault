<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Empire and Colonialism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The British Empire is not just a backdrop to <em>The Sign of Four</em> — it drives the entire plot. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The system by which European nations controlled territories and peoples around the world, exploiting their resources and labour.">colonial</dfn> origins of the Agra treasure connect every crime in the novel to India. The treasure was stolen from a merchant during the Indian Rebellion of 1857. Major Sholto and Captain Morstan — British officers in India — became entangled in its story. Jonathan Small lost his leg serving the empire. Tonga was brought from the colonised Andaman Islands. The wealth that Victorians celebrated as a product of empire is shown by Doyle to be built on theft, violence and exploitation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Watson himself embodies the empire’s human cost. He is a wounded veteran of the Second Anglo-Afghan War, injured at the Battle of Maiwand. Yet Watson does not question the empire — he is proud of his service. This tension is important for contextual analysis: Doyle does not explicitly criticise the empire, but the plot he constructs shows its destructive consequences. Examiners reward students who can identify this implicit critique rather than simply stating “the novel is about the empire.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How does each of these characters connect to the British Empire: Watson, Small, Tonga, the Sholtos?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Doyle does not overtly condemn the British Empire, but the plot demonstrates its destructive consequences. Colonial violence in India creates crime in London. Watson is a proud imperial veteran, yet the treasure that drives the plot is stolen colonial wealth. Examiners reward students who identify this implicit critique through close analysis of Doyle’s choices.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme 2: Justice and Revenge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Who is truly guilty in <em>The Sign of Four</em>? The question is more complex than it first appears. Small murdered the merchant and orchestrated Bartholomew’s death. But Sholto betrayed Small and stole the treasure. The original merchant was carrying wealth extracted from colonial India. Doyle complicates the idea of <dfn class="term" data-def="Fairness in the way people are treated, including punishment for wrongdoing and protection of the innocent.">justice</dfn> by showing that every act of theft and violence flows from an earlier injustice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Holmes restores a kind of order: Small is captured, the mystery is solved. But the treasure is lost, Tonga is dead without mourning, and the underlying injustice of empire remains untouched. Doyle suggests that the justice system can solve individual crimes but cannot address the systemic <dfn class="term" data-def="A wrong or unfair situation, especially one that affects many people over a long period.">injustice</dfn> that created them. This is sophisticated territory for an exam essay: you can argue that Doyle both supports and questions the idea of justice.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Agra treasure is the novel’s central symbol of <dfn class="term" data-def="An excessive desire for wealth or possessions, often at the expense of others.">greed</dfn>. Everyone who pursues it suffers. The merchant is murdered. The four conspirators are imprisoned for decades. Sholto steals it and dies tormented by guilt. Morstan dies in an argument about it. Bartholomew is murdered for it. Small throws it into the Thames rather than let anyone else have it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Doyle’s moral is clear: stolen wealth brings only destruction. Yet there is an irony — the treasure’s loss is what makes Watson’s happiness possible. If Mary had inherited the treasure, Watson would have felt unable to propose because of the class difference. The elimination of wealth enables love. Doyle suggests that emotional wealth (love, friendship) is more valuable than material wealth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Theme 4: Science and Reason</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Holmes embodies the Victorian faith in <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of logic, evidence and observation to understand the world, central to Victorian intellectual culture.">reason</dfn>. His deductive method is essentially the scientific method applied to crime: observe, hypothesise, test, conclude. His superiority over Athelney Jones demonstrates that evidence-based reasoning is more effective than assumption. Doyle, a trained doctor, champions this worldview through his hero.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">However, Doyle also shows the limits of pure reason. Holmes cannot sustain human relationships. He dismisses Watson’s emotions with the cocaine syringe. His brilliance brings no personal fulfilment — only temporary relief from boredom. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where the ending returns to the same situation as the beginning, here Holmes returning to cocaine.">cyclical structure</dfn> (cocaine to cocaine) suggests that reason alone cannot create a meaningful life. Watson, with his warmth and capacity for love, achieves something Holmes cannot.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: How does Doyle both champion and critique the Victorian ideal of science and reason?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Doyle simultaneously champions reason (Holmes’s method solves the crime) and critiques its limitations (Holmes cannot achieve personal fulfilment). Science and deduction triumph professionally but fail personally. Watson, who combines intellect with emotion, achieves the happier ending. This duality is rich exam material.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Theme 5: Love and Relationships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The romance between Watson and Mary Morstan runs parallel to the detective plot. Watson is attracted to Mary from the moment she arrives, but class anxiety holds him back — if the treasure is found, she would be wealthy and ‘above’ him. When the treasure is lost, Watson is relieved: “the treasure is lost and the case is closed,” but he gains something more valuable. Doyle uses the romance to argue that <dfn class="term" data-def="Connections between people based on love, friendship or loyalty, presented by Doyle as more valuable than wealth.">human connection</dfn> matters more than material wealth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Holmes, by contrast, is entirely indifferent to love. When Watson announces his engagement, Holmes reaches for the cocaine. This juxtaposition at the novel’s close is structurally deliberate: Doyle places the two outcomes side by side to highlight what each character values and what each character lacks.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Extract + Whole Novel: Essay Technique</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">A typical nineteenth-century novel question gives you a printed extract of roughly 400 words and asks you to write about both the extract AND the novel as a whole. Your response needs a clear argument, close analysis of methods and embedded context. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact mark weightings that apply to you.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Step 1: Read the extract carefully.</strong> Underline key words, phrases, and techniques. Identify what the extract is about (character? theme? setting?).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Step 2: Plan your response.</strong> Aim for 3–4 paragraphs. Each paragraph should include: a point about Doyle’s presentation, a quotation (from the extract or the wider novel), analysis of language or structure, and a contextual link.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step 3: Write about the extract first.</strong> Give close analysis of specific words and phrases. Then extend to the novel as a whole — link the extract’s themes to other moments in the text.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Step 4: Embed context.</strong> Do not write a separate context paragraph. Instead, weave it into your analysis: “Doyle’s use of dehumanising language for Tonga reflects the racial prejudice that underpinned Victorian imperial culture.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Timing:</strong> Spend about 45–50 minutes on this question. Allow 5 minutes for planning and 5 for checking.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Key Quotations to Learn</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Most nineteenth-century novel exams are closed book, but the extract is printed for you. For the ‘novel as a whole’ part of your response, you need to refer to events, characters and ideas from other parts of the text. Memorising a few key quotations will strengthen your response significantly, even though precise wording is not required.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n26">“When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth” — Holmes’s deductive principle (theme: science and reason).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">“A seven-per-cent solution” — the cocaine injection (character: self-destruction, cyclical structure).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">“The sign of the four” — the card at the murder scene (plot: connecting all crimes to the original pact).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29">“An unhallowed dwarf with his hideous face” — description of Tonga (context: racial prejudice, empire).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">“It is a capital mistake to theorise before you have all the evidence” — Holmes warning against assumption (theme: science vs prejudice).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">The five central themes — empire, justice, wealth, science and love — are all interconnected: the Agra treasure links colonial greed to personal destruction, and only when the treasure is lost can love flourish.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Doyle both champions and critiques Victorian ideals: science solves crimes but cannot bring fulfilment; the empire creates wealth but also violence; justice is achieved for individuals but not for systems.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">In the AQA exam, analyse the extract closely first (specific words, phrases, techniques), then extend to the novel as a whole, embedding context in every paragraph.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">For the AQA Paper 1 Section B question, structure your response around the extract first, then expand to the novel as a whole. Use the PEAL pattern: Point (what Doyle presents), Evidence (quotation), Analysis (how language/structure creates meaning), Link (contextual connection). Aim for 3–4 well-developed paragraphs rather than 6 thin ones. Every paragraph should contain analysis of Doyle’s methods (AO2) and embedded context (AO3).</p>
