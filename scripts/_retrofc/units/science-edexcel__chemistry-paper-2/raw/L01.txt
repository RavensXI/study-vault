How collision theory, temperature, concentration, surface area and catalysts control how fast reactions happen.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Controls How Fast a Reaction Happens?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chemical reactions happen at different speeds. Some, like an explosion, are over in a fraction of a second. Others, like rusting, take months or years. The <dfn class="term" data-def="How quickly reactants are turned into products in a chemical reaction.">rate of reaction</dfn> measures how quickly reactants are converted into products. Understanding what affects this rate is a key part of the Edexcel specification.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What two things must happen during a collision for a reaction to occur?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For a reaction to occur, particles must <strong>collide</strong> with sufficient energy and in the correct orientation. The minimum energy needed for a successful collision is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum amount of energy that colliding particles need in order for a reaction to take place.">activation energy</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Collision Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that reactions occur when particles collide with enough energy and in the correct orientation.">Collision theory</dfn> explains how reactions happen at the particle level. Reactant particles are constantly moving and colliding with each other. However, not every collision leads to a reaction. Only collisions where particles have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy — and where the particles are orientated correctly — result in a successful reaction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The rate of reaction increases when:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n7">The <strong>frequency of collisions</strong> increases (more collisions per second).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8">The <strong>energy of collisions</strong> increases (a greater proportion of collisions exceed the activation energy).</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Factors Affecting the Rate of Reaction</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Four main factors change the rate of a reaction. Each one works by changing either the frequency or the energy of particle collisions.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Temperature</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Increasing the <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.">temperature</dfn> makes particles move faster. This has two effects: particles collide more frequently, and each collision carries more energy. A greater proportion of collisions now exceed the activation energy, so the rate increases significantly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Concentration (and Pressure for Gases)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Increasing the <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of solute dissolved in a given volume of solution.">concentration</dfn> of a solution means there are more reactant particles in the same volume. They are closer together and collide more frequently, increasing the rate. For reactions involving gases, increasing the <dfn class="term" data-def="The force exerted by gas particles on the walls of their container per unit area.">pressure</dfn> has the same effect — the particles are pushed closer together, increasing collision frequency.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Surface Area to Volume Ratio</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Breaking a solid into smaller pieces increases its <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of exposed surface compared to the overall size of a solid; smaller pieces have a higher ratio.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn>. More particles on the surface are exposed to the other reactant, so collisions happen more often and the reaction is faster. Powdered marble chips react with acid much faster than large lumps of the same mass.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Catalysts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up; it is chemically unchanged and has the same mass at the end.">catalyst</dfn> is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. It is not chemically changed and has the same mass at the end of the reaction. It does not alter the products formed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A catalyst works by providing an <strong>alternative reaction pathway</strong> that has a lower activation energy. This means a greater proportion of particle collisions have enough energy to react, so the rate increases — even though the temperature has not changed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: What three things are true about a catalyst at the end of a reaction?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A catalyst speeds up a reaction by lowering the activation energy. It provides an alternative pathway. The catalyst is <strong>unchanged chemically</strong> and <strong>unchanged in mass</strong> at the end of the reaction. It does <strong>not</strong> change the products.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Biological catalysts (proteins) that speed up reactions in living organisms.">Enzymes</dfn> are biological catalysts found in living organisms. The specification notes that enzymes are used in the production of alcoholic drinks, where yeast enzymes catalyse the fermentation of sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Measuring the Rate of Reaction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The rate of a reaction can be determined experimentally by measuring how quickly a product is formed or a reactant is used up. Two common methods are:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Measuring gas production</strong> — collect gas in a gas syringe or measure loss of mass on a balance over time. For example, reacting hydrochloric acid with marble chips produces \(CO_2\) gas.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Observing a colour change</strong> — in the reaction between sodium thiosulfate and hydrochloric acid, a yellow sulfur precipitate forms. The time taken for the solution to become opaque (a cross beneath the flask disappears) indicates the rate.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Interpreting Rate Graphs</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Graphs of mass, volume or concentration against time can be used to compare rates. A steeper gradient means a faster reaction. The gradient of the curve at any point gives the rate of reaction at that moment. As reactants are used up, the gradient decreases and the line levels off — the reaction is complete. Two curves on the same axes can show the effect of changing a variable (e.g. higher temperature gives a steeper initial gradient but the same final amount of product).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: sketch two rate curves on the same axes — one for a reaction at 20 °C and one at 40 °C. Label which is faster and where both finish.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a graph of volume of gas against time, the <strong>gradient</strong> (steepness) of the line represents the rate. A steeper line = faster rate. The line levels off when the reaction is complete and all the limiting reactant has been used up.</p>
</div>
