--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Crude Oil?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A dark, thick liquid found underground, made up of a complex mixture of hydrocarbons formed from the remains of ancient organisms over millions of years.">Crude oil</dfn> is a naturally occurring mixture found deep underground. It formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms buried under layers of sediment. Heat and pressure converted this organic matter into a complex mixture of <dfn class="term" data-def="Compounds that contain only hydrogen and carbon atoms.">hydrocarbons</dfn> — compounds made of carbon and hydrogen atoms only.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What two elements make up a hydrocarbon, and what type of resource is crude oil?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Crude oil is a <strong>complex mixture</strong> of hydrocarbons. It contains molecules in which carbon atoms are joined in <strong>chains</strong> or <strong>rings</strong>. Crude oil is an important source of useful substances (fuels and feedstock for the petrochemical industry) and is a <strong>finite</strong> (non-renewable) resource.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Fractional Distillation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Crude oil is not useful as it comes out of the ground. It must be separated into simpler, more useful <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of hydrocarbons with similar boiling points that are separated from crude oil during fractional distillation.">fractions</dfn> by the process of <dfn class="term" data-def="The separation of a mixture of liquids with different boiling points by heating them so they evaporate and condense at different levels in a fractionating column.">fractional distillation</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In a fractionating column:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n7">Crude oil is heated until most of it <strong>evaporates</strong>.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8">The vapour rises up the column, which is <strong>hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top</strong>.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n9">Hydrocarbons with high boiling points <strong>condense</strong> near the bottom; those with low boiling points rise further before condensing near the top.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n10">Each fraction is collected at a different level.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fractions and Their Uses</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">You need to know the names and uses of these six fractions (from top to bottom of the column):</p>
    <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Gases</strong> (e.g. methane, propane, butane) — used in domestic heating and cooking.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Petrol</strong> — used as fuel for cars.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Kerosene</strong> — used as fuel for aircraft.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Diesel oil</strong> — used as fuel for some cars and trains.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Fuel oil</strong> — used as fuel for large ships and in some power stations.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Bitumen</strong> — used to surface roads and roofs.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">How Fractions Differ</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">As you go down the fractionating column (from gases to bitumen), the hydrocarbon molecules get larger. This causes a pattern in their physical properties:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Number of carbon and hydrogen atoms</strong> increases (longer chains).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Boiling point</strong> increases (stronger intermolecular forces between larger molecules need more energy to overcome).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Viscosity</strong> increases (the liquid becomes thicker and harder to pour).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Ease of ignition</strong> decreases (larger molecules are harder to ignite).</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the trend in boiling point, viscosity and flammability as chain length increases.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Longer hydrocarbon molecules have <strong>higher boiling points</strong>, <strong>higher viscosity</strong> and are <strong>harder to ignite</strong>. Shorter molecules have lower boiling points, are less viscous and ignite more easily. This is because larger molecules have stronger <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between molecules; they increase with molecular size.">intermolecular forces</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Alkanes — A Homologous Series</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Most hydrocarbons in crude oil belong to a family called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of organic compounds with the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and a gradual variation in physical properties. Each member differs from the next by CH\u2082.">alkanes</dfn>. Alkanes are a <dfn class="term" data-def="A family of organic compounds that share the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and show a gradual trend in physical properties.">homologous series</dfn> — a family of compounds with a pattern. Every homologous series has four defining features:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n27">They share the same <strong>general formula</strong>. For alkanes, this is \(C_nH_{2n+2}\).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Each member differs from the next by \(CH_2\) in their molecular formula.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">They show a <strong>gradual variation in physical properties</strong> (e.g. boiling point increases steadily as chain length grows).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">They have <strong>similar chemical properties</strong> because they share the same functional group (or in the case of alkanes, the same type of bonding).</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The First Four Alkanes</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Using the general formula \(C_nH_{2n+2}\):</p>
    <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Methane</strong> \(CH_4\) — 1 carbon. Found in natural gas.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Ethane</strong> \(C_2H_6\) — 2 carbons.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Propane</strong> \(C_3H_8\) — 3 carbons. Used in camping gas.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Butane</strong> \(C_4H_{10}\) — 4 carbons. Used in lighters.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n36">Alkanes are <dfn class="term" data-def="A hydrocarbon in which all carbon-carbon bonds are single bonds; it contains the maximum number of hydrogen atoms.">saturated</dfn> hydrocarbons — all carbon–carbon bonds are single bonds, so they contain the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n37" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: What is the general formula for alkanes, and name the first four members?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Crude oil is a finite resource. Petrol, kerosene and diesel oil are <strong>non-renewable fossil fuels</strong> obtained from crude oil. Methane is a non-renewable fossil fuel found in natural gas. Once used, these cannot be replaced on a human timescale.</p>
</div>
