<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pure Substances and Mixtures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In chemistry, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A single element or compound, not mixed with any other substance. It melts and boils at specific, sharp temperatures.">pure substance</dfn> is a single element or compound that is not mixed with anything else. A pure substance has a sharp, fixed melting point and boiling point. A <dfn class="term" data-def="Two or more elements or compounds that are not chemically bonded together and can be separated by physical methods.">mixture</dfn> contains two or more substances that are not chemically bonded. Mixtures melt and boil over a range of temperatures, which is how they can be distinguished from pure substances.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How can you tell the difference between a pure substance and a mixture using melting point data?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A pure substance has a sharp, fixed melting point. A mixture melts over a range of temperatures. Melting point data can therefore be used to check the purity of a substance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Separation Techniques</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Different mixtures require different separation methods, depending on the properties of the components. The main techniques are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Filtration</strong> — separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel; the solid residue stays on the paper while the liquid <dfn class="term" data-def="The liquid that passes through filter paper during filtration, leaving the insoluble solid behind.">filtrate</dfn> passes through.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Crystallisation</strong> — used to obtain a dissolved solid (solute) from a solution. The solution is heated to evaporate some solvent, then left to cool so crystals form.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Simple distillation</strong> — separates a solvent from a solution. The solution is heated until the solvent evaporates, then the vapour is cooled and condensed in a condenser and collected as a pure liquid.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Fractional distillation</strong> — separates a mixture of liquids with different boiling points. A fractionating column provides a temperature gradient so that each liquid condenses at a different height.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Paper Chromatography</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating mixtures of soluble substances by allowing a solvent to travel through the mixture on paper, causing components to move at different rates.">Paper chromatography</dfn> separates mixtures of soluble substances. A spot of the mixture is placed on a pencil line near the bottom of chromatography paper. The paper is placed in a solvent (the <dfn class="term" data-def="The solvent that moves through the paper during chromatography, carrying the dissolved substances with it.">mobile phase</dfn>), which rises up the paper by capillary action. As the solvent moves through the mixture, different substances travel at different rates because they have different attractions to the paper (the <dfn class="term" data-def="The chromatography paper itself, which the soluble substances interact with as the solvent carries them upwards.">stationary phase</dfn>) and the solvent.</p>

A pure substance produces a single spot. A mixture produces multiple spots, one for each component. <h2 data-narration-id="n13">Rf Values</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Each substance in a chromatogram can be identified by its <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the distance moved by a substance to the distance moved by the solvent front in chromatography. Rf = distance moved by substance / distance moved by solvent.">Rf value</dfn>. This is calculated using:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">The Rf value is always between 0 and 1. It depends on the solvent used, so Rf values can only be compared when the same solvent and conditions are used. A substance with a higher Rf value is more soluble in the mobile phase and travels further up the paper.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the Rf formula and explain what an Rf value of 0.6 tells you about a substance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front. Rf values are unique to each substance in a given solvent and can be used to identify unknown compounds.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">In this core practical, spots of different inks are placed on chromatography paper along a pencil baseline. The paper is lowered into a beaker of solvent so the baseline is just above the solvent level. As the solvent rises, it separates the dyes in each ink. A pencil line (not pen) is used for the baseline because pencil is insoluble and will not dissolve in the solvent. The solvent front is marked with a pencil when the paper is removed, and Rf values are calculated for each spot.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Chemical Tests for Gases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Several important gases can be identified using simple chemical tests:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Oxygen</strong> — relights a glowing splint.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Hydrogen</strong> — burns with a squeaky pop when a lit splint is held near the mouth of a test tube.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Carbon dioxide</strong> — turns <dfn class="term" data-def="A solution of calcium hydroxide used to test for carbon dioxide. It turns milky (cloudy white) when carbon dioxide is bubbled through it.">limewater</dfn> milky (cloudy white).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Chlorine</strong> — bleaches damp <dfn class="term" data-def="An indicator paper that turns red in acid and blue in alkali. Damp litmus paper is bleached white by chlorine gas.">litmus paper</dfn>, turning it white.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the test and result for oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and chlorine.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Oxygen relights a glowing splint. Hydrogen gives a squeaky pop. Carbon dioxide turns limewater milky. Chlorine bleaches damp litmus paper white.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Potable Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="Water that is safe to drink. It is not pure water in the chemical sense but has sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microorganisms.">Potable water</dfn> is water that is safe to drink. It is not the same as pure water in chemistry — it contains low levels of dissolved substances. In the UK, potable water is produced from fresh water sources by sedimentation (letting solids settle), filtration (removing fine particles) and chlorination (killing bacteria). In areas without fresh water, sea water can be made potable by distillation or reverse osmosis, but these are energy-intensive processes.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Water used in chemical analysis must not contain any dissolved salts. This is because dissolved ions could interfere with test results, giving false positives or inaccurate measurements. Deionised or distilled water is used instead of tap water in laboratory analysis.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n29">
  <p>When answering questions about chromatography, always state both the mobile phase (solvent) and the stationary phase (paper). If asked to calculate Rf values, show your working clearly — divide the distance moved by the substance by the distance moved by the solvent front.</p>
</div>
