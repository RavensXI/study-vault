--- DESCRIPTION ---
Complete and incomplete combustion, pollution from fossil fuels, and why cracking produces alkenes for polymers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Complete Combustion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Hydrocarbon fuels release energy when they burn. When there is a plentiful supply of oxygen, <dfn class="term" data-def="The burning of a fuel in excess oxygen, producing only carbon dioxide and water.">complete combustion</dfn> occurs. The products are <strong>carbon dioxide</strong> and <strong>water</strong>, and energy is given out — the reaction is exothermic.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For example, the complete combustion of methane:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What two products form during complete combustion of a hydrocarbon?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces <strong>carbon dioxide</strong> (\(CO_2\)) and <strong>water</strong> (\(H_2O\)). Energy is released (exothermic). This only happens when there is <strong>plenty of oxygen</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Incomplete Combustion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When the oxygen supply is limited, <dfn class="term" data-def="The burning of a fuel in insufficient oxygen, producing carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot) instead of carbon dioxide.">incomplete combustion</dfn> occurs. Instead of all the carbon atoms forming carbon dioxide, some form <strong>carbon monoxide</strong> (\(CO\)) or <strong>carbon</strong> (soot). Water is still produced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Incomplete combustion is a problem because:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A colourless, odourless, toxic gas (CO) produced by incomplete combustion of hydrocarbons.">Carbon monoxide</dfn> is a <strong>toxic gas</strong>. It is colourless and odourless, so it cannot be detected without a detector. It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells, preventing them from carrying oxygen. This can be fatal.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Soot</strong> (carbon particles) causes breathing problems, contributes to smog, and blackens buildings.</li>
</ul>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Carbon Monoxide Dangers in the Home</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Gas boilers, fires and cookers can produce carbon monoxide if they are faulty or poorly ventilated. Because carbon monoxide is colourless and has no smell, people may not realise it is present. Carbon monoxide detectors should be fitted in homes with gas appliances. Regular servicing ensures complete combustion and reduces the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Pollution from Burning Fuels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Burning hydrocarbon fuels causes several environmental problems beyond carbon monoxide and soot:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sulfur Dioxide and Acid Rain</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Some hydrocarbon fuels contain <strong>sulfur impurities</strong>. When these fuels burn, the sulfur reacts with oxygen to produce <dfn class="term" data-def="A toxic gas (SO\u2082) formed when fuels containing sulfur impurities are burned; it dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain.">sulfur dioxide</dfn> (\(SO_2\)). Sulfur dioxide dissolves in rainwater to form <dfn class="term" data-def="Rain that has become acidic due to dissolved pollutant gases such as sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.">acid rain</dfn>, which damages buildings, kills aquatic life in lakes and rivers, and harms trees and other vegetation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Oxides of Nitrogen</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Inside car engines and power stations, the temperatures are extremely high. At these temperatures, nitrogen and oxygen from the air react together to form <dfn class="term" data-def="Pollutant gases (NO and NO\u2082) formed when nitrogen and oxygen in the air react at the high temperatures inside engines.">oxides of nitrogen</dfn> (\(NO_x\)). These gases also contribute to acid rain and can cause breathing problems. Note that the nitrogen comes from the air, not from the fuel.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: Name three pollutants produced by burning fossil fuels and one problem each causes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Burning fossil fuels produces: \(CO_2\) (greenhouse gas, climate change), \(CO\) (toxic, binds to haemoglobin), soot (breathing problems), \(SO_2\) (acid rain from sulfur impurities), and \(NO_x\) (acid rain, formed at high temperatures from \(N_2\) and \(O_2\) in air).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Hydrogen as an Alternative Fuel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The specification asks you to evaluate hydrogen as an alternative to petrol for cars:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Advantages:</strong> The only product of burning hydrogen is water — no \(CO_2\), \(CO\), soot or \(SO_2\). It does not contribute to climate change or acid rain at the point of use.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Hydrogen is difficult to store (it must be kept at very high pressure or very low temperature). It is highly flammable and explosive. Most hydrogen is currently produced from fossil fuels, which still releases \(CO_2\). Hydrogen fuel cells and filling stations are expensive and not widely available.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Cracking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of breaking down larger, less useful hydrocarbon molecules (long-chain alkanes) into smaller, more useful ones, some of which are unsaturated (alkenes).">Cracking</dfn> is the process of breaking down larger, less useful hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more useful ones. It is carried out because the demand for short-chain hydrocarbons (like petrol and gases) is much greater than the supply from fractional distillation alone. There is a surplus of long-chain fractions (like fuel oil and bitumen).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Why is cracking necessary? Name the two types of product it produces.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cracking is necessary because the supply of long-chain hydrocarbons from crude oil exceeds demand, while demand for short-chain hydrocarbons exceeds supply. Cracking produces <strong>shorter alkanes</strong> (saturated, for fuels) and <strong>alkenes</strong> (unsaturated, used to make polymers).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="A homologous series of unsaturated hydrocarbons with at least one carbon-carbon double bond and the general formula C\u2099H\u2082\u2099.">Alkenes</dfn> are <dfn class="term" data-def="A hydrocarbon that contains at least one carbon-carbon double bond; it does not contain the maximum number of hydrogen atoms.">unsaturated</dfn> hydrocarbons — they contain at least one carbon–carbon <strong>double bond</strong> (\(C=C\)). This makes them more reactive than alkanes. Alkenes are used as the starting material for making <dfn class="term" data-def="Large molecules made by joining many small monomer molecules together in a polymerisation reaction.">polymers</dfn> (plastics) through polymerisation.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Cracking in Practice</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Cracking involves heating long-chain hydrocarbons to high temperatures and/or passing them over a catalyst. In the school practical, paraffin oil (a long-chain alkane mixture) is vaporised and passed over heated broken pottery (a catalyst). The products include shorter alkanes and alkenes. Alkenes can be tested for using <strong>bromine water</strong> — it turns from orange to colourless when an alkene is added, because the double bond reacts with bromine.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
