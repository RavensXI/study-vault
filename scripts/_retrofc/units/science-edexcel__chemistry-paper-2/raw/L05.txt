<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Polymers?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A very large molecule made up of many repeating smaller units (monomers) joined together in a long chain.">polymer</dfn> is a very large molecule made from many smaller molecules called <dfn class="term" data-def="A small molecule that can join together with other identical molecules to form a polymer.">monomers</dfn>. These monomers bond together in long chains through a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction in which many small monomer molecules join together to form a large polymer molecule.">polymerisation</dfn>. The most familiar example is <dfn class="term" data-def="A polymer made from ethene monomers, widely used in plastic bags and packaging.">poly(ethene)</dfn>, which is made from ethene monomers and used in everyday plastic products such as bags, bottles and packaging.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is a polymer, and how is it formed from monomers?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Polymers consist of large molecules containing long chains of carbon atoms. They are formed when many small monomer molecules join together in a polymerisation reaction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Addition Polymerisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of polymerisation in which unsaturated monomer molecules (alkenes) open their double bonds and join together with no other substance produced.">Addition polymerisation</dfn> occurs when unsaturated monomers, such as <dfn class="term" data-def="A hydrocarbon with a carbon-carbon double bond, with the general formula CnH2n.">alkenes</dfn>, open their carbon–carbon double bonds and join together. No other substance is produced — the only product is the polymer. For example, many ethene molecules join to form poly(ethene):</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">\(n\text{CH}_2\text{=CH}_2 \rightarrow -(\text{CH}_2\text{-CH}_2)_n\text{-}\)</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The double bond in each ethene monomer breaks, and each carbon atom forms a new single bond with the next monomer in the chain. The ‘n’ in the equation represents a very large number of repeating units.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Alkenes — The Monomers for Addition Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Alkenes are <dfn class="term" data-def="A hydrocarbon that contains at least one carbon-carbon double bond and is described as unsaturated.">unsaturated hydrocarbons</dfn> because they contain a carbon–carbon double bond (\(\text{C=C}\)). This double bond makes them reactive and able to undergo addition reactions. The general formula for alkenes is \(\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}\). Common examples include ethene (\(\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\)) and propene (\(\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\)).</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the general formula for alkenes and explain why they are called unsaturated.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alkenes contain a carbon–carbon double bond (\(\text{C=C}\)), making them unsaturated. This double bond allows them to undergo addition reactions, including addition polymerisation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Testing for Alkenes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">To test whether a hydrocarbon is an alkene, add <dfn class="term" data-def="An orange-brown solution that decolourises (turns colourless) when shaken with an unsaturated compound such as an alkene.">bromine water</dfn>. An alkene will decolourise bromine water, turning it from orange–brown to colourless. This happens because bromine adds across the double bond in an addition reaction. Alkanes do not decolourise bromine water because they have no double bond to react with.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Naming Addition Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Addition polymers are named by placing the word ‘poly’ in front of the monomer name in brackets. Ethene becomes poly(ethene), propene becomes poly(propene), and chloroethene becomes poly(chloroethene) — better known by its common name PVC. The brackets around the monomer name are important because they distinguish the polymer from the monomer itself.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">When you are given an unfamiliar polymer and asked to identify its monomer, reverse the process: take the repeating unit, replace the single bonds on each side with a double bond between the two carbon atoms, and you have the monomer. This skill is commonly tested in the exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Properties and Uses of Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Different polymers have different properties depending on their monomer and structure. Poly(ethene) is flexible and waterproof, making it ideal for plastic bags and bottles. Poly(propene) is tougher and more heat-resistant, so it is used for food containers, ropes and carpets. Poly(chloroethene), commonly called PVC, is rigid and durable, widely used in window frames and pipes.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Not all polymers are synthetic. <dfn class="term" data-def="A polymer that occurs naturally in living organisms, such as DNA, starch, cellulose and proteins.">Natural polymers</dfn> include DNA, starch, cellulose and proteins. DNA is a polymer made from nucleotide monomers. Starch and cellulose are both polymers of glucose, but their different structures give them very different properties. Proteins are polymers of amino acids. These natural polymers are essential for life.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Problems with Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Most synthetic polymers are not <dfn class="term" data-def="Able to be broken down naturally by microorganisms in the environment.">biodegradable</dfn>. This means they persist in landfill sites and the natural environment for hundreds of years, causing pollution. Burning polymers can release toxic gases such as carbon monoxide, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen cyanide. Recycling helps reduce waste, but different polymer types must be separated first, which adds cost and complexity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three problems caused by the disposal of synthetic polymers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Most synthetic polymers are not biodegradable. They take hundreds of years to decompose in landfill and can release toxic gases when burned. Recycling reduces waste but requires sorting different polymer types.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The thermal decomposition of large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more useful ones, producing alkenes and shorter alkanes.">Cracking</dfn> breaks down larger, less useful hydrocarbon molecules from crude oil into smaller, more useful ones. This process produces alkenes, which are the monomers used in addition polymerisation. Without cracking, there would not be enough alkene monomers to meet the demand for plastic production. Cracking also produces shorter-chain alkanes that are in higher demand as fuels.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <p>When asked to draw the repeating unit of a polymer, remember to remove the double bond from the monomer and extend single bonds out of each side of the bracket. Always include the ‘n’ subscript outside the bracket to show many units are joined.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
  <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n27">Polymers are large molecules made from many small monomers joined by polymerisation.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n28">Addition polymerisation uses alkene monomers — their double bonds open and join end-to-end with no other product.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n29">Most synthetic polymers are not biodegradable, creating disposal challenges including pollution and toxic gases from incineration.</li>
  </ul>
