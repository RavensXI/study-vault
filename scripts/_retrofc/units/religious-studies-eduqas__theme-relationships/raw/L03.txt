<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Same-Sex Relationships in the Modern World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 2013, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013, which came into force in March 2014, legalising same-sex marriage in England and Wales.">Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013</dfn> came into force in March 2014, allowing same-sex couples to marry in England and Wales. <dfn class="term" data-def="Legal partnerships introduced in the UK in 2005 giving same-sex couples rights equivalent to marriage, later extended under the Civil Partnerships Act.">Civil partnerships</dfn> had already been available since 2005. These legal changes have prompted significant debate within religious communities about whether such relationships align with their teachings. Different traditions &mdash; and different groups within each tradition &mdash; have reached different conclusions, often based on how they interpret the same scriptural texts.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what year did same-sex marriage become legal in England and Wales, and what 2005 law preceded it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 came into force in March 2014, legalising same-sex marriage. Civil partnerships had been available since 2005. Religious communities vary widely in how they respond to these legal changes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Christianity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Christianity does not hold a single view on same-sex relationships. Attitudes range from full acceptance to traditional opposition, often shaped by how believers interpret specific biblical passages. The texts most frequently cited in opposition include Leviticus 20:13 and 1 Timothy 1:8&ndash;10. Those who take a <dfn class="term" data-def="A conservative reading of scripture that understands religious texts as straightforwardly applicable across all times and cultures.">traditionalist</dfn> approach argue these verses clearly prohibit same-sex sexual activity. Those who take a more <dfn class="term" data-def="A reading of scripture that considers historical and cultural context when applying ancient texts to contemporary situations.">contextual</dfn> approach argue these passages addressed specific practices in ancient cultures and cannot simply be applied to committed, loving relationships today.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <strong>Catholic Church</strong> teaches, in Catechism paragraphs 2357&ndash;2359, that same-sex attraction is not sinful in itself, but that same-sex sexual activity is contrary to natural law. The Church calls gay and lesbian people to live chastely and to be treated with respect and compassion, while maintaining that marriage is between a man and a woman. Catholic teaching therefore opposes same-sex marriage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Anglican</strong> views are more diverse. The Church of England has not authorised same-sex marriage ceremonies, though it has permitted prayers of blessing for same-sex couples. Some Anglican dioceses and individual clergy are more affirming than official guidance, creating significant internal debate. By contrast, Quakers in Britain have affirmed same-sex marriage since 2009 and perform same-sex ceremonies in their meeting houses, arguing that love between two people reflects the divine light present in every person.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Leviticus 20:13 is part of the Holiness Code in the Hebrew Bible and has historically been read by many Christians as a prohibition on male same-sex intercourse. Critics of this application note that the same code forbids many things modern Christians do not observe, such as eating shellfish, and argue consistency requires understanding the text in its ancient context.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">1 Timothy 1:8&ndash;10 lists various behaviours the writer regards as contrary to sound teaching. Traditionalists include same-sex activity within this list. Progressive scholars dispute the translation of the Greek terms used and argue the passage targets exploitative or violent behaviour rather than consensual same-sex relationships.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The creation account in Genesis 1:27 states that God created human beings &ldquo;male and female,&rdquo; which some Christians take as establishing heterosexual partnership as the norm. Others note that Genesis does not explicitly discuss same-sex relationships at all, and that Galatians 3:28 &mdash; which declares there is &ldquo;neither male nor female&rdquo; in Christ &mdash; points toward an inclusive vision of humanity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Judaism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Jewish attitudes divide significantly along <dfn class="term" data-def="A traditional stream of Judaism that adheres closely to halakha (Jewish law) and traditional practices.">Orthodox</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A progressive stream of Judaism that reinterprets tradition in light of modern understanding and values.">Reform</dfn> lines. Orthodox Judaism maintains that same-sex sexual activity is prohibited by Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13, which form part of the Torah&rsquo;s Holiness Code. Orthodox communities do not recognise or perform same-sex marriages, though they call for respectful treatment of gay and lesbian individuals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Reform Judaism, by contrast, has moved to full affirmation of same-sex relationships. Liberal and Reform synagogues in Britain perform same-sex commitment ceremonies and, following the Marriage Act, same-sex marriages. The movement grounds this in the principle of human dignity (<em>kavod ha-beriyot</em>) and the commandment to love one&rsquo;s neighbour. This represents a genuine diversity of interpretation within a single religious tradition.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Mainstream Islamic scholarship holds that same-sex sexual activity is prohibited. This position draws on Qur&rsquo;an 7:80&ndash;81, which refers to the people of Lut (Lot) and describes their practice as a transgression. Muslim scholars across different schools of jurisprudence have generally agreed that sexual relationships are intended for marriage between a man and a woman, and that this reflects the natural order as God designed it. The majority of Muslim communities in Britain oppose same-sex marriage, though individual Muslims hold a range of personal views. Islam teaches that people experiencing same-sex attraction should be treated with dignity while being encouraged to live according to Islamic law.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Hinduism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Traditional Hindu teaching has linked sexual activity closely to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The second of the four Hindu stages of life (ashramas), characterised by marriage, household life, and procreation.">householder ashrama</dfn>, where the purpose of sexual relations is connected to procreation and the continuation of family lineage, as part of one&rsquo;s <em>dharma</em>. Under this framework, same-sex relationships may be seen as conflicting with the dharma of the householder. However, views within Hinduism are diverse. Hinduism has no single central authority, and there are ancient texts, art, and temple sculptures that depict same-sex intimacy without condemnation. Many Hindus in Britain take a more accepting view of same-sex relationships, and the Hindu Council UK has acknowledged diverse opinions within the community.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Sikhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <em>Lavan</em>, the four sacred stanzas recited during the Sikh marriage ceremony, describes the progressive union of a man and a woman with the divine. The Sikh Rehat Maryada (code of conduct) defines marriage as between a man and a woman, and most Sikh religious authorities oppose same-sex marriage. Nine of the ten Gurus were married to women, and marriage is seen as a spiritual journey that the Lavan describes in gendered terms. As with other traditions, however, individual Sikhs hold a range of views, and some Sikh voices call for greater inclusion of LGBTQ+ members in the community.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write one sentence explaining how the Lavan shapes Sikh teaching on marriage, and one sentence on the Catechism 2357-2359 Catholic position.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sikh teaching draws on the <em>Lavan</em> (the four marriage stanzas) to understand marriage as between a man and a woman. The Catholic Catechism (2357&ndash;2359) distinguishes between same-sex attraction (not sinful) and same-sex sexual activity (contrary to natural law).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Buddhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Buddhism has no single official teaching on same-sex relationships. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The third of the Five Precepts in Buddhism, which calls practitioners to refrain from sexual misconduct.">Third Precept</dfn> calls Buddhists to refrain from sexual misconduct, but what counts as misconduct is interpreted differently across traditions. Some Theravada teachers argue that the Third Precept implies sexual activity should be within a committed, procreative context; others argue it is about the harm caused by an action rather than the gender of the parties. The Triratna Buddhist Community (formerly the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order) has been broadly affirming of LGBTQ+ members. There is no pan-Buddhist consensus, and the lack of an official position reflects Buddhism&rsquo;s decentralised nature.</p>

From a humanist standpoint, a loving, committed same-sex relationship is as morally equivalent to a heterosexual relationship, because what matters is the quality of the relationship &mdash; honesty, care, and mutual respect &mdash; not the genders of the people involved. Humanists argue that laws and social norms should reflect evidence about human flourishing rather than ancient religious texts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Evaluating the Debate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The debate over same-sex relationships within and between religious traditions illustrates a broader question: how should ancient scriptural texts be applied in a contemporary legal and social context? Conservative voices argue that scripture provides timeless moral guidance and that reinterpreting it to suit modern values undermines its authority. Progressive voices argue that faithfulness to the spirit of a tradition &mdash; love, compassion, human dignity &mdash; sometimes requires updating its specific applications. Students should be able to present both positions with supporting evidence and reach a justified conclusion.</p>
