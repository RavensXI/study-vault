<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sexual Ethics Across Traditions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sexual ethics is one of the areas where religious traditions show the greatest internal diversity &mdash; from strong absolute positions rooted in natural law or scripture, to more flexible approaches that weigh pastoral context and the well-being of individuals. This lesson examines what each tradition teaches about the purposes of sex, the use of contraception, and attitudes towards divorce, presenting these as legitimate disagreements between and within traditions rather than matters with a single correct answer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Christianity &mdash; Natural Law and the Purpose of Sex</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Thomas Aquinas (1225&ndash;1274) developed the theory of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ethical theory, associated with Aquinas, that moral rules can be derived from the natural purposes God has built into human nature and the world.">Natural Law</dfn>, arguing that God has built purposes into human nature that can be discerned by reason. Aquinas identified five primary precepts &mdash; fundamental human goods &mdash; the second of which is <em>reproduction</em>. From this, Aquinas argued that sexual acts must be open to the possibility of new life in order to be morally permissible. Any sexual act that by its nature cannot result in procreation (for example, contraceptive sex) violates natural law in this approach. This absolutist position has been enormously influential in Catholic moral theology.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Catholic Church&rsquo;s position on contraception is set out in the encyclical <dfn class="term" data-def="An official papal letter addressed to all Catholic bishops. Pope Paul VI&rsquo;s encyclical on human sexuality, issued in 1968, which rejected artificial contraception.">Humanae Vitae</dfn> (1968), issued by Pope Paul VI. The encyclical taught that each act of sexual intercourse must remain open to the transmission of life, and that artificial contraception breaks the God-given link between the unitive and procreative dimensions of sex. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (2362&ndash;2363) affirms that sexual love within marriage is good and holy, united with fruitfulness. Natural family planning (tracking fertility cycles) is permitted as it works with rather than against the body&rsquo;s natural processes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Church of England and most Protestant traditions take a different position. The Lambeth Conference 1930 was a turning point: for the first time, Anglican bishops accepted that contraception could be morally acceptable within a faithful marriage if used responsibly. Most Anglican, Methodist and other Protestant churches today leave the decision about contraception to couples, trusting their moral discernment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who wrote Humanae Vitae, in what year, and what did it say about contraception?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Humanae Vitae</strong> (1968) by Pope Paul VI rejected artificial contraception, teaching that every act of sex must remain open to new life. <strong>CCC 2362&ndash;2363</strong> affirms the goodness of sexual love within marriage, united with its procreative purpose. These two sources are the key Catholic authorities on this topic.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Judaism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Jewish teaching on sex and contraception varies significantly between Orthodox and Reform (Progressive) communities. In Orthodox Judaism, the first commandment in the Torah is to &ldquo;be fruitful and multiply&rdquo; (Genesis 1:28), meaning that openness to children is a high religious duty. However, the Talmud and rabbinic authorities have historically permitted contraception for women in certain circumstances &mdash; for example, where pregnancy would pose a health risk. The form of contraception matters: methods that do not destroy male seed are generally more acceptable than those that do in traditional halachic reasoning. Reform and Progressive Judaism generally leaves contraceptive decisions to individuals, supporting modern medical practice and reproductive autonomy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">On divorce, the Torah (Deuteronomy 24:1&ndash;4) makes provision for a man to give his wife a bill of divorce (<dfn class="term" data-def="The Hebrew term for the Jewish divorce document, which must be given by the husband to the wife to dissolve an Orthodox Jewish marriage.">get</dfn>). In Orthodox Judaism, a religious divorce requires the husband to issue the get; a woman cannot unilaterally obtain one. This has created a significant social problem where some husbands refuse to issue the get, leaving women as <em>agunot</em> (chained women) &mdash; unable to remarry in the eyes of Orthodox law. Reform Judaism has addressed this by granting courts the power to dissolve marriages on behalf of either party.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Islam teaches that sexual relations outside marriage are forbidden. The Qur&rsquo;an (17:32) states that one should not go near unlawful sexual relations, as it is an indecent act and a wrong way. Within marriage, sexuality is regarded as a gift from God and a source of spiritual closeness as well as procreation. On contraception, many Muslim scholars permit it where there is a valid reason (spacing of children, health concerns) using the principle that temporary prevention of birth is acceptable, though permanent sterilisation is generally opposed. Majority opinion holds that abortion as contraception is not permitted.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Divorce is permitted in Islam but strongly discouraged. The Qur&rsquo;an (2:229) sets out a structured process for <dfn class="term" data-def="The form of Islamic divorce in which the husband pronounces the divorce formula, with a waiting period to allow for reconciliation.">talaq</dfn> (divorce) with a waiting period (<em>iddah</em>) to allow for reconciliation. The Qur&rsquo;an (4:35) encourages arbitration by family members before divorce is finalised. Women can seek divorce through a legal process (<em>khul&rsquo;</em>). Remarriage after divorce is permitted.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">In Hindu teaching, sex within the context of the householder (<em>grihastha</em>) stage of life fulfils a legitimate purpose as part of dharmic duty. The Bhagavata Purana (5.5.8) suggests that sex motivated solely by bodily pleasure, divorced from its role in maintaining righteous family life, leads to negative consequences in terms of karma. However, many contemporary Hindu communities in Britain take a more flexible view, with traditional and modern perspectives coexisting. Contraception is not categorically prohibited; the intention behind family planning matters. Arranged marriage is still common, and divorce, while not ideal, is recognised as necessary in some cases. Views on same-sex relationships differ: traditional dharmic frameworks associate marriage with the householder ashrama designed for the continuation of family, while many Hindu communities in Britain take a more open position.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Sikhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The Guru Granth Sahib (335) contains teachings about the spiritual dimension of human relationships, emphasising that the body is sacred and that life should be lived in accordance with God&rsquo;s will (<em>hukam</em>). Sikhism does not have a formal prohibition on contraception and generally leaves such decisions to couples. Sex within marriage is regarded as a positive part of human life; sex outside marriage is discouraged as it can lead to attachment and suffering. On divorce, the Rehat Maryada (Sikh code of conduct, Chapter 11, Article 18) does not explicitly prohibit it but emphasises that marriage is a lifelong commitment. Guru Granth Sahib 274 stresses the importance of faithfulness in marriage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Buddhism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Third Precept in Buddhism is to refrain from sexual misconduct. In traditional Theravada interpretation, this means avoiding adultery, sex with those who are &ldquo;already taken,&rdquo; and relationships based on exploitation or harm. It does not necessarily mean sex outside marriage is always wrong, since Buddhism does not require a formal marriage ceremony &mdash; what matters is whether the relationship causes harm. Monks and nuns in the Theravada tradition are celibate; in the Zen tradition, monks may marry. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A British Buddhist community (formally the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order) that has inclusive views on sexual relationships, distinguishing between ethical and unethical conduct rather than imposing a rigid set of rules.">Triratna Buddhist Community</dfn> (formerly the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order) takes an inclusive approach, focusing on whether a relationship is ethical and free from exploitation rather than on its formal structure. On contraception, there is no Buddhist prohibition &mdash; responsible family planning is generally accepted.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words: what is the Third Precept in Buddhism, and how does Triratna interpret it differently from Theravada?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Third Precept prohibits sexual misconduct. The Triratna Buddhist Community takes an inclusive approach, distinguishing between ethical and unethical relationships based on the presence or absence of harm and exploitation &mdash; not on whether the couple are formally married.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Non-Religious Perspectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Humanists support individual autonomy in sexual ethics, arguing that consenting adults have the right to make their own decisions about relationships and contraception. They strongly support access to contraception as a matter of reproductive health and human rights. On divorce, Humanists argue that unhappy or harmful marriages should be dissoluble, and that the quality of a relationship matters more than its legal status. They typically oppose religious restrictions on contraception or divorce that they consider harmful to individuals, particularly women.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n22">Most traditions prohibit adultery. The Ten Commandments include &ldquo;You shall not commit adultery&rdquo; (Exodus 20:14), and all six traditions regard sexual faithfulness within marriage as important. On divorce and remarriage, responses differ significantly. Catholic Christianity does not permit divorce (only annulment in exceptional cases where the marriage was invalid from the start). Most Protestant churches permit divorce in cases of adultery or irretrievable breakdown, citing Matthew 19:8&ndash;9 (Jesus acknowledging that Moses permitted divorce &ldquo;because of the hardness of your hearts&rdquo;). Mark 10:9 records Jesus saying &ldquo;what God has joined together, let no one separate.&rdquo; Islam permits divorce through talaq (Qur&rsquo;an 2:229) and remarriage. Judaism requires a get for a valid Orthodox divorce. Buddhism and Sikhism do not prohibit divorce, though both regard marriage as a serious commitment not to be ended lightly.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Catholic Natural Law (Aquinas) holds that sex must always be open to new life; Humanae Vitae (1968, Pope Paul VI) rejects artificial contraception on these grounds; CCC 2362&ndash;2363 affirms sexual love within marriage as holy. Most Protestant traditions accept contraception after the Lambeth Conference 1930.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Islam permits contraception for valid reasons and allows divorce through talaq with a waiting period; Judaism requires the get for an Orthodox divorce and varies on contraception between Orthodox and Reform communities.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Buddhism&rsquo;s Third Precept prohibits sexual misconduct rather than sex outside marriage per se; the Triratna community focuses on whether a relationship is ethical and free from harm rather than on formal marital status.</li>
</ul>
