--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to hear binary, ternary, rondo, theme and variations, and sonata form rather than just read about them, using examples from Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">You already know that pieces from the classical and romantic periods are built from small musical ideas, repeated and rearranged. Composers writing between 1650 and 1910 relied on a handful of standard structures to organise a whole movement. Learn to recognise five of them &mdash; binary, ternary, rondo, theme and variations, and sonata form &mdash; and you can label the structure of almost any extract in the listening exam, even one you have never heard before.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n2">Binary form</h2><p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="A two-section structure, usually heard as AABB, with a change of key between the sections.">Binary form</dfn> has two sections, usually called A and B. Both are heard once, then normally repeated immediately, so the pattern you actually hear is AABB. Section A often ends in a different key from the one it started in, and section B leads the music back home. Listen for a firm cadence &mdash; a clear stopping point &mdash; marking the end of each section, and for the same music returning almost exactly when a repeat happens.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n90"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; a complete binary form (AABB)</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/western-classical-1650-1910/binary-demo.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> two sections, each played twice: A ends away from home (on the dominant), B finds its way back &mdash; count A, A, B, B.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">StudyVault study miniature &mdash; composed so the whole form fits in under a minute.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ternary form</h2><p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A three-part structure, ABA, where a contrasting middle section is followed by a return of the opening idea.">Ternary form</dfn> is ABA: an opening idea, a contrasting middle section, then a return of the opening. The middle section usually changes key, tempo, mood or texture, so it stands apart clearly. The giveaway is the return &mdash; when the opening tune comes back largely unchanged after the contrast, you are hearing ternary form, not just a repeat.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n91"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; a complete ternary form (ABA)</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/western-classical-1650-1910/ternary-demo.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> a bright opening section, a contrasting middle in the minor, then the opening returns &mdash; A, B, A.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">StudyVault study miniature &mdash; composed so the whole form fits in under a minute.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n6">Rondo form</h2><p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A structure in which a main theme keeps returning between contrasting episodes, such as ABACA.">Rondo form</dfn> is built from a main theme, called the refrain, that keeps coming back between contrasting sections called episodes. The pattern is often ABACA or ABACABA &mdash; the letters after A are new episodes, and each return of A is the same tune you heard first, sometimes lightly varied. The signpost is repetition: if a specific tune keeps interrupting new material, you are listening to a rondo.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n8"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Mozart, Clarinet Concerto K.622, movement 3</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/western-classical-1650-1910/lesson-03.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> the solo clarinet stating the rondo theme first, the orchestra repeating it straight after, and that same lyrical, legato tune returning between contrasting episodes. Notice the grace-note ornaments in the clarinet line each time the theme comes back.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n9">Theme and variations</h2><p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A structure in which a theme is stated in full, then repeated several times with changes to elements such as ornamentation, dynamics, instrumentation, harmony or rhythm.">Theme and variations</dfn> form states a theme in full, then repeats it several times with changes. Composers vary the ornamentation, the dynamics, the instrumentation, the harmony or the rhythm, while keeping the theme&rsquo;s phrase structure recognisable underneath. Listen for the same underlying shape returning each time, dressed differently &mdash; a melody moved from strings to woodwind, or a plain tune gaining decoration on its repeat.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n11"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Haydn, Symphony No. 94 &lsquo;Surprise&rsquo;, movement 2</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/western-classical-1650-1910/lesson-04.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> the opening theme is itself in binary form &mdash; two 8-bar sections, each repeated &mdash; played very quietly by strings alone. A sudden fortissimo chord from the full orchestra crashes in at about 0:42, interrupting the quiet. Across the variations that follow, listen for added ornamentation, changing dynamics, and the melody moving between different instruments.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sonata form</h2><p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A three-stage structure of exposition, development and recapitulation, built around two contrasting musical subjects.">Sonata form</dfn> is the most demanding structure in the specification. It has three main stages: exposition, development and recapitulation. The exposition presents the musical material, the development reshapes and unsettles it, and the recapitulation brings it home again &mdash; think of it as introduce, explore, resolve.</p><p data-narration-id="n14">In the <dfn class="term" data-def="The opening section of sonata form, where the first and second subjects are presented.">exposition</dfn>, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The opening theme of a sonata-form exposition, stated in the tonic key.">first subject</dfn> is stated in the home key, called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The home key of a piece or section of music.">tonic</dfn>. A transition passage then <dfn class="term" data-def="A change from one key to another within a piece of music.">modulates</dfn> before the <dfn class="term" data-def="The contrasting theme of a sonata-form exposition, usually stated in the dominant key of a major-key movement.">second subject</dfn> arrives in a contrasting key, usually the dominant if the movement is in a major key. The two subjects often contrast in character: the first energetic, the second more lyrical.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover the box, then write out the five structures and their letter patterns: Binary AB, Ternary ABA, Rondo ABACA, Sonata exposition-development-recapitulation."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>In a major-key sonata-form movement, the second subject arrives in the dominant key during the exposition &mdash; but returns in the tonic key in the recapitulation. That shift back to the home key is one of the clearest signposts you can listen for.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The middle section of sonata form, where themes are fragmented, reworked and passed through unstable keys.">development</dfn> takes fragments of the exposition&rsquo;s themes and works them through unstable, quickly-changing keys. Textures can thin out or thicken, short motifs get passed between instruments, and the harmony feels unsettled &mdash; this is the most unpredictable part of the movement.</p><p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The final section of sonata form, where both subjects return in the tonic key.">recapitulation</dfn> restates the first and second subjects in order, but now both stay in the tonic key, resolving the tension the development created. Some movements add a short coda afterwards to round things off.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n18"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Beethoven, Symphony No. 1, movement 1</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/western-classical-1650-1910/lesson-01.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> the slow introduction delaying the home key &mdash; the very first chord belongs to F major, not the C major you would expect from a symphony in that key. From about 1:50, the energetic first subject arrives, driven by short repeated motifs, sforzando accents and a rapid crescendo, all in a brisk 2/2 metre.</p></figure><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Quick ear-test checklist</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n19">Binary: AABB &mdash; a repeat with a firm cadence at the end of each section. Ternary: ABA &mdash; a changed middle section, then an almost exact return. Rondo: a specific tune keeps coming back between new episodes. Theme and variations: the same underlying shape returns each time, dressed differently. Sonata form: two contrasting subjects that separate in key, then reunite in the tonic at the end.</p></div></div></div><p data-narration-id="n20">In the exam, do not just name the structure &mdash; say what you heard that proves it: a returning theme, a change of key, a repeated section. That evidence is what earns the marks.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<p>Five structures cover almost everything you will meet in this unit: binary and ternary for shorter forms, rondo for a returning refrain, theme and variations for a tune that keeps changing its clothes, and sonata form for the most tightly argued of all &mdash; exposition, development, recapitulation. Now open the matching listening drill for Area of Study 1 and practise spotting each signpost by ear, not from memory.</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>When a Section A question asks you to identify structure, name the term &mdash; binary, ternary, rondo, theme and variations, or sonata form &mdash; and then give one piece of evidence: a repeated section, a returning theme, or a change of key. A bare label without evidence rarely scores full marks.</p>
