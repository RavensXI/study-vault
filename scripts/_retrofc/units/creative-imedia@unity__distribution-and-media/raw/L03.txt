--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sample rate, bit depth, audio compression, and the key audio file formats you need to know.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Digital Audio Works</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sound travels through the air as <dfn class="term" data-def="Continuous vibrations that travel through the air, representing sound in its natural form before digital conversion.">analogue</dfn> sound waves. When we want to store or distribute sound using a computer, the analogue signal must be converted into a digital format that the computer can understand. This process is called <strong>digital sampling</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of measuring an analogue sound wave at regular intervals to convert it into a digital format.">Sampling</dfn> works by measuring the sound wave at regular intervals — taking thousands of tiny “snapshots” of the sound every second. Each snapshot records the amplitude (loudness) of the wave at that exact moment. When these snapshots are played back in sequence, they recreate the original sound.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the audio terms and technical specifications here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The two key properties of digital audio are <strong>sample rate</strong> (how many times per second the sound is measured) and <strong>bit depth</strong> (how accurately each measurement is recorded). Higher values of both mean higher quality sound — but also larger file sizes.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sample Rate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The number of times per second that a sound wave is measured during digital sampling, expressed in Hertz (Hz).">Sample rate</dfn> is the number of times per second that the sound wave is measured. It is expressed in <strong>Hertz (Hz)</strong>. A sample rate of 44,100 Hz (often written as 44.1 kHz) means the sound wave is sampled 44,100 times every second.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The higher the sample rate, the more accurately the digital version captures the original analogue sound. Common sample rates include:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>22,050 Hz (22.05 kHz)</strong> — lower quality, suitable for speech recordings and podcasts where file size needs to be small. <strong>44,100 Hz (44.1 kHz)</strong> — CD quality, the standard for music distribution. This is the most commonly referenced sample rate in the exam. <strong>48,000 Hz (48 kHz)</strong> — used in professional video production and broadcast audio. <strong>96,000 Hz (96 kHz)</strong> — high-resolution audio for professional studio recording.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why 44,100 Hz Is the Standard</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">Human hearing can detect frequencies up to about 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). To accurately capture all audible frequencies, the sample rate must be at least double the highest frequency — this is known as the Nyquist theorem. Double 20,000 Hz gives 40,000 Hz as the minimum. The standard of 44,100 Hz provides a small safety margin above this minimum, ensuring all audible sound is captured faithfully.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Bit Depth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The number of bits used to record each individual sample, determining how accurately the amplitude of the sound wave is measured.">Bit depth</dfn> determines how accurately each individual sample is recorded. It measures how many possible amplitude values each sample can represent. Think of it as the “detail” of each snapshot.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">An 8-bit recording can represent 256 different amplitude levels. A <strong>16-bit</strong> recording (CD quality) can represent 65,536 levels, capturing far more subtle variations in volume. A <strong>24-bit</strong> recording (professional studio quality) offers over 16.7 million levels. The higher the bit depth, the more faithfully the digital file captures quiet sounds, subtle dynamics, and the full range of the original recording.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/creative-imedia/distribution-and-media/diagram_audio_file_properties_and_formats.jpg" alt="Diagram: Audio File Properties and Formats" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the audio terms and technical specifications here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>CD-quality audio uses a sample rate of <strong>44,100 Hz</strong> and a bit depth of <strong>16 bits</strong>. Remember: sample rate = how often, bit depth = how detailed.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Channels: Mono and Stereo</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The number of separate audio tracks in a recording — mono uses one channel, stereo uses two.">Channels</dfn> refer to the number of separate audio tracks in a recording. <strong>Mono</strong> audio has one channel — the same sound plays through all speakers. <strong>Stereo</strong> audio has two channels (left and right), allowing different sounds to be directed to different speakers for a more immersive listening experience. Stereo files are roughly double the size of mono files because they contain twice the data.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Audio File Formats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Audio file formats fall into three categories: <strong>uncompressed</strong>, <strong>lossless compressed</strong>, and <strong>lossy compressed</strong>. Each has different properties that suit different purposes.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Uncompressed Audio Formats</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>WAV (.wav)</strong> — Waveform Audio File. Excellent sound quality with no compression at all. Widely supported across all editing software and operating systems. Standard for audio editing and production. However, file sizes are very large (a 3-minute song at CD quality is approximately 30 MB), making WAV unsuitable for streaming or limited storage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>AIFF (.aiff)</strong> — Audio Interchange File Format. Apple’s equivalent of WAV. High quality, uncompressed, widely used in professional audio on Mac systems. Large file sizes, and less common outside the Apple ecosystem.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Lossy Compressed Audio Formats</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>MP3 (.mp3)</strong> — The most popular lossy audio format. Reduces file size significantly (typically to about one-tenth of the uncompressed size) by removing audio data that most listeners cannot hear. Widely supported on virtually all devices and platforms. Ideal for music streaming and downloads. Limitation: some sound quality is permanently lost, especially at very low bitrates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>AAC (.aac)</strong> — Advanced Audio Coding. Produces better sound quality than MP3 at the same file size. Used by Apple (iTunes, Apple Music), YouTube, and many streaming services. Limitation: not as universally supported as MP3 on older devices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>OGG Vorbis (.ogg)</strong> — An open-source lossy format with good quality at small file sizes. No licensing fees, unlike MP3. Used in some games and open-source software. Limitation: limited support on some mainstream devices and music players.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Lossless Compressed Audio Formats</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>FLAC (.flac)</strong> — Free Lossless Audio Codec. Compresses audio to about half the size of WAV while preserving 100% of the original quality. Open-source with no licensing fees. Widely supported on most modern devices and music players. Limitation: larger file sizes than MP3/AAC, and not supported on all older devices (particularly some Apple products).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>ALAC (.m4a)</strong> — Apple Lossless Audio Codec. Apple’s equivalent of FLAC. Works seamlessly with iTunes and Apple devices. Full quality preservation with moderate file sizes. Limitation: mainly used within the Apple ecosystem.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the file formats and their properties from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Uncompressed</strong> (WAV, AIFF) = best quality, largest files, used for editing. <strong>Lossy</strong> (MP3, AAC, OGG) = small files, some quality lost, used for streaming and sharing. <strong>Lossless</strong> (FLAC, ALAC) = full quality preserved, moderate file sizes, used by audiophiles.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Choosing the Right Audio Format</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">The right format depends on the intended use. For <strong>streaming and downloads</strong>, MP3 or AAC is preferred because small file sizes allow fast loading and minimal data usage. For <strong>CD distribution</strong>, uncompressed WAV at 44.1 kHz / 16-bit is the standard. For <strong>professional editing</strong>, WAV or AIFF preserves full quality during production. For <strong>archiving</strong>, FLAC offers a good balance of quality preservation and reduced storage space.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n30">Sample rate (Hz) measures how many times per second sound is sampled; bit depth measures how accurately each sample is recorded. Higher values = better quality but larger files.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Uncompressed formats (WAV, AIFF) offer the best quality; lossy formats (MP3, AAC) trade quality for smaller files; lossless formats (FLAC, ALAC) preserve full quality at moderate file sizes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32">Format choice depends on context: MP3/AAC for streaming, WAV for editing, FLAC for archiving, and WAV at 44.1 kHz / 16-bit for CD quality.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">When the exam asks about audio properties, always define sample rate and bit depth separately — they are two different things. Sample rate is <strong>how often</strong> the sound is measured (in Hz). Bit depth is <strong>how accurately</strong> each measurement is recorded (in bits). A common mistake is confusing the two. If asked to compare compressed and uncompressed formats, always state what is gained (smaller file size) and what is lost (some sound quality for lossy) or preserved (full quality for lossless).</p>
