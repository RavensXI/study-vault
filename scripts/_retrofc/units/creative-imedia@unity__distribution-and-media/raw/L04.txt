<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Moving Image Files?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Digital files containing a sequence of still images (frames) played in rapid succession to create the appearance of motion, often combined with audio.">Moving image files</dfn> include videos and animations. Whether digital or traditional film, video is fundamentally a series of still images that, when played in sequence at a certain speed, create the appearance of motion. Each individual image is called a <strong>frame</strong>. Moving image files are used for DVDs, streamed programmes, social media videos, animations, and live footage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The two key properties that determine the quality of a moving image file are <strong>frame rate</strong> and <strong>resolution</strong>. Understanding these properties is essential for choosing the right file format and settings for different distribution methods.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Frame Rate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The number of individual frames (still images) displayed per second in a video, measured in frames per second (fps).">Frame rate</dfn> is the speed at which frames are displayed, expressed as <strong>frames per second (fps)</strong>. If a video is captured and played back at 24 fps, that means 24 distinct still images are shown every second. The higher the frame rate, the smoother the motion appears on screen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Common frame rates include:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>24 fps</strong> — the standard for cinema and feature films. Gives a slightly “cinematic” look with natural motion blur. <strong>25 fps</strong> — the standard for UK television broadcasting (PAL). <strong>30 fps</strong> — common for online video, social media content, and US television (NTSC). <strong>60 fps</strong> — used for sports broadcasts, gaming, and slow-motion footage. Produces very smooth motion but generates larger file sizes because twice as many frames are captured per second.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the video/animation terms and their definitions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Higher frame rates produce smoother video but create larger file sizes. A video at 60 fps contains twice as many frames as the same video at 30 fps, so the file will be approximately twice as large.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Resolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of pixels displayed on screen, expressed as width multiplied by height, determining the clarity and detail of the image.">Resolution</dfn> describes the total number of pixels on screen. It is expressed as pixel dimensions (width × height). The more pixels, the sharper and more detailed the image. The specification requires you to know five resolution standards:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>SD (Standard Definition)</strong> — 720 × 576 pixels (PAL) or 720 × 480 pixels (NTSC). The original digital TV standard. Good enough for older TVs, DVD playback, and video sharing at lower quality. Limited detail compared to modern standards.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>HD (High Definition)</strong> — 1280 × 720 pixels (720p) or 1920 × 1080 pixels (1080p, also called Full HD). Much clearer than SD with significantly more pixels. The standard for most HD broadcasts, streaming services, Blu-ray, and modern TV. 1080p is the most widely used resolution for online video today.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>UHD (Ultra High Definition) / 4K</strong> — 3840 × 2160 pixels. Four times the pixel count of 1080p Full HD. Used in cinema, some Blu-ray releases, high-end gaming, and premium streaming (Netflix 4K, YouTube 4K). Requires powerful hardware to edit and play back smoothly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>8K</strong> — 7680 × 4320 pixels. Sixteen times the pixel count of 1080p. Currently used in very high-end production, visual effects work, and some experimental TV broadcasts. Very few consumers have 8K displays. File sizes are enormous.</p>

<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Higher resolution means more pixels, sharper images, and better detail — but also much larger file sizes. 4K has four times as many pixels as 1080p Full HD. 8K has sixteen times as many.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Moving Image File Formats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Moving image file formats serve different purposes depending on quality requirements, compatibility, and distribution method. These formats are often described as <strong>containers</strong> — they package together video data, audio data, and metadata (such as subtitles and chapter markers) into a single file.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Video File Formats</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>MP4 (.mp4)</strong> — The most widely used video format. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A file format that packages together video, audio, and metadata into a single file.">container</dfn> format that supports high-quality video with efficient compression. Compatible with virtually all devices, browsers, and platforms. Used by YouTube, social media platforms, and most streaming services. Supports both H.264 and H.265 codecs. Very few limitations — the standard choice for most video distribution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>MOV (.mov)</strong> — Apple’s video container format, developed by Apple for QuickTime. Widely used for video files from digital cameras, providing good quality. Well supported on Mac systems and professional editing software. Limitation: not as universally supported as MP4, particularly on some Windows and Android devices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>AVI (.avi)</strong> — Audio Video Interleave, developed by Microsoft. An older, typically uncompressed video format that produces very high quality. Often used during video editing before exporting to a compressed format. Limitation: file sizes can be extremely large. Not all devices can play AVI files without installing additional software (codecs).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>MPEG / MPG (.mpg)</strong> — An older compressed video format using lossy compression for smaller file sizes and faster loading. Used on DVDs and some broadcast systems. Limitation: compression can noticeably lower video quality compared to modern formats like MP4.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>MKV (.mkv)</strong> — Matroska Video. An open-source container that can hold virtually any combination of video, audio, and subtitle tracks. Popular for high-quality video distribution and media libraries. Limitation: not supported natively by all devices and may require third-party players.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>WMV (.wmv)</strong> — Windows Media Video. Microsoft’s proprietary format. Produces small files with reasonable quality. Limitation: mainly supported on Windows systems, with limited compatibility on Mac and mobile devices.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>GIF (.gif)</strong> — Supports short, looping animations with no audio. Limited to 256 colours, so quality is low but file sizes are very small. Widely used for social media reactions, memes, and simple web animations. Not suitable for high-quality video.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>SWF (.swf)</strong> — Adobe Flash format, previously used for interactive animations and games on websites. Supports lossless compression and interactivity. Largely obsolete since Adobe discontinued Flash Player in 2020. Most modern browsers no longer support SWF files.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>WebM (.webm)</strong> — An open web media format developed by Google. Uses both lossy and lossless compression. Designed for efficient web video and animation. Supported by most modern browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Edge). Limitation: not compatible with all devices and older software.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Codecs and Compression</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Software that encodes and decodes video data, compressing it for storage and decompressing it for playback.">codec</dfn> (coder-decoder) is the software that compresses video data for storage and decompresses it for playback. The container (MP4, MOV, AVI) holds the data; the codec determines how that data is compressed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>H.264</strong> (also called AVC) is the most widely used video codec. It provides good quality at relatively small file sizes and is supported on virtually all devices. <strong>H.265</strong> (also called HEVC) is the successor to H.264. It achieves similar quality at roughly half the file size, making it ideal for 4K content. However, H.265 requires more powerful hardware to encode and decode.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the resolution or dimension specifications here.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Rendering</strong> is the process of using specialist software to compile edited video, audio, effects, and transitions into a final playable file. It can take significant processing time, especially for high-resolution or effects-heavy projects.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Choosing the Right Format</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Format choice depends on the intended distribution method and audience. For <strong>web and social media</strong>, MP4 with H.264 codec is the standard — widely compatible with small file sizes. For <strong>professional editing</strong>, AVI or MOV preserves quality during production. For <strong>DVD distribution</strong>, MPEG format is used. For <strong>high-quality archiving</strong> or media libraries, MKV can store multiple audio and subtitle tracks in one file.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n36">Frame rate (fps) controls how smooth motion looks — 24 fps for cinema, 30 fps for online video, 60 fps for sport and gaming. Higher frame rates mean larger files.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Resolution ranges from SD (720 × 576) through HD (1080p) and 4K (3840 × 2160) to 8K. More pixels means sharper images but much larger file sizes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">MP4 is the standard format for most video distribution; the codec (H.264 or H.265) determines how efficiently the video is compressed within the container.</li></ul>

For example: “MP4 because it uses compression to keep the file size small (1), which is important for streaming online where bandwidth is limited (1).” Also be prepared to explain the difference between frame rate and resolution — frame rate is about <strong>smoothness of motion</strong>, resolution is about <strong>sharpness of image</strong>.</p>
