--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Allegorical Framework</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Animal Farm is a sustained <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative where characters, events and settings consistently represent real-world equivalents.">allegory</dfn> of the Russian Revolution and its aftermath. Understanding the allegorical mapping is not optional for OCR — it is the foundation of AO3 context. But sophisticated responses go beyond simple matching to explore <em>why</em> Orwell chose allegory as his form and what it achieves that a straightforward political essay could not.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain three advantages of allegory over direct political argument.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Allegory: (1) makes complex politics accessible through familiar characters (animals); (2) creates emotional engagement (we care about Boxer in a way we might not care about statistics); (3) achieves universality (the story applies beyond Russia to any revolution corrupted by power). These are AO2 points about Orwell’s choice of form.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Windmill</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure representing Soviet industrialisation (the Five-Year Plans), used as a tool of control and false promise of progress.">windmill</dfn> symbolises Soviet industrialisation — Stalin’s Five-Year Plans that promised modernisation but were achieved through forced labour and immense human cost. In the novella, the windmill is built, destroyed, rebuilt and ultimately used to mill corn for profit rather than to ease the animals’ workload. It represents the gap between the revolution’s promises (technology serving the people) and its reality (the people serving technology).</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Seven Commandments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The commandments symbolise the constitution or founding principles of a revolution. Their gradual corruption — each one subtly altered to accommodate the pigs’ behaviour — mirrors how Soviet law was manipulated to serve the ruling elite. Orwell’s structural choice to show the changes <em>after</em> the acts they justify makes the satire devastating: the law follows power, not the other way round.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sugarcandy Mountain and Religion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Moses’s tales of Sugarcandy Mountain — a paradise where “it was Sunday seven days a week, clover was in season all the year round, and lump sugar and linseed cake grew on the hedges” — represent religion’s role in pacifying the oppressed. The animals listen hopefully because their present lives are miserable. Napoleon tolerates Moses because a population focused on the afterlife will not rebel in the present. This parallels Marx’s famous critique of religion as “the opium of the people.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">“Beasts of England”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The revolutionary anthem symbolises the idealism of the revolution itself. Its banning after the purges represents the suppression of revolutionary memory — totalitarian regimes always destroy the original vision because it exposes how far they have fallen. The replacement anthem, praising Napoleon, shows how <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of art, music and culture to promote loyalty to a regime rather than to shared ideals.">cultural control</dfn> serves political power.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the symbolic significance of ‘Beasts of England’ being banned.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The banning of ‘Beasts of England’ symbolises the destruction of revolutionary idealism. The song represented the original dream of equality. Napoleon replaces it with a personality cult anthem because the original song, if remembered, would expose the gap between what was promised and what was delivered. Cultural control is essential to totalitarian power.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Farmhouse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The farmhouse symbolises the privileges of the ruling class. Jones lived there; now Napoleon does. The animals are forbidden from entering, just as Soviet citizens were excluded from the Kremlin. By moving into the farmhouse, the pigs physically occupy the space of the oppressor they overthrew. The beds, china and whisky are specific symbols of luxury that the revolution was supposed to abolish.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Farm Itself</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The farm is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small world that represents a larger one.">microcosm</dfn> for any society undergoing revolution. Its boundaries create a closed world where Orwell can trace the full arc from rebellion to tyranny. The neighbouring farms represent other nations: Pilkington (England/the West), Frederick (Germany/Hitler). Their shifting alliances with Animal Farm mirror the USSR’s wartime diplomacy, including the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the eventual alliance with the West.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The power of Orwell’s allegory is that it works simultaneously as a specific critique of Soviet history and a universal warning about power. A student who can discuss both levels — the specific (Stalin, Trotsky, the Purges) and the universal (how power corrupts, how propaganda works, how revolutions fail) — will achieve the top band.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name five key symbols and explain what each represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The windmill (Soviet industrialisation/false progress), the commandments (corrupted founding principles), Sugarcandy Mountain (religion pacifying the oppressed), ‘Beasts of England’ (revolutionary idealism, suppressed), the farmhouse (ruling-class privilege). For each, explain both the allegorical meaning and Orwell’s wider purpose.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Allegory gives Orwell emotional engagement, accessibility and universality that a political essay could not achieve.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The windmill, commandments, anthem and farmhouse are all symbols that work on both allegorical and universal levels.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The farm as microcosm allows Orwell to trace the full arc from rebellion to tyranny in a compact, powerful narrative.</li></ul>
