--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Characters as Allegorical Constructs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every character in Animal Farm serves a double function: they are believable animals AND <dfn class="term" data-def="Characters that represent real historical figures or social groups within an allegorical framework.">allegorical figures</dfn> representing real people or groups. In the OCR exam, you must analyse both layers. Use “Orwell presents [character] as…” to signal AO2 awareness.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Napoleon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Napoleon represents Stalin. He says little, works behind the scenes, trains the dogs secretly and seizes power through violence. Key quotation: Napoleon “had a reputation for getting his own way.” Orwell makes Napoleon less charismatic than Snowball deliberately — Stalin was not a great orator but a ruthless political operator. Napoleon’s power comes from fear and manipulation, not inspiration.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what makes Napoleon effective as both a character and an allegory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Napoleon works as a character because his behaviour (cunning, patient, ruthless) is convincingly pig-like. He works as an allegory because his rise to power precisely mirrors Stalin’s: eliminating a charismatic rival (Trotsky/Snowball), using secret police (NKVD/dogs), rewriting history, and building a cult of personality.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Snowball</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Snowball represents Trotsky — the intellectual, the planner, the genuine revolutionary. His windmill idea and education programmes show sincere commitment to improving the animals’ lives. After his expulsion, he becomes a scapegoat — everything that goes wrong is blamed on him, exactly as Trotsky was demonised after exile.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Squealer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Squealer represents Soviet propaganda (particularly <em>Pravda</em>). He is described as being able to “turn black into white” — Orwell’s shorthand for the power of propaganda to make lies seem true. His techniques: emotional manipulation, statistics that contradict lived experience, the “Jones would come back” threat and the gradual rewriting of the commandments. Squealer shows that <dfn class="term" data-def="Control exercised through language, narrative and the manipulation of information.">linguistic power</dfn> is as effective as physical force.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Boxer (the cart-horse) represents the loyal, exploited working class. His mottos — “I will work harder” and “Napoleon is always right” — show selfless dedication corrupted into blind obedience. His death (sold to the knacker) is the novella’s most powerful moment. Benjamin (the donkey) represents cynical intellectuals who see the truth but refuse to act. He knows the revolution is failing but says nothing until it is too late. Orwell critiques both blind loyalty (Boxer) and apathetic cynicism (Benjamin) as enabling tyranny.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what Boxer and Benjamin represent and why Orwell critiques both.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Boxer = blind loyalty (the working class who believe and obey). Benjamin = cynical inaction (intellectuals who see the truth but do nothing). Orwell shows that both responses enable tyranny: Boxer’s trust is exploited; Benjamin’s silence is complicity. Only a combination of awareness AND action could challenge the pigs — but no character achieves both.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Mollie and the Sheep</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Mollie (the vain horse who misses her ribbons and sugar) represents the bourgeoisie who fled Russia after the revolution, preferring comfort to equality. She disappears early, choosing human luxury over animal freedom. The sheep represent the masses who mindlessly repeat slogans (“Four legs good, two legs bad”) without understanding. They are easily re-programmed to chant “Four legs good, two legs better” — Orwell’s darkest comment on how propaganda exploits the uneducated.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Dogs, Moses and Minor Characters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The dogs represent the NKVD/secret police. Trained from puppyhood by Napoleon, they are instruments of pure terror. Moses the raven represents organised religion (the Russian Orthodox Church). He tells the animals about “Sugarcandy Mountain” — a paradise after death. Napoleon tolerates Moses because religion keeps the workers docile, promising reward in the afterlife rather than justice in the present. This parallels Marx’s view of religion as “the opium of the people.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Every character serves Orwell’s argument about how power operates. The genius of the allegory is that it works on both levels simultaneously: you can read it as a story about animals, or as a story about the Russian Revolution, and both readings enrich the other.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: match every major character to their allegorical counterpart.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Napoleon = Stalin. Snowball = Trotsky. Old Major = Marx/Lenin. Squealer = Pravda/propaganda. Boxer = exploited working class. Benjamin = cynical intellectuals. Mollie = the bourgeoisie. The sheep = mindless masses. The dogs = NKVD/secret police. Moses = organised religion. Mr Jones = Tsar Nicholas II.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Every character is both a believable animal and an allegorical figure — analyse both levels in the exam.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Orwell critiques both blind loyalty (Boxer) and cynical inaction (Benjamin) as enabling tyranny.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The sheep represent how propaganda exploits the uneducated, and Moses shows how religion keeps workers docile.</li></ul>
