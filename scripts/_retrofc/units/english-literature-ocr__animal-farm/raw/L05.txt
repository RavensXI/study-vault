<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Boxer’s Death (Chapter 9)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Boxer collapses while working on the windmill. He has worked himself to destruction — the very quality that made him valuable is what kills him. Napoleon announces that Boxer will be sent to a hospital. A van arrives with the words “Horse Slaughterer” painted on its side. Benjamin, who can read, raises the alarm: “They are taking you to your death!” But it is too late. Boxer is sent to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A factory where horses are killed and their bodies processed — representing the ultimate betrayal of the working class by the regime they served.">knacker’s</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why Boxer’s death is the novel’s most devastating moment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Boxer represents the loyal, hardworking proletariat who give everything to the revolution. His motto “I will work harder” embodies selfless dedication. Napoleon sells him to the knacker for whisky money. This is Orwell’s most powerful allegorical point: totalitarian regimes use the working class and discard them when they are no longer useful. Boxer’s death shows that loyalty to a corrupt system is rewarded with betrayal.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Squealer announces that Boxer died peacefully in hospital, with Napoleon at his side. He explains away the “Horse Slaughterer” sign by claiming the van had been bought from the knacker and not yet repainted. The animals accept this lie — they want to believe it. Orwell shows that propaganda succeeds partly because its audience is complicit: they choose comforting lies over painful truths.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Final Chapter: Full Circle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Chapter 10 jumps forward in time. The windmill is built but used to mill corn for profit, not to ease the animals’ workload as Snowball promised. Most of the original revolutionaries are dead. The pigs now walk on two legs, wear clothes and carry whips. The sheep have been taught a new slogan: “Four legs good, <em>two legs better</em>!”</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Final Commandment</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The Seven Commandments have been replaced by a single statement painted on the barn wall: “ALL ANIMALS ARE EQUAL BUT SOME ANIMALS ARE MORE EQUAL THAN OTHERS.” This is Orwell’s most famous line — a paradox that perfectly captures the hypocrisy of totalitarianism. It is the logical endpoint of every small alteration that has come before. The revolution that began with “All animals are equal” has produced its exact opposite.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Pigs and Humans: Indistinguishable</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The novella’s final scene shows the pigs entertaining human farmers in the farmhouse. Napoleon announces that the farm will revert to its original name: Manor Farm. The animals look through the window from pig to man, from man to pig: “it was impossible to say which was which.” This is the novella’s devastating final image — the revolution has come full <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to a pattern that returns to its starting point, suggesting that revolutionary change has produced no real improvement.">circle</dfn>. The pigs have become the humans they overthrew.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what the final scene reveals about Orwell’s message.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The final scene shows pigs indistinguishable from humans. Manor Farm’s name is restored. The revolution has produced no real change — one oppressor has been replaced by another. Orwell’s message is that power corrupts regardless of ideology: whether capitalist (Jones) or communist (Napoleon), the ruling class exploits the working class. The cyclical structure suggests this pattern may be inevitable.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Cyclical Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Orwell’s structural choice to end where the story began — with the farm under the same name, ruled by the same kind of tyrant — is profoundly pessimistic. The word “revolution” literally means “turning” — and the farm has turned full circle. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure that returns to its starting point, suggesting repetition or futility.">cyclical structure</dfn> implies that revolutions do not create new worlds; they repeat old patterns.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Is the Ending Entirely Hopeless?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Some readers argue the ending contains a flicker of hope. The animals <em>see</em> the pigs and humans as identical — they are no longer fooled. Benjamin, the cynical donkey who has watched everything with knowing silence, is still alive. Orwell may be suggesting that awareness, even belated awareness, is the first step towards genuine change. However, the novella offers no mechanism for that change, and Orwell’s tone is deeply pessimistic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The final chapters force the reader to confront Orwell’s central question: is the corruption of revolutionary ideals inevitable, or can it be prevented? Orwell does not answer definitively, but his structure suggests deep scepticism. The fable form, with its promise of a moral lesson, delivers a lesson that is itself morally ambiguous: <em>beware of all who seek power, even those who promise liberation</em>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what is the novella’s final line and what does it mean?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which.” The pigs have become indistinguishable from the humans they overthrew. The revolution has failed. Power has corrupted absolutely.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Boxer’s death is the novella’s most devastating allegory: the working class used and discarded by the regime they served.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">“All animals are equal but some animals are more equal than others” is the logical endpoint of every small corruption.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The cyclical ending — pigs indistinguishable from humans, Manor Farm restored — suggests revolution has changed nothing.</li></ul>
