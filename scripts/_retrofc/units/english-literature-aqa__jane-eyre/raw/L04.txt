Rochester’s proposal, the interrupted wedding, and the revelation of Bertha Mason.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Proposal in the Garden</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In Chapter 23, Rochester proposes to Jane in the garden at Thornfield, beneath a chestnut tree. This scene is one of the most important in the novel because it is where Jane most powerfully asserts her equality. When she believes Rochester is about to marry Blanche Ingram and send her away, Jane breaks down: “Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong! — I have as much soul as you — and full as much heart!”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Brontë structures this speech as a climactic declaration of selfhood. The list of adjectives — “poor, obscure, plain, and little” — acknowledges everything Victorian society values (wealth, status, beauty, stature) and then demolishes it. Jane asserts that her soul and heart make her Rochester’s equal regardless of external differences. The exclamatory “You think wrong!” is a direct challenge not just to Rochester but to the entire class system.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does Jane say in her proposal speech and why is it significant?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane’s declaration — “I have as much soul as you — and full as much heart!” — is the novel’s central statement of equality. She adds: “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me: I am a free human being with an independent will.” The bird metaphor rejects the idea of women as decorative, caged creatures. For AQA, this is the most quotable passage on gender equality in the entire novel.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Chestnut Tree</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Brontë uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The attribution of human feelings or characteristics to natural phenomena, objects, or abstract ideas.">pathetic fallacy</dfn> to foreshadow disaster. During the proposal, a storm breaks: “the great horse-chestnut at the bottom of the orchard had been struck by lightning in the night, and half of it split away.” The split tree symbolises the relationship itself — two halves torn apart by a destructive force. It foreshadows the interrupted wedding and the separation that follows. The storm also represents the passion and turmoil of their love, suggesting that their union will face violent disruption.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Brontë uses bird imagery throughout the novel to represent Jane’s desire for freedom. Rochester frequently calls Jane his “bird” — his “lark,” his “eager bird.” These <dfn class="term" data-def="Pet names or intimate forms of address that can reveal power dynamics in a relationship.">terms of endearment</dfn> are also terms of possession: a bird in a cage is beautiful but trapped. Jane resists this language: “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me.” After the engagement, Rochester tries to dress Jane in jewels and silks — essentially decorating her like a caged bird. Jane resists every attempt, insisting on her plain Quaker-like dress. She understands intuitively that accepting Rochester’s gifts would make her financially dependent and therefore unequal.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Interrupted Wedding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The wedding scene in Chapter 26 is the novel’s dramatic turning point. As the clergyman asks whether anyone objects to the marriage, a solicitor named Mr Briggs steps forward with Richard Mason and declares that Rochester has a living wife: <dfn class="term" data-def="Rochester’s first wife, a Creole woman from Jamaica who has been confined to Thornfield’s third floor due to her violent madness; she is the source of the mysterious laughter and disturbances.">Bertha Antoinetta Mason</dfn>, confined to the third floor of Thornfield. Rochester does not deny it. Instead, he takes the entire wedding party upstairs to see Bertha for themselves.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brontë describes Bertha in animalistic terms: she “grovelled, seemingly, on all fours; it snatched and growled like some strange wild animal.” The pronoun “it” dehumanises Bertha completely. Rochester describes her as a “demon” and a “lunatic.” These descriptions reflect Victorian attitudes towards mental illness: madness was seen as monstrous, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of locking away mentally ill people in private rooms or institutions, often for life, under conditions of secrecy and shame.">confinement</dfn> was the standard response. Bertha has been locked in the attic for years with only Grace Poole as her keeper.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down what the revelation of Bertha means for Jane’s principles and her decision to leave.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The revelation of Bertha Mason is the novel’s crisis point. Rochester begs Jane to stay as his mistress, arguing that his marriage to Bertha was arranged and loveless. But Jane refuses: “I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself.” She chooses moral integrity over love — she will not compromise her principles even for the man she loves.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Jane’s Decision to Leave</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Jane’s decision to leave Thornfield is one of the most significant moments in Victorian fiction. Rochester offers her a life of comfort and passion. He argues that no one will know, that Bertha is insane and their marriage is meaningless. But Jane refuses to become his mistress because it would destroy her self-respect. She tells him: “Laws and principles are not for the times when there is no temptation: they are for such moments as this.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">This statement reveals Brontë’s moral philosophy. Principles only matter when they are difficult to uphold. Jane’s departure is an act of extraordinary courage: she leaves with no money, no friends, and nowhere to go. She nearly dies of starvation on the moors. Brontë makes the cost of moral integrity painfully real — but she also makes clear that self-respect is more valuable than comfort, security, or even love.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Bertha is one of the most discussed characters in English literature. Victorian readers saw her as a monster — a mad, dangerous woman who justified Rochester’s deception. Modern critics, particularly Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar in their influential 1979 study <em>The Madwoman in the Attic</em>, read Bertha as Jane’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who mirrors another, representing suppressed aspects of their personality; Bertha can be read as the embodiment of Jane’s repressed anger and passion.">dark double</dfn>. Where Jane channels her anger into words, Bertha expresses it through physical violence — tearing the wedding veil, setting fires, attacking Mason. Bertha is also a victim: a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person of mixed European and Caribbean descent; Bertha’s Creole heritage connects her to Britain’s colonial exploitation of the Caribbean.">Creole</dfn> woman from Jamaica, she was married off for her money, brought to England, and imprisoned when she became inconvenient. Post-colonial readings argue that Bertha’s treatment reflects British imperial attitudes towards colonised peoples.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Jane’s proposal speech — “I have as much soul as you” and “I am no bird” — is the novel’s most powerful statement of equality, rejecting class and gender hierarchies.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The split chestnut tree is pathetic fallacy, foreshadowing the relationship’s rupture. Bertha’s revelation shatters Jane’s happiness at the altar.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Jane’s decision to leave — “Laws and principles are not for the times when there is no temptation” — proves that her moral integrity outweighs her desire for love and comfort.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">The proposal and wedding scenes are among the most likely extracts for the exam. When analysing Jane’s speeches, focus on Brontë’s methods: the list of self-deprecating adjectives (‘poor, obscure, plain, and little’) that Jane then overturns, the bird imagery, and the declarative force of ‘I care for myself.’ Always link Jane’s words to Brontë’s challenge to Victorian gender and class expectations (AO3).</p>
