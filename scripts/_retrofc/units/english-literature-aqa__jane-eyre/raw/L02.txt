--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s childhood at Gateshead and Lowood — oppression, resilience, and moral growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Gateshead: Cruelty and Confinement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with Jane as a ten-year-old orphan living at Gateshead Hall, the home of her late uncle’s family. Mrs Reed, Jane’s aunt by marriage, treats her with cold hostility, and her three children — particularly the bullying John Reed — torment her constantly. Jane is excluded from the family circle: “Me, she had dispensed from joining the group.” From the very first page, Brontë establishes Jane as an outsider, isolated and unloved.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">John Reed embodies the entitlement of the Victorian upper-class male. He tells Jane: “You have no business to take our books; you are a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who relies on another for financial support; in Victorian society, being a dependant (especially a poor relation) carried deep social stigma.">dependant</dfn>.” He physically assaults her and reminds her that she has no money, no parents, and no rights. Brontë uses John to represent the patriarchal power structure that oppresses Jane throughout her childhood — he is male, wealthy, and cruel, and the adults around him allow his behaviour.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what happens in the red-room scene and what does it symbolise?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>After Jane fights back against John Reed, she is locked in the red-room — the room where Mr Reed died. The red-room is a key Gothic symbol: its colour suggests anger and danger, the locked door represents confinement, and Jane’s terror at seeing a ghostly light shows how patriarchal authority controls through fear. This scene establishes the novel’s central conflict: Jane’s passionate spirit versus a society that demands her submission.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Jane’s Rebellion at Gateshead</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane’s response to the Reed family is extraordinary for a Victorian child — she fights back. When John attacks her, she retaliates physically. When Mrs Reed sends her to the red-room, Jane screams: “You are like a murderer — you are like a slave-driver — you are like the Roman emperors!” These <dfn class="term" data-def="A direct comparison using ‘like’ or ‘as’ that draws a parallel between two things.">similes</dfn> are remarkable because Jane compares her aunt to figures of absolute power and cruelty. She does not accept her suffering passively — she names it and condemns it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Brontë makes a deliberate choice to give the child Jane a voice. Victorian children — especially poor, female ones — were expected to be silent and obedient. Jane’s outburst at Mrs Reed before leaving for Lowood is even more defiant: “I am glad you are no relation of mine: I will never call you aunt again as long as I live.” This speech gives Jane a moment of power that shocks both Mrs Reed and the reader. Brontë shows that speaking truth to power has a cost (Jane is sent away) but also a reward (she feels “the strangest sense of freedom”).</p>

Both women are confined by patriarchal authority (Jane in the red-room, Bertha in the attic). Both express rage physically — Jane fights John Reed, Bertha tears her wedding veil. Brontë suggests that when women’s legitimate emotions are suppressed, they find other, more destructive outlets.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Lowood: Suffering and Survival</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jane is sent to Lowood Institution, a charity school for orphaned girls. It is run by Mr Brocklehurst, a clergyman who preaches <dfn class="term" data-def="Strict self-denial and simplicity, often imposed in the name of religion or morality.">austerity</dfn> for the students while his own wife and daughters dress in silk and velvet. Brontë exposes his hypocrisy through vivid contrast: the Lowood girls eat burnt porridge and freeze in thin dresses, while the Brocklehurst women arrive “splendidly attired in velvet, silk, and furs.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Conditions at Lowood are appalling. The food is so poor that the girls are constantly hungry. In spring, a <dfn class="term" data-def="An acute infectious disease spread by lice and fleas, distinct from consumption (tuberculosis). Typhus caused deadly outbreaks in overcrowded institutions like workhouses and charity schools.">typhus</dfn> epidemic sweeps through the school, killing many students. Jane’s closest friend Helen Burns, however, does not die of typhus — she has been quietly dying of consumption (tuberculosis) throughout her time at Lowood. In Chapter 9, Jane steals away to Miss Temple’s room to be with Helen and wakes to find she has died with Jane’s arms around her neck. Brontë based these scenes on her own experience at the Clergy Daughters’ School at Cowan Bridge, where her sisters Maria and Elizabeth contracted tuberculosis. They were removed from the school and died at home in Haworth, their illness worsened by the school’s harsh conditions — the same disease that takes Helen Burns in the novel.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down what Helen Burns teaches Jane and how her death affects Jane’s development.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Helen Burns represents patient, enduring faith — she accepts suffering without complaint, believing in heavenly reward. Her death in Jane’s arms is one of the novel’s most moving scenes. Jane learns from Helen’s kindness and moral strength, but ultimately rejects her passivity. Jane’s mature faith combines Helen’s patience with her own fierce sense of justice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Jane’s Growth at Lowood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Despite the harsh conditions, Lowood provides Jane with something she never had at Gateshead: education and friendship. Miss Temple, the kind superintendent, becomes a maternal figure who treats Jane with dignity. After Brocklehurst publicly humiliates Jane by labelling her a liar (on Mrs Reed’s instruction), Miss Temple investigates and clears Jane’s name. This moment teaches Jane that truth and justice can prevail over cruelty.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Jane spends eight years at Lowood — six as a student and two as a teacher. When Miss Temple leaves to marry, Jane realises that her contentment depended on one person, not the institution itself. She decides to seek a new position and advertises for a governess role, showing her growing independence. Brontë emphasises that Jane’s education gives her the means to support herself — a radical achievement for a penniless orphan girl in Victorian society.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Brontë uses Lowood to criticise the Victorian approach to charity. Institutions like these claimed to help the poor, but in reality they often punished them. Brocklehurst justifies starvation and cold by claiming it builds “fortitude” and spiritual strength. He tells the teachers: “Oh, madam, when you put bread and cheese, instead of burnt porridge, into these children’s mouths, you may indeed feed their vile bodies, but you little think how you starve their immortal souls!” This <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of exaggeration for emphasis or effect.">hyperbole</dfn> reveals how religion was weaponised to justify cruelty towards the most vulnerable members of society.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">At Gateshead, Jane is oppressed by class (the Reeds remind her she is a dependant) and gender (John’s physical dominance). The red-room is a Gothic symbol of patriarchal confinement.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Jane’s rebellion against Mrs Reed — “You are like a murderer” — establishes her as a character who refuses to accept injustice silently, even at great personal cost.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Lowood teaches Jane endurance (from Helen Burns) and self-respect (from Miss Temple), but Brontë uses Brocklehurst to expose the hypocrisy of Victorian charitable institutions.</li></ul>
