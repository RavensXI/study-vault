<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Returning to Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Jane returns to Thornfield, she finds it destroyed. Bertha Mason set fire to the house and jumped from the roof to her death. Rochester tried to save her and the servants but was badly injured: he lost his sight and one hand. The grand house that once symbolised hidden secrets and patriarchal power is now a “blackened ruin.” Brontë uses the fire to <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary technique where objects, settings, or events represent something beyond their literal meaning; the destruction of Thornfield symbolises the removal of barriers to equality.">symbolically</dfn> destroy everything that stood between Jane and Rochester as equals: Bertha (the legal obstacle), Thornfield (the site of deception), and Rochester’s physical dominance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The destruction of Thornfield is not simply a plot device — it is Brontë’s structural answer to the problem the novel has posed. How can a poor, plain governess and a wealthy, powerful landowner ever meet as equals? The answer is: the powerful man must be brought low, and the powerless woman must be raised up. Rochester’s injuries humble him; Jane’s inheritance empowers her.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three things have changed to make Jane and Rochester’s marriage equal?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three things have changed to enable an equal marriage: (1) Bertha is dead, removing the legal barrier; (2) Rochester is blinded and maimed, removing his physical and social dominance; (3) Jane has inherited money and gained family connections, removing her financial dependence. Brontë ensures that the final union is one of genuine equality, not the unbalanced power dynamic of the earlier proposal.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Rochester’s Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Rochester at Ferndean is a profoundly changed man. He has lost his sight, his left hand, and his grand estate. He is dependent on servants and isolated from society. Jane describes him as a “caged eagle, whose gold-ringed eyes cruelty has extinguished.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A direct comparison using ‘like’ or ‘as’; here Jane compares Rochester to a caged eagle to show his diminished power and noble suffering.">simile</dfn> of the caged eagle is significant: Rochester, once the powerful master, is now confined and powerless — just as Jane was once confined at Gateshead and Lowood.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">His blindness serves multiple symbolic functions. It represents <dfn class="term" data-def="The suffering or punishment a character endures as a consequence of their past actions; Rochester’s blindness can be read as penance for his attempted bigamy.">penance</dfn> for his deception and attempted bigamy. It also reverses the power dynamic: Rochester can no longer judge Jane by her appearance and must value her entirely for her inner qualities. Some critics argue that Brontë’s decision to blind Rochester reflects a disturbing message — that a man must be physically diminished for a woman to be his equal. Others argue it shows that true partnership requires the surrender of power and ego.</p>

Where Thornfield was grand, Gothic, and full of secrets, Ferndean is small, secluded, and surrounded by nature. It is deep in a wood, hidden from the world. Brontë chooses this intimate, natural setting for Jane and Rochester’s reunion because it is a space free from society’s judgements. There are no grand drawing rooms, no high-society guests, no locked floors. Just two people meeting honestly, stripped of pretence. The natural setting reflects the Romantic ideal that truth and happiness are found in nature, away from the corruption of civilisation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Equal Marriage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jane’s famous declaration in the final chapter — “Reader, I married him” — is one of the most celebrated lines in English literature. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique where the narrator speaks directly to the reader, breaking the boundary between story and audience.">direct address</dfn> to the reader is assertive and triumphant. Crucially, the grammar gives Jane the active role: “I married him,” not “he married me.” In a society where women were “given away” by their fathers and absorbed into their husbands’ identities under coverture, this phrasing is revolutionary. Jane is the subject of the sentence, not the object.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jane describes their marriage in terms of perfect equality: “I am my husband’s life as fully as he is mine… To be together is for us to be at once as free as in solitude, as gay as in company.” This vision of marriage as mutual fulfilment — where neither partner sacrifices their identity — was radical in 1847. Brontë’s ideal is not the Angel in the House submitting to her husband but two independent people choosing each other freely.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down why ‘Reader, I married him’ is significant in terms of grammar, gender, and Victorian conventions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Reader, I married him” gives Jane grammatical agency — she is the subject, not the object. In a society where women were “given away” in marriage and lost their legal identity, this active construction is a final assertion of independence. The direct address also invites the reader to celebrate with Jane, making her triumph feel personal and immediate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Rochester’s Partial Recovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In the final pages, Brontë reveals that Rochester eventually regains sight in one eye and is able to see their first-born child. This partial recovery is carefully judged. Rochester is not fully restored — he remains marked by the consequences of his past actions. But his recovery is enough to suggest that redemption is possible. He tells Jane that during his suffering he prayed to God for the first time, suggesting a genuine spiritual transformation rather than mere physical punishment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Brontë ends the novel not with Jane and Rochester but with St John Rivers, who is dying in India, consumed by his missionary zeal. This structural choice is deliberate. St John chose duty over love and is burning out in service to a cold God; Jane chose love guided by principle and is thriving. The contrast reinforces Brontë’s central message: a life without love and passion is not truly virtuous — it is simply incomplete.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">Critics disagree about whether the ending is radical or conservative. On one hand, Jane achieves independence, equality, and a loving marriage — a feminist triumph. On the other hand, she still ends up married, domesticated, and caring for a disabled husband, which some see as a conventional resolution. The most balanced view is that Brontë works within Victorian conventions (marriage as the expected ending) while subverting them (Jane marries on her own terms, as an equal, after refusing two proposals that would have diminished her). The ending is both realistic and idealistic — it shows what equality might look like within the constraints of Victorian society.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Thornfield’s destruction removes the barriers to equality: Bertha dies, Rochester is humbled, and Jane arrives with money and family. The fire symbolically clears the way for a genuine partnership.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">“Reader, I married him” gives Jane grammatical and narrative agency in a society where women were objects of marriage, not its subjects.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The ending contrasts Jane’s fulfilled life with St John’s dying in India, reinforcing Brontë’s message that a life without love is incomplete.</li></ul>
