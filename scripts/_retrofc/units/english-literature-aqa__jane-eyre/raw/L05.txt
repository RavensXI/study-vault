--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jane on the Moors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After leaving Thornfield, Jane wanders the moors for three days with no money and no shelter. She sleeps outdoors, begs for food, and nearly dies of starvation. Brontë makes the physical cost of Jane’s moral decision brutally real. The moors represent both freedom and danger — Jane is finally free from Rochester’s deception, but that freedom almost kills her. She is rescued when she collapses on the doorstep of <dfn class="term" data-def="A house on the Yorkshire moors where St John, Diana, and Mary Rivers live; Jane discovers they are her cousins and she inherits a fortune from their shared uncle.">Moor House</dfn> (also called Marsh End) and is taken in by St John, Diana, and Mary Rivers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The moors sequence is structurally important. It strips Jane of everything: money, shelter, identity, and social status. She is reduced to her most basic self — a human being who needs food and warmth. Brontë uses this period to prove that Jane’s worth does not depend on her position at Thornfield or her relationship with Rochester. She survives through her own resilience and the kindness of strangers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three things does Jane gain at Moor House?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Moor House, Jane gains three crucial things: (1) a family — she discovers the Rivers siblings are her cousins; (2) financial independence — she inherits £20,000 from her uncle John Eyre and divides it equally with her cousins; and (3) meaningful work — she opens a village school for poor girls. These gains mean she can return to Rochester as an equal, not a dependant.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Diana and Mary Rivers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Diana and Mary are kindred spirits for Jane. They are intelligent, well-read, and warm. Their names carry symbolic weight: <dfn class="term" data-def="In Roman mythology, Diana is the goddess of the moon and the hunt — she represents independence, strength, and the natural world.">Diana</dfn> (goddess of the hunt, associated with independence and nature) and Mary (the biblical figure, associated with devotion). Both sisters must work as governesses because their family has lost its money — their situation mirrors Jane’s own. Through them, Brontë shows that poverty does not diminish intellect or worth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Jane’s friendship with Diana and Mary provides the female companionship she has lacked since Helen Burns. Unlike the superficial relationships available to her at Thornfield, these friendships are based on mutual respect and shared intellectual interests. Brontë suggests that genuine human connection comes from equality, not from wealth or social position.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">When Jane discovers she has inherited £20,000 from her uncle John Eyre (a vast sum in the 1840s), her first instinct is to share it equally with her three cousins. This decision is extraordinary. In Victorian society, wealth meant power, independence, and social status. Jane could have kept the entire fortune and lived in comfort. Instead, she values family and fairness over financial advantage. Her generosity contrasts sharply with the selfishness of the Reeds at Gateshead, who took her uncle’s money while treating Jane as a burden. Brontë uses the inheritance to prove Jane’s moral superiority — she does not hoard wealth but distributes it justly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">St John Rivers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A clergyman and Jane’s cousin who represents cold, duty-driven religion; he proposes a loveless marriage to Jane so she can accompany him as a missionary to India.">St John Rivers</dfn> is one of the novel’s most complex characters. He is handsome, principled, and deeply religious — but his faith is cold and controlling. He suppresses all emotion, including his love for the beautiful Rosamond Oliver, because he believes earthly passion is a distraction from God’s work. His ambition is to become a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person sent on a religious mission to promote Christianity in a foreign country; Victorian missionaries combined evangelism with Britain’s colonial expansion.">missionary</dfn> in India, and he sees Jane as a useful partner for this mission — not because he loves her, but because she is resilient, intelligent, and obedient.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Brontë presents St John as a foil to Rochester. Where Rochester is passionate, impulsive, and morally flawed, St John is controlled, calculating, and outwardly virtuous. But Brontë suggests that St John’s cold duty is just as dangerous as Rochester’s deception. Both men, in different ways, want to possess Jane and reshape her to fit their needs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down how St John’s proposal differs from Rochester’s and why Jane rejects it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>St John’s proposal is the opposite of Rochester’s: it offers duty without love. He tells Jane: “God and nature intended you for a missionary’s wife.” Jane recognises this as a different kind of imprisonment: “If I join St John, I abandon half myself.” She refuses because a loveless marriage would destroy her spirit just as surely as becoming Rochester’s mistress would have destroyed her integrity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Jane’s Rejection of St John</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">St John’s proposal tests Jane in a different way from Rochester’s. Rochester offered passion without morality; St John offers morality without passion. Jane is almost swayed by his powerful rhetoric — she describes his influence as “an iron shroud” contracting around her. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a word or phrase is applied to something to which it is not literally applicable, to suggest a resemblance.">metaphor</dfn> of the iron shroud suggests suffocation and death. Brontë shows that religious duty, when it demands the suppression of all feeling, becomes a form of tyranny.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">At the moment Jane is about to yield to St John, she hears Rochester’s voice calling her name across the moors: “Jane! Jane! Jane!” This could be supernatural (a telepathic connection) or psychological (Jane’s subconscious reasserting her true desires). Either way, it is the catalyst that frees her from St John’s control and sends her back to Thornfield. Brontë suggests that genuine feeling — the voice of the heart — is a more reliable guide than cold religious duty.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Brontë structures the novel so that Jane faces two proposals that are mirror images. Rochester offers love without legality (bigamy); St John offers legality without love (a duty marriage). Both men want Jane to sacrifice part of herself: Rochester asks her to give up her principles, St John asks her to give up her passion. Jane rejects both because she refuses to be incomplete. Her final marriage to Rochester works because circumstances have changed — Bertha is dead, Rochester is humbled, and Jane is financially independent. Only when both partners meet as equals can a true union occur.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Moor House gives Jane what she has always lacked: family (the Rivers cousins), financial independence (£20,000 inheritance shared equally), and meaningful work (the village school).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">St John’s cold, duty-driven proposal — “God and nature intended you for a missionary’s wife” — is the opposite of Rochester’s passionate deception; both threaten to erase Jane’s identity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Jane hears Rochester’s voice calling across the moors, breaking St John’s hold and proving that genuine feeling is a more reliable guide than cold religious duty.</li></ul>
