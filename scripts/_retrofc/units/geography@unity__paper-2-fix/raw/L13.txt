--- DESCRIPTION ---
How India’s employment structure is changing, the role of transnational corporations like Hyundai, and how trade is driving economic growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">India’s Changing Employment Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">India’s economy has shifted significantly over the past few decades. Like most <dfn class="term" data-def="A country that has begun to experience high rates of economic development, usually with rapid industrialisation.">NEEs</dfn>, India has moved from an economy dominated by agriculture (primary sector) towards one with growing manufacturing (secondary) and services (tertiary) sectors. This pattern of change is explained by the <dfn class="term" data-def="A model showing how employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary sectors as a country develops economically.">Clark Fisher model</dfn>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">The Four Employment Sectors</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n4">
<li><strong>Primary sector</strong> — extraction of raw materials and natural resources. Examples: farming, fishing, mining.</li>
<li><strong>Secondary sector</strong> — taking raw materials and manufacturing products from them. Examples: car manufacturing, food processing, textiles.</li>
<li><strong>Tertiary sector</strong> — providing services to other people. Examples: teaching, healthcare, retail, transport.</li>
<li><strong>Quaternary sector</strong> — knowledge-based work involving research and development of new technology. Examples: scientific research, IT, software development.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the approximate percentage of India's primary sector employment in 2021, then compare to the UK's tertiary sector.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In 2021, India’s employment structure was approximately: <strong>primary 44%</strong>, <strong>secondary 25%</strong> and <strong>tertiary 31%</strong>. Compare this to the UK, where tertiary jobs account for around 81% of employment. India’s primary sector has been shrinking while its secondary and tertiary sectors are growing — a clear sign of economic development.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Why Is India’s Secondary Sector Growing?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Since 2010, India’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The total value of all goods and services produced by a country in one year.">GDP</dfn> has more than doubled. This rapid growth shifted the economy from an agricultural base towards more manufacturing. India is home to major manufacturing industries including Tata Motors, Ford, Boeing, Hyundai and Volvo. These are <dfn class="term" data-def="Large companies that operate in more than one country, often with headquarters in an HIC.">transnational corporations (TNCs)</dfn> attracted to India because of its large, low-cost workforce, central global location and government policies that encourage foreign investment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">When a new TNC sets up in India, it triggers the <dfn class="term" data-def="When the introduction of a new industry encourages growth in other sectors, creating more jobs and wealth in the local area.">multiplier effect</dfn>. The factory creates jobs, workers spend their wages locally, new shops and services open to meet demand, and the government collects more tax to invest in infrastructure. This cycle of growth spreads benefits across the wider economy.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Case Study: Hyundai in India</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Hyundai is a South Korean car manufacturer and one of the world’s most recognisable TNCs. Its headquarters are in Seoul, South Korea, but it operates 12 manufacturing factories in 10 countries. In May 1996, Hyundai opened its first factory in India to compete with existing brands in the Indian market.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Hyundai quickly gained popularity among Indian consumers. Since 2008, it has been voted “Indian Car of the Year” seven times. In 2023, India produced 25.34 million vehicles in total — car manufacturing accounts for 7.1% of India’s GDP and 35% of its manufacturing GDP, worth over $222 billion (higher than the entire GDP of Greece).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n12">Why Did Hyundai Choose India?</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li>India has a huge population of 1.4 billion, providing a large workforce willing to accept lower wages.</li>
<li>India is the second-largest English-speaking country in the world, making communication easier for international companies.</li>
<li>India has specialised factories already set up for car manufacturing, reducing start-up costs.</li>
<li>India’s central global location makes trade routes between Europe and Asia easier.</li>
<li>The Indian government offers policies that encourage TNCs to invest.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Advantages of Hyundai in India</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
<li>Hyundai employs over <strong>50,000 people</strong> in India, providing stable incomes for families. These wages are taxed, generating revenue for government services like schools and hospitals.</li>
<li>Hyundai has planned to invest <strong>$2.45 billion</strong> into electric vehicles in India over the next decade, showing long-term commitment to the country.</li>
<li>Lower assembly line costs allow cheaper production, increasing profits and competitiveness.</li>
<li>Hyundai can directly target its Indian audience and tailor products to local needs, maximising sales.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Disadvantages of Hyundai in India</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n17">
<li><strong>Low wages</strong> — the average wage for car manufacturing workers in India is roughly £2,500 per year. Some workers earn as little as £2 per day, meaning they struggle to afford basic living costs.</li>
<li><strong>Higher-skilled jobs go to foreigners</strong> — of Hyundai’s 2,864 head office employees, 138 are from other countries, filling roles in marketing and accountancy. Local workers have limited chances of promotion.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="When profits earned by a TNC are sent back to the company’s home country rather than being reinvested locally.">Economic leakage</dfn></strong> — profits made in India are often sent back to Hyundai’s headquarters in South Korea rather than being reinvested locally. This means less money goes into improving local facilities and services, and the Indian government receives less tax revenue.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">India and the UK have very different employment structures because they are at different stages of development. India (an NEE) has 44% primary, 25% secondary and 31% tertiary employment. The UK (an HIC) has just 1% primary, 18% secondary and 81% tertiary. As India continues to develop, its employment structure is expected to shift further towards tertiary and quaternary sectors, with primary employment declining — following the same path the UK took over the past 150 years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">India’s Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">India’s exports are worth approximately $860 billion, while its <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods brought into a country from abroad for sale.">imports</dfn> cost $979 billion. This means India has a <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between the value of a country’s exports and imports. A negative balance means imports exceed exports.">negative balance of trade</dfn> — it spends about $119 billion more on imports than it earns from exports.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Imports</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">India’s biggest imports include oil, precious and semi-precious stones, and electrical machinery and equipment. The highest value of imports come from China ($94 billion), the UAE ($42 billion) and the USA ($39 billion). The fact that India imports large quantities of oil and machinery suggests its manufacturing sector is growing rapidly, especially in the secondary sector.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Exports</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n24">India’s biggest <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods sent to another country for sale.">exports</dfn> include petroleum products, jewellery, pharmaceutical products and rice. The highest value of exports go to the USA ($72 billion), UAE ($25 billion), China ($23 billion) and Bangladesh ($14 billion). The UK is the seventh-largest importer of Indian products. It is beneficial for India to import cheaper raw materials and export more expensive finished products, as this adds greater value.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Trade Agreements</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n26">India is part of 13 <dfn class="term" data-def="An arrangement between two or more countries to trade goods with reduced or no tariffs.">trade agreements</dfn>. The largest is the <strong>GSTP</strong> (Global System of Trade Preferences), which includes over 42 countries worldwide, from Pakistan and Vietnam to Brazil and Mexico. A more localised example is <strong>SAFTA</strong> (South Asian Free Trade Agreement), covering seven South Asian countries including Pakistan, Nepal and Sri Lanka. These agreements help India access wider markets and maintain positive relationships with trading partners.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state India's military spending in 1992 and 2023, and name two countries which partly motivate it.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>India has invested heavily in its military since 1992, spending approximately <strong>$73 billion per year</strong> by 2023 (up from $8 billion in 1992). Much of this money comes from trade revenue, reflecting India’s growing economic power and its need to maintain security alongside neighbours China and Pakistan.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>India’s economy is shifting from primary to secondary and tertiary sectors as TNCs like Hyundai invest in manufacturing. While this brings jobs and economic growth, disadvantages such as low wages, limited promotion for local workers and economic leakage remain significant. India’s trade is growing through agreements like GSTP and SAFTA, though it currently has a negative balance of trade.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Employment structure</strong> — India’s primary sector is shrinking (44%) while secondary (25%) and tertiary (31%) sectors grow, following the Clark Fisher model of economic development.</li>
<li><strong>Hyundai case study</strong> — 50,000 jobs and $2.45 billion EV investment, but low wages (£2,500/year), foreign-held senior roles and economic leakage to South Korea.</li>
<li><strong>Trade</strong> — India has a negative balance of trade ($119 billion deficit), but membership of GSTP and SAFTA gives it access to global markets.</li>
</ul>
