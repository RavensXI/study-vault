--- DESCRIPTION ---
Different types of aid to India, environmental challenges including rising emissions, and the wider picture of India’s rapid economic growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Types of Aid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Help given by one country, organisation or group to another, usually to support development or respond to emergencies.">Aid</dfn> is help given from one group to another. It can come from governments, international organisations or charities, and it takes several different forms:</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Bilateral Aid</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Aid given directly by one government to the government of another country, often with conditions attached.">Bilateral aid</dfn> is when one government gives money directly to another government, usually with conditions about how the money must be spent. India receives bilateral aid from countries including the UK, France, the Netherlands, Germany and the USA. UK bilateral aid to India peaked at £421 million in 2010 but has since fallen to around £91 million by 2021 — reflecting India’s growing ability to fund its own development.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n5">Multilateral Aid</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Aid given by governments to international organisations like the United Nations or World Bank, which then distribute it to countries in need.">Multilateral aid</dfn> is when governments contribute money to international organisations such as the United Nations (UN), the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF). These organisations pool the funds and distribute them to countries that need help most. The UN is made up of over 190 countries and provides aid to tackle issues such as food security, climate change and conflict.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Emergency Aid</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Immediate assistance provided in response to a sudden disaster or crisis, such as food, shelter and medical supplies.">Emergency aid</dfn> is short-term help given in response to sudden crises such as earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tropical storms or pandemics. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, the USA donated <strong>$41 million</strong> to India to help support its response, including funding for vaccines and protective measures.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Development Aid</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Long-term aid given to support a country’s economic, social, environmental and political development.">Development aid</dfn> is longer-term assistance aimed at improving a country’s economic, social and political development. In 2021, the USA was the largest provider of development aid globally, contributing $42.3 billion. India received $1.38 billion in development aid in 2006, rising to $3.12 billion by 2021 — an increase of $1.74 billion over 15 years.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall 2 problems with bilateral aid, versus community-led aid.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Aid helps close the <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference in wealth and quality of life between richer and poorer countries.">development gap</dfn> between richer and poorer countries. However, bilateral aid can come with conditions (“tied aid”) that benefit the donor country. The most effective aid empowers local communities to develop their own solutions rather than creating dependence on outside help.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Environmental Impacts of India’s Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">India’s rapid economic growth has come at a significant environmental cost. As manufacturing has expanded, so has the burning of <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources formed from the remains of ancient organisms over millions of years, including coal, oil and gas.">fossil fuels</dfn>, leading to serious pollution and environmental damage.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Rising Carbon Emissions</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n15">India’s carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels have risen sharply since 1980. Coal use has increased from 200 million metric tonnes in 1980 to over 900 million metric tonnes in 2020–21 — a difference of around 700 million metric tonnes. Oil emissions have also risen significantly. This increase contributes to global warming and worsening air quality across India.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Coal is cheap and abundant — India has large reserves that keep energy costs low. It provides a reliable, constant energy supply, unlike weather-dependent renewables. High energy demand from a growing population, expanding cities and increasing industry drives coal use. Coal also supports economic development by powering factories that create jobs and exports. Although renewable energy is expanding, it is not yet affordable or reliable enough to replace coal across all of India.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">The Steel Industry and Pollution</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n18">India’s steel industry is one of the country’s biggest coal consumers, using around 85–90 million tonnes of coking coal per year. The industry is responsible for approximately 10–12% of India’s total greenhouse gas emissions. Harmful pollutants including fine particulate matter, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides are released into the air, significantly damaging air quality and human health. India is now the second-largest steel producer in the world, having increased production from about 101 million tonnes in 2017 to over 144 million tonnes in 2023–24.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Motor Vehicles and Air Quality</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n20">As India develops, more people earn higher incomes and can afford to buy cars. The growing number of motor vehicles increases the volume of emissions released into the atmosphere. Northern and central India — where secondary industries and car ownership are concentrated — have the poorest air quality in the country. India has 13 of the most polluted cities in the world, largely because of a combination of industrial emissions and vehicle exhaust fumes.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state the increase in coal consumption since 1980 and a % of emissions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The environmental cost of India’s development is a global concern. Coal consumption has risen by around 700 million metric tonnes since 1980, the steel industry produces 10–12% of national greenhouse emissions, and India has 13 of the world’s most polluted cities. Balancing economic growth with environmental protection is one of India’s greatest challenges.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">India’s Economic Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">India’s rapid development has brought clear benefits. Literacy rates have increased, leading to more people going on to higher education and securing better-paid jobs. Life expectancy has risen from 38 years in 1950 to about 68 years today, and wages have increased alongside growing <dfn class="term" data-def="The total income earned by a country’s population, usually measured per person per year.">GNI</dfn>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Uneven Development Across India</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Growth has not been shared equally across the country. There are approximately 236,000 millionaires in India, concentrated in cities like Mumbai (200+ people with a net worth above $150 million) and New Delhi. However, around <strong>300 million people</strong> — roughly 22% of the population — still live below the <dfn class="term" data-def="The estimated minimum level of income needed to secure the necessities of life.">poverty line</dfn>, earning less than $1.25 per day. Many of these people work in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The part of the economy that is not taxed or monitored by the government, such as street selling.">informal sector</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">GNI varies significantly between states. Goa has a GNI per capita of $8,863, driven largely by international tourism (140,000 tourists visited in 2022–2023, creating a positive multiplier effect with 250,000–300,000 jobs). In contrast, Bihar has a GNI per capita of just $901 — nearly ten times lower. People in higher-income areas have greater access to education, healthcare, food and clean water, while those in poorer states have significantly lower quality of life.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n27">India’s poverty rate fell overall between 1993 and 2012, with the overall poverty rate dropping by approximately 20%. However, since 2012, while urban poverty has continued to decline, rural poverty has risen from 25% to 29.6%. This is likely because rural areas lack the industrial development that creates formal jobs, so many people still rely on low-paid agricultural work or subsistence farming.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>India receives various types of aid — bilateral, multilateral, emergency and development — which help close the development gap. However, rapid industrialisation has caused severe environmental damage, including rising carbon emissions, steel industry pollution and worsening air quality. Economic growth has been uneven, with millionaires concentrated in major cities while 300 million people remain below the poverty line.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Aid</strong> — India receives bilateral aid (from countries like the UK), multilateral aid (UN, World Bank), emergency aid (e.g. $41 million from the USA for COVID-19) and development aid ($3.12 billion in 2021).</li>
<li><strong>Environmental damage</strong> — coal use has risen by around 700 million metric tonnes since 1980, the steel industry produces 10–12% of greenhouse emissions, and India has 13 of the world’s most polluted cities.</li>
<li><strong>Uneven growth</strong> — Goa (GNI $8,863) vs Bihar (GNI $901) shows stark regional inequality, with 300 million people below the poverty line despite 236,000 millionaires.</li>
</ul>
