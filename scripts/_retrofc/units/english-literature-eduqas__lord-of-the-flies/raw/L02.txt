--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 1: The Sound of the Shell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with Ralph and Piggy discovering each other on a tropical island after their plane has crashed. Golding immediately establishes their contrasting characters. Ralph is <strong>‘the boy with fair hair’</strong> — attractive, athletic, and instinctively drawn to play. He strips naked and swims, delighting in freedom from adults. Piggy, by contrast, is overweight, asthmatic, wears thick spectacles, and speaks with a lower-class accent. From the first page, Golding sets up a tension between the appealing and the intelligent: the boys will choose Ralph’s charisma over Piggy’s wisdom.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Piggy discovers a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large spiral seashell used as a trumpet to summon the boys to assemblies. It symbolises democratic order, civilised speech, and legitimate authority.">conch</dfn> shell, and Ralph blows it to summon the other survivors. The conch becomes the novel’s first and most important symbol: whoever holds it has the right to speak. This simple rule represents democracy, order, and civilisation. Golding makes a pointed choice — it is Piggy who finds the conch and knows what it is, but it is Ralph who blows it and receives the credit. Intelligence is already being overlooked in favour of physical presence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who finds the conch, who blows it, and what this reveals about how the boys value intelligence vs appearance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The conch is found by Piggy but blown by Ralph. This immediately establishes Golding’s critique: the boys instinctively follow physical appeal over intellectual ability. Piggy’s ideas are essential, but his appearance means they are consistently dismissed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Ralph’s Election and Jack’s Humiliation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When the boys hold an election, Ralph wins — largely because he holds the conch and looks the part. Golding writes that there was <strong>‘a stillness about Ralph as he sat that marked him out… there was his size, and attractive appearance; and most obscurely, yet most powerfully, there was the conch’</strong>. The boys vote for image, not competence. Jack Merridew, who arrives leading his choir in military formation (<strong>‘marching approximately in step in two parallel lines’</strong>), is visibly furious at losing. His face goes <strong>‘ugly without silliness’</strong> — a flash of the dangerous pride that will drive him to tyranny.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Ralph’s first decision is diplomatic: he gives Jack control of the choir as hunters. This attempt at compromise plants the seed of the novel’s central conflict. Jack now has a private army, loyal to him rather than to the democratic group. Golding signals early that democracy’s well-meaning concessions can empower its enemies.</p>

Jack commands them with <strong>‘arrogance’</strong>, and Simon faints from the heat because the group is forbidden to break formation. This introduction foreshadows Jack’s authoritarianism, the choir’s blind obedience, and the way institutional order (school, church) will be stripped away to reveal something savage underneath.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Chapter 2: Fire on the Mountain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Ralph calls an assembly and proposes building a signal fire to attract rescue. The boys rush up the mountain in uncontrolled excitement, tearing the island apart for fuel. Piggy is appalled: <strong>‘Acting like a crowd of kids!’</strong> His protest is ironic — they <em>are</em> kids — but his point is serious. Without discipline, even good intentions become destructive. The fire rages out of control and a <dfn class="term" data-def="Young children on the island, roughly aged six. Their fears and vulnerability highlight the lack of adult protection.">littlun</dfn> with a mulberry-coloured birthmark disappears, almost certainly killed. Nobody acknowledges his death. This is the first casualty of the boys’ inability to maintain order.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what happens to the littlun with the birthmark, and why is this event significant for the novel’s themes?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The unnamed littlun’s death in the fire is the novel’s first casualty. The boys’ failure to acknowledge it shows how quickly the value of individual life erodes without adult accountability. Golding never names the boy, emphasising how the group has already dehumanised its most vulnerable members.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Chapter 3: Huts on the Beach</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Chapter 3 reveals the first major split between Ralph and Jack. Ralph is trying to build shelters but the other boys have wandered off to play. Only Simon helps. Meanwhile, Jack is deep in the forest, obsessively hunting pigs. He has become <strong>‘a furtive thing, ape-like’</strong>, his eyes <strong>‘bolting and nearly mad’</strong>. Golding’s language is crucial: Jack is already losing his humanity, regressing to something pre-civilised. The adjective ‘ape-like’ connects him to a primal, animal state.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The chapter ends with a confrontation. Ralph insists shelters and rescue matter; Jack insists on meat. Neither can articulate the deeper conflict, but Golding makes it clear: Ralph represents <dfn class="term" data-def="In the novel, the impulse to maintain rules, build shelters, keep the fire lit, and work towards rescue.">civilisation</dfn> (shelter, fire, rescue) and Jack represents <dfn class="term" data-def="In the novel, the impulse towards hunting, violence, and tribal dominance.">savagery</dfn> (hunting, dominance, primal satisfaction). Simon, significantly, slips away alone into the forest — a spiritual, contemplative figure who exists outside this binary.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">At the end of Chapter 3, Simon crawls into a hidden clearing surrounded by candle-bud flowers. This private, almost sacred space sets Simon apart from the other boys. He is the only character who seeks solitude and spiritual connection with the natural world. This clearing will later become the site of his encounter with the Lord of the Flies (Chapter 8) — Golding transforms a place of beauty into a place of confrontation with evil, mirroring the corruption of the island itself.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The conch symbolises democratic order from the outset, but the boys already value charisma (Ralph) over intelligence (Piggy).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Jack’s humiliation at losing the election and his control of the choir-turned-hunters plants the seed of the central conflict between civilisation and savagery.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The unnamed littlun’s death in the Chapter 2 fire is the first sign that the boys cannot maintain order, and their failure to acknowledge it shows the beginning of moral collapse.</li></ul>
