--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 10: The Shell and the Glasses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After Simon’s murder, Ralph and Piggy are in denial. Ralph says: <strong>‘That was murder.’</strong> But Piggy cannot face it: <strong>‘It was dark. There was that — that bloody dance. There was lightning and thunder and rain. We was scared!’</strong> His excuses reveal how even the most rational character uses self-deception to avoid guilt. Sam and Eric also participated but refuse to admit it. Golding shows that complicity and denial are universal responses to collective violence — the boys are not simply ‘bad’; they are human.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jack’s tribe, now fully established at Castle Rock, raids Ralph’s camp at night. They do not come for the conch — it is worthless to them. They steal Piggy’s glasses, the only means of making fire. This is a strategic act: Jack understands that controlling fire means controlling survival. The theft of the glasses also represents the seizure of <dfn class="term" data-def="In the novel, the capacity for clear thinking, scientific understanding, and practical problem-solving, primarily associated with Piggy.">intellect</dfn> by brute force — reason has been literally ripped from the hands of the rational and placed in the service of tyranny.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: why does Jack steal the glasses and not the conch, and what does this reveal about power?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jack steals Piggy’s glasses, not the conch, because the glasses have practical power (fire) while the conch has only symbolic authority. This moment crystallises the novel’s argument: in a world without civilisation, brute utility matters more than democratic ideals. Power belongs to those who control resources, not those who hold symbols.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Chapter 11: Castle Rock — Piggy’s Death</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ralph, Piggy, Sam and Eric march to Castle Rock to demand the glasses back. Piggy carries the conch, believing it still commands respect. He delivers his final, impassioned speech: <strong>‘Which is better — to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill?’</strong> This is Golding’s central question, put directly to the reader. The boys do not answer. Roger, perched above on a cliff, deliberately levers a boulder that strikes Piggy, killing him. The conch is <strong>‘exploded into a thousand white fragments’</strong> simultaneously. Democracy and reason die together in a single, devastating moment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Roger’s act is significant because it is deliberate, not frenzied like Simon’s murder. Earlier in the novel (Chapter 4), Roger threw stones at a littlun but aimed to miss, restrained by <strong>‘the taboo of the old life’</strong>. Now that taboo has been destroyed. Roger has progressed from inhibition to murder — a trajectory that maps Golding’s argument about what happens when civilisation’s constraints are removed. <dfn class="term" data-def="Roger’s journey from throwing stones to miss in Chapter 4 to deliberately killing Piggy in Chapter 11 — Golding’s most complete demonstration of how removing civilisation’s constraints reveals innate cruelty.">Roger’s arc</dfn> is arguably the novel’s most chilling evidence for Golding’s thesis.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The conch is destroyed at the exact moment Piggy dies. Golding makes the symbolic connection explicit: the shell ‘ceased to exist’ alongside the only character who truly believed in its power. The verb ‘exploded’ suggests violent, irreversible destruction. From this point, there is no democratic authority left on the island. Jack declares himself chief unopposed, and Sam and Eric are forced to join his tribe through torture. The novel has moved from democracy to dictatorship.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Chapter 12: Cry of the Hunters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Ralph is now alone, hunted across the island. Sam and Eric, forced into Jack’s tribe, secretly warn him that Roger has <strong>‘sharpened a stick at both ends’</strong> — implying they plan to mount Ralph’s head as they did the sow’s. Ralph has been reduced from elected leader to prey. Jack’s tribe sets the island on fire to smoke him out, destroying the fruit trees, the shelters, and the natural paradise. The island’s destruction mirrors the destruction of the boys’ humanity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Ralph stumbles onto the beach and collapses at the feet of a naval officer. The officer is <strong>‘moved and a little embarrassed’</strong>. Ralph <strong>‘wept for the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart, and the fall through the air of the true, wise friend called Piggy’</strong>. This final sentence encapsulates the novel’s meaning: the loss of innocence is irreversible, evil is innate (‘darkness of man’s heart’), and wisdom (Piggy) has been destroyed. The officer turns to his <dfn class="term" data-def="A trim warship, here representing the adult world’s own violence and undermining the supposed ‘rescue’.">cruiser</dfn>, and the novel ends with the reader understanding that the ‘civilised’ world is no better.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what three things does Ralph weep for, and why is each significant?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ralph weeps for three things: ‘the end of innocence’ (childhood destroyed), ‘the darkness of man’s heart’ (Golding’s central thesis about innate evil), and ‘the fall through the air of the true, wise friend called Piggy’ (the death of reason and intellect). This single sentence summarises the entire novel’s argument.</p>
</div>

The boys have moved from hunting animals to hunting a human being, and the ritual offering has escalated from a pig to a person. This detail, understated in the text, represents the novel’s most extreme point of savagery: human sacrifice.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n16">Jack steals the glasses (utility) not the conch (symbol) — proving that in a world without civilisation, practical power trumps democratic ideals.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Piggy’s death and the conch’s destruction happen simultaneously: democracy and reason die together. Roger’s deliberate act contrasts with the frenzy of Simon’s murder, showing a chilling progression.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Ralph’s final weeping — for innocence, for the darkness of man’s heart, and for Piggy — is Golding’s direct statement of the novel’s meaning. The officer’s cruiser ensures there is no comfortable resolution.</li></ul>
