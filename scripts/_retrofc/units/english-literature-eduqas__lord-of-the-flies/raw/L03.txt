--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Chapter 4: Painted Faces and Long Hair</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter 4 marks a turning point. Jack paints his face with clay and charcoal, creating a mask that <strong>‘compelled them’</strong>. Behind the paint, Jack is <strong>‘liberated from shame and self-consciousness’</strong>. This is one of the novel’s most important moments: the mask does not create savagery but <em>releases</em> it. Golding’s word choice is precise — ‘liberated’ suggests the savagery was always there, imprisoned by civilisation’s rules. The paint removes <dfn class="term" data-def="The awareness of being watched and judged by others, which Golding argues is one of the main forces keeping people civilised.">self-consciousness</dfn>, the internal voice that says ‘I am being watched; I must behave’. Without it, anything becomes possible.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">While Jack and the hunters are out killing their first pig, the signal fire goes out. A ship passes the island — and nobody sees it. This is the pivotal moment where rescue is lost because Jack prioritised hunting over the fire. The hunters chant <strong>‘Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood’</strong> — a rhythmic, tribal mantra that will grow more savage. Jack responds to Ralph’s anger by punching Piggy and breaking one lens of his glasses — simultaneously an attack on intellect, reason, and the group’s ability to make fire.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does Jack’s face paint liberate him from, and why is this significant for Golding’s theme?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jack’s face paint does not create savagery — it <em>releases</em> it. Golding’s use of ‘liberated from shame and self-consciousness’ reveals that civilised behaviour is only maintained by the fear of being judged. Remove that fear, and the innate capacity for violence emerges.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Missed Rescue: Civilisation’s Last Chance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The ship passing while the fire is out is structurally crucial. Golding places the boys’ best chance of rescue at exactly the moment Jack’s hunting obsession triumphs. Ralph is <strong>‘savage with unformulated knowledge’</strong> — he understands instinctively that something irretrievable has been lost. Piggy tries: <strong>‘You and your blood, Jack Merridew! You and your hunting! We might have gone home!’</strong> His direct accusation earns him the broken glasses. Speaking truth to power is punished with violence.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>‘Kill the pig. Cut her throat. Spill her blood.’</strong> This chant first appears in Chapter 4 and recurs throughout, growing in intensity. The three short imperative sentences create a <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of three words, phrases, or clauses used for emphasis and rhetorical effect.">tricolon</dfn> with escalating violence. The rhythmic, monosyllabic language mimics a drumbeat, stripping away individual thought and replacing it with collective bloodlust. By Chapter 9, the chant mutates to ‘Kill the beast! Cut his throat! Spill his blood!’ — language designed for animals turned on a human being.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Chapter 5: Beast from Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Ralph calls an assembly to reassert order. But the meeting is derailed by the littluns’ fears about <strong>‘the beast’</strong>. A littlun called Phil describes a nightmare creature; another, Percival Wemys Madison, tries to recite his name and address — a connection to civilisation — but breaks down crying. Ralph asks: <strong>‘What are we? Humans? Or animals? Or savages?’</strong> Nobody can answer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Simon attempts to articulate the truth: <strong>‘maybe it’s only us’</strong>. He is trying to say that the beast is not a physical creature but the darkness within the boys themselves. The others shout him down. Golding presents Simon as a prophet whose insight is rejected by a group that cannot face its own nature. Jack exploits the fear: <strong>‘Bollocks to the rules! We’re strong — we hunt!’</strong> His rhetoric replaces reason with force.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Simon mean by ‘maybe it’s only us’, and why do the other boys reject this idea?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Simon’s line ‘maybe it’s only us’ is the novel’s central insight: the beast is not an external creature but the inherent evil within human nature. The boys reject this truth because it is too frightening to accept, preferring to project their fear onto an imaginary external threat.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Chapter 6: Beast from Air</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A dead parachutist lands on the mountain during the night — a casualty of the aerial battle above. Sam and Eric, tending the fire, see the body’s parachute inflating in the wind and believe it is the beast. Golding uses <dfn class="term" data-def="An ironic situation where the audience or reader knows something that the characters do not.">dramatic irony</dfn>: the reader understands that the ‘beast’ is a dead human, a product of the very civilisation the boys long for.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Jack leads an expedition to Castle Rock. Even Ralph is momentarily seduced: <strong>‘What a place for a fort!’</strong> Golding shows that the impulse towards savagery is not unique to Jack; it exists in Ralph too. The difference is that Ralph catches himself and reasserts the need for the fire. Castle Rock will later become Jack’s stronghold, making this scene grim foreshadowing.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">In Chapter 5, Percival Wemys Madison tries to recite his full name and address — standard identification taught to British children. He manages the name but breaks down. By Chapter 12, he has forgotten it entirely. Golding uses this as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring element that develops significance across a text.">motif</dfn> for the loss of civilisation: the boy’s identity, his connection to the adult world, literally fades as savagery takes hold.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Jack’s face paint liberates him from ‘shame and self-consciousness’, revealing that civilised behaviour depends on the fear of being watched.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The missed rescue and broken glasses mark the moment savagery overpowers civilisation, and truth-telling (Piggy) is punished with violence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Simon’s insight — ‘maybe it’s only us’ — identifies the beast as internal evil, but the boys reject this truth, preferring an external monster they can hunt.</li></ul>
