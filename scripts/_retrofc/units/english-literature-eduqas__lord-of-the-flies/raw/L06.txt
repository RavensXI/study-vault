--- DESCRIPTION ---
Ralph, Jack, Piggy, Simon, Roger, and Sam & Eric — what each represents and key quotations.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Ralph: The Flawed Democrat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Ralph is elected leader in Chapter 1 because of his physical presence and the conch, not his ideas. He represents <dfn class="term" data-def="Government by the people, where decisions are made through collective agreement and fair representation.">democracy</dfn> and civilisation: he insists on shelters, the signal fire, and rules for speaking. But Golding refuses to make Ralph a simple hero. He is often confused, unable to articulate what he feels: <strong>‘Ralph was a specialist in thought now, and could recognise thought in another.’</strong> He needs Piggy to think for him. Most damagingly, Ralph participates in Simon’s murder (Chapter 9) and feels the thrill of the boar hunt (Chapter 7). Golding’s point: even the best of us carry the potential for savagery.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">By the end, Ralph has lost everything: his authority, his allies, and his innocence. His weeping at the novel’s close — for <strong>‘the end of innocence, the darkness of man’s heart’</strong> — shows he has gained painful self-knowledge. He is the only character who fully understands what has happened.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall three moments where Ralph’s behaviour contradicts his role as the ‘civilised’ leader.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ralph represents democracy and civilisation, but Golding deliberately complicates him: he enjoys the boar hunt, participates in Simon’s murder, and needs Piggy to think. He is not a simple hero but a flawed leader whose moral growth comes too late to prevent disaster.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Jack Merridew: The Dictator</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jack arrives leading his choir like a military unit and never recovers from the humiliation of losing the election. He represents <dfn class="term" data-def="Authoritarian rule based on force, intimidation, and the suppression of opposition.">tyranny</dfn> and the seductive power of violence. His face paint ‘liberates’ him from shame; his chanting rituals replace thought with bloodlust. Jack offers the boys what Ralph cannot: excitement, meat, certainty, and a tribe to belong to. Golding uses him to show how <dfn class="term" data-def="Unthinking loyalty to a group or cause, especially one that demands the surrender of individual judgment.">mob mentality</dfn> and fear can be weaponised by a charismatic leader.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Jack’s power is built on three pillars: violence (Roger as enforcer), ritual (the hunting chant), and control of resources (Piggy’s glasses/fire). He never engages with ideas or debate. When Piggy asks ‘Which is better — to have rules and agree, or to hunt and kill?’, Jack’s answer is a boulder. In a post-war context, Jack can be read as an allegory for fascist leaders who exploit fear and tribalism.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Piggy represents intellect, reason, and scientific thinking. His glasses are the only means of making fire — a symbol linking knowledge to survival. He finds the conch, understands its significance, and consistently argues for rules. But Piggy is physically weak, asthmatic, and overweight; his lower-class accent marks him for mockery. Golding uses Piggy to show that in a society that values strength over intellect, reason is fatally vulnerable. His death — struck by a boulder while holding the conch — destroys democracy and reason simultaneously. <strong>‘His head opened and stuff came out and turned red.’</strong> The bluntness of this description strips the death of dignity, reflecting how the boys have dehumanised him.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Simon: The Visionary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Simon is the novel’s spiritual and moral centre. He is the only character who understands that the beast is internal: <strong>‘maybe it’s only us.’</strong> He retreats to a secret clearing in the forest, communes with nature, and has a hallucinatory encounter with the Lord of the Flies. Golding presents Simon as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary character whose experiences parallel aspects of Jesus Christ, particularly sacrifice, truth-telling, and being killed by those they try to save.">Christ figure</dfn>: he speaks uncomfortable truths, is killed by the very people he tries to save, and is given a transcendent departure (his body carried out to sea under the stars).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Simon is also associated with <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition causing sudden seizures, which in ancient cultures was sometimes associated with prophetic visions.">epilepsy</dfn> — he faints repeatedly, and his encounter with the Lord of the Flies resembles a seizure. In many ancient cultures, epilepsy was seen as a sign of prophetic or spiritual insight. Golding draws on this tradition to present Simon as someone who perceives truths invisible to others.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the Christ-figure parallels between Simon and Jesus.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Simon is a Christ figure: he perceives the truth about evil (‘maybe it’s only us’), is killed while trying to bring this truth to the others, and is given a transcendent, almost sacred departure. His murder is the novel’s moral catastrophe — the moment the boys destroy their own possibility of redemption.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Roger: The Sadist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Roger is the novel’s most disturbing character because his cruelty is not born of passion (like Jack’s) but of something cold and deliberate. In Chapter 4, he throws stones at a littlun but aims to miss, restrained by <strong>‘the taboo of the old life’</strong>. By Chapter 11, he levers the boulder that kills Piggy with <strong>‘a sense of delirious abandonment’</strong>. Golding tracks Roger’s arc to show that the removal of civilisation’s constraints does not merely permit violence — it unleashes a capacity for <dfn class="term" data-def="Gaining pleasure from inflicting pain or suffering on others.">sadism</dfn> that was always there, waiting to be released.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sam and Eric (Samneric): The Pressure to Conform</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The twins Sam and Eric function almost as a single character (‘Samneric’). They are loyal to Ralph but ultimately forced into Jack’s tribe through torture after Piggy’s death. Golding uses them to explore <dfn class="term" data-def="Adjusting one’s behaviour to match the expectations of a group, often out of fear or pressure.">conformity</dfn> — ordinary, decent people who lack the moral strength to resist tyranny. They secretly warn Ralph about the manhunt but cannot act openly. Their capitulation represents how most people behave under authoritarian pressure: they comply, even when they know it is wrong.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Ralph represents flawed democracy — well-intentioned but unable to prevent the rise of tyranny. His complicity in Simon’s murder proves that savagery is universal, not limited to ‘bad’ characters.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Simon is the Christ figure who perceives the truth (‘the beast is us’) and is killed for it. His transcendent sea-burial contrasts with the brutal murder, creating Golding’s most powerful juxtaposition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Roger’s trajectory from restrained stone-throwing to deliberate murder is Golding’s most complete demonstration of the novel’s thesis: remove civilisation, and innate cruelty emerges.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">For Eduqas character questions, always link the character to Golding’s wider purpose. Do not just describe what a character does — explain what they represent and why Golding created them. For example: ‘Golding uses Roger to demonstrate that civilisation’s constraints are the only thing preventing innate sadism from emerging’ is analysis; ‘Roger throws stones at a littlun’ is description. The marks are in the ‘why’, not the ‘what’.</p>
