<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Birmingham as the UK Case Study</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Edexcel asks for a detailed case study of one major UK city. We will use <strong>Birmingham</strong> — the UK’s second largest city, with 1.16 million people in the local authority and around 4 million across the wider West Midlands conurbation. Birmingham earns its place as a case study because it shows almost every classic urban story in one place: a manufacturing past, a service-economy reinvention, immigration, deindustrialisation, regeneration. Whether your answer is on site or structure or change, the figures sit ready to use.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Site & Situation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Geographers separate <dfn class="term" data-def="The actual physical land on which a settlement is built — the local relief, soil, drainage and water supply.">site</dfn> from <strong>situation</strong>. Birmingham’s site sits on a low sandstone plateau roughly 140 m above sea level, well drained but with several streams (the Rea, the Tame) that powered early water mills. There is no major navigable river — an unusual feature for a major UK city — which is why canals later became so vital. <strong>Situation</strong> is about position relative to other places. Birmingham sits at the heart of England, almost equidistant from London, Manchester, Bristol and Liverpool. That central position made it the meeting-point of national road, canal and (later) rail networks. Today the M6, M42 and M5 motorways converge at Spaghetti Junction; New Street is the busiest railway station outside London.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why central England suited a major industrial city.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birmingham’s central location in England, plus access to nearby coalfields and the canal network linking it to the Mersey, Severn and Thames, made it the natural hub of the Industrial Revolution’s metalworking trade.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">National, Regional & Global Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In its <strong>national</strong> context, Birmingham is the second-largest UK city and a major financial, retail, education and convention hub. The 2022 Commonwealth Games and HS2 high-speed rail connection (under construction since 2019) reinforce its national role. <strong>Regionally</strong>, it anchors the West Midlands conurbation, including Wolverhampton, Walsall and Solihull. Around 25% of the UK’s manufacturing output still comes from this region. <strong>Globally</strong>, Birmingham connects through Birmingham Airport (12 million passengers a year), HSBC’s UK retail bank headquarters at Centenary Square, and a population that is now 51% non-white — one of the most diverse cities in Europe by share.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Connectivity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Connectivity is what makes a city function in the wider world. Birmingham’s connectivity has four layers. <strong>Road</strong>: the M6, M40, M5, M42 and A38(M) Aston Expressway converge here. <strong>Rail</strong>: New Street handles 53 million passenger journeys a year, with Moor Street and Snow Hill nearby. HS2 will cut London-Birmingham travel time to 49 minutes when fully open. <strong>Air</strong>: Birmingham Airport links to over 150 destinations. <strong>Digital</strong>: the city is part of the Midlands fibre-optic backbone and was an early UK 5G rollout site. Connectivity is one of Birmingham’s strongest assets and underpins everything from JLR car exports to the city’s university research economy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Urban Structure: From CBD to Fringe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Most UK cities — Birmingham included — show four concentric zones. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The commercial core of a city, with the highest land values, tallest buildings and most office and retail activity.">Central Business District (CBD)</dfn> in central Birmingham covers New Street, Colmore Row, Brindleyplace and the Bullring. Buildings are tall (the Cube, 103 Colmore Row), land values are the highest in the city, and the use is offices, retail and conferences. The <strong>inner city</strong> (Aston, Sparkbrook, Handsworth) is older, mainly Victorian terraces with high population density, more deprivation but also strong community networks and a youthful population. The <strong>suburbs</strong> (Bournville, Edgbaston, Erdington) date from the late 19th and 20th centuries, with semi-detached and detached housing, gardens, parks, more car ownership. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The transition zone at the edge of a city where urban land uses meet farmland and commuter villages.">urban-rural fringe</dfn> (around Bromsgrove and Solihull) holds business parks, retail centres (Touchwood), the airport and farmland transitioning into commuter villages.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Functions Across the Zones</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Each zone has different functions. The CBD’s function is <strong>commercial</strong> — banks, shops, hotels, conferences. The inner city’s function is mainly <strong>residential</strong>, increasingly mixed with start-ups in regenerated areas like Digbeth’s Custard Factory creative quarter. The suburbs are <strong>residential and educational</strong> — the University of Birmingham’s Edgbaston campus has 38,000 students. The fringe is <strong>industrial and commercial</strong> — logistics warehouses near the M42, JLR’s Solihull plant, the National Exhibition Centre and Birmingham Airport. Building age generally falls as you move outwards: CBD has the newest skyscrapers, inner city has the oldest housing, suburbs are mid-20th-century, the fringe is post-1980.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Older British cities show a paradox — the CBD usually has both the oldest historic buildings (the Town Hall, St Philip’s Cathedral) AND the newest skyscrapers, because central land is recycled most aggressively. The inner city is a band of mid-Victorian housing untouched since 1900. The suburbs grew between 1900 and 1970 with consistent semi-detached architecture. The fringe is dominated by post-1980 industrial sheds, commuter estates and out-of-town shopping. This pattern is not random: it reflects when each ring was built and how often each is redeveloped.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name each of the four city zones with one Birmingham example and one typical function.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birmingham’s four zones: CBD (Bullring, Brindleyplace — offices, retail), inner city (Aston, Sparkbrook — older housing, denser population), suburbs (Edgbaston, Bournville — family housing), urban-rural fringe (Solihull, NEC — airport, logistics, retail).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Birmingham’s site is a low sandstone plateau without a navigable river; its situation is central England, well-connected by motorway, rail, canal and air.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Connectivity (M6/M40/M42, New Street rail, HS2, Birmingham Airport) underpins its national, regional and global roles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Like most UK cities, Birmingham’s structure runs from CBD (commercial) through inner city (residential) and suburbs (family) to urban-rural fringe (industrial/airport).</li>
</ul>
