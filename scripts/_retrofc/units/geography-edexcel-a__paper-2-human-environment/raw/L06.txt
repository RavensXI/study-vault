--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why some places are richer than others, and the top-down and bottom-up responses used to close the gap.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A World That Is Not Flat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">If you map global GDP per capita, the result is striking: a wide band of high-income countries across North America, Western Europe, the Gulf, East Asia and Australasia, and a wide band of low-income countries across sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South Asia and Central America. The gap between richest and poorest is huge — Norway’s GDP per capita is around 35 times that of Burundi. Geographers call this <dfn class="term" data-def="The differences in levels of development between and within countries.">uneven development</dfn>. The Edexcel A specification asks why those differences exist, what consequences they cause, and which strategies have been used to close the gap.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Physical Causes of Uneven Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Physical geography is one starting point. Landlocked countries (Niger, Bolivia, Mongolia) trade more slowly than coastal ones because exporting goods costs more. Tropical climates spread diseases like malaria more easily, hurting the workforce; the World Health Organization estimates malaria still kills around 600,000 people a year. Drought-prone regions (the Sahel, north-east Brazil) struggle with food security. Tectonic and weather hazards — Haiti’s 2010 earthquake or repeated cyclones in Bangladesh — can wipe out years of investment. And uneven mineral wealth (oil in Norway and Saudi Arabia, copper in Chile) creates lasting differences when these resources are managed well or badly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down three physical and three human causes of uneven development with one country example each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Physical (climate, hazards, landlockedness, resources), historic (colonialism, conflict) and economic (trade, debt, technology) causes interact to produce uneven development. Norway’s GDP per capita is roughly 35 times that of Burundi.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Historic and Economic Causes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">History matters too. <dfn class="term" data-def="The historic process by which European powers controlled territories overseas and shaped their economies for export.">Colonialism</dfn> reshaped the economies of much of Africa, Asia and the Americas to serve imperial powers, leaving newly independent countries with infrastructure designed for export rather than internal development. Trade rules still favour wealthier countries: rich-country tariffs on processed cocoa or cotton encourage poorer countries to export raw materials at lower prices. Debt repayment can absorb a fifth or more of national income, leaving little for hospitals or schools. Conflict erases progress — the World Bank estimates Yemen has lost almost half its GDP since 2015. Within the UK, history echoes too: the North-South divide owes much to the location of coalfields and ports two centuries ago.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Consequences of Uneven Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The consequences run through every part of life. In housing, around 1.1 billion people worldwide live in informal settlements (UN-Habitat, 2024). In health, life expectancy ranges from 84 years in Japan to 53 in the Central African Republic. In education, secondary school enrolment is over 95% in OECD countries but below 40% in parts of the Sahel. In employment, formal jobs are concentrated in richer countries while two billion people work in the informal economy. In food and water security, around 735 million people went hungry in 2023 according to the UN, mostly in low-income countries. Technology amplifies the gap: 4G coverage is near-universal in Western Europe but only around 30% in rural sub-Saharan Africa, which limits the chance to leapfrog into mobile banking, e-learning and online business.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Uneven development is not only a global story. The UK’s GDP per capita varies from around £57,000 in inner London to £19,000 in parts of Cornwall and the North East. Healthy life expectancy is around 65 in the South East but as low as 58 in Glasgow City. Causes include the long shadow of deindustrialisation in the Midlands and the North, concentration of finance and high-skilled service jobs in London, and historic underinvestment in northern transport. The Levelling Up White Paper (2022) and the cancelled HS2 northern leg (2023) show how policy choices can either narrow or widen these gaps.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">International Strategies: Aid and Agreements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Many strategies aim to reduce uneven development. <dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of money, goods, services or expertise from one country to another, usually from richer to poorer countries.">International aid</dfn> can be bilateral (one government to another), multilateral (through organisations like the World Bank or UN) or non-governmental (charities such as Oxfam). The UK aid budget was £15.4 billion in 2023, though the share of national income spent on aid was cut from 0.7% to 0.5% in 2021. Inter-governmental agreements include the UN Sustainable Development Goals (17 targets to 2030), the Paris Climate Agreement (2015) and trade-policy steps like the EU’s Everything But Arms scheme, which removes tariffs on most exports from Least Developed Countries. Critics argue some aid creates dependency, especially when it props up bad governance, but well-designed aid can save lives quickly — the GAVI vaccine alliance has helped immunise over a billion children since 2000.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words the key difference between top-down and bottom-up development. Test yourself again tomorrow.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Top-down development is decided centrally by governments or large organisations and tends to be large-scale (dams, highways, vaccine programmes). Bottom-up development is led by communities and tends to be small-scale (water pumps, micro-loans, schools).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Top-Down Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Top-down approaches use scale and central planning. Examples include the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, the Three Gorges Dam in China, and the World Bank’s funding of the Mumbai-Ahmedabad rail corridor. Strengths: they can deliver infrastructure quickly, attract private investment, and unlock economies of scale. Weaknesses: they can ignore local knowledge, displace residents (the Three Gorges Dam moved 1.3 million people), and saddle countries with debt if benefits don’t match the cost. Transnational corporations (TNCs) can act top-down too — Shell’s investments in the Niger Delta have brought tax income but caused serious environmental damage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Bottom-Up Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Bottom-up approaches start with the community. <dfn class="term" data-def="Small loans (often under $200) made to entrepreneurs without traditional collateral, helping to start or expand small businesses.">Micro-loans</dfn> from groups like the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh have allowed millions of women to start businesses since the 1980s. Practical Action’s rope pumps in Kenya are made by local welders, fixed by villagers and cost a tenth of an imported diesel pump. WaterAid’s borehole programmes are designed jointly with the village water committee. Strengths: low cost, appropriate technology, builds local skills, sustains itself after donors leave. Weaknesses: small scale, slow to spread, and harder to coordinate across regions. Many geographers argue the most effective programmes pair the two — large infrastructure provides the backbone, community schemes deliver the last mile.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Comparing the Two Approaches</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Imagine two villages without piped water. The top-down option is a $200 million regional pipeline funded by a World Bank loan: clean water at every tap within four years, but a debt that takes 20 years to pay off and limited say for villagers in where the pipes run. The bottom-up option is a community well drilled with $5,000 of NGO support, repaired by a trained villager, plus a savings group for hygiene products: clean water within months for one village only, but at a fraction of the cost and with skills that stay in the community. The choice depends on the scale of the problem, the strength of local institutions and how much risk the country can absorb.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Uneven development is caused by physical, historic and economic factors that interact — no single cause explains it.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Consequences range from health and housing to technology access, both globally and within the UK.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Top-down strategies provide scale and infrastructure; bottom-up schemes provide affordability and ownership; the best programmes combine the two.</li>
</ul>
