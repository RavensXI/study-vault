<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Genetic Engineering</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of modifying the genome of an organism by introducing a gene from another organism to give a desired characteristic.">Genetic engineering</dfn> is a process where scientists modify the genetic material of an organism. A gene that codes for a useful characteristic is “cut” from one organism’s DNA and inserted into another organism’s genome. The organism that receives the new gene is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="An organism that has had its DNA altered by genetic engineering, containing a gene from a different species.">genetically modified organism</dfn> (GMO).</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the three steps of genetic engineering: which tools isolate genes, what carries them, and what the cells produce.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In genetic engineering, enzymes are used to isolate the required gene. This gene is then inserted into a vector (such as a bacterial plasmid or virus) which carries it into the cells of the target organism. The organism’s cells then produce the desired protein.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The process of genetic engineering follows a clear sequence of steps. First, the gene that produces the desired characteristic is identified and isolated using <dfn class="term" data-def="Proteins that act as biological catalysts, speeding up chemical reactions without being used up. Restriction enzymes cut DNA at specific sequences.">restriction enzymes</dfn>, which cut DNA at specific recognition sequences. The gene is then inserted into a <dfn class="term" data-def="A carrier used to transfer a gene into a cell. Common vectors include bacterial plasmids and viruses.">vector</dfn> — typically a small ring of bacterial DNA called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small circular ring of DNA found in bacteria, often used as a vector in genetic engineering.">plasmid</dfn>. The vector carries the gene into the host organism’s cells, where it is incorporated into the DNA and expressed to produce the desired protein.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">One of the most important applications is the production of <strong>human insulin</strong> by genetically modified bacteria. The human insulin gene is inserted into bacterial plasmids, and the bacteria then produce insulin in large quantities. This replaced the older method of extracting insulin from pig or cow pancreases, which sometimes caused allergic reactions in patients.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>GM crops</strong> have been engineered to be resistant to insect attack or to produce higher yields. For example, Bt corn contains a gene from a soil bacterium that produces a natural insecticide, reducing the need for chemical pesticides. Golden rice has been modified to produce beta-carotene (a source of vitamin A) to help combat vitamin A deficiency in developing countries.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Benefits and Risks of Genetic Engineering</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Genetic engineering has significant potential benefits. In agriculture, GM crops can increase food production by improving resistance to pests, disease and herbicides. This can help feed a growing world population. In medicine, genetic engineering allows large-scale production of useful proteins such as insulin and growth hormone.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">However, there are concerns. Some people worry that GM crops could have unknown effects on human health if consumed over long periods. There are also ecological concerns — genes from GM plants could spread to wild populations through cross-pollination, potentially creating “superweeds” that are resistant to herbicides. Ethical concerns exist about whether it is right to modify the genes of living organisms, particularly when it comes to genetic modification of animals or potential future modifications of human embryos.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list 3 benefits of genetic engineering (disease-resistant crops, higher yields, medical proteins) and 3 main risks.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You are expected to evaluate the benefits and risks of genetic engineering. Benefits include: disease-resistant crops, higher yields, medical protein production. Risks include: unknown long-term health effects, gene transfer to wild populations, and ethical concerns.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Ecosystems and Communities</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The interaction of a community of living organisms with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment.">ecosystem</dfn> is the interaction of a community of living organisms (biotic) with the non-living (abiotic) parts of their environment. Ecosystems can range in size from a small pond to an entire ocean. Within an ecosystem, organisms are organised into levels.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="All the populations of different species living and interacting in the same area at the same time.">community</dfn> is all the populations of different species living and interacting together in an area. A <dfn class="term" data-def="All the organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time.">population</dfn> is all the organisms of one species in a given area. A <dfn class="term" data-def="The place where an organism lives, providing it with the conditions and resources it needs to survive.">habitat</dfn> is the place where an organism lives. Each species has its own role within the community, known as its <dfn class="term" data-def="The role of a species within an ecosystem, including what it eats, what eats it, and how it interacts with its environment.">niche</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Abiotic and Biotic Factors</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Non-living factors that affect an ecosystem, such as temperature, light intensity, moisture level, soil pH, wind speed and carbon dioxide levels.">Abiotic factors</dfn> are non-living conditions that influence where organisms can live. These include light intensity, temperature, moisture level, soil pH and mineral content, wind speed and direction, and carbon dioxide levels (for plants) or oxygen levels (for aquatic organisms).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Living factors that affect an ecosystem, such as availability of food, new predators, new pathogens, and competition between species.">Biotic factors</dfn> are living factors that affect organisms in an ecosystem. These include availability of food, the introduction of new predators or competitors, and the arrival of new pathogens (disease-causing organisms). Changes in any biotic or abiotic factor can affect the distribution and population size of species in a community.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Competition and Interdependence</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Organisms within a community compete for resources. Animals compete for food, territory, mates and water. Plants compete for light, water, space and mineral ions from the soil. If two species are competing for the same niche, one will usually out-compete the other. Within a community, species depend on each other for food, shelter, seed dispersal and pollination — this is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of relationships within a community where species rely on each other for survival, such as for food, pollination, shelter or seed dispersal.">interdependence</dfn>. If one species is removed, the whole community can be affected.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Stable Communities</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A <strong>stable community</strong> is one where all the species and environmental factors are in balance so that population sizes remain roughly the same. Examples include tropical rainforests and ancient oak woodlands. In these ecosystems, the populations of predators and prey, producers and consumers are balanced over time.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Feeding Relationships and Trophic Levels</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="The level at which an organism feeds in a food chain. Level 1 = producers, level 2 = primary consumers, level 3 = secondary consumers, level 4 = tertiary consumers.">Trophic levels</dfn> describe the position of an organism in a food chain. <strong>Producers</strong> (usually green plants or algae) are at trophic level 1 — they photosynthesise to make their own food. <strong>Primary consumers</strong> (herbivores) eat the producers and are at trophic level 2. <strong>Secondary consumers</strong> (carnivores) eat the primary consumers and are at trophic level 3. <strong>Tertiary consumers</strong> are at trophic level 4.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why producers are the starting point of food chains and how they transfer energy from the Sun into biomass.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Producers are the starting point of all food chains because they transfer energy from the Sun into biomass through photosynthesis. All consumers depend — directly or indirectly — on producers for their energy.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n22">Predators kill and eat other animals (their prey). In a stable community, the populations of predators and prey rise and fall in cycles. When the prey population increases, more food is available for predators, so the predator population also increases. This then causes the prey population to fall (more are eaten), which in turn causes the predator population to decline. This repeating pattern is known as the <strong>predator-prey cycle</strong>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Decomposers and Nutrient Cycling</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="Organisms such as bacteria and fungi that break down dead plant and animal material, returning nutrients to the soil.">Decomposers</dfn> (mainly bacteria and fungi) break down dead plant and animal matter through the process of decay. This returns mineral ions and nutrients to the soil, where they can be taken up again by plants. Decomposition is faster in warm, moist, aerobic conditions. Decomposers are essential for nutrient cycling in ecosystems.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n24">Energy is transferred along food chains, but not all energy passes from one trophic level to the next. At each level, energy is lost to the surroundings through life processes — organisms use energy for movement, keeping warm, and other metabolic reactions. Energy is also lost in waste products (faeces and urine) and through respiration. This is why food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels — there is not enough energy left to support organisms beyond that point.</p>
</div>
