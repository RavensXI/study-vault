--- DESCRIPTION ---
Aerobic and anaerobic respiration, how cells release energy, and why we need it.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Every Cell Needs Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the final metres of a 400-metre race, a runner&rsquo;s muscles begin to burn. Oxygen can no longer be delivered fast enough to sustain the pace, and a different energy pathway kicks in &mdash; one that works without oxygen but produces a painful build-up of lactic acid. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="A universal chemical process occurring continuously in all living cells that breaks down glucose to transfer energy, supplying ATP for life processes.">cellular respiration</dfn> at its limits. But respiration is not just for athletes: it happens continuously in every cell of every living organism, from bacteria to blue whales, twenty-four hours a day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The energy transferred by respiration powers all life processes: muscle contraction, nerve impulse transmission, active transport across membranes, protein synthesis and cell division. This energy is stored in a molecule called <dfn class="term" data-def="Adenosine triphosphate &mdash; the molecule that stores and transfers energy within cells for biological processes.">ATP</dfn>, which acts as the cell&rsquo;s immediate energy currency. Respiration is <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, usually as heat, causing the temperature of the surroundings to increase.">exothermic</dfn> &mdash; it releases energy to the surroundings, which is why your body stays warm even on a cold day.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write a clear distinction between respiration and breathing. Then write the word equation for aerobic respiration from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cellular respiration is NOT breathing. Respiration is an exothermic chemical reaction inside every living cell that transfers energy from glucose to ATP. Breathing (ventilation) is the physical movement of air in and out of the lungs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Aerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Aerobic respiration</strong> uses oxygen and takes place in the <strong>mitochondria</strong>. It completely breaks down glucose and produces a large yield of ATP, making it the most efficient form of respiration. The word equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} + \text{energy (ATP)}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The balanced symbol equation: \(C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Because glucose is completely broken down, aerobic respiration extracts the maximum possible ATP from each glucose molecule. This is why cells with high energy demands &mdash; such as muscle cells, liver cells and sperm cells &mdash; contain large numbers of mitochondria. The folded inner membranes of the mitochondria (cristae) increase the surface area available for respiration reactions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Anaerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Anaerobic respiration</strong> occurs when oxygen supply is insufficient &mdash; for example, during intense exercise, or in waterlogged soil. Unlike aerobic respiration, it takes place in the <strong>cytoplasm</strong> and produces far less ATP because glucose is only partially broken down. The products depend on the type of organism.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">In animal cells (and some bacteria), glucose is partially broken down to produce <dfn class="term" data-def="A waste product of anaerobic respiration in animal cells that builds up in muscles during intense exercise, causing fatigue and cramps.">lactic acid</dfn>:<br>\(\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{lactic acid}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Lactic acid builds up in muscles during intense exercise, lowering pH and interfering with enzyme function &mdash; causing the burning sensation and fatigue athletes experience. After exercise stops, you continue to breathe deeply to supply the extra oxygen needed to convert accumulated lactic acid back to glucose (in the liver). This extra oxygen requirement is called the <strong>oxygen debt</strong> (or excess post-exercise oxygen consumption).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Anaerobic Respiration in Yeast and Plants: Fermentation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">In yeast and plant cells, anaerobic respiration produces ethanol and carbon dioxide:<br>\(\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">This process is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Anaerobic respiration in yeast and plant cells that converts glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide.">fermentation</dfn>. It has important commercial applications. In <strong>brewing and wine-making</strong>, yeast ferments sugar in fruit juice or malt to produce ethanol. In <strong>bread-making</strong>, yeast ferments sugars in dough, and the CO\(_2\) produced forms bubbles that make the bread rise. The ethanol evaporates during baking.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Draw a comparison table: aerobic vs anaerobic. Columns: oxygen needed?, location in cell, products, ATP yield (high/low). Learn the equations for both animal and yeast anaerobic respiration.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aerobic respiration (with oxygen, mitochondria): high ATP yield, products are CO\(_2\) + water. Anaerobic in animals (no oxygen, cytoplasm): low ATP, product is lactic acid. Anaerobic in yeast/plants: low ATP, products are ethanol + CO\(_2\).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Respiration During and After Exercise</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">During exercise, muscle cells need more energy so the rate of respiration increases. The body responds by increasing <strong>heart rate</strong> (to pump more oxygenated blood to muscles), increasing <strong>breathing rate and depth</strong> (to take in more O\(_2\) and remove more CO\(_2\)), and breaking down <strong>glycogen</strong> stores in muscles and the liver into glucose. If exercise is intense enough that oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough, muscles switch to anaerobic respiration, producing lactic acid. The oxygen debt is repaid after exercise as lactic acid is oxidised to CO\(_2\) and water in the liver.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">You can detect the products of respiration experimentally. Breathing out through <strong>limewater</strong> turns it milky, confirming CO\(_2\) production. Placing a temperature probe in a sealed flask with germinating seeds shows a temperature increase over time, confirming that respiration is exothermic. The rate can be measured by monitoring CO\(_2\) output using a carbon dioxide sensor or pH indicator solution.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">When interpreting data from these experiments, consider: whether the change observed could have another cause (a confounding variable), whether the results are repeatable, and whether a control is needed (e.g. dead seeds in a sealed flask) to confirm that any heat or gas is coming from the biological process and not a chemical contaminant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Explain the oxygen debt in your own words. Include: why it builds up during exercise, what substance needs to be broken down, and where in the body this happens.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The oxygen debt is the extra oxygen needed after exercise to break down lactic acid built up during anaerobic respiration. It is repaid by continued heavy breathing after exercise stops. Lactic acid is converted back to glucose in the liver.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Cellular respiration is an exothermic process in all living cells that transfers energy from glucose to ATP. Aerobic respiration (in mitochondria, with oxygen) produces a large ATP yield. Equation: \(C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Anaerobic respiration (in the cytoplasm, without oxygen) produces less ATP. In animals, it produces lactic acid; in yeast and plants, it produces ethanol and CO\(_2\) (fermentation).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">During intense exercise, lactic acid builds up (oxygen debt). After exercise, continued breathing supplies the extra oxygen needed to convert lactic acid back to glucose in the liver.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">A common mistake is confusing respiration with breathing &mdash; make this distinction explicit in every answer. When comparing aerobic and anaerobic respiration, always state the location (mitochondria vs cytoplasm), the oxygen requirement and the ATP yield. For &lsquo;explain&rsquo; questions about exercise responses (increased heart rate, breathing rate), use causal language: explain <em>why</em> each response helps (e.g. &lsquo;increased breathing rate delivers more oxygen to muscles, allowing more aerobic respiration and greater ATP production&rsquo;). Examiners expect this chain of reasoning, not just a list of changes.</p>
