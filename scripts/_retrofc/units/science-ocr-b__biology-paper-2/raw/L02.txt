--- DESCRIPTION ---
Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, organelles, and how microscopy revealed the inside of the cell.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How We Came to Know the Cell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1665, Robert Hooke placed a thin slice of cork under a simple microscope and saw a pattern of tiny compartments. He called them &ldquo;cells&rdquo; because they reminded him of the small rooms in a monastery. Hooke could not see inside these compartments &mdash; his instrument was far too weak. Yet that single observation launched two centuries of investigation, ultimately revealing that cells are the fundamental unit of all life. The history of our understanding of cell structure is a story of technology driving science: each new generation of microscopes unlocked a new layer of biological reality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This lesson explores two fundamental cell types &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="A cell with a true nucleus enclosed by a membrane, plus membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic.">eukaryotic</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, simple cell with no true nucleus. The genetic material is a single DNA loop in the cytoplasm. Bacteria are prokaryotic.">prokaryotic</dfn> &mdash; and traces how improvements in microscopy revealed the internal structures we now know so well.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, draw a comparison table of eukaryotic vs prokaryotic cells. Include: true nucleus (yes/no), DNA form, organelles present, typical size.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi) have a true nucleus that encloses DNA within a nuclear membrane. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no true nucleus &mdash; their DNA is a single loop in the cytoplasm. Prokaryotes are typically 10&ndash;100 times smaller than eukaryotes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Organelles and Their Functions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Both animal and plant cells contain a core set of organelles whose design is closely matched to their function. The exam requires you to go beyond naming structures &mdash; you must explain <em>why</em> each structure&rsquo;s shape, position or composition allows it to carry out its role efficiently.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Organelles in Animal Cells</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Nucleus</strong> &mdash; contains the chromosomes (DNA) and controls the cell&rsquo;s activities. The nuclear membrane keeps the genetic material separate from the cytoplasm. When a cell needs a particular protein, the genetic instructions are copied from DNA and sent out through pores in the nuclear membrane to the ribosomes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cell membrane</strong> &mdash; a thin, selectively permeable layer that controls which substances enter and leave. It contains <dfn class="term" data-def="Molecules on the surface of a cell membrane that detect and bind to specific chemical signals such as hormones, triggering a cellular response.">receptor molecules</dfn> that allow the cell to detect and respond to hormones and other chemical signals.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Mitochondria</strong> &mdash; the site of aerobic respiration. Their folded inner membranes (<strong>cristae</strong>) greatly increase the surface area available for respiration enzymes, making energy transfer more efficient. Cells with very high energy demands &mdash; such as muscle cells or sperm cells &mdash; are packed with mitochondria.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Ribosomes</strong> &mdash; tiny structures found in both the cytoplasm and on the endoplasmic reticulum. They are the site of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which ribosomes use the genetic code from mRNA to assemble amino acids into protein molecules.">protein synthesis</dfn>. Ribosomes are present in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, though prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Additional Structures in Plant Cells</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Plant cells have all the animal cell structures listed above, plus three additional ones:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Cell wall</strong> &mdash; made of cellulose, it surrounds the cell membrane and provides structural strength. It is fully permeable (allows all dissolved substances through) but prevents the cell from bursting when water enters by osmosis, keeping the cell turgid and supporting the plant.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Chloroplasts</strong> &mdash; contain the green pigment <dfn class="term" data-def="The green pigment found in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.">chlorophyll</dfn> and are the site of photosynthesis. They are found mainly in cells near the surface of leaves and stems where light is available.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Permanent vacuole</strong> &mdash; a large fluid-filled space containing cell sap (dissolved sugars and salts). It presses the cytoplasm against the cell wall, keeping the cell turgid and helping to support the plant structure.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the three structures that plant cells have but animal cells do not. For each, state one function. Then name one structure that bacteria have but animal cells lack.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plant cells have three additional structures compared to animal cells: cell wall (cellulose, structural support), chloroplasts (photosynthesis) and permanent vacuole (cell sap, turgidity). Bacterial cells have a cell wall but no true nucleus, no mitochondria and no chloroplasts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Prokaryotic Cell Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Bacterial cells are prokaryotic: smaller, simpler and without membrane-bound organelles. Their genetic material is a single circular loop of DNA floating in the cytoplasm. Many bacteria also contain small, extra loops of DNA called <dfn class="term" data-def="Small circular rings of extra DNA found in prokaryotic cells, separate from the main chromosomal DNA. They can replicate independently and often carry antibiotic-resistance genes.">plasmids</dfn>, which can carry genes for antibiotic resistance and can be passed between bacteria. Bacteria do have ribosomes (for protein synthesis) and a cell wall (though made of a different material from plant cell walls, usually peptidoglycan).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">From Hooke to the Electron Microscope</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Light microscopes improved steadily after Hooke&rsquo;s time. By the 19th century, biologists could see nuclei, cell divisions and the broad outlines of organelles. Light microscopes have a useful magnification of about &times;1500 and a <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a microscope to distinguish between two closely spaced points as separate objects. Higher resolution means finer detail is visible.">resolution</dfn> of around 200 nm. To prepare a slide, a thin sample is placed on glass, stained (iodine to reveal plant cell walls and nuclei; methylene blue for animal cells), and covered with a coverslip lowered at an angle to avoid air bubbles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The real breakthrough came in the 20th century with the invention of the <strong>electron microscope</strong>. Instead of light, it fires a beam of electrons at the specimen. This gives a resolution of around 0.2 nm &mdash; approximately 1,000 times better than a light microscope. Transmission electron microscopes (TEMs) can magnify specimens up to about two million times. This level of detail revealed the internal folds of mitochondria (cristae), the structure of ribosomes and the double membrane of the nucleus &mdash; all previously invisible. Each discovery confirmed and refined scientists&rsquo; models of cell structure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The development of electron microscopy is a clear example of how scientific knowledge develops with technology (a key Ideas about Science theme): new instruments allow observations that lead to revised or extended explanations. Hooke proposed cells existed; electron microscopists revealed how they worked from the inside.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Magnification Calculations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">The magnification formula is:
    $$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{real size}}$$
    Always convert both measurements to the same unit before dividing. Scientists often work in micrometres (&micro;m) for cells and nanometres (nm) for organelles and molecules. \(1 \text{ mm} = 1000 \text{ &micro;m} = 1{,}000{,}000 \text{ nm}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Worked example: A drawing of a cell measures 45 mm on the page. The actual cell is 0.015 mm. Magnification = \(\frac{45}{0.015} = \times 3000\). Always state the multiplication symbol and the number, e.g. &times;3000. If asked to find the real size: real size = image size &divide; magnification.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Practice the magnification formula. Cover and calculate: image = 60 mm, actual = 0.02 mm, what is the magnification? Then explain what extra detail the electron microscope revealed that light microscopes could not.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Magnification = image size &divide; real size. Electron microscopes (~0.2 nm resolution, up to &times;2,000,000) revealed mitochondrial cristae, ribosome structure and nuclear membrane detail that light microscopes (~200 nm resolution, &times;1500) could not resolve.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Eukaryotic cells (animals and plants) have a nucleus enclosing DNA; prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have a DNA loop in the cytoplasm and no true nucleus. Plant cells have three extra structures: cell wall, chloroplasts and permanent vacuole.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Organelle design matches function: mitochondrial cristae increase surface area for respiration; ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis; the cell membrane&rsquo;s receptor molecules enable chemical signalling.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Advances in microscopy &mdash; from Hooke&rsquo;s light microscope to the electron microscope &mdash; progressively revealed finer detail. Magnification = image size &divide; real size. Electron microscopes offer around 1,000 times better resolution than light microscopes.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Magnification calculations are reliably examined. The most common mistake is using different units for image size and real size &mdash; always convert to the same unit first. When asked to &lsquo;explain&rsquo; how microscopy has improved our understanding of cells, the examiners want you to use causal language: state what the technology allowed scientists to see, then explain how this changed or extended the scientific explanation (model). Listing microscope types without linking them to specific discoveries will not score the highest marks.</p>
