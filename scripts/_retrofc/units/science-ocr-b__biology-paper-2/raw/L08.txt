--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Was the Theory of Evolution Developed? (IaS3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Scientific theories do not appear fully formed &mdash; they are built gradually from observations, hypotheses and accumulating evidence. The theory of evolution by <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which organisms with characteristics best suited to their environment are more likely to survive, reproduce and pass on their advantageous alleles to the next generation.">natural selection</dfn> is a powerful example of this process. <strong>Charles Darwin</strong> (1809&ndash;1882) developed his ideas during a five-year voyage around the world, recording variation in animals and plants across different environments. His observations of finch beak shapes on the Gal&aacute;pagos Islands &mdash; each suited to a different food source &mdash; helped him form the idea that species change in response to their environment over many generations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Alfred Russel Wallace</strong> (1823&ndash;1913) reached similar conclusions independently during fieldwork in South America and Southeast Asia. He and Darwin jointly presented their ideas to the Linnean Society in 1858. Their combined evidence &mdash; from different continents and different organisms &mdash; strengthened the case that natural selection was a real and general process, not just an observation specific to one place. This is an important example of how scientific explanations develop: independent evidence from multiple sources makes a theory more robust.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the four steps of natural selection in order. Then name the two scientists associated with the theory and state one piece of evidence that supports it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Natural selection works in four steps: (1) <dfn class="term" data-def="Differences in the characteristics of individuals of the same species, arising from genetic differences, environmental differences, or both.">variation</dfn> exists within a population; (2) individuals compete for limited resources; (3) those with the most advantageous characteristics are more likely to survive and reproduce; (4) advantageous alleles are inherited by the next generation. Over many generations, this changes the characteristics of the population.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Evidence for Evolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Multiple independent lines of evidence support the theory of evolution. The <strong>fossil record</strong> shows gradual changes in the structure of organisms over geological time &mdash; transitional fossils show intermediate forms between ancestral and modern species. <strong>Comparative anatomy</strong> reveals similar bone structures across very different animals (such as the human arm, bat wing and whale flipper), suggesting they share common ancestors. <strong>DNA analysis</strong> shows that closely related species share more DNA sequences than distantly related ones &mdash; a finding that was entirely unknown to Darwin and Wallace, yet confirms their theory from a completely independent direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The peppered moth provides a well-documented example of natural selection observed over a short timescale. Before industrialisation, pale-coloured moths were camouflaged against lichen-covered trees and survived in greater numbers; dark (melanic) moths were more visible to predators and were rarer. When industrial pollution killed the lichens and darkened tree bark with soot, dark moths gained a survival advantage and became more common. After the introduction of pollution controls, pale moths became dominant again. This demonstrates that the direction of natural selection depends on the environment &mdash; and can reverse when conditions change.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The formation of a new species when populations of the same species become isolated and evolve independently until they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.">Speciation</dfn> occurs when a population becomes geographically or reproductively isolated. Once isolated, each group is exposed to different environmental pressures and accumulates different mutations through random genetic change. Over many generations, the two groups diverge until they are so genetically different that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring &mdash; at this point they are considered separate species. <dfn class="term" data-def="The permanent disappearance of a species when no living individuals remain, often caused by inability to adapt to changing conditions.">Extinction</dfn> occurs when a species cannot adapt quickly enough; the fossil record documents this throughout Earth's history.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">It is important to remember that <strong>individuals</strong> do not evolve &mdash; <strong>populations</strong> evolve over many generations as the frequency of advantageous alleles increases. Evolution has no direction or goal; it simply reflects which traits happen to be best suited to the current environment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Classification and Phylogenetic Trees</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Classification is the process of grouping organisms based on their evolutionary relationships. The traditional Linnaean hierarchy (kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) has been refined with modern DNA evidence. <dfn class="term" data-def="A branching diagram that represents the evolutionary relationships between groups of organisms based on shared characteristics and DNA evidence.">Phylogenetic trees</dfn> show how species are related by shared ancestry &mdash; the more recently two species share a common ancestor, the more similar their DNA.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Modern classification uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Classification based on evolutionary relationships using DNA evidence, also called cladistics.">evolutionary (phylogenetic) classification</dfn> rather than grouping organisms solely by appearance. Two organisms can look similar because they share a recent common ancestor (homology) or because they evolved similar features independently in response to similar environments (convergent evolution). DNA evidence helps distinguish between these possibilities.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Darwin&rsquo;s original theory was based on observed variation and the fossil record. Wallace&rsquo;s independent observations from a different continent added further support. Since then, genetics, molecular biology and direct observation of evolution in bacteria and insects have added independent lines of evidence. When a theory is supported by multiple, independent strands of evidence from different disciplines, it becomes extremely well established. This is why evolution is one of the most strongly supported theories in all of science, despite being controversial when first proposed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A key skill in science is recognising the difference between data (what is measured) and explanation (why it happens). The fossil record is <em>data</em>; natural selection is the <em>explanation</em> for why it shows the patterns it does. Both are needed for a complete scientific account.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Biodiversity and Its Threats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of living organisms in a given area, including the number of different species and the genetic variation within species.">Biodiversity</dfn> &mdash; the variety of species and genetic diversity within species &mdash; is essential for healthy ecosystems. Greater biodiversity makes ecosystems more stable and resilient to change; loss of species can cause cascading effects through food webs. Human activities are the primary cause of the current rapid decline in biodiversity worldwide: habitat destruction (especially deforestation), pollution, overexploitation of species, introduction of invasive species, and climate change all reduce biodiversity at rates far exceeding natural background extinction levels.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="List five human activities that threaten biodiversity. For each one, write one sentence explaining the mechanism by which it reduces species diversity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Biodiversity is threatened by habitat destruction, pollution, overexploitation, invasive species and climate change. These all reduce biodiversity at rates much faster than natural processes, destabilising ecosystems and reducing their resilience.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Conservation and Protection Strategies</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Conservation strategies aim to protect biodiversity and allow natural ecosystems to recover. <strong>Protected areas</strong> (national parks, nature reserves) limit damaging human activity in key habitats. <strong>Captive breeding programmes</strong> maintain viable populations of endangered species in zoos, with the aim of eventual reintroduction to the wild. <strong>Seed banks</strong> preserve the genetic diversity of plant species as insurance against extinction. <strong>Fishing quotas and logging regulations</strong> prevent over-exploitation of natural resources. <strong>Reforestation projects</strong> restore destroyed habitats and increase carbon absorption.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Effective conservation requires long-term scientific monitoring to assess whether biodiversity is recovering, and international cooperation since many threatened species and habitats cross national borders. Scientific data from monitoring programmes informs policy decisions &mdash; but these decisions also involve economic trade-offs and values, not just science.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Evolution by natural selection was proposed independently by Darwin and Wallace from observations in different parts of the world. The theory is now supported by multiple independent lines of evidence: fossils, comparative anatomy and DNA.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Speciation occurs when isolated populations diverge through different selection pressures until they can no longer interbreed. Classification now uses DNA-based phylogenetic trees to reflect true evolutionary relationships.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Biodiversity is threatened by human activities including habitat destruction, pollution and overexploitation. Conservation strategies include protected areas, breeding programmes, seed banks and reforestation.</li>
</ul>
