--- DESCRIPTION ---
How organisms grow, the role of cell division, and the science and ethics of stem-cell therapy.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Growth Through Cell Division</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A human being begins as a single fertilised cell no wider than a human hair. Over the following nine months, that cell divides billions of times, eventually producing the trillions of specialised cells that form a newborn baby. The key question in developmental biology is not just <em>how</em> cells divide, but <em>how</em> they become so different from one another &mdash; a liver cell, a neuron and a skin cell all contain identical DNA, yet they look and behave completely differently.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.">Mitosis</dfn> is the form of cell division responsible for growth and repair in multicellular organisms. Before mitosis begins, during a phase called <strong>S phase</strong>, the cell&rsquo;s DNA replicates so that every chromosome is copied. Then during mitosis itself, the copies are separated and two genetically identical daughter cells are produced, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent. It is crucial to understand that DNA replication happens <em>before</em> mitosis starts, not during it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and correct a common mistake: when does DNA replication happen relative to mitosis? Write the answer and explain why this matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>DNA replication occurs during S phase, <strong>before</strong> mitosis begins &mdash; not during mitosis itself. Mitosis then separates the copied chromosomes to produce two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Cell Differentiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a cell becomes specialised in structure and function, switching on only those genes needed for its particular role while silencing the rest.">Differentiation</dfn> is the process by which a cell becomes specialised. Although every cell in the body contains the same DNA, different genes are switched on or off in different cell types. This means each specialised cell produces only the proteins needed for its specific function.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The design of specialised cells directly reflects their function. <strong>Red blood cells</strong> are biconcave discs with no nucleus &mdash; this removes the nucleus and increases the surface area for oxygen-carrying haemoglobin. <strong>Neurones</strong> have long axons that allow electrical impulses to travel over large distances quickly. <strong>Root hair cells</strong> have long thin extensions that dramatically increase their surface area for absorbing water and mineral ions from the soil.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sperm cells</strong> have a streamlined head containing an acrosome (with enzymes to penetrate the egg), a midpiece packed with mitochondria to supply energy for swimming, and a long flagellum tail for motility. Every feature serves the single purpose of delivering genetic material to an egg.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Muscle cells</strong> (muscle fibres) are elongated cells that can shorten and extend. They contain large numbers of mitochondria (for ATP production during contraction) and specialised proteins (actin and myosin) that slide past each other to generate force. Cells with highly specific roles like these are the endpoint of the differentiation process.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Stem Cells: Undifferentiated and Versatile</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Undifferentiated cells that can divide by mitosis and have the potential to differentiate into one or more specialised cell types.">Stem cells</dfn> are cells that have not yet differentiated. They retain the ability to divide and produce cells that can become specialised. There are two main types of stem cell relevant to your course: <strong>embryonic stem cells</strong> and <strong>adult stem cells</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Stem cells derived from an early-stage embryo (3–5 days after fertilisation) that can differentiate into any cell type in the body. They are described as pluripotent.">Embryonic stem cells</dfn> are found in the embryo in the first few days after fertilisation. They are described as <strong>pluripotent</strong> &mdash; they can differentiate into any cell type in the body. This makes them potentially very useful for treating diseases caused by cell loss or damage, such as Parkinson&rsquo;s disease, type 1 diabetes, or spinal cord injury.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Stem cells found in specific tissues in adult organisms (e.g. bone marrow) that can differentiate into a limited range of cell types relevant to that tissue.">Adult stem cells</dfn> are found in specific tissues, such as bone marrow (which produces blood stem cells that can differentiate into all blood cell types). They are more limited than embryonic stem cells &mdash; they can only produce the range of cells relevant to their tissue. Bone marrow transplants, which replace diseased blood stem cells, are an established medical therapy using adult stem cells.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (iPS Cells)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Scientists have developed a technique to take ordinary adult cells (such as skin cells) and &ldquo;reprogramme&rdquo; them genetically to behave like embryonic stem cells. These are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Ordinary adult body cells that have been genetically reprogrammed to an embryonic-like stem cell state, giving them the ability to differentiate into many cell types.">induced pluripotent stem cells</dfn> (iPS cells). Because they can be made from a patient&rsquo;s own cells, they would avoid the risk of immune rejection and sidestep many of the ethical concerns associated with embryonic stem cells. iPS cell research is an active and rapidly developing field.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Compare embryonic and adult stem cells: write down the cell types each can produce, one advantage and one limitation of each. Can you explain what iPS cells are and why they might be preferable?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent &mdash; they can become any cell type. Adult stem cells (e.g. bone marrow) can only produce cells of their own tissue type. iPS cells are reprogrammed adult cells that regain pluripotency without using embryos.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Ethics of Stem Cell Research</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Using embryonic stem cells requires destroying a human embryo at an early stage of development (3&ndash;5 days old). This raises serious ethical questions. People who hold that human life begins at fertilisation argue that destroying any embryo is morally wrong, regardless of the medical benefits. Others argue that the potential to alleviate suffering for millions of people outweighs concerns about a cluster of cells at such an early stage, which has no consciousness or capacity for pain.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">This is a genuine <strong>values-based disagreement</strong>, not a scientific one. The science can establish what embryonic stem cells are capable of and what risks they carry. But the question of whether they <em>should</em> be used involves moral, religious and philosophical judgements that science alone cannot resolve. Being able to separate these two types of question is itself an important scientific skill &mdash; the distinction between an evidence-based claim and a values-based claim.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Risks and Challenges in Stem Cell Therapy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Even setting ethics aside, stem cell therapy faces practical challenges. Transplanted cells may be rejected by the patient&rsquo;s immune system, requiring immunosuppressant drugs that leave the patient vulnerable to infection. There is also a risk that undifferentiated stem cells could divide uncontrollably after transplantation, forming tumours. Researchers must demonstrate that transplanted cells differentiate correctly and integrate safely into the host tissue before therapies can be considered safe enough for widespread clinical use.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The risk&ndash;benefit framework applied here mirrors wider scientific decision-making: weighing evidence-based risks (immune rejection, tumour formation) against potential benefits (restored function for people with otherwise untreatable conditions) under conditions of uncertainty. This is a classic IaS4 scenario &mdash; science provides the risk data, but the decision about acceptable risk involves values as well as evidence.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Explain in two sentences why the stem cell ethics debate cannot be resolved by science alone. Then name two practical (non-ethical) risks of stem cell transplantation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The ethics of embryonic stem cell use involves values about when human life begins &mdash; a question science cannot answer. Practical risks include immune rejection and uncontrolled cell division (tumour risk). iPS cells and adult stem cells partially address both concerns.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells and is used for growth and repair. DNA replication occurs in S phase before mitosis begins. Differentiation produces specialised cells by switching genes on or off.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Embryonic stem cells are pluripotent (any cell type). Adult stem cells are more limited. iPS cells are reprogrammed adult cells that regain pluripotency without using embryos.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Stem cell ethics involve both scientific evidence (risks, capabilities) and values (beliefs about embryonic life). Science can inform but cannot resolve the values-based dimension of the debate.</li>
</ul>

If you merge these, you lose marks. Always name the type of stem cell you are describing &mdash; &lsquo;stem cell&rsquo; alone is insufficient. For the mitosis misconception: state clearly that DNA replication happens before mitosis, in S phase. Do not say DNA doubles &lsquo;during mitosis&rsquo;.</p>
