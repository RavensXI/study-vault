--- DESCRIPTION ---
Diffusion, osmosis, active transport, and why large organisms need lungs, intestines and a heart.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Size Changes Everything</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A single-celled organism like an amoeba gets all the oxygen it needs simply by diffusion across its cell surface. No circulatory system required. But a blue whale &mdash; the largest animal ever to have lived &mdash; needs a massive heart, kilometres of blood vessels, enormous lungs and elaborate exchange surfaces in its intestines. The fundamental reason is mathematics: as organisms grow, their <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of an organism's surface area to its volume. As organisms get larger, this ratio decreases, making diffusion alone insufficient to supply all cells.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn> (SA:V) decreases. The volume (and therefore the number of cells needing supplies) grows faster than the surface area available for exchange.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Consider cubes: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm<sup>2</sup> and a volume of 1 cm<sup>3</sup> &mdash; SA:V ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm<sup>2</sup> and volume of 8 cm<sup>3</sup> &mdash; SA:V ratio of only 3:1. A 3 cm cube has SA:V of 2:1. As the cube grows, its surface area&ndash;to&ndash;volume ratio halves and then halves again. For a large, complex organism, simple diffusion across the body surface cannot supply oxygen and nutrients fast enough to reach every cell.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Calculate the SA:V ratio for a 4 cm cube from memory. Then explain in one sentence why large organisms cannot rely on diffusion alone.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>As organisms get larger, their surface area to volume ratio decreases. This means the surface area available for gas exchange and nutrient uptake is insufficient to supply all the cells by diffusion alone. Large organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Moving Substances Across Cell Membranes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Cells exchange substances with their surroundings by three mechanisms. <dfn class="term" data-def="The net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient. No energy is required.">Diffusion</dfn> is the passive net movement of particles down a <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference in concentration of a substance between two areas. The steeper the gradient, the faster diffusion occurs.">concentration gradient</dfn> &mdash; from high to low concentration. No energy is needed. Oxygen diffuses into cells for respiration; carbon dioxide and urea diffuse out.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated solution (low water potential) through a partially permeable membrane.">Osmosis</dfn> is a special case of diffusion involving only water molecules moving through a <dfn class="term" data-def="A membrane that allows water and small molecules through, but blocks larger solute molecules.">partially permeable membrane</dfn>. Water moves from a region of high water concentration (dilute solution) to a region of low water concentration (concentrated solution). This is important in, for example, kidney function and plant cell water regulation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of substances from a lower concentration to a higher concentration against the concentration gradient, requiring energy from respiration.">Active transport</dfn> moves substances <em>against</em> the concentration gradient &mdash; from low to high concentration. This requires energy in the form of ATP from respiration. Root hair cells use active transport to absorb nitrate ions from the soil even when their internal concentration already exceeds that in the soil. Intestinal epithelial cells also use active transport to absorb glucose from the gut lumen into the blood.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting the Rate of Diffusion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The rate of diffusion is increased by: a <strong>steeper concentration gradient</strong> (greater difference in concentration between the two sides); a <strong>larger surface area</strong> (more molecules can cross at once); a <strong>shorter diffusion distance</strong> (thinner membrane means less distance to travel); and a <strong>higher temperature</strong> (molecules have more kinetic energy and collide more frequently).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">These four factors explain the design of every specialised exchange surface in the body: alveoli have a huge surface area, very thin walls and are richly supplied with blood capillaries that maintain a steep gradient. Villi in the small intestine have a large surface area (with microvilli), a thin epithelium and a dense capillary network. Both surfaces are optimised to maximise diffusion rate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Draw a comparison table: diffusion vs osmosis vs active transport. Include: energy required, direction (with or against gradient), what moves, and one example from the body.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Diffusion and osmosis are passive (no energy). Active transport requires ATP and moves substances against the concentration gradient. All three are essential for supplying cells &mdash; the correct mechanism depends on whether the substance moves with or against its gradient.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Specialised Exchange Surfaces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Because large organisms cannot rely on diffusion alone, evolution has produced highly specialised exchange surfaces shared by several structures in the body. All share the same design principles: large surface area, thin walls and a good supply of blood capillaries that maintain steep concentration gradients.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Alveoli: Gas Exchange in the Lungs</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny air sacs in the lungs with walls one cell thick, providing a large surface area for gas exchange between air and blood.">Alveoli</dfn> are tiny air sacs clustered at the ends of the smallest airways (bronchioles). Each alveolus is surrounded by a dense network of capillaries. Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveolus, across the single-cell-thick alveolar wall and capillary wall, into the blood. Carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction. The human lungs contain several hundred million alveoli, giving a combined surface area estimated at around 70 m<sup>2</sup> in an average adult &mdash; roughly the floor area of a studio flat.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In lung diseases such as emphysema (caused primarily by smoking), alveolar walls break down, reducing the total surface area available for gas exchange. This means less oxygen enters the blood, leaving patients breathless even at rest. This clinical context illustrates why the design features of alveoli &mdash; surface area, thin wall, blood supply &mdash; are so important: any reduction in these factors directly reduces the efficiency of gas exchange.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Villi: Nutrient Absorption in the Small Intestine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny finger-like projections lining the small intestine that increase the surface area for absorption of digested nutrients into the blood.">Villi</dfn> are finger-like projections that line the small intestine, increasing its surface area enormously. Each villus has a thin epithelium (one cell thick), a dense network of blood capillaries for absorbing glucose and amino acids into the blood, and a lacteal (lymph vessel) for absorbing fatty acids and glycerol. The epithelial cells themselves also have microvilli (tiny projections on their surface) that further multiply the surface area &mdash; this folded structure within a folded structure is a striking example of biological design maximising exchange.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Circulatory System: Delivering and Collecting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Even with efficient exchange surfaces, substances still need to be transported throughout the body. In humans, this is achieved by the double circulatory system: one circuit carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs for gas exchange (the pulmonary circuit); a second carries oxygenated blood from the heart to all body tissues (the systemic circuit). Having two circuits maintains high blood pressure for efficient delivery.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Arteries</strong> carry blood away from the heart at high pressure and have thick, elastic, muscular walls to withstand this. <strong>Veins</strong> carry blood back to the heart at lower pressure, with thinner walls and valves to prevent backflow. <strong>Capillaries</strong> are the key exchange vessels: walls just one cell thick allow substances to diffuse efficiently between blood and surrounding tissues.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="For each blood vessel (artery, vein, capillary): write its direction of blood flow, wall thickness and one structural feature that relates to its function. Then explain what capillaries have in common with alveoli.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Capillaries, alveoli and villi all share the same design principle: thin walls (one cell thick), large total surface area and a good blood supply to maintain steep concentration gradients. These features maximise the rate of substance exchange.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">As organisms grow, their surface area to volume ratio decreases. This makes diffusion alone insufficient, requiring specialised exchange surfaces (alveoli, villi) and transport systems (the circulatory system).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Diffusion and osmosis are passive (no energy, down the gradient). Active transport uses ATP to move substances against the gradient. All three are essential in the body.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Efficient exchange surfaces share the same design: large surface area, thin walls (short diffusion distance), steep concentration gradient (maintained by blood flow). Capillaries, alveoli and villi all exemplify this design.</li>
</ul>
