--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Shakespeare’s Late Play</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>The Tempest</em> was written around 1610–1611 and is widely considered Shakespeare’s last major play. It belongs to the group of works scholars call the <dfn class="term" data-def="Shakespeare’s final plays (including The Tempest, The Winter’s Tale, and Cymbeline), which blend tragedy and comedy, ending in reconciliation and forgiveness.">late romances</dfn> — plays that combine elements of tragedy and comedy, featuring loss, separation, and eventual reconciliation. Unlike the tragedies, these plays end with forgiveness rather than death, suggesting a mature Shakespeare reflecting on mercy, art, and the human capacity for change.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The play was first performed at the court of King James I in November 1611 and was performed again in 1613 as part of the celebrations for the marriage of James’s daughter, Princess Elizabeth. Its themes of magic, power, and forgiveness would have resonated with a <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the reign of King James I (1603–1625) — the period during which Shakespeare wrote his late plays.">Jacobean</dfn> audience that valued order, hierarchy, and the monarch’s authority.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall what 1609 event inspired The Tempest and who wrote the account Shakespeare read.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>The Tempest</em> was written during the early years of British colonialism. In 1609, a fleet heading for the Virginia colony was wrecked in a storm near Bermuda. Accounts of the shipwreck — particularly William Strachey’s letter — are widely believed to have inspired the play’s setting and storm.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Colonial Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The most debated aspect of <em>The Tempest</em>’s context is its relationship to <dfn class="term" data-def="The policy or practice of acquiring political control over another country or territory, often exploiting its people and resources.">colonialism</dfn>. Prospero arrives on the island, takes control, and enslaves its only inhabitant, Caliban. Prospero teaches Caliban language and claims the island as his own. Caliban protests: “This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak’st from me.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A theoretical approach that examines literature through the lens of colonialism, focusing on the power dynamics between coloniser and colonised.">postcolonial</dfn> reading of the play sees Prospero as a European coloniser who displaces the island’s indigenous inhabitant, imposes his language and culture, and uses Caliban as forced labour. Caliban’s complaint — “You taught me language, and my profit on’t / Is I know how to curse” — becomes a powerful statement about how colonised peoples are given the tools of the coloniser only to express their own oppression.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">In 1609, the ship <em>Sea Venture</em>, carrying settlers to the Virginia colony, was caught in a hurricane and wrecked on the Bermuda islands. The survivors built new ships and eventually reached Jamestown. William Strachey’s account, <em>A True Reportory of the Wreck</em> (circulated privately from 1610), describes a terrifying storm followed by the discovery of a seemingly enchanted island. Shakespeare almost certainly read this account. The play’s opening storm, the island setting, and the themes of civilisation versus nature all echo the Bermuda narrative.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Power and Hierarchy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Like all of Shakespeare’s plays, <em>The Tempest</em> reflects Jacobean ideas about social <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of ranking or organising people according to status or authority — Jacobean England believed in a divinely ordained social order.">hierarchy</dfn>. Prospero is the rightful Duke of Milan, overthrown by his brother Antonio. The play’s plot is driven by Prospero’s desire to restore his legitimate authority. For a Jacobean audience, the usurpation of a duke was a crime against the natural order — similar to regicide in <em>Macbeth</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The island creates a microcosm of power relations. Prospero controls everyone: Ariel through a promise of freedom, Caliban through threat of punishment, and the shipwrecked nobles through magic. The play asks whether power exercised through fear and control is legitimate, or whether true authority requires mercy and the willingness to let go.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Prospero’s power can be read in two ways: as a legitimate ruler restoring order (Jacobean reading), or as a colonial master exploiting an island and its people (postcolonial reading). The exam rewards students who explore multiple interpretations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Magic, Art, and Shakespeare’s Farewell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Many scholars read <em>The Tempest</em> as Shakespeare’s personal farewell to the stage. Prospero’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A speech at the end of a play in which a character addresses the audience directly, often asking for their approval or applause.">epilogue</dfn> — “Now my charms are all o’erthrown, / And what strength I have’s mine own” — can be read as Shakespeare the playwright surrendering his creative powers. Prospero’s famous speech, “Our revels now are ended,” compares life to a theatrical performance: “We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Whether or not Shakespeare intended this autobiographical reading, the play undeniably explores the relationship between art and power. Prospero’s magic is repeatedly described in terms of theatre — masques, shows, visions. When he breaks his staff and drowns his book, he renounces both his supernatural power and, symbolically, the playwright’s art of creating illusions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An elaborate court entertainment combining music, dance, poetry, and spectacle, popular in Jacobean England.">masque</dfn> was a lavish court entertainment popular under James I, featuring music, dance, and allegorical spectacle. In Act 4, Prospero stages a masque of goddesses (Iris, Ceres, Juno) to celebrate Ferdinand and Miranda’s betrothal. This reflects the real court culture in which Shakespeare’s play was first performed. The masque also demonstrates Prospero’s power as a creator of illusions — he is simultaneously a magician and a director, controlling what others see and experience.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19"><em>The Tempest</em> was written around 1610–1611 during the early years of British colonialism — the Bermuda shipwreck of 1609 is a key source.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The play can be read through multiple lenses: as a Jacobean drama about restoring legitimate authority, as a postcolonial critique of European power, or as Shakespeare’s farewell to the stage.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Prospero’s power is central to the play — exam answers should explore whether his control of the island is just or oppressive.</li></ul>
