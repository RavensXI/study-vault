<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Storm (Act 1, Scene 1)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The play opens with a violent storm at sea — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The storm that opens the play, created by Prospero's magic to shipwreck his enemies on the island.">tempest</dfn> of the title. Shakespeare plunges the audience into chaos: the Boatswain shouts commands, nobles panic, and the ship breaks apart. The storm immediately establishes a key theme — the disruption and restoration of order. On the ship, social hierarchy collapses: the Boatswain tells the courtiers that "the elements" care nothing for their rank, asking "What cares these roarers for the name of king?"</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This challenge to authority is significant. In Jacobean England, the idea that nature could override the power of a king was both thrilling and disturbing. The storm also functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique where weather or natural events reflect the emotional or moral state of characters — the storm mirrors the political turmoil Prospero seeks to resolve.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>, reflecting the political chaos caused by Antonio's usurpation of Prospero's dukedom twelve years earlier.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who creates the storm, and what does it reveal about Prospero's power?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The storm is not natural — it is created by Prospero's magic, performed by Ariel. No one is actually harmed. This reveals Prospero's immense power and his ability to manipulate others through illusion, establishing the theme of art and control.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Prospero's Exposition (Act 1, Scene 2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Act 1, Scene 2 is the longest scene in the play and serves as the <dfn class="term" data-def="Background information about characters and events that happened before the play begins, delivered through dialogue.">exposition</dfn>. Prospero tells Miranda their history: he was the rightful Duke of Milan, but his brother Antonio, "in my trust," conspired with Alonso, King of Naples, to overthrow him. Prospero and the infant Miranda were set adrift in a "rotten carcass of a butt" — a barely seaworthy boat — and left to die.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Prospero's account reveals his own flaw: he neglected his ducal duties to study "the liberal arts" and magic, giving Antonio the opportunity to seize power. The phrase "my library / Was dukedom large enough" suggests Prospero valued intellectual power over political responsibility. This self-awareness is important — he is not a simple victim but a character who contributed to his own overthrow.</p>

She asks questions so Prospero can explain the backstory. However, she is also presented as deeply empathetic: "O, I have suffered / With those that I saw suffer!" Her compassion for the shipwrecked sailors establishes her as the play's moral heart — she represents the natural goodness that Prospero's control sometimes lacks.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ariel and the Bond of Servitude</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A spirit of air who serves Prospero in exchange for the promise of freedom — Ariel was trapped in a tree by the witch Sycorax.">Ariel</dfn> is an airy spirit who serves Prospero. Before Prospero arrived, Ariel was imprisoned in a "cloven pine" by the witch Sycorax for refusing to carry out her "earthy and abhorred commands." Prospero freed Ariel but bound the spirit to serve him in return. When Ariel requests freedom — "Is there more toil?" — Prospero responds with anger and threats, reminding Ariel of the imprisonment and asking "Dost thou forget / From what a torment I did free thee?"</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This exchange is morally complex. Prospero freed Ariel from Sycorax's cruelty, but he has replaced one form of servitude with another. Ariel serves willingly but is not free. The promise of liberty — "After two days / I will discharge thee" — keeps Ariel obedient. A postcolonial reading sees Ariel as a colonised subject who cooperates with the coloniser in exchange for eventual freedom, contrasting with Caliban's open resistance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the contrast between Ariel and Caliban's responses to Prospero's authority.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ariel and Caliban represent two contrasting responses to Prospero's power: willing service versus rebellious resistance. Shakespeare uses this contrast to explore whether cooperation or defiance is the more effective response to authority.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Caliban's Introduction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Caliban enters cursing: "A southwest blow on ye / And blister you all o'er!" Prospero calls him "poisonous slave" and threatens him with cramps and pinches. Caliban's response reveals the history of their relationship: Prospero arrived, Caliban showed him "all the qualities o' th' isle," and in return Prospero enslaved him. Caliban's bitterest complaint is about language: "You taught me language, and my profit on't / Is I know how to curse."</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Yet Shakespeare complicates our sympathy for Caliban. Prospero states that Caliban attempted to "violate / The honour of my child" — an attempted assault on Miranda. This alleged crime is used to justify Caliban's subjugation. Whether we accept this justification depends on our interpretive lens: a Jacobean audience might see it as proof of Caliban's savagery; a modern audience might question whether the coloniser's narrative is trustworthy.</p>

She is described by Prospero as a "foul witch" whose "sorceries" were terrible. But we only hear Prospero's version. Caliban calls her his mother and claims the island through her. Shakespeare creates an interesting parallel: both Sycorax and Prospero are magicians who arrived on the island from elsewhere. The difference is that Prospero's story is the one we hear.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The storm disrupts social hierarchy and is created by Prospero's magic — establishing the theme of art as a tool of power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Ariel serves willingly in exchange for freedom; Caliban resists openly — two contrasting responses to colonial power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Prospero's account of his overthrow reveals his own flaw: he neglected political duty for intellectual pursuits, contributing to Antonio's betrayal.</li></ul>
