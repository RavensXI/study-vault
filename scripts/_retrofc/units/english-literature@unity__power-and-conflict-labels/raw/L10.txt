<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Poetry Comparison Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the English Literature exam, the poetry anthology section gives you <strong>one poem</strong> printed on the page and asks you to compare it with <strong>one other poem</strong> of your choice from the Power and Conflict anthology. The question is worth <strong>30 marks</strong> and you should spend approximately <strong>45 minutes</strong> on it, including planning time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The question will always focus on a theme or idea — for example: “How does the poet present the effects of conflict?” or “How is the power of nature presented?” You need to discuss <strong>both poems throughout</strong> your answer, comparing their methods and effects on the reader. This is not two separate essays — it is one <dfn class="term" data-def="An essay that discusses the similarities and differences between two texts throughout, rather than writing about each text separately.">comparative essay</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the three assessment objectives on this question and the marks each one carries.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 30 marks are split across three <dfn class="term" data-def="The skills and knowledge areas that examiners use to assess your work.">Assessment Objectives</dfn>: <strong>AO1</strong> (ideas and understanding) is worth 12 marks, <strong>AO2</strong> (analysis of methods — language, form, structure) is worth 12 marks, and <strong>AO3</strong> (context — when, why, and how the poem was written) is worth 6 marks. No marks are given for spelling and punctuation here, and comparison earns its marks inside AO1 and AO2 themselves.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Choosing Your Comparison Poem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The poem on the paper is chosen for you. Your job is to pick the best poem to compare it with. Choose a poem that shares the same <strong>theme</strong> but presents it <strong>differently</strong>. The strongest essays compare poems that have both similarities and differences — this gives you more to write about.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">For example, if the printed poem is <em>Remains</em> (the psychological effects of war), strong comparison choices include: <em>War Photographer</em> (both show trauma, but soldier vs. witness), <em>Bayonet Charge</em> (both show the reality of combat, but present vs. remembered experience), or <em>Kamikaze</em> (both show lasting consequences of war, but PTSD vs. social shame).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Power of Nature:</strong> <em>The Prelude</em>, <em>Storm on the Island</em>, <em>Exposure</em>, <em>Ozymandias</em>. Nature overwhelms human confidence or outlasts human power.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Effects of Conflict / Psychological Damage:</strong> <em>Remains</em>, <em>War Photographer</em>, <em>Bayonet Charge</em>, <em>Exposure</em>, <em>Poppies</em>. Trauma, guilt, fear, and the lasting impact on soldiers, witnesses, and families.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Power of Humans / Abuse of Power:</strong> <em>Ozymandias</em>, <em>My Last Duchess</em>, <em>Checking Out Me History</em>, <em>London</em>. How individuals or institutions control, oppress, or display power.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Identity and Belonging:</strong> <em>Kamikaze</em>, <em>The Emigrée</em>, <em>Checking Out Me History</em>, <em>Tissue</em>. How identity is shaped, threatened, or reclaimed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Loss and Absence:</strong> <em>Poppies</em>, <em>Kamikaze</em>, <em>War Photographer</em>, <em>The Emigrée</em>. The pain of separation, whether through death, duty, or exile.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Planning Under Time Pressure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Spend <strong>5 minutes planning</strong> before you start writing. A good plan saves time because you know exactly what each paragraph will cover. Use a simple grid:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this box and recreate the planning grid structure: question theme at top, two poem columns, arrows between quotes for paragraphs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Planning grid:</strong> Write the question theme at the top. Below, create two columns (Poem 1 and Poem 2). For each poem, note 3–4 short quotations with the method used (e.g. “metaphor,” “personification”). Then draw arrows between quotes that connect — these become your comparison points. Each arrow is one paragraph.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The SEAS Paragraph Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Each paragraph should follow the <dfn class="term" data-def="A paragraph structure for poetry comparison: Statement, Example, Analysis, Speculation/comparison.">SEAS</dfn> method to hit all three assessment objectives:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>S — Statement:</strong> Make a clear point about how one poem presents the theme. Link this directly to the question. For example: “Armitage presents the effects of conflict as a haunting, inescapable memory that replays in the speaker’s mind.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>E — Example:</strong> Support your statement with a short, relevant quotation. Keep quotes brief — 3–6 words is ideal. For example: “The speaker sees ‘every round as it rips through his life.’”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>A — Analysis:</strong> Identify the method (metaphor, verb, personification) and explain its effect on the reader. Use analytical language: ‘suggests,’ ‘connotes,’ ‘implies,’ ‘reflects.’ For example: “The violent verb ‘rips’ has connotations of destruction and conveys the fragility of human life.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>S — Speculation/Comparison:</strong> Compare with the second poem. Use comparative connectives and explain how the second poet presents the same idea similarly or differently. For example: “Similarly, Duffy presents the photographer as haunted by ‘a half-formed ghost’ — the metaphor suggests the memories are incomplete yet inescapable, much like the soldier’s flashbacks in Remains.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">

    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Difference:</strong> In contrast, Contrastingly, However, While [Poet A]… [Poet B]…, On the other hand, Unlike [Poet A], [Poet B]…</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Development:</strong> Furthermore, Additionally, Moreover, Building on this idea, This is further developed in [Poem B] where…</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Worked Example: Comparing Remains and War Photographer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Question:</strong> “How does the poet present the effects of conflict in Remains? Compare this to one other poem from Power and Conflict.”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Model Paragraph</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Armitage presents the effect of conflict as a haunting and troubling memory that becomes inescapable for the speaker. When describing the moment of violence, the speaker states he sees “every round as it rips through his life.” The violent verb ‘rips’ has connotations of destruction and fragility, suggesting the man’s life was torn apart as easily as paper. This violent image stays with the soldier even when he is “home on leave” — the memory is so powerful that “the drink and the drugs won’t flush him out,” showing that no substance can remove the trauma embedded in his brain. The metaphor of the dead man being “dug in behind enemy lines” turns the speaker’s own mind into a battlefield, suggesting his mental health is under siege.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Similarly, Duffy presents the war photographer as deeply traumatised by what he has witnessed, even when he is safely back in “Rural England.” His hands “did not tremble then though seem to now” — the verb ‘tremble’ connotes fear and fragility, showing the delayed effect of trauma. Like the soldier in <em>Remains</em>, the photographer was able to function in the war zone but falls apart at home. However, while the soldier in <em>Remains</em> is a direct participant who pulled the trigger, the photographer is a witness whose guilt comes from documenting suffering rather than preventing it. Duffy’s use of the accusatory pronoun ‘they’ in “they do not care” adds anger to his isolation — he is alienated not just by his memories but by a public that refuses to engage with the reality of conflict.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>1. Writing two separate essays:</strong> The biggest mistake is writing about Poem 1 for half the essay, then Poem 2 for the other half. You must compare <em>throughout</em> — every paragraph should reference both poems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>2. Feature-spotting without analysis:</strong> Don’t just say “the poet uses a metaphor.” Explain <em>what</em> the metaphor means, <em>why</em> the poet chose it, and <em>how</em> it affects the reader.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>3. Ignoring context:</strong> AO3 marks require you to link the poem to when and why it was written. For example, Armitage based <em>Remains</em> on real soldier testimonies; Duffy was friends with war photographers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>4. Long quotations:</strong> Keep quotes short (3–6 words). Embed them in your sentences rather than writing out whole lines. Short quotes show you can identify the most important words.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n36">The poetry anthology question (30 marks, 45 minutes) requires a comparative essay on a printed poem plus one of your choice — compare methods and effects throughout, never write two separate essays.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Use the SEAS structure (Statement, Example, Analysis, Speculation/Comparison) in every paragraph to cover ideas (AO1), methods (AO2) and context (AO3) together.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Know the key themes (Power of Nature, Effects of Conflict, Abuse of Power, Identity, Loss) and which poems pair well together so you can make a confident choice under exam pressure.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n34">In the exam, <strong>plan for 5 minutes, write for 35 minutes, check for 5 minutes.</strong> Aim for 3–4 SEAS paragraphs, each comparing both poems. Start each paragraph with a clear statement linked to the question. Use the poet’s surname (not first name): ‘Armitage presents’ not ‘Simon presents.’ Always “parrot the question” — repeat its key words in your answer.</p>
