--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Dharker explores the fragility of power through paper and Weir captures a mother’s grief for her soldier son.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Tissue — Imtiaz Dharker</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A poet born in Pakistan, raised in Glasgow, who lives in Britain and India, often exploring themes of identity, freedom, and the role of women.">Imtiaz Dharker</dfn> wrote <em>Tissue</em> as part of her 2006 collection <em>The Terrorist at My Table</em>. The poem uses paper as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A metaphor that runs throughout an entire poem, comparing one thing to another across multiple stanzas.">extended metaphor</dfn> for the fragility of human life, power, and the structures we build to control the world around us.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The poem opens with a clear statement: “Paper that lets the light shine through, this is what could alter things.” Light is a symbol of truth, hope, and transparency throughout the poem. Dharker suggests that if we let the light in — if we were more open and less obsessed with rigid structures — we could change the world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this box and list the 4 types of information families write in the Koran according to the poem: names, histories, and two other categories.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The poem references the <dfn class="term" data-def="The holy book of Islam, treated with great respect and reverence.">Koran</dfn>, where families write “names and histories, who was born to whom, the height and weight, who died where and how, on which sepia date.” Paper records the most important moments of human life — births, deaths, family connections — yet it is thin and fragile. This paradox is at the heart of the poem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Paper, Buildings, and Borders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Dharker imagines what would happen “if buildings were paper” — we would “feel their drift, see how easily they fall away on a sigh, a shift in the direction of the wind.” Buildings represent human power and permanence, but Dharker suggests they are less important than we think. The rhyme of ‘drift’ and ‘shift’ plays on the idea of movement and impermanence — even the most solid structures could be blown away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">“Maps too. The sun shines through their borderlines.” The short sentence “Maps too” is blunt and decisive. Borders and national boundaries are drawn on paper — they are human inventions, not natural laws. The sun shining through the “borderlines” suggests these divisions are arbitrary and could be dissolved by something as natural as light.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">“Fine slips from grocery shops” — even receipts control our lives. The simile “might fly our lives like paper kites” suggests that money and capitalism have the power to lift us up or carry us away, and that our reliance on material wealth is as fragile and temporary as a kite in the wind.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">In the final stanzas, Dharker shifts from paper to human beings. An “architect” could “place layer over layer, luminous script over numbers over line, and never wish to build again with brick or block.” The repetition of ‘over’ reinforces the idea of layers. The architect is a metaphor for anyone who creates — a poet, a parent, a society. Dharker prefers fragile, luminous structures to solid, permanent ones.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">“Let the daylight break through capitals and monoliths, through the shapes that pride can make.” ‘Capitals’ refers to government buildings; ‘monoliths’ to imposing stone structures. ‘Pride’ is criticised — human arrogance creates big, permanent buildings that are ultimately unimportant compared to human life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The poem culminates with “find a way to trace a grand design with living tissue, raise a structure never meant to last.” <dfn class="term" data-def="The biological material that makes up the human body — skin, flesh, and organs.">Tissue</dfn> now refers to human skin and flesh. The “grand design” is humanity itself — more complex and precious than any building, yet temporary. The final line stands alone: “turned into your skin.” The direct address (‘your’) makes this deeply personal — we are all made of tissue that will not last.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Form and Structure of Tissue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The poem is built from short stanzas of mostly four lines, with extensive <dfn class="term" data-def="When a line of poetry continues into the next line without punctuation, creating a flowing, continuous effect.">enjambment</dfn>. The stanzas are like thin layers of paper themselves — the form mirrors the content. The final stanza is just one line (“turned into your skin”), isolating it for impact. The lack of regular rhyme reflects the freedom and fluidity that Dharker advocates — she resists the rigid structures she criticises in the poem.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Poppies — Jane Weir</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Manchester-based poet known for exploring themes of loss, motherhood, and domestic life.">Jane Weir</dfn> wrote <em>Poppies</em> from the perspective of a mother whose son has left to join the army. The poem is deeply personal and emotional, tracing her grief through domestic imagery and sensory details. It is set around <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sunday nearest to 11 November, when the UK remembers those who died in wars.">Armistice Sunday</dfn>, connecting private grief to the nation’s collective remembrance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The poem opens: “Three days before Armistice Sunday and poppies had already been placed on individual war graves.” The word ‘individual’ is significant — each poppy represents a single life, a single family devastated by loss. The mother then describes preparing her son to leave: she smooths down his collar, resisting the urge to hold him back.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this tip and recall the 3 specific dressmaking terms Weir uses to describe the mother's stomach: tucks, darts, and one other.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Weir uses textile and sewing metaphors throughout: “my stomach busy making tucks, darts, pleats.” These are dressmaking terms that describe her anxiety in physical terms — her stomach is knotting itself like fabric being folded. As a textile designer herself, Weir draws on her own experience to make the mother’s grief tangible and physical.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Key Techniques in Poppies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The poem traces a chronological journey from the son’s departure to the mother’s solitary walk to a war memorial. At the memorial, she leans against it and listens, hoping to hear her son’s voice: “I listened, hoping to hear your playground voice catching on the wind.” The word ‘playground’ brings us back to childhood innocence, contrasting sharply with the reality of war.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Quotations and Their Effects</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">“The world overflowing like a treasure chest” — this simile shows the son’s excitement about leaving home. To him, the world is full of possibility. This contrasts painfully with the mother’s fear and sadness.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">“I was brave” — the mother uses the same word (‘brave’) typically associated with soldiers. She is fighting her own battle: the battle to let her son go without breaking down.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">“Released a song bird from its cage” — the metaphor compares her son to a bird she has set free. There is beauty in the image, but also loss — once released, the bird cannot be called back. The ‘cage’ represents the safety of home and childhood.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">“Sellotape bandaged around my hand” — the verb ‘bandaged’ links a domestic object to injury and war. Even small, everyday actions carry the weight of her grief. The poem blurs the boundary between the domestic world and the world of conflict.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Comparing Tissue and Poppies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Both poems deal with fragility and loss, but from very different angles. <em>Tissue</em> is philosophical and abstract — Dharker meditates on the fragility of all human structures, power, and life itself. <em>Poppies</em> is deeply personal and emotional — Weir focuses on one mother’s specific, tangible grief.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">In <em>Tissue</em>, fragility is presented positively: Dharker suggests we should embrace impermanence rather than cling to rigid power structures. In <em>Poppies</em>, fragility is painful: the mother’s world is fragile because her son’s life is at risk. Both poets use tactile imagery — Dharker’s pages “smoothed and stroked” and Weir’s sewing metaphors — but for opposite purposes: Dharker celebrates the delicacy of paper, while Weir uses domestic textures to express barely contained grief.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n29"><em>Tissue</em> uses paper as an extended metaphor for the fragility of human life and power — Dharker argues that impermanence should be embraced, not feared, and that human life is more ‘grand’ than any building.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30"><em>Poppies</em> presents a mother’s grief through domestic imagery, sewing metaphors, and a chronological journey from her son’s departure to a war memorial — the poem shows how conflict devastates families, not just soldiers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Both poems explore fragility, but Dharker sees it as liberating while Weir presents it as a source of profound pain and loss.</li></ul>
