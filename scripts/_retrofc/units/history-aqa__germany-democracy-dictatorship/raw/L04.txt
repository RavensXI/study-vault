--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reparations, the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation — three crises that nearly broke Weimar.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">The young Weimar Republic faced three connected post-war crises that almost destroyed it before it had finished writing its constitution. Reparations made Germany a debtor nation overnight. The 1923 Ruhr occupation stripped its industrial heartland. Hyperinflation in the same year wiped out savings and pensions. By the autumn of 1923 a loaf of bread cost 200 billion marks. Understanding why these crises happened &mdash; and who they hurt &mdash; is essential because right-wing parties, including the Nazis, would feed off this trauma for the next decade.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Treaty of Versailles, June 1919</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Germany was not invited to negotiate. Allied delegates handed German representatives a 440-article treaty in May 1919 with no real opportunity to bargain. To Germans this made it a <dfn class="term" data-def="German term meaning 'dictated peace' &mdash; the popular description of the Treaty of Versailles because Germany was given no chance to negotiate.">diktat</dfn>, a dictated peace. The terms were severe: Germany lost Alsace-Lorraine to France, the Saar coalfield to Allied control, parts of Posen to Poland, and all of its overseas colonies. The army was capped at 100,000 men, conscription was banned, the Rhineland was demilitarised, and tanks, submarines and an air force were forbidden.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The two clauses Germans hated most were Article 231, the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, in which Germany accepted moral responsibility for the war; legally vague but politically explosive in Weimar Germany.">war guilt clause</dfn>&rsquo; making Germany accept responsibility for starting the war, and the open-ended commitment to <dfn class="term" data-def="Compensation payments imposed by the Treaty of Versailles, fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks (about &pound;6.6 billion).">reparations</dfn>. The exact figure was set by an Allied commission in 1921: 132 billion gold marks, payable over 42 years.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four categories of Treaty of Versailles term and one example from each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Treaty of Versailles imposed territorial losses (Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor, all colonies), military restrictions (army capped at 100,000; no air force; demilitarised Rhineland), financial costs (132 billion gold marks reparations from 1921), and moral burden (Article 231 war guilt). Together these gave the political right a permanent grievance against Weimar democracy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Stab in the Back Myth and the November Criminals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The new Republic had to sign Versailles or face renewed Allied invasion. Foreign Minister Hermann M&uuml;ller signed the treaty on 28 June 1919. Right-wing parties immediately branded the SPD-led government the &lsquo;November Criminals&rsquo; for accepting the armistice and Versailles. The Dolchstosslegende &mdash; the legend that the army had been &lsquo;stabbed in the back&rsquo; by socialists, Jews and democrats &mdash; spread quickly. It mattered because it transferred the blame for losing the war from the generals (where it belonged) to the politicians who had to pick up the pieces.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In August 1921 right-wing assassins murdered Matthias Erzberger, the Centre Party politician who had signed the armistice. In June 1922 they killed Walter Rathenau, the Jewish Foreign Minister who had pursued reconciliation with the Allies. Political violence was running ahead of any agreed national strategy for paying reparations.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Economists then and now disagree. Germany was the largest economy in continental Europe in 1919 even after war losses. The bill of 132 billion gold marks was painful but not impossible &mdash; spread across forty-two years it was around 6&ndash;7% of national income, broadly comparable to French reparations after 1871. The deeper problem was political: paying meant accepting Versailles, raising taxes on the rich, and losing voters. So instead Weimar governments printed money to cover the gap.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Ruhr Occupation, January 1923</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Germany missed reparations payments in late 1922. France's Prime Minister Raymond Poincar&eacute; chose enforcement over renegotiation. On 11 January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr valley &mdash; Germany's industrial heartland, producing 80% of its coal and steel. The plan was to seize coal, coke and timber by force.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno's government called for <dfn class="term" data-def="Weimar Germany's response to the 1923 Ruhr occupation: workers were paid by Berlin to refuse to mine coal or run trains for the French and Belgians.">passive resistance</dfn>. Workers went on strike. Trains stopped. Mines lay idle. Berlin paid the strikers from central government funds. There was sporadic sabotage; 132 Germans were killed in clashes with French troops. The occupation lasted until late 1923 and cost both sides far more than it produced &mdash; the French got little coal; the Germans lost industrial output and tax revenue.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Hyperinflation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">To pay strikers without crushing the rest of the economy, the Reichsbank simply printed paper marks. Inflation already running since 1919 became a runaway collapse. <dfn class="term" data-def="An extreme rise in prices &mdash; in Germany 1923, prices doubled every few days at the peak.">Hyperinflation</dfn> peaked in November 1923. A loaf of bread that cost 250 marks in January 1923 cost 200 billion marks by November. Workers were paid twice a day. Restaurants printed menus in pencil so prices could be updated. The dollar bought 4.2 trillion marks at the peak.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Who Lost and Who Gained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Hyperinflation did not hurt all Germans equally &mdash; this nuance is what examiners want to see. <strong>Losers</strong>: pensioners on fixed incomes saw savings vanish; war widows lost government allowances; small savers lost everything in the banks; landlords on long leases were ruined. The middle-class Mittelstand &mdash; clerks, civil servants, teachers &mdash; lost most because their identity rested on respectable savings. <strong>Winners</strong>: anyone with debts (mortgages, business loans) saw them effectively cancelled; large industrialists like Hugo Stinnes bought up smaller firms cheaply; farmers with land kept producing food and barter; manual workers in employment generally kept their jobs. The middle class lost both their savings and their faith in the Republic. Many would later vote Nazi.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Crisis Ends</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In August 1923 Gustav Stresemann became Chancellor. He called off passive resistance in late September, accepting that the Ruhr struggle could not be won. In November he introduced a temporary new currency, the Rentenmark, backed by a mortgage on land and industry. One Rentenmark equalled one trillion old paper marks. The currency stabilised because Germans, exhausted, agreed to trust it. Reparations were renegotiated through the 1924 Dawes Plan.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The political damage stayed. The middle class never forgot 1923. The far right had its grievance machine fully assembled. And Adolf Hitler &mdash; whose Munich Putsch attempt on 8&ndash;9 November 1923 almost coincided with the inflation peak &mdash; had received his first national publicity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why hyperinflation was politically more damaging than economically catastrophic.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hyperinflation was contained within a year by the Rentenmark, but the damage to middle-class trust in Weimar democracy lasted a decade. Voters who lost savings in 1923 carried that grievance into the Depression elections of 1930&ndash;33.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Treaty of Versailles imposed territorial, military, financial and moral costs on Weimar Germany &mdash; reparations were fixed at 132 billion gold marks in 1921 and Article 231's war-guilt clause gave the right a permanent grievance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Ruhr occupation of January 1923 and the government's policy of passive resistance combined with reckless money-printing to trigger hyperinflation peaking in November 1923.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Hyperinflation hurt different Germans differently: savers, pensioners and the middle class were ruined; debtors and large industrialists often gained &mdash; and middle-class anger fed into later support for the Nazis.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">Examiners punish students who claim hyperinflation hurt all Germans equally &mdash; you must show who lost (savers, pensioners, the middle class) and who gained (debtors, industrialists, sometimes manual workers in jobs). For the 8-mark Evaluate an Interpretation question, watch for sources that simplify the Treaty of Versailles as either the cause of all later problems (Nazi argument) or a peace Germany could have absorbed (some modern historians) &mdash; you need to weigh both. Lock in two date pairs: 1919 (Treaty signed) / 1921 (132 billion gold marks fixed), and Jan 1923 (Ruhr occupied) / Nov 1923 (Rentenmark issued). Precision lifts answers from generic to substantiated.</p>
