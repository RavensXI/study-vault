--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Three Ks for women, the Hitler Youth pledge and a fight to control the pulpit.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">The Nazi state did not stop at controlling parliament, the police and the economy. It set out to remake the German family, the German classroom and the German pulpit. Women, young people and churches were each redirected towards Volksgemeinschaft &mdash; the racial national community. This lesson tracks how policy targeted each group, what it achieved, and where it ran into resistance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Women: Kinder, Kuche, Kirche</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Nazi ideology assigned women a single primary role: bearing and raising racially-pure German children. The slogan was <dfn class="term" data-def="Nazi slogan &lsquo;children, kitchen, church&rsquo; defining the proper role of German women under the regime; promoted through marriage loans, the Mother's Cross and exclusion from the professions.">Kinder, Kuche, Kirche</dfn> &mdash; children, kitchen, church. The 1933 Law for the Encouragement of Marriage offered interest-free loans of 1,000 marks to newly-wed couples on condition that the wife left work; one quarter of the loan was written off for each child born. Around 800,000 loans were taken up by 1937.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Birth-rate incentives followed. The Mother's Cross was instituted in 1938: bronze for four children, silver for six, gold for eight. Women in &lsquo;racially-valuable&rsquo; categories were honoured publicly. The <dfn class="term" data-def="SS-run network of maternity homes from 1936 where racially-approved unmarried women could give birth in secret to children fathered by SS men; aimed to raise the &lsquo;Aryan&rsquo; birth rate.">Lebensborn</dfn> programme, run by Himmler from 1936, set up SS-controlled maternity homes where unmarried women deemed racially &lsquo;valuable&rsquo; could give birth secretly to children fathered by SS men. Women were squeezed out of the professions: by 1934 the number of female teachers, doctors and civil servants had been cut sharply. Married women were taxed more heavily on second incomes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write a one-line definition of each of the Three Ks &mdash; with one specific Nazi policy that promoted it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nazi ideology defined women's role as Kinder, Kuche, Kirche. The 1933 Marriage Loans, the 1938 Mother's Cross and Himmler's Lebensborn homes all targeted the birth rate. By 1939, however, war labour shortages had forced the regime to push women back into factories, contradicting its own propaganda.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Women: From Hearth to Wartime Factory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Three Ks ran into the iron rules of arithmetic. Rearmament from 1935 created a labour shortage that could not be filled by men alone. From 1937 women were quietly encouraged back into work; by 1939 there were more women in employment than in 1933. From 1939 the RAD (Reich Labour Service) was extended to women. By 1944 around 14 million German women worked. The Nazi message remained traditional but the practice was contradictory: women were both honoured as mothers and conscripted as workers.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">It depends which woman. Working-class women already in factories saw little material change. Professional women lost most: female lawyers, judges and university teachers were excluded under early laws. Married rural women may have welcomed marriage loans and child subsidies. Jewish women lost their citizenship rights under the 1935 Nuremberg Laws and were excluded from welfare entirely. Women in the BDM gained subsidised travel, sport and a peer group, but lost the chance to enter male-dominated professions. Avoid the blanket statement &lsquo;Nazi policy banned women from work&rsquo; &mdash; the picture is uneven and changes between 1933 and 1944.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Hitler Youth and the BDM</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Nazi youth policy aimed to capture every German child between 10 and 18. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Hitler Youth &mdash; main Nazi youth organisation for boys aged 14&ndash;18 (Jungvolk for younger boys); compulsory by stages from 1936, with paramilitary training and ideological indoctrination.">Hitler Youth (HJ)</dfn> took boys from 14 to 18; the Jungvolk took boys from 10 to 14; the <dfn class="term" data-def="League of German Maidens &mdash; the female branch of the Hitler Youth from 1930; took girls 14&ndash;18, with sport, camping and a curriculum of motherhood and racial purity.">BDM</dfn> (Bund Deutscher Madel) took girls from 14 to 18, with the Jungmadel for younger girls. Membership grew from 100,000 in 1933 to 8.5 million by 1939.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Activity for boys was paramilitary: marching, weapons handling, map-reading, camping. Activity for girls was domestic and physical: gymnastics, cooking, child-care, racial-purity instruction. All members swore an oath of personal loyalty to Hitler. The 1936 Hitler Youth Law made the HJ the only legal youth organisation. The 1939 Youth Service Law made membership compulsory for all German children of relevant age. Older youth groups &mdash; Catholic, Protestant, scout &mdash; were disbanded or absorbed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Education and the Curriculum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Schools were brought into line under Bernhard Rust as Reich Education Minister from 1934. Textbooks were rewritten. Maths problems calculated the cost of disabled people to the state or the trajectory of artillery shells. Biology became <dfn class="term" data-def="Pseudo-scientific Nazi biology curriculum teaching Aryan racial superiority, measurement of skulls and the supposed dangers of mixed marriages; compulsory in German schools from 1935.">racial hygiene</dfn>: skull measurement, Aryan superiority, the dangers of mixed marriage. PE rose to fifteen periods a week to build soldiers and mothers. Jewish teachers and pupils were excluded under the 1933 Civil Service Law and the 1938 expulsion of Jewish children. Classroom prayers were replaced with Nazi morning songs in many schools.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For elite training, three school networks were set up. Napola schools (National Political Educational Institutions) trained future SS officers and civil servants. Adolf Hitler Schools, controlled by the Hitler Youth, trained party administrators. Ordensburgen castles trained the senior cadre of the future Reich. Universities were also brought into line: 15% of teaching staff were dismissed by 1934, and student membership of Nazi organisations became effectively compulsory.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Most German children passed through HJ or BDM and a Nazified curriculum, but reception was uneven. Younger children absorbed the propaganda most thoroughly &mdash; they had no comparison. Older boys often complained that the HJ was repetitive and bullying; the Edelweiss Pirates and Swing Youth (covered in lesson 14) were partly a response. Catholic and Confessional Church families resisted in pockets. After the war, oral histories collected by historians like Detlev Peukert showed that hostility to the regime grew especially after 1942 as casualty figures rose. Indoctrination was wide but not always deep.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Churches: The Concordat and the Reich Church</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Hitler had to manage Germany's two main Christian denominations: roughly 22 million Catholics and 40 million Protestants. With the Catholic Church he signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Treaty of 20 July 1933 between the Vatican and the Nazi government in which the Church accepted political non-interference in exchange for the regime's promise to protect Catholic schools and lay associations; quickly broken by the Nazis.">Concordat</dfn> on 20 July 1933 &mdash; the Vatican accepted political non-interference in exchange for protection of Catholic schools and lay organisations. The deal gave Hitler international respectability and removed the Centre Party as a political force. Within months the regime had begun violating its terms by attacking Catholic youth groups and lay associations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For Protestants, the regime created the Reich Church under Ludwig Muller in 1933. The Reich Church merged regional Lutheran churches under Nazi influence, removed the Old Testament from teaching where possible (because of its Jewish origins), and tried to introduce a Nazified version of Christianity. Crosses were replaced with swastikas in some Reich Church services.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The Confessional Church and Resistance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A breakaway group of Protestant pastors refused to accept the Reich Church. They formed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Breakaway Protestant church formed in 1934 under Niemoller and Bonhoeffer's Barmen Declaration; refused to accept the Reich Church and Nazi racial doctrine; thousands of pastors imprisoned, Bonhoeffer executed in 1945.">Confessional Church</dfn> in 1934, led initially by Martin Niemoller. The Barmen Declaration of May 1934 rejected the Nazi claim that the church should serve the state. Roughly 6,000 pastors signed up. Niemoller was arrested in 1937 and held in concentration camps until 1945. Dietrich Bonhoeffer joined the resistance and was executed in April 1945. Pope Pius XI's encyclical Mit brennender Sorge of March 1937 condemned Nazi racial ideology &mdash; smuggled into Germany and read from Catholic pulpits on Palm Sunday.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">By 1939 the regime had not destroyed the churches but had not converted them either. Most Germans went on attending Sunday services. The state had infiltrated the leadership but failed to win the pews.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down four key dates from this lesson with the policy each launched: 1933, 1934, 1935 (Mother's Cross), 1936 (HJ Law).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Concordat (July 1933) bought Catholic neutrality but was quickly broken. The Reich Church (1933) failed to absorb the Confessional Church (1934). The Hitler Youth Law (1936) made the HJ the only legal youth movement. The Youth Service Law (1939) made membership compulsory. Each step extended Nazi control of social life, but resistance from the churches and the white-flight of older youth showed it never reached every German home.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Women were assigned the Three Ks (Kinder, Kuche, Kirche) and rewarded for childbearing through Marriage Loans (1933), the Mother's Cross (1938) and Lebensborn (1936) &mdash; but rearmament forced them back into factories from 1937.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Hitler Youth and BDM membership rose from 100,000 (1933) to 8.5 million (1939); the curriculum was rewritten around racial hygiene, military training for boys and motherhood for girls.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">The Concordat with Rome (1933) and the Reich Church experiment failed to silence opposition: the Confessional Church, Niemoller and Bonhoeffer maintained genuine resistance through the Third Reich.</li>
</ul>
