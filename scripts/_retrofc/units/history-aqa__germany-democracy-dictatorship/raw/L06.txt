--- DESCRIPTION ---
New currency, Dawes and Young Plans, Locarno — were the Golden Years built on borrowed time?

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">From the wreckage of November 1923 a different Germany emerged. By 1928 unemployment was below 1.4 million, foreign investment was flooding in, and Berlin was the cultural capital of Europe. Historians call the years 1924&ndash;1929 the &lsquo;Golden Years&rsquo; or &lsquo;Stresemann era&rsquo; after the Foreign Minister who steered the recovery. But how golden were they? The German historian Detlev Peukert argued Weimar was &lsquo;dancing on a volcano&rsquo; &mdash; that the recovery was real but built on US loans that could be recalled at any moment. The 1929 Wall Street Crash would prove him right.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Stresemann: Pragmatist, Not Saviour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Gustav <dfn class="term" data-def="German politician (1878&ndash;1929); Chancellor for 100 days in 1923, then Foreign Minister 1923&ndash;29; chief architect of Weimar's economic and diplomatic recovery.">Stresemann</dfn> became Chancellor in August 1923 at the height of hyperinflation. His chancellorship lasted only 100 days, but it was the most decisive 100 days in Weimar history. He called off passive resistance in the Ruhr in late September 1923, accepting that the strike could not be won. He then introduced the temporary new currency, the <dfn class="term" data-def="Temporary German currency introduced by Stresemann in November 1923, backed by a mortgage on land and industry; replaced the worthless paper mark and ended hyperinflation.">Rentenmark</dfn>, in November 1923. One Rentenmark exchanged for one trillion old paper marks. The new currency was backed by a mortgage on land and industrial property, and Germans agreed to trust it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In August 1924 the Rentenmark was replaced by the permanent Reichsmark, backed partly by gold reserves. Stresemann himself was pushed out as Chancellor in November 1923, but he then served as Foreign Minister in every government from 1923 until his death in 1929 &mdash; six years of continuity in a system famous for its instability.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the order of currency reforms: which currency replaced what, and when.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Rentenmark replaced the worthless paper mark in November 1923 (1 Rentenmark = 1 trillion old marks). It was itself replaced by the permanent Reichsmark in August 1924, ending hyperinflation. Stresemann then served as Foreign Minister from 1923 until his death in 1929.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Dawes Plan, 1924</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Reparations remained the political wound that would not close. Stresemann's strategy was to admit Germany could not refuse them but argue it could not pay them at the 1921 schedule. American banker Charles Dawes was invited to chair an international commission. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Agreement of 1924 chaired by US banker Charles Dawes that restructured German reparations payments and provided 800 million gold marks in US loans to stabilise the German economy.">Dawes Plan</dfn> of August 1924 set graduated payments &mdash; smaller in early years, rising as Germany recovered &mdash; and crucially provided 800 million gold marks in American loans to kick-start the economy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Over the next five years roughly 25 billion marks of US capital flowed into Germany. Industry rebuilt. Berlin's U-Bahn extended. New housing replaced wartime slums. Industrial output passed 1913 levels by 1928. By 1928 Germany was paying reparations broadly out of US loans &mdash; a circular flow that worked only as long as American money kept arriving.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Locarno, the League and the Young Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Stresemann's foreign policy aim was to ease Germany back into the European system as a respected partner, not a defeated outcast. In October 1925 he signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Treaties signed in October 1925 in which Germany voluntarily accepted its western borders with France and Belgium; signed by Stresemann and welcomed across Europe as marking reconciliation.">Locarno Treaties</dfn>, in which Germany voluntarily accepted its western borders with France and Belgium. Crucially the treaties left the eastern borders &mdash; with Poland and Czechoslovakia &mdash; open to future revision. To Germans this hinted that Versailles could be peacefully chipped at.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In September 1926 Germany was admitted to the League of Nations as a permanent member of its Council, equal in rank with Britain, France, Italy and Japan. In 1928 Germany signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact renouncing war as an instrument of policy &mdash; alongside fifteen other major powers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Young Plan of 1929, named after another American banker (Owen Young), reduced reparations from 132 billion gold marks to 112 billion and stretched payments to 1988. The political right hated it because it accepted reparations existed at all; right-wing parties including the small Nazi Party joined a referendum campaign against the plan in 1929. Their campaign lost &mdash; but it gave Hitler national exposure and respectable allies for the first time.</p>

Memorise the pair as Dawes Restructure, Young Reduce.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Weimar Culture: Bauhaus, Brecht and Berlin Cabaret</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">As Germany stabilised politically, an extraordinary cultural flowering followed. Walter Gropius founded the <dfn class="term" data-def="German design and architecture school founded by Walter Gropius in 1919; influential modernist movement combining functionality with art; closed by the Nazis in 1933.">Bauhaus</dfn> design school in 1919 &mdash; its modernist buildings, furniture and graphic design defined the era. Bertolt Brecht's plays (<em>The Threepenny Opera</em>, 1928) reinvented theatre. Berlin's cabarets, immortalised by Christopher Isherwood and later by the musical <em>Cabaret</em>, blended jazz, satire and sexual freedom unprecedented in European cities. The German film industry rivalled Hollywood: Fritz Lang's <em>Metropolis</em> (1927) and the first synchronised-sound film <em>The Blue Angel</em> (1930) were international hits.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For modernists this was liberation. For conservatives, particularly in rural Germany and the Catholic south, it was a moral collapse &mdash; what the Nazis later called &lsquo;cultural Bolshevism&rsquo;. Even some Weimar supporters worried that Berlin's cabarets and the Bauhaus's flat-roofed houses had little in common with how most Germans actually lived.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Limits of the Recovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For all the headlines, the &lsquo;Golden Years&rsquo; were patchy. Unemployment never fell below 1.3 million. Agriculture was already in depression by 1927 as world grain prices collapsed; small farmers struggled with debts. The recovery depended on US loans that could be recalled at three months&rsquo; notice. Coalition governments remained short-lived: there were six Chancellors between 1923 and 1929. President Friedrich Ebert died in 1925 and was replaced by 78-year-old Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg &mdash; a war hero of the right who privately disliked the Republic he was now meant to defend.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Optimists like the historian Eberhard Kolb point to genuine industrial recovery, peace with neighbours, and democratic stability. Pessimists like Detlev Peukert argue the recovery was hollow &mdash; rural depression, dependence on US loans, an ageing right-wing President. The truth is mixed: Weimar genuinely recovered, but its recovery sat on foundations only as solid as the New York stock market. When Stresemann died on 3 October 1929, weeks before the Wall Street Crash, even contemporaries felt his death marked the end of the era. The streets of Berlin filled with mourners. Within five months of Wall Street's collapse, German unemployment doubled.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why historians disagree about whether 1924&ndash;1929 was a real recovery or a 'dance on a volcano'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stresemann's recovery rested on three pillars: a stable currency, US loans through the Dawes Plan, and diplomatic reintegration through Locarno and the League. All three were genuine achievements &mdash; but the second was beyond German control, which is why the 1929 Wall Street Crash hit Germany harder than any other major economy.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Stresemann's Rentenmark of November 1923 ended hyperinflation; the Dawes Plan of 1924 restructured reparations and brought US loans worth roughly 25 billion marks over five years.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Locarno (1925), League membership (1926) and the Young Plan (1929) reintegrated Germany into European diplomacy &mdash; the right hated all three but the campaign against them gave Hitler new exposure.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Weimar culture flourished &mdash; Bauhaus, Brecht, Berlin cabaret &mdash; but the recovery was uneven (rural depression, persistent unemployment) and dependent on US loans that could be recalled at three months&rsquo; notice.</li>
</ul>
