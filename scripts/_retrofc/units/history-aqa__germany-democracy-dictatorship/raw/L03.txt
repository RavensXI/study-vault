--- DESCRIPTION ---
How four years of war turned a confident empire into a starving, mutinous, broken state.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In August 1914 millions of Germans cheered the outbreak of war. By November 1918 sailors were mutinying, workers were striking and the Kaiser was on a train to Holland. Understanding how a confident empire collapsed in just over four years is essential because almost every problem of the Weimar Republic &mdash; reparations, the &lsquo;stab in the back&rsquo; myth, hyperinflation &mdash; began here.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Burgfrieden and the Mood of August 1914</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">When Wilhelm II declared war on Russia and France he announced that &lsquo;I no longer recognise parties; I recognise only Germans&rsquo;. The SPD agreed. On 4 August 1914 every party in the Reichstag, including the Social Democrats, voted for war credits. This political truce was called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The political truce of August 1914 in which all major parties, including the SPD, suspended their differences and supported the war effort.">Burgfrieden</dfn> &mdash; literally &lsquo;castle peace&rsquo; &mdash; meaning class and party conflict would be paused for the duration. Crowds in Berlin and Hamburg sang patriotic songs. For a few weeks Weltpolitik appeared to have done its job.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The unity rested on a short-war assumption. Generals expected Paris to fall in six weeks (the Schlieffen Plan). Workers expected a Christmas victory. When the war stalemated in muddy trenches that autumn, those expectations curdled. By 1916 fewer than half of military-age men remained outside uniform.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why the Burgfrieden of 1914 was so important politically and why it could not survive a long war.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Burgfrieden of August 1914 united the Reichstag &mdash; including the SPD &mdash; behind the war effort. It depended on the assumption of a short victory and broke down once stalemate, hunger and casualties exposed the gap between elite war aims and working-class suffering.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Allied Blockade and the Turnip Winter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Royal Navy began blockading German ports in August 1914. Germany imported about a third of its food before the war &mdash; fertiliser from Chile, grain from Russia, dairy from Denmark. The blockade cut all of that. Inside Germany, the army requisitioned horses, men and machinery away from farms. By 1916 food rations had been cut to roughly 1,000 calories a day for civilians, well below subsistence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In the autumn of 1916 the potato crop failed. Civilians fell back on the only thing left: turnips, normally fed to livestock. The winter of 1916&ndash;17 became known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The German winter of 1916&ndash;17 in which the potato crop failed and civilians survived largely on turnips because the Allied blockade cut imports.">turnip winter</dfn>. Around 750,000 German civilians died of hunger or disease linked to malnutrition over the course of the war. Birth rates collapsed. Mothers queued for hours for bread.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The blockade was not just a foreign-policy tool. By starving urban Germany it broke the Burgfrieden from below. Workers struck for food in Berlin in April 1917 and again in January 1918, when over a million workers downed tools across major cities. The same workers had cheered for war in 1914. Their anger by 1917 was at the regime that had failed to feed them, not just at Britain or France.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">War Weariness and Political Splits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">By 1917 the SPD itself split. A breakaway party, the Independent SPD (USPD), refused further war credits. From the USPD's far-left fringe came the Spartacus League, led by Karl Liebknecht and Rosa Luxemburg, calling for revolution. Strikes spread. Soldiers home on leave reported that civilians on trains and in queues now openly criticised the war. Inflation made things worse: by 1918 the mark had lost half its 1914 value.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">From 1916 the army effectively ran the country. Field Marshal Paul von <dfn class="term" data-def="German general who became Chief of the General Staff in 1916; with Ludendorff exercised effective dictatorship over Germany during the second half of the First World War.">Hindenburg</dfn> was Chief of the General Staff with Erich Ludendorff as his deputy. They imposed a &lsquo;silent dictatorship&rsquo;, directing factories, food rationing and even Reichstag debates by sheer force of military prestige. Civilian Chancellors became figureheads.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Spring 1918 Offensive and Defeat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Russia's collapse in 1917 freed German divisions for one final gamble. In March 1918 Ludendorff launched a massive offensive on the Western Front. It made the largest gains of the war &mdash; but stalled forty miles from Paris when American troops arrived in numbers. From August 1918 the Allies counter-attacked. By late September the German army was in retreat. Ludendorff lost his nerve and told the Kaiser the war was lost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Crucially, this conversation happened privately. The German army was still on French and Belgian soil. To civilians, defeat seemed sudden and unexplained. This gap &mdash; between an army that had not been beaten in battle on home ground and a government suddenly suing for peace &mdash; would later be exploited by the right-wing &lsquo;stab in the back&rsquo; myth.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Ludendorff insisted that civilian politicians, not generals, sign the armistice. He wanted the army's reputation untouched and the blame placed elsewhere. In late October a new civilian government under Prince Max of Baden took office, and on 9 November the SPD's Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a republic from a Reichstag balcony. The same day the Kaiser fled across the border into the neutral Netherlands. Within forty-eight hours the SPD's Friedrich Ebert had signed the armistice on 11 November 1918.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Kiel, Revolution and the Republic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The trigger was the navy. In late October 1918 admirals at Kiel ordered the High Seas Fleet to sea for one last battle. Sailors refused. The mutiny spread along the coast. Workers&rsquo; and soldiers&rsquo; councils, modelled on Russia's soviets, sprang up in Hamburg, Munich and Berlin. The empire collapsed in less than a week. Wilhelm II, abandoned by his army, abdicated on 9 November after his generals told him the troops would no longer fight for him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">In four years Germany had gone from cheering crowds to revolutionary councils, from confident empire to mutinous republic. Every problem the new democracy would face &mdash; food shortages, returning soldiers, the &lsquo;stab in the back&rsquo; lie &mdash; had its roots in this collapse.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: what was the gap between the army's actual position in November 1918 and what civilians saw, and why did that matter for Weimar politics?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>When the armistice was signed on 11 November 1918 the German army was still on French and Belgian soil &mdash; retreating, but not crushed. The hidden truth that Ludendorff had told the Kaiser the war was lost in late September made the right-wing &lsquo;stab in the back&rsquo; myth possible, blaming civilian politicians for a military collapse the generals had concealed.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Burgfrieden of August 1914 united Germans behind the war but broke down as stalemate, the Allied blockade and the 1916&ndash;17 turnip winter exposed the gap between elites and workers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The army's silent dictatorship under Hindenburg and Ludendorff from 1916 sidelined civilian politicians, but Ludendorff's 1918 retreat forced a sudden civilian armistice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The Kiel mutiny and the November 1918 revolution forced Wilhelm II's abdication and the proclamation of the republic &mdash; an end shaped by hunger and exhaustion as much as by military defeat.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Be precise about <em>how</em> Germany lost the war. The army was not crushed on home soil &mdash; it was retreating in France and Belgium when the generals told politicians to seek peace. This is what made the &lsquo;stab in the back&rsquo; myth possible. For an 8-mark Explain Effects question on the war's impact, link the blockade and turnip winter to working-class strikes, then to the SPD split, then to the Kiel mutiny &mdash; not as a list but as a chain of cause and effect. For 12-mark essays on the end of the monarchy, weigh military defeat against civilian collapse rather than treating them as separate factors.</p>
