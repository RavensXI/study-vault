--- DESCRIPTION ---
Spartacists, Kapp Putsch and the Munich Putsch — Weimar was attacked from both left and right.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Between January 1919 and November 1923 the new Weimar Republic faced four serious attempts to overthrow it. Two came from the far left, two from the far right. None succeeded, but each exposed weaknesses in the new democracy and shaped the political mood that would later carry Hitler to power. Examiners regularly flag that students confuse these uprisings &mdash; particularly the Spartacist rising and the Kapp Putsch &mdash; so chronological precision matters here.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Weimar Constitution and Its Hidden Faults</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In August 1919 a new constitution was signed in the small town of Weimar (chosen because Berlin was still volatile). It was, on paper, one of the most democratic in Europe. All adults aged 20 or over could vote, including women. There was a Bill of Rights guaranteeing free speech, religion and assembly. The President was elected directly. The Chancellor and his cabinet were responsible to the Reichstag.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The constitution had two structural weaknesses that examiners want students to spot. First, <dfn class="term" data-def="An electoral system in which seats in parliament are awarded in proportion to the share of the vote each party receives.">proportional representation</dfn> meant that even tiny parties won Reichstag seats. Coalition governments became fragile. Between 1919 and 1933 Germany had twenty-one different cabinets, averaging seven months each. Second, <dfn class="term" data-def="Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, allowing the President to suspend civil rights and rule by emergency decree without Reichstag approval.">Article 48</dfn> let the President suspend civil rights and rule by emergency decree in a crisis. It was meant as a safety valve. By 1932 it would be used routinely to bypass parliament.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two strengths and two weaknesses of the Weimar Constitution.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Weimar Constitution gave universal suffrage at 20, a Bill of Rights and direct election of the President &mdash; one of the most democratic constitutions of its time. But proportional representation produced unstable coalitions, and Article 48 gave the President an emergency power that would later be abused to dismantle democracy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Spartacist Uprising, January 1919</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The first attack came from the far left. The Spartacus League, led by Karl <dfn class="term" data-def="Far-left politician and Spartacist leader; murdered by Freikorps in January 1919 along with Rosa Luxemburg.">Liebknecht</dfn> and Rosa <dfn class="term" data-def="Polish-German Marxist theorist and Spartacist leader; murdered by Freikorps in Berlin, January 1919.">Luxemburg</dfn>, had broken away from the SPD during the war. Inspired by the Russian Revolution of 1917, they declared a German Communist Party (KPD) on 30 December 1918 and on 5 January 1919 launched an uprising in Berlin. Strikers occupied newspaper offices and railway stations and called for soviet-style government.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Friedrich Ebert, the SPD leader and acting head of state, was terrified that Bolshevism would seize Germany as it had Russia. He turned to the army's General Groener and to the <dfn class="term" data-def="Right-wing paramilitary units of demobilised soldiers used by the early Weimar government to crush left-wing uprisings; many later helped the right.">Freikorps</dfn> &mdash; right-wing paramilitary bands of demobilised soldiers. Within ten days the Freikorps had crushed the rising. On 15 January 1919 they captured Liebknecht and Luxemburg, beat them, shot them and dumped Luxemburg's body in a Berlin canal. Around 200 Spartacists were killed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Ebert had saved the Republic from the left, but at a cost: he had put himself in the army's debt and let an unaccountable right-wing militia loose on Germany's streets.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The Spartacists never had majority support. The SPD itself, with millions of votes and trade-union backing, opposed them. Most workers wanted a parliamentary republic, not a Russian-style soviet. The Spartacists were also militarily out-organised &mdash; they were a few thousand against well-armed Freikorps. Liebknecht and Luxemburg's deaths left the new Communist Party leaderless and embittered. The SPD-KPD divide on the German left would never heal &mdash; a fault line that would help the Nazis rise unopposed in 1932&ndash;33.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Kapp Putsch, March 1920</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In March 1920 the right-wing Wolfgang <dfn class="term" data-def="Right-wing journalist and politician; led the failed March 1920 attempt to overthrow the Weimar Republic with the help of Freikorps and an army officer, Walther von L&uuml;ttwitz.">Kapp</dfn>, a journalist and nationalist politician, allied with army officer Walther von L&uuml;ttwitz to overthrow the Republic. Their immediate trigger was a government order disbanding the 5,000-strong Ehrhardt Brigade Freikorps. Instead of disbanding, the brigade marched on Berlin. The army refused to fire on them &mdash; General von Seeckt famously said &lsquo;Reichswehr does not shoot at Reichswehr&rsquo;. Ebert and his cabinet fled to Stuttgart.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Kapp declared himself Chancellor. The Republic survived because of the workers, not the army. The SPD called a general strike. Berlin shut down: no transport, no electricity, no water, no newspapers. Civil servants refused Kapp's orders. Within four days Kapp's regime collapsed. He fled to Sweden.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The aftermath was telling. Kapp himself died awaiting trial in 1922. Few of his fellow conspirators received serious sentences. Compare the leniency to the Spartacists: the same German judiciary that hanged left-wing rebels gave right-wing rebels suspended sentences. Political assassinations followed: Erzberger killed in 1921, Rathenau in 1922.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Munich Putsch, November 1923</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By autumn 1923 hyperinflation was at its peak and the Ruhr occupation was still grinding on. In Bavaria a small Munich-based party, the National Socialist German Workers&rsquo; Party (NSDAP), had grown to about 55,000 members. Its leader Adolf Hitler decided this was the moment to seize power. On the evening of 8 November 1923 he and General Erich Ludendorff burst into a beer hall where the Bavarian state commissioner Gustav von Kahr was speaking. Hitler fired a pistol into the ceiling and announced a national revolution.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">It was a fiasco. Kahr promised support, then phoned the regular army the moment Hitler left the building. The next morning Hitler and Ludendorff led 2,000 Nazi supporters in a march on Munich's Feldherrnhalle. Bavarian state police opened fire. Sixteen Nazis and four police were killed. Hitler dislocated his shoulder fleeing and was arrested two days later.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The trial in February 1924 turned defeat into propaganda. The sympathetic Bavarian judge let Hitler give long speeches that were reported in newspapers across Germany. He was sentenced to five years in prison &mdash; the minimum &mdash; and served only nine months in a comfortable cell at Landsberg, where he dictated <em>Mein Kampf</em>. Ludendorff was acquitted entirely. The lessons Hitler took: do not try to seize power by force; build a mass party and win elections instead. The contrast with Spartacist sentences could not be sharper.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the Kapp Putsch and Munich Putsch both failed but had very different long-term consequences.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Kapp Putsch failed because of a workers' general strike, not because the army or judiciary opposed it. The Munich Putsch failed because the Bavarian state turned on Hitler at the last moment &mdash; but his trial gave him a national platform he had previously lacked.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Weimar Constitution of August 1919 looked highly democratic but contained proportional representation and Article 48 &mdash; weaknesses that would later be exploited.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Spartacist uprising (January 1919) was crushed by Freikorps with state backing; the Kapp Putsch (March 1920) was defeated by a workers&rsquo; general strike, not by the army.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Munich Putsch (November 1923) was a fiasco that arrested Hitler within hours, but his five-month trial turned him from a regional agitator into a national figure.</li>
</ul>
