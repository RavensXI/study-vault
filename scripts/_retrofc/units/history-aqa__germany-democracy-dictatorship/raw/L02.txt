--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why a battleship-building programme mattered for jobs, taxes and the politics at home.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">When students hear &lsquo;Navy Laws&rsquo; they often think of dreadnoughts and the road to war. That is the foreign-policy story. The AQA spec wants something different: the <em>domestic</em> importance of the Navy Laws &mdash; what they meant for jobs in Hamburg, taxes in Berlin and the politics of the Reichstag. <dfn class="term" data-def="Wilhelm II's policy from 1897 of building a global empire and navy to make Germany a world power equal to Britain.">Weltpolitik</dfn> was as much about controlling people inside Germany as it was about challenging Britain at sea.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Weltpolitik: Why the Kaiser Wanted a Navy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">From 1897 onwards Wilhelm II announced that Germany would pursue Weltpolitik &mdash; a &lsquo;world policy&rsquo; of colonies, naval power and global prestige. On paper the goal was diplomatic: a place at the top table alongside Britain and France. In practice the Kaiser and his advisers also saw Weltpolitik as a way of <strong>uniting Germans</strong> behind the throne. A confident, imperial Germany, the argument ran, would distract working-class voters from socialism and reassure the middle class that the monarchy still mattered.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The man who turned this slogan into ships was <dfn class="term" data-def="German Secretary of State for the Navy from 1897; architect of the Navy Laws and the doctrine that a strong fleet would force Britain into alliance.">Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz</dfn>, Secretary of State for the Navy from 1897 until 1916. Tirpitz argued that a navy two-thirds the size of Britain's would be enough to deter the Royal Navy from ever risking a fight. To pay for it he needed the Reichstag to vote large, long-term spending packages. Five separate Navy Laws were passed between 1898 and 1912.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the years of the five Navy Laws and one domestic effect of the building programme.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Five Navy Laws &mdash; 1898, 1900, 1906, 1908 and 1912 &mdash; committed Germany to building dozens of battleships and cruisers. The programme created tens of thousands of jobs in shipyards at Kiel, Hamburg and Wilhelmshaven and orders for steel from the Ruhr industrialists.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Domestic Politics of Steel and Wages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Building battleships was popular with the steel barons of the Ruhr (Krupp, Thyssen) because they got vast government contracts. It was popular with shipyard workers in the north because it meant secure jobs at a time when imports from America were undercutting traditional crafts. It was popular with patriotic middle-class voters who joined the Navy League, founded in 1898, which had over a million members by 1914.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">It was less popular with farmers in the east, whose wheat tariffs Tirpitz traded away in exchange for naval votes &mdash; cheap imported grain hurt the Junkers. And it had to be paid for. By 1912 the navy was costing the equivalent of around half the entire imperial budget. The constitutional problem was simple: the Reichstag controlled the budget, which meant the Kaiser had to bargain with the parties to get the money.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Tirpitz pushed each Navy Law through by stitching together the Conservatives (who liked uniforms and prestige), the National Liberals (who represented heavy industry) and the Catholic Centre Party (who could be persuaded by jobs in their constituencies). The SPD voted against every Navy Law. The pattern showed how the Kaiser's government still depended on parliament &mdash; it could not raise the new taxes Tirpitz needed without horse-trading with the very Reichstag the Kaiser claimed to ignore.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Rise of the SPD</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Social Democratic Party (SPD) was the single biggest beneficiary of industrial growth. Its membership rose from 100,000 in 1890 to over a million by 1914. Trade unions affiliated with the SPD organised millions more workers. The SPD's programme called for universal suffrage, an end to the three-class Prussian voting system, parliamentary government and welfare reform. Its leader August Bebel was the most experienced parliamentarian in Germany.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the January 1912 election the SPD won 4.25 million votes &mdash; over a third of the total &mdash; and 110 seats. It was now the largest party in the Reichstag. Yet not one Social Democrat ever sat in Wilhelm II's cabinet. The Kaiser called them &lsquo;a gang of traitors&rsquo;. This refusal to share power with the largest elected party became one of the deepest faults in the imperial system.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The SPD did not grow in a vacuum. Bismarck himself had introduced sickness insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old-age pensions (1889) precisely to undercut socialism by giving workers a stake in the empire. The strategy half-worked: workers welcomed the welfare but kept voting SPD. By 1911 these schemes were extended into a comprehensive insurance code, making Germany the world's most generous welfare state. <dfn class="term" data-def="State-organised insurance schemes (sickness, accident, old age) introduced in Germany under Bismarck from the 1880s to undercut socialist support.">Bismarck welfare reforms</dfn> were a model later copied by Britain's 1911 National Insurance Act.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">A Constitution Under Stress</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1912 three forces were pulling against each other. The Kaiser and his court wanted a stronger navy, lower taxes on the rich, and no concessions to socialism. The SPD and the trade unions wanted democratic reform and welfare. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Voluntary worker associations that bargained with employers over wages and conditions; closely linked to the SPD by 1914.">trade unions</dfn> increasingly used strikes &mdash; over a million workers struck in 1910 alone. Tirpitz was running out of cash. New taxes, including a small inheritance tax in 1913, had to be passed with SPD votes. The system was visibly creaking.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">When war broke out in August 1914 the SPD declared a <em>Burgfrieden</em> &mdash; a political truce &mdash; and voted for war credits. For a moment, Weltpolitik appeared to have done its job: it had united Germans behind the throne. The truce would not last.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the Navy Laws were as much about domestic politics as foreign policy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Navy Laws delivered jobs, contracts and prestige &mdash; tools the Kaiser used to bind workers and middle-class voters to the throne and away from socialism. They also forced him to bargain with a Reichstag he distrusted, exposing the limits of his autocratic power.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Weltpolitik and the five Navy Laws (1898&ndash;1912) were aimed at uniting Germans behind the Kaiser and creating jobs for workers and contracts for industrialists, not just challenging Britain.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The SPD grew rapidly thanks to industrial expansion and Bismarck-era welfare, becoming the largest Reichstag party in 1912 with 110 seats &mdash; yet was excluded from government.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Kaiser still had to bargain with parties he distrusted because the Reichstag controlled budgets &mdash; revealing the cracks in the autocratic system before the war began.</li>
</ul>
