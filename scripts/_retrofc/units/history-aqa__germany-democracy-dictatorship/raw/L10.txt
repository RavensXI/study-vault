--- DESCRIPTION ---
Public works, the Four Year Plan and the price of guns over butter for ordinary Germans.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">When Hitler took power in January 1933 there were six million unemployed Germans. By 1939 official unemployment was under 200,000. Nazi propaganda presented this as a miracle. The reality was more complicated: the figures depended on who was counted, work creation was largely a side-effect of rearmament, and consumers paid for the recovery in lower wages, longer hours and shortages of basic goods. This lesson tracks the Nazi economy from Schacht's New Plan in 1934 to the Four Year Plan of 1936 and the choices behind the slogan &lsquo;guns or butter&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Schacht's New Plan, 1934</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The first phase of recovery was managed by Hjalmar <dfn class="term" data-def="German banker; President of the Reichsbank and Reich Economics Minister 1934&ndash;37; designed the New Plan to stabilise German finance; broke with Hitler over the cost of rearmament and was replaced by Goring.">Schacht</dfn>, a conservative banker who had helped end the 1923 hyperinflation. Schacht became Reichsbank President in March 1933 and Economics Minister in August 1934. His <dfn class="term" data-def="Schacht's 1934 plan to stabilise the German economy through bilateral trade agreements, currency controls and import licensing &mdash; aimed at conserving foreign exchange while reducing unemployment.">New Plan</dfn> tackled three problems at once: shortage of foreign exchange, dependence on imports, and mass unemployment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Schacht imposed strict import controls and signed bilateral trade agreements with Balkan states (Yugoslavia, Romania, Bulgaria), paying for raw materials in blocked Reichsmarks rather than hard currency. He used Mefo bills &mdash; promissory notes issued by a shell company &mdash; to disguise the true scale of military spending. By 1936 unemployment was down to one million. But Schacht warned Hitler that rearmament was now outrunning what the economy could support without inflation. Hitler was unimpressed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the Nazi unemployment figure for 1933, 1936 and 1939 &mdash; and one reason each figure flatters the regime.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Official German unemployment fell from six million in 1933 to one million by 1936 and under 200,000 by 1939. The figures are flattering: Jews dismissed under the April 1933 civil service law, women pushed out of professions, political prisoners and the 1.4 million conscripted from 1935 were all redefined out of the count.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Public Works and the Reich Labour Service</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Job creation began before Hitler took power, but the Nazis claimed credit and expanded it. The most visible programme was the autobahn network: 7,000 kilometres planned, 3,000 km completed by 1938. Around 125,000 men worked on autobahn construction at peak. Other programmes included public housing, draining marshes, planting forests and building government offices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">From 1935 the <dfn class="term" data-def="Reich Labour Service &mdash; compulsory six-month labour service for young German men (and from 1939 women) on land drainage, forestry, road and harvest work; provided cheap labour and pre-military discipline.">RAD</dfn> (Reichsarbeitsdienst) made six months of labour service compulsory for young men aged 18&ndash;25. It paid pocket money rather than a wage, lived under quasi-military discipline, and worked on agricultural drainage, road-building and forestry. From 1939 it was extended to women too. The RAD was a labour scheme, a propaganda spectacle and a pre-conscription training pipeline rolled into one.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">In March 1935 Hitler announced conscription in defiance of the Treaty of Versailles, raising the army from 100,000 to 550,000 by 1936. Conscription alone removed roughly 1.4 million young men from the unemployment count. Rearmament also pulled steel, chemicals, coal and engineering firms back into mass production: military spending rose from 1% of GDP in 1933 to around 18% by 1938. Krupp, Thyssen and IG Farben all expanded sharply. The recovery was real for these firms; for consumers it meant their resources were diverted to weapons. The historiographical debate over whether the Nazis &lsquo;solved&rsquo; unemployment turns on whether you count conscripts and dismissed Jews and women as employed, unemployed, or simply absent from the figures.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Four Year Plan, 1936</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In August 1936 Hitler issued a secret memorandum demanding that &lsquo;the German army must be operational and the German economy fit for war within four years&rsquo;. He put Hermann Goring &mdash; not Schacht &mdash; in charge of a new <dfn class="term" data-def="Hermann Goring's economic plan from 1936 to make Germany militarily and economically ready for war within four years; emphasised autarky, synthetic substitutes for imported raw materials and prioritised arms production over consumer goods.">Four Year Plan</dfn>. The aim was <dfn class="term" data-def="Economic policy of self-sufficiency &mdash; producing key goods (oil, rubber, food, textiles) inside Germany so the country could fight a war without depending on imports.">autarky</dfn>: economic self-sufficiency that would let Germany fight a war without depending on imports.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Plan poured investment into synthetic substitutes &mdash; synthetic oil from coal, synthetic rubber (Buna), artificial textiles. New plants were built at Leuna and Schkopau. Steel production was forced through new plants at Salzgitter (the Hermann Goring Werke). Imports of consumer goods were squeezed. By 1939 Germany still imported a third of its raw materials. Autarky was a goal, not a reality. But arms production rose sharply: by 1939 Germany was producing 8,000 aircraft a year. Schacht resigned as Economics Minister in November 1937, warning Hitler that the country was running out of foreign exchange.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Strength Through Joy and Beauty of Labour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For workers, the Nazis offered carrots as well as sticks. The German Labour Front (DAF) under Robert Ley ran two flagship organisations. <dfn class="term" data-def="Strength Through Joy &mdash; Nazi DAF leisure organisation offering subsidised holidays, theatre trips, sports events and the Volkswagen savings scheme to workers; aimed to compensate for low wages and political controls.">Strength Through Joy (KdF)</dfn> offered cheap holidays, theatre trips, sports events and concerts. By 1938 ten million Germans had taken a KdF holiday &mdash; mostly day trips, but cruises to Madeira and Norway also ran. Beauty of Labour (SdA) improved factory canteens, washrooms and lighting.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The famous KdF promise was the Volkswagen &mdash; the &lsquo;people's car&rsquo; that workers could buy through a savings scheme launched in 1938. Roughly 336,000 Germans paid in. Not one civilian Volkswagen was delivered before 1945; the factory was switched to making the Kubelwagen for the army. Like much of Nazi welfare, KdF combined real benefits, real propaganda value and an undertow of state fraud.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Drawbacks for Workers and Consumers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Behind the propaganda, ordinary German workers paid in three currencies. First, real wages fell or stagnated for most of the 1930s &mdash; not until 1938 did average industrial wages match 1928 levels. The DAF set wages; strikes were illegal; workbooks restricted job changes from 1935. Second, the working week lengthened &mdash; from 43 hours in 1933 to over 49 by 1939, with bonus pay for overtime gradually eroded. Third, consumer goods were rationed in all but name &mdash; meat, butter, dairy and textiles were all in short supply by 1939 because resources had gone into arms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Goring put the choice plainly in 1936: &lsquo;Guns will make us powerful; butter will only make us fat.&rsquo; The Nazi economy delivered on guns. It did not deliver on butter.</p>

The qualified answer is partial &mdash; subtract roughly 1.4 million conscripts, perhaps half a million Jews dismissed under the April 1933 law, women redefined out of full-time work, and political prisoners in the camps, and the figure of true productive employment is much smaller. Historians of the Nazi economy (Tooze; Overy) generally accept the recovery was real but driven by rearmament rather than productive demand &mdash; an &lsquo;armaments boom&rsquo; that could only end in war or collapse.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the slogan &lsquo;guns or butter&rsquo; captures the Nazi economic choice &mdash; with one fact about each side.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1938 around 18% of German GDP went to the military, up from 1% in 1933. Average industrial wages did not match 1928 levels until 1938, the working week stretched to over 49 hours, and consumer goods were rationed in all but name. The economy worked &mdash; for armaments. It did not work for consumers.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Schacht's New Plan (1934) and the Four Year Plan (1936) drove unemployment from six million to under 200,000 by 1939 &mdash; mostly through public works, conscription and rearmament rather than productive consumer demand.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Autarky was the goal of the Four Year Plan but was never achieved &mdash; Germany still imported a third of raw materials in 1939; the New Plan and Mefo bills disguised a deepening foreign-exchange crisis.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Workers gained jobs and KdF leisure but lost free unions, real-wage growth and consumer choice &mdash; Goring's choice of &lsquo;guns over butter&rsquo; defines the trade-off behind the recovery.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Three precision points. First, do not write that the Nazis &lsquo;solved unemployment&rsquo; without qualification &mdash; conscription, sackings of Jews and women, and prison labour all flatter the figures. Second, do not credit the autobahns as the main jobs programme &mdash; rearmament and conscription mattered more from 1935. Third, do not assume real wages rose in the 1930s &mdash; for most workers they fell or stagnated. For 8-mark Explain Effects questions, link the policy (Four Year Plan, RAD, KdF) to specific groups (industrialists, young men, workers, consumers) rather than &lsquo;the German people&rsquo; in general.</p>
