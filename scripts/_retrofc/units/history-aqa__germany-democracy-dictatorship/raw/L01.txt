--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the Kaiser, the Reichstag and Prussian generals struggled to share power before 1914.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In 1890 Germany was barely twenty years old as a unified country &mdash; and already the strain was showing. On the surface it looked like a modern industrial power with a written constitution and an elected parliament. Underneath, real power sat with one man: <dfn class="term" data-def="Hereditary monarch and head of state of the German Empire from 1871 to 1918.">Kaiser</dfn> Wilhelm II. Understanding how power was shared (or, more accurately, how it was not) is the foundation for everything that follows in this period.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Empire Wilhelm Inherited</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Germany only became a single country in 1871, when Otto von Bismarck stitched together twenty-five states under the leadership of Prussia. The constitution he wrote handed enormous power to the Kaiser. The Kaiser commanded the army, controlled foreign policy, appointed the Chancellor, and could dissolve the elected parliament whenever it became inconvenient. Bismarck had used this system skilfully for nineteen years, balancing the competing interests of socialists, Catholics, industrialists and aristocrats.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In 1890, the new Kaiser dismissed Bismarck. Wilhelm II was twenty-nine, ambitious and determined to be his own Chancellor. The men who replaced Bismarck &mdash; Caprivi, Hohenlohe, then Bernhard von B&uuml;low from 1900 &mdash; were weaker, more pliable figures. Power drifted upwards to the throne, and important decisions were increasingly taken by the Kaiser and a small circle of advisers rather than negotiated through parliament.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down three powers the Kaiser had under the 1871 constitution and one major power the Reichstag did NOT have.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under the 1871 constitution the Kaiser commanded the army, controlled foreign policy, appointed the Chancellor and could dissolve the Reichstag. The Reichstag was elected but could only debate budgets and laws &mdash; it could not appoint or sack the government.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Reichstag, the Bundesrat and the Limits of Democracy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Germany did have an elected lower house, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The elected lower house of the German Empire; men over 25 could vote, but it could not appoint or sack the government.">Reichstag</dfn>, chosen by all men over 25. By European standards in 1890 this was a wide franchise. The catch was that the Reichstag had no power over the executive. It could approve or refuse the budget and pass laws, but it could not choose the Chancellor or remove him. That decision belonged to the Kaiser alone.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Above the Reichstag sat the <dfn class="term" data-def="The upper house of the German Empire, made up of representatives of the 25 German states; Prussia held seventeen of the fifty-eight seats and could veto changes to the constitution.">Bundesrat</dfn>, an upper house made up of representatives sent by the twenty-five states. Prussia, the largest state by population and territory, held seventeen of its fifty-eight seats &mdash; enough to veto any change to the constitution. In effect, Germany was a federation in which Prussia could always say no.</p>

If the Reichstag voted down a budget the Chancellor often simply ruled by emergency decree. If the Reichstag became too troublesome the Kaiser could dissolve it and call a fresh election. Real influence did not run upwards from voters &mdash; it ran downwards from the throne, through the Chancellor, to the parties.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Prussian Dominance and the Junkers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Prussia was not just the largest state &mdash; it was the most conservative. Its parliament was elected by a three-class voting system that gave the wealthiest men votes worth seventeen times those of the poorest. This kept Prussian politics dominated by the <dfn class="term" data-def="Prussian aristocratic landowners who supplied most of the army's officer corps and senior civil servants in Imperial Germany.">Junkers</dfn>, the aristocratic landowners east of the river Elbe. The Junkers ran the army, the senior civil service and most of the diplomatic corps. The Kaiser himself was the King of Prussia. So when southern, more liberal states such as Bavaria or Baden tried to push reform, Prussian votes in the Bundesrat usually blocked them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Wilhelm II spoke openly of his belief in the divine right of kings. He wore military uniform almost every day and surrounded himself with generals. This atmosphere of <dfn class="term" data-def="A culture in which military values, ranks and uniforms dominate political and civilian life.">Prussian militarism</dfn> set the tone of the whole government. To advance in the imperial civil service or court life, an officer's commission was almost essential.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">An Industrial Giant Outgrowing Its Politics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">While the political structure stayed locked in 1871, the economy was transforming. By 1900 Germany produced more steel than Britain. Coal output doubled between 1890 and 1914. Cities such as Berlin, Hamburg and Essen grew rapidly as peasants moved off the land into factories. The population rose from 49 million in 1890 to 68 million by 1914.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Industrial growth produced two new political forces the old constitution struggled to absorb. First, the urban working class, which voted in huge numbers for the Social Democratic Party (SPD). Second, a confident middle class of engineers, lawyers and businessmen who wanted a real say in government. By 1912 the SPD was the largest single party in the Reichstag &mdash; yet not one of its members ever sat in Wilhelm II's cabinet.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Bismarck had managed the contradictions through twenty years of practice. Wilhelm II had no such patience. He chopped between policies, sacked ministers, gave inflammatory interviews to foreign newspapers, and lost the trust of even his own conservative allies after the 1908 <em>Daily Telegraph</em> affair. Chancellor B&uuml;low resigned in 1909; his successor, Theobald von Bethmann-Hollweg, inherited a system where the Reichstag, the Junkers, the SPD and the Kaiser all wanted different things and none could outvote the others. By 1914 the political system was paralysed long before the war broke it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the SPD becoming the largest Reichstag party in 1912 did NOT mean Germany was becoming a true democracy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1912 the SPD was the largest Reichstag party with over a third of the vote, yet no Social Democrat ever served as a minister under Wilhelm II. This gap between elected support and real government power is the central tension of pre-war German politics.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The 1871 constitution gave the Kaiser real power and the Reichstag only the appearance of it; Wilhelm II's dismissal of Bismarck in 1890 made this imbalance worse.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Prussia and the Junker class dominated the Bundesrat, the army and the civil service, blocking reform from the more liberal southern states.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Rapid industrialisation produced an SPD-voting working class and a confident middle class that the old political system could not absorb &mdash; ruling Germany was already hard before the First World War began.</li>
</ul>
