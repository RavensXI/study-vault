--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a Wall Street crash 5,000 miles away put Hitler within reach of power.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In May 1928, three months before the Wall Street Crash, the Nazi Party polled 2.6% in the Reichstag election. By July 1932 it polled 37.4% and was the largest party in Germany. Nothing about the Nazi programme had changed in those four years &mdash; what changed was the world economy. The Great Depression handed Hitler the votes that the Munich Putsch had failed to seize by force. Examiner reports flag this every series: it was the Depression that caused Hitler's rise, not the other way around. Direction of causation matters.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Wall Street Crash, October 1929</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">On 24 October 1929 (&lsquo;Black Thursday&rsquo;) the New York Stock Exchange collapsed. By the following Tuesday share prices had lost a quarter of their value. American banks called in their international loans &mdash; including those that had funded German industry under the Dawes Plan. Germany had borrowed roughly 25 billion marks of US capital between 1924 and 1929. Almost overnight that flow reversed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The result was the deepest economic collapse Germany had ever experienced. Industrial production fell by 40% between 1929 and 1932. Banks failed. By 1932 over six million Germans were unemployed &mdash; about a third of the workforce. Many more were on short-time work. Soup queues stretched through major cities. Young men in particular saw no prospect of work; many drifted into Communist or Nazi paramilitary units which offered uniforms, food and a sense of purpose.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the Nazi seat totals in 1928, July 1932 and November 1932 &mdash; and what each tells you about cause and effect.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Nazi Party rose from 12 Reichstag seats in 1928 to 230 seats in July 1932 &mdash; a thirteenfold rise in four years. The cause was the Depression, not Nazi policy: the party's programme had not changed. Nazi support actually fell to 196 seats in November 1932, showing the surge was unstable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Bruning and the Politics of Austerity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In March 1930 the SPD-led grand coalition collapsed over how to fund unemployment insurance. President Hindenburg appointed the Centre Party's Heinrich <dfn class="term" data-def="German Centre Party Chancellor 1930&ndash;32; nicknamed the &lsquo;Hunger Chancellor&rsquo; for cutting wages, benefits and government spending during the Depression; ruled increasingly by emergency decree.">Bruning</dfn>. Bruning had no Reichstag majority. Instead Hindenburg used Article 48 of the constitution to let him rule by emergency decree. Five separate emergency decrees passed in 1931 alone. Bruning's policy was deflation: cut wages, cut benefits, cut government spending. He believed this would force prices down, restore competitiveness and let Germany trade its way out.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">It made the Depression worse. Cutting benefits when six million were unemployed simply meant more starvation and more political extremism. Bruning was nicknamed the &lsquo;Hunger Chancellor&rsquo;. By 1932 the use of Article 48 had become routine: Hindenburg passed 66 emergency decrees compared to only five Reichstag laws. Weimar parliamentary democracy was effectively dead before Hitler ever became Chancellor.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Hindenburg was 84 by 1932 and surrounded by a small camarilla of right-wing advisers including his son Oskar and General Kurt von Schleicher. They saw the Depression as an opportunity to build an authoritarian government that bypassed the SPD altogether. Article 48 had been designed as an emergency safety valve in 1919; under Hindenburg it became a routine governing tool. This was the political space the Nazis would exploit: a President willing to govern without parliament, a Chancellor pursuing austerity that immiserated millions, and a Reichstag that no longer functioned.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Nazi Surge, 1930&ndash;1932</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the September 1930 Reichstag election the Nazi vote leapt from 2.6% to 18.3%. The party won 107 seats, becoming the second-largest party. The Communists (KPD) also doubled their vote, reaching 13.1%. Together the two extreme parties controlled almost a third of the Reichstag. The political centre &mdash; SPD, Centre, liberals &mdash; was being squeezed from both sides.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In April 1932 Hitler stood for President against Hindenburg. He lost, but won 36% of the vote in the second round &mdash; not bad for a man who had only become a German citizen in February 1932. In July 1932 Reichstag elections he polled 37.4% and won 230 seats, becoming the largest single party. By this point the Nazi vote had grown thirteenfold in four years. But it had not grown enough: the Nazis still needed coalition partners or a presidential appointment to take power.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Role of the SA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Nazi vote was secured on the streets as well as at the ballot box. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Sturmabteilung &mdash; the Nazi paramilitary &lsquo;brownshirts&rsquo; who broke up rival meetings, intimidated voters and offered food and uniforms to unemployed young men.">SA</dfn> (Sturmabteilung), led by Ernst R&ouml;hm after his return from Bolivia in 1930, grew from around 100,000 in 1930 to roughly 400,000 by July 1932 &mdash; larger than the Reichswehr army. SA &lsquo;brownshirts&rsquo; broke up Communist and SPD meetings, marched in uniformed columns through working-class districts, and provided street muscle at every Nazi rally.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Crucially, the SA also offered <em>welfare</em> the state had stopped providing. Soup kitchens, dormitories, free uniforms and a sense of belonging drew unemployed young men in. By 1932 over half the SA was unemployed. Street violence escalated: in the summer of 1932 alone around 100 people were killed in Nazi&ndash;Communist clashes. To frightened middle-class voters this looked like civil war &mdash; and the Nazis successfully painted themselves as the only force capable of restoring order.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Hitler's Appeal &mdash; Multiple Audiences</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Hitler offered different things to different audiences, held together by Joseph Goebbels's modern propaganda machine. To the unemployed: jobs, food, dignity, work creation. To the middle class: protection from Communism, restoration of order, recovery of lost savings. To farmers: subsidies and protectionism against cheap imports. To industrialists: an end to trade-union militancy and a commitment to rearmament. To nationalists: revision of Versailles. To everyone: <dfn class="term" data-def="Nazi concept of a unified, racially-pure German national community that transcended class, regional and religious divisions.">Volksgemeinschaft</dfn>, a unified national community transcending class. Goebbels's posters, films and modern aircraft tours (Hitler flew to twenty cities in five days during the 1932 campaign) reached audiences other parties could not.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">November 1932: The Vote Falls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In November 1932 fresh elections were called. The Nazi vote fell from 37.4% to 33.1% &mdash; from 230 seats to 196. Party finances were desperate. Goebbels's diary recorded morale collapsing. The KPD vote rose by another two percentage points. If parliamentary politics had simply continued, the Nazi surge might have run out of steam. But by November 1932 parliamentary politics had ceased to function. Hindenburg's small circle of advisers were already negotiating a way to bring Hitler into a controlled cabinet. The Depression had not fixed itself; it had broken the political system in which moderates might have survived.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why fear of communism was as important as Nazi appeal in driving voters to Hitler.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Communist KPD also grew rapidly during the Depression &mdash; from 10.6% in 1928 to 16.9% in November 1932. Many middle-class Germans voted Nazi not from love of Hitler but from fear of a Bolshevik revolution. Bruning's austerity, the SA's street violence and the KPD's rise together convinced enough voters that only an authoritarian regime could restore order.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The 1929 Wall Street Crash recalled US loans to Germany, triggering a 40% fall in industrial output and six million unemployed by 1932 &mdash; this was the cause of the Nazi surge, not Nazi policy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Bruning's deflationary austerity from 1930 worsened the Depression and replaced parliamentary government with rule by Article 48 emergency decree, hollowing out Weimar democracy before Hitler took power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Hitler's appeal combined targeted promises (jobs, order, anti-Communism, anti-Versailles) with mass propaganda and SA street violence &mdash; but Nazi support peaked at 37.4% in July 1932 and fell back in November, showing the surge was unstable.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">Two examiner trip-wires for this lesson. First, do not write that Hitler caused the Depression &mdash; the Depression caused Hitler's rise. Direction of causation matters and gets flagged every series. Second, do not assume the Nazis were always popular. They polled only 2.6% in 1928 and their vote actually fell from 37% to 33% between July and November 1932. For the 8-mark Evaluate an Interpretation question, watch for sources that overstate Hitler's appeal as the cause of his rise, when fear of Communism, Bruning's austerity and the SA's street violence all mattered too. Lock in the seat totals: 12 (1928), 107 (1930), 230 (July 1932), 196 (Nov 1932).</p>
