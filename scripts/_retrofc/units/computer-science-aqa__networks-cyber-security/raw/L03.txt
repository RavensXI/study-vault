--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Cyber Security?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The processes, practices and technologies designed to protect networks, computers, programs and data from attack, damage or unauthorised access.">Cyber security</dfn> is the field concerned with defending digital systems and data. It matters to everyone: a UK bank holding millions of customer accounts, a school storing pupil data, a teenager with a social media profile. Cyber threats do not require physical proximity &mdash; an attacker can target a system from anywhere in the world, which is what makes them so difficult to defend against.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Threats come from multiple directions. Some exploit technical weaknesses in software or networks. Others exploit the most unpredictable component of any system: the people using it. Understanding how attacks work is the first step towards defending against them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Social Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The art of manipulating people into giving up confidential information or taking actions that compromise security, by exploiting trust rather than technical vulnerabilities.">Social engineering</dfn> targets human psychology rather than technical flaws. Attackers create believable scenarios to trick people into doing something they would not normally do &mdash; revealing a password, clicking a link, or unlocking a door. The three forms you need to know are blagging, phishing, and shouldering.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write a one-sentence definition for each of the three social engineering techniques.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three social engineering techniques: <strong>blagging (pretexting)</strong> &mdash; creating a fabricated scenario to trick a victim into revealing information; <strong>phishing</strong> &mdash; using fraudulent emails or messages to obtain private information; <strong>shouldering</strong> &mdash; observing someone&rsquo;s private information over their shoulder, such as watching a PIN being entered at a cashpoint.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Social Engineering in Practice</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A social engineering technique in which an attacker creates a fabricated scenario (a pretext) to engage a target and manipulate them into revealing information or performing an action.">Blagging (pretexting)</dfn> involves inventing a convincing story. An attacker might call a company helpdesk pretending to be an IT contractor who urgently needs a user&rsquo;s login reset, or claim to be a bank employee asking a customer to &ldquo;verify&rdquo; their account details. The invented scenario is designed to create a sense of urgency or authority that overrides the target&rsquo;s normal caution.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A social engineering attack that uses fraudulent emails, text messages or fake websites to trick users into revealing passwords, bank details or other private information.">Phishing</dfn> typically arrives as an email or text message that appears to come from a trusted source &mdash; a bank, a delivery company, or a well-known retailer. The message creates urgency (&ldquo;Your account will be suspended&rdquo;) and directs the target to a fake website designed to harvest their login credentials. Spear phishing is a targeted form aimed at a specific individual, making the deception more convincing.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Observing a person&rsquo;s private information over their shoulder, for example watching someone enter a PIN at a cashpoint or reading their password as they type it.">Shouldering (shoulder surfing)</dfn> is the simplest form of social engineering: physically watching someone enter private information. It happens at cashpoints, on public transport when someone reads emails over a stranger&rsquo;s shoulder, or in an office when someone watches a colleague type a password.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Malware</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="An umbrella term for any software that is hostile, intrusive or designed to damage, disrupt or gain unauthorised access to a computer system.">Malware</dfn> (malicious software) covers a range of programs designed to cause harm. The three types specified for this course are: computer viruses, trojans, and spyware.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Virus, Trojan and Spyware &mdash; Compared</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of malware that attaches itself to legitimate files and spreads by copying itself to other files or systems when those files are opened or shared.">computer virus</dfn> attaches itself to an existing file or program. When the infected file is opened or shared, the virus copies itself to other files. Viruses require a host file and rely on user action to spread &mdash; for example, opening an infected email attachment or copying a file from an infected USB drive.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Malware disguised as legitimate software. Once installed, a trojan can give an attacker remote access or install other malware, without the user being aware.">trojan</dfn> (sometimes called a trojan horse) disguises itself as useful software &mdash; a free game, a codec, an update. Once installed, it operates silently in the background. Unlike a virus, a trojan does not self-replicate; instead, it typically opens a back door that allows an attacker to access the infected system remotely.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Malware that secretly monitors and records a user&rsquo;s activity &mdash; such as keystrokes and websites visited &mdash; and transmits this information to an attacker without the user&rsquo;s knowledge.">Spyware</dfn> runs hidden in the background and monitors the user&rsquo;s activity. A keylogger is a common form: it records every key pressed, capturing passwords, bank details, and messages. The data is transmitted back to the attacker without the user knowing the software is even present.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Other Threats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A cyber attack that redirects a user from a legitimate website to a fraudulent one, typically by corrupting DNS entries or modifying local hosts files, without the user clicking a malicious link.">Pharming</dfn> redirects users to a fake website without them clicking any suspicious link. Unlike phishing (which relies on deceiving the user into clicking), pharming tampers with the underlying address resolution process &mdash; so even typing a correct bank web address into a browser might lead to a fake copy of that site.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Several other conditions create vulnerabilities. <strong>Weak and default passwords</strong> are easily guessed or cracked: using &ldquo;password&rdquo;, &ldquo;admin&rdquo;, or a device&rsquo;s factory-set password leaves an obvious entry point. <strong>Misconfigured access rights</strong> occur when users have more permissions than their role requires &mdash; for example, a data entry clerk with admin rights could accidentally or deliberately delete critical records. <strong>Removable media</strong> (USB drives, external disks) can introduce malware when plugged into a device, either deliberately or because the drive was infected elsewhere. <strong>Unpatched or outdated software</strong> contains known security vulnerabilities that developers have since fixed in updates &mdash; systems running old software are exposed to attacks that exploit those known weaknesses.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Penetration Testing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="The authorised process of attempting to gain access to a system&rsquo;s resources without normal credentials, in order to identify and fix security vulnerabilities before attackers can exploit them.">Penetration testing</dfn> is a defensive technique: organisations hire specialist testers to attack their own systems legally and intentionally. The goal is to discover weaknesses before a malicious attacker does. There are two key approaches:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe in your own words the difference between the two types of penetration testing.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two types of penetration test: <strong>internal (white-box)</strong> &mdash; the tester has knowledge of the system and possibly basic credentials, simulating a malicious insider such as a rogue employee; <strong>external (black-box)</strong> &mdash; the tester has no knowledge of the system or credentials, simulating an outside attacker with no prior access.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Social engineering exploits people rather than technology: blagging uses a fake scenario, phishing uses fraudulent messages, and shouldering means physically watching someone enter private data.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Malware types to know: viruses (attach to files and spread), trojans (disguised as legitimate software, open back doors), and spyware (secretly monitor and transmit user activity).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Penetration testing is a defensive measure: internal testing simulates a rogue insider; external testing simulates an outside attacker. Both find vulnerabilities before real attackers do.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">For &lsquo;define&rsquo; questions on social engineering terms, precision matters. Blagging involves creating an invented scenario (not just lying &mdash; there must be a fabricated backstory). Shouldering means physically observing private information &mdash; it is not a digital attack. In malware questions, remember the scope: for this specification, the malware types are virus, trojan and spyware. Worms and ransomware are not in the required list &mdash; do not bring them in. For penetration testing, always distinguish which type simulates an insider threat (internal) and which simulates an external attacker.</p>
