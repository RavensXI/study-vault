How to structure a comparison essay: thesis, AO2+AO3 integration, model paragraphs and linking.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Comparison Skills for Section B Part 1</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Edexcel Component 2, Section B Part 1 question asks you to compare a named poem (printed on the paper) with one poem of your choice from the Belonging collection. The question is worth 20 marks and assesses AO2 (language, form, structure) and AO3 (context). Your chosen poem must come entirely from memory — it is closed book. This lesson teaches you how to structure a high-quality comparison essay.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Step 1: Choose Your Comparison Poem Wisely</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">You should prepare three or four poems in depth so that whatever the named poem is, you can pair it with a strong comparison. A good comparison poem shares the <em>theme</em> of the named poem but treats it <em>differently</em>. The best essays explore both similarities and differences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For example, if the named poem is “Island Man” (cultural displacement), you might choose “Kumukanda” (also cultural displacement, but inherited rather than lived). If the named poem is “To My Sister” (nature and family), you might choose “Clear and Gentle Stream” (also nature, but the speaker is alone). The key is having enough poems prepared that you are never stuck.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall all the key details listed here from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Prepare a “comparison grid” before the exam. For each poem, identify two or three possible partners and note the key similarities and differences. This means you can choose your comparison poem within 30 seconds of reading the question, leaving more time for writing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Step 2: Write a Strong <dfn class="term" data-def="The central argument or claim of an essay, stated at the beginning, which the rest of the essay supports and develops.">Thesis</dfn></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Your opening paragraph should name both poems, identify the shared theme, and state your argument. A thesis is not a description — it is a claim. For example:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">“Both Nichols and Chingonyi explore the pain of cultural displacement, but while Nichols presents belonging as split between two physical worlds — the Caribbean and London — Chingonyi explores a deeper fracture: belonging to a culture he has inherited but never experienced.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">This thesis does three things: it names both poems, identifies the shared theme (cultural displacement), and signals the key difference (physical vs cultural displacement). Everything in the essay should develop this argument.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Step 3: Structure Your Paragraphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Each paragraph should make a <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific, focused argument within an essay paragraph, supported by evidence and analysis.">topic point</dfn> that develops your thesis. Use the following structure:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>PETAL Paragraph Structure</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>P — Point:</strong> Start with a comparative point. “Both poets use colour imagery to convey belonging, but with contrasting effects.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>E — Evidence:</strong> Quote from the named poem (you can see it on the paper). “Nichols contrasts ‘blue surf’ with ‘grey metallic soar.’”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>T — Technique:</strong> Name the method (AO2). “This colour contrast creates a sensory opposition between warmth and coldness.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>A — Analysis:</strong> Explain the effect and link to the theme. “The blue of the Caribbean represents the world where the Island Man truly belongs, while London’s grey represents alienation.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>L — Link:</strong> Connect to the comparison poem (from memory). “Similarly, Chingonyi uses the Luvale word ‘Kumukanda’ to reach towards a heritage that is linguistically present but experientially absent, creating a different kind of sensory gap.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Step 4: Integrate AO2 and AO3</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The 20 marks are split across AO2 (language, form, structure) and AO3 (context). You must address both, but they should be <em>integrated</em>, not separated into different paragraphs. Context should explain <em>why</em> a poet makes certain language choices, not appear as a detached biographical paragraph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Weak AO3:</strong> “Nichols was born in Guyana and moved to Britain in 1977.” (This is a fact, not analysis.)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Strong AO3:</strong> “Nichols’s enjambment, which blurs the boundary between Caribbean and London, reflects the experience of Caribbean migrants who carried sensory memories of their homeland into a grey, industrial new country.” (This connects context to technique to theme.)</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe the structural feature and explain why the writer chose it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The highest-level answers weave AO2 and AO3 together in the same sentence. A useful formula: “[Poet] uses [technique] to [effect], which reflects [context].” This shows the examiner that you understand <em>how</em> and <em>why</em> the poet writes as they do.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Step 5: Use Comparative <dfn class="term" data-def="Words or phrases used to link ideas between texts, showing similarities, differences or connections.">Connectives</dfn></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Every paragraph should compare, not just describe. Use phrases like:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Similarity:</strong> “Similarly,” “In the same way,” “Both poets…” “Like [Poet A], [Poet B] also…”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Difference:</strong> “However,” “In contrast,” “While [Poet A] presents…, [Poet B] suggests…” “Where [A] uses [technique], [B] instead…”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Development:</strong> “[Poet B] takes this further by…” “This idea is complicated in [Poem B], where…”</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Common Mistakes in Comparison Essays</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Writing about each poem separately:</strong> This is the most common error. Do not write three paragraphs about Poem A and then three about Poem B. Every paragraph should discuss both poems.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Retelling the poem:</strong> The examiner knows what happens. Focus on <em>how</em> and <em>why</em>, not <em>what</em>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Dropping in context without connecting it:</strong> A sentence about when the poet was born earns no marks unless you link it to the poem’s meaning.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Choosing a poem you barely know:</strong> If you cannot remember at least two quotations from your chosen poem, pick a different one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n34">Prepare a comparison grid before the exam so you can choose your poem quickly and confidently.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">Every paragraph must compare both poems — never write about each poem separately.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Integrate AO2 (technique) and AO3 (context) in the same sentences for the highest marks.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">Spend the first 3–4 minutes planning. Annotate the named poem on the paper (it is printed for you). Choose your comparison poem. Jot down 2–3 quotations from memory. Write your thesis. Then write 3–4 comparative paragraphs using PETAL. Leave 2 minutes to check your spelling and add any missed points. Aim for about 35 minutes total on Part 1.</p>
