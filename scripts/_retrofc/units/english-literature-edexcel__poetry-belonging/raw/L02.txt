<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Nature & Spiritual Belonging</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Several poems in the Belonging anthology present nature not just as scenery, but as a place where the speaker truly feels at home. In this lesson we focus on three: Emily Brontë’s “Mild the Mist Upon the Hill,” Robert Bridges’s “Clear and Gentle Stream” and Letitia Elizabeth Landon’s “Captain Cook (To My Brother).” Each poet finds belonging in the natural world, but the relationship between self and landscape works differently in each case.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">“Mild the Mist Upon the Hill” — Emily Brontë</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Emily Brontë (1818–1848) grew up on the Yorkshire moors, and her poetry is saturated with the landscape around Haworth. In this poem, the mist, the hill and the sky are not just a backdrop — they are companions. The opening line, “Mild the mist upon the hill,” uses the adjective “mild” to personify the mist as gentle and unthreatening, as if nature is welcoming the speaker.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Brontë uses a series of natural images — mist, cloud, wind — to build a sense of the landscape as a living, breathing presence. The poem’s short lines and simple <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of poetry, creating rhythm.">metre</dfn> give it a hymn-like quality, suggesting a spiritual connection between the speaker and the moors. For Brontë, belonging is not about people or buildings — it is about being absorbed into the natural world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the key contextual details and how they connect to the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brontë’s poem presents nature as a source of spiritual belonging. The moors are not a place of loneliness but of deep connection. This reflects the Romantic belief that nature could offer a sense of the <dfn class="term" data-def="Awe-inspiring beauty or grandeur in nature that evokes strong emotion, often associated with Romantic poetry.">sublime</dfn> — a transcendent experience beyond ordinary human life.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">“Clear and Gentle Stream” — Robert Bridges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Robert Bridges (1844–1930) was a Literary Heritage poet who became Poet Laureate in 1913. His poem addresses a stream directly, using <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which a speaker addresses an absent person, an abstract idea, or an object as if it could respond.">apostrophe</dfn> to create a personal relationship between speaker and landscape. The stream is “clear and gentle” — these adjectives suggest purity, calm and trustworthiness, as though the stream is a reliable companion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The poem explores how the stream remains constant while human life changes around it. Bridges uses the stream as a symbol of continuity — it was there in childhood, and it is still there now. This gives the speaker a sense of rootedness: even if people move away or relationships change, the natural world endures. The regular rhyme scheme and gentle rhythm mirror the steady flow of the water, reinforcing the idea of nature as calm and unchanging.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">“Captain Cook (To My Brother)” — Letitia Elizabeth Landon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Letitia Elizabeth Landon (1802–1838), known as L.E.L., was a popular Romantic-era poet. Her poem is addressed to her brother and uses the figure of Captain Cook — the explorer — as a way of talking about adventure, departure and the bonds of family. The poem’s title itself signals a dual focus: exploration of the unknown world <em>and</em> the intimate relationship with a sibling.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Landon presents belonging as something that can stretch across distance. Even as Cook sailed to the other side of the world, the idea of home and family remained a <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed point to which something is attached, providing stability; metaphorically, something that keeps a person grounded.">anchor</dfn>. The poem uses the natural imagery of the sea and sky to evoke both freedom and longing. For Landon, nature is the medium through which separated people remain connected — they look at the same sky, are carried by the same wind.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Landon’s poem explores belonging across physical distance. Unlike Brontë, who finds belonging by being absorbed <em>into</em> nature, Landon uses nature as a bridge <em>between</em> separated people. The sea and sky connect brother and sister even when they are apart. This is a distinctive take on belonging that contrasts well with poems about displacement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Comparing the Three Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Speaker’s relationship with nature:</strong> Brontë is immersed — she <em>becomes</em> part of the landscape. Bridges observes and addresses nature as a companion. Landon sees nature as a connecting thread between people. Each represents a different model of how the natural world can create belonging.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Brontë’s short lines and hymn-like metre reflect simplicity and spiritual intensity. Bridges’s regular rhyme scheme mirrors the steady, reliable flow of the stream. Landon uses a more expansive, flowing structure that reflects the vastness of the sea and the reach of exploration. In each case, form reinforces content — a key AO2 point.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Context (AO3):</strong> All three poets belong to or are influenced by the <dfn class="term" data-def="A worldview, common in Romantic poetry, that sees God or the divine as present within nature rather than separate from it.">pantheistic</dfn> Romantic tradition that saw nature as spiritually significant. Brontë’s isolation at Haworth intensified her reliance on the moors. Bridges, writing later in the Victorian/Edwardian period, was more measured and formal. Landon wrote during the age of exploration, when travel meant long, dangerous separations from family.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Brontë’s “Mild the Mist Upon the Hill” presents nature as a spiritual home, using hymn-like form and personification.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Bridges’s “Clear and Gentle Stream” uses apostrophe to address nature as a constant, reliable source of belonging that outlasts human change.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Landon’s “Captain Cook (To My Brother)” presents nature as a bridge connecting separated family members across distance.</li></ul>
