<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Four Major Characters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Around the World in 80 Days has a small core company at its dramatic centre, surrounded by the dozens of minor characters produced by the eight-actor <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of performers of equal status working as a unified company, often sharing roles and creating visual images collectively.">ensemble</dfn>. Understanding each major character&rsquo;s function &mdash; and the precise physical and vocal choices a performer might make &mdash; is essential for the extended-response questions you will encounter in the exam. Let&rsquo;s take them in turn.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Phileas Fogg: the Still Centre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Fogg is one of the most unusual protagonists in the set-play list. Where most leads carry the play through visible emotional change &mdash; Proctor's moral disintegration, Mickey's rage-filled final scene &mdash; Fogg&rsquo;s dramatic function is almost the opposite. His <dfn class="term" data-def="Physical stillness, economy of gesture, and an outward absence of visible emotion used as a deliberate performance choice to communicate inner control.">stillness</dfn> is the comic engine. Every obstacle that throws Passepartout into comic crisis simply registers on Fogg as a minor inconvenience to be noted in his schedule. A performer playing Fogg should resist the temptation to play him as broadly comic. Instead, the performance choices are rooted in understatement: an upright posture, precise and unhurried movement, a measured vocal delivery with minimal variation in pitch &mdash; and then, in the moments that matter (the rescue of Aouda, the final London revelation), a tiny shift of vocal warmth or physical openness that carries enormous weight precisely because of the stillness that surrounds it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">From a design perspective, Fogg&rsquo;s visual identity is equally precise. A costume designer might use a dark Victorian frock coat, waistcoat and pocket watch as the anchoring constant &mdash; the one costume that never changes throughout the journey while everything around him transforms. This contrast between Fogg&rsquo;s fixed costume and the constantly shifting <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance between performers on stage, used intentionally to communicate power, relationship, threat or intimacy.">proxemics</dfn> of the ensemble around him gives the audience a visual anchor in every new location.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is Fogg's key performance quality and why does it create comedy?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Fogg&rsquo;s stillness and emotional restraint are his defining performance quality. The comedy arises from the gap between his unflustered calm and the chaos erupting around him &mdash; and this gap is entirely dependent on Passepartout&rsquo;s energy as contrast.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Jean Passepartout: the Audience's Way In</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">If Fogg is the still centre, Passepartout is the kinetic perimeter. The French valet functions as the audience&rsquo;s emotional guide through the journey: he worries when we worry, panics when the plan threatens to unravel, and celebrates when the adventure turns in their favour. A performer playing Passepartout has much greater physical and vocal range to work with than the performer playing Fogg. <dfn class="term" data-def="The spatial positioning of a performer on stage relative to other performers and the audience, communicating relationship and status.">Blocking</dfn> choices for Passepartout should be energetic and reactive &mdash; he might move frequently across the stage, use larger gestures, speak with more variation in pitch and pace, and respond visibly in his body to each new development. His relationship to the audience can include moments of direct comedy: a wide-eyed look to the audience when Fogg announces the next leg of the journey, a physical double-take, a sudden stillness of comic alarm.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Crucially, examiners reward students who understand that Passepartout and Fogg&rsquo;s comedy is entirely dependent on their contrast. If a performer plays Fogg as energetic, the play loses its engine. If a performer plays Passepartout as restrained, the comic double-act collapses. The proxemics between them &mdash; Fogg typically upstage centre or slightly elevated, Passepartout closer to the audience at <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of the stage closest to the audience.">downstage</dfn> centre &mdash; reinforces the status difference while keeping Passepartout as the audience&rsquo;s primary point of identification.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Consider the moment in the play when Fogg announces each new leg of the journey after an obstacle has threatened to derail the schedule. As a performer playing Fogg: hold an upright posture, speak the announcement in a measured pace with a level pitch, and allow a brief pause before delivering the key line &mdash; the pause creates audience anticipation. As a performer playing Passepartout in the same moment: stand in contrast &mdash; slightly hunched from exertion or anxiety, reacting visibly in the shoulders and face. When Fogg delivers his line, allow a beat of stunned stillness followed by a physical release of breath that communicates relief or disbelief. This is the double-act mechanism at work: Fogg&rsquo;s understatement amplifies Passepartout&rsquo;s reaction, and Passepartout&rsquo;s reaction amplifies the comedy of Fogg&rsquo;s stillness.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Inspector Fix: the Pursuing Antagonist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Fix is a Scotland Yard detective who is convinced Fogg is the perpetrator of a bank robbery. He pursues the party from the Suez Canal onwards, attempting to delay Fogg at each stop so that a warrant can catch up with him. Fix creates dramatic irony: the audience knows Fogg is innocent, Fix does not, and this gap generates comedy as well as tension. A performer playing Fix must walk a line between genuine threat (he has the legal power to detain Fogg) and comic obsession (his certainty is transparently wrong, and increasingly desperate). Vocally, Fix might employ a slightly faster pace than Fogg &mdash; suggesting agitation beneath a professional exterior &mdash; and physical choices that place him in the gaps between the main party: always slightly upstage, slightly to one side, watching from <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of the stage furthest from the audience.">upstage</dfn> in a way that signals surveillance without interrupting the main action.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Aouda: from Rescued to Active</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Aouda is the young Indian woman rescued by Fogg from a dangerous situation in India. Students often under-write her, treating her as a passive figure &mdash; rescued, grateful, silent. This is a significant misconception. While Verne&rsquo;s original novel is a product of its time in its portrayal of Indian women, Eason&rsquo;s adaptation (and the performance traditions that have grown around it) understand Aouda as someone whose arc moves from shock and vulnerability in her first scenes to active participation in the company as the journey continues. By the final London scenes she is part of the decision-making group, not simply an observer. A performer playing Aouda should track this arc physically: an initial containedness &mdash; quieter movement, closer proximity to Fogg as her protector &mdash; gradually opening into a more assured spatial presence, with more confident eye contact with both other characters and the audience, as her trust in the company grows.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what is Aouda's character arc across the play, and how might a performer show this physically?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aouda moves from a rescued, vulnerable figure in the India scenes to an active member of Fogg&rsquo;s company by the London ending. A performer should track this arc through changes in posture, spatial confidence and eye contact across the play.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Multi-Roled Ensemble: Character Through Transformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Beyond the four core characters, the eight-actor ensemble plays every other role in the play &mdash; the Reform Club wager-makers, the Suez customs official, the Bombay merchant, ship captains, train conductors, Native American warriors, Yokohama acrobats, and more. Each transformation requires the performer to signal a new character quickly and clearly. The technique is not <dfn class="term" data-def="A performance style that seeks to replicate real-life behaviour as accurately as possible on stage.">naturalistic</dfn> psychology &mdash; there is no time for a Stanislavskian interior journey into each minor character&rsquo;s backstory. Instead, the physical-theatre vocabulary of the play demands clear <dfn class="term" data-def="A physical attitude or stance, associated with Brecht, that communicates a character's social status and attitude in a single physical image.">gestus</dfn> &mdash; a posture or gesture that instantly communicates the character&rsquo;s social function and status. The Suez customs official stands with an officious squared-up posture, arms slightly out to signal authority; the Yokohama acrobat drops into a low centre of gravity with loose, ready limbs. These images are legible to the whole audience in an in-the-round configuration because they read from every angle.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">A common student error is to describe minor characters as &ldquo;the same performers in different costumes.&rdquo; In Eason&rsquo;s play, the theatrical pleasure of multi-roling comes from the distinctiveness of each transformation. The skill is in the precision of the gestus &mdash; how fully and quickly a performer can inhabit a completely different physical and vocal register. When writing as a performer for the exam, always specify what changes: the posture, the pace of movement, the pitch or accent of the voice. &ldquo;The performer would act differently&rdquo; scores no marks; &ldquo;the performer would shift from Passepartout&rsquo;s loose, reactive physicality to a squared, authoritative stillness and a more clipped English accent for the Reform Club gentleman&rdquo; scores well.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Fogg&rsquo;s stillness and emotional restraint are deliberate performance choices that create the comic double-act with Passepartout &mdash; playing him energetically would destroy the play&rsquo;s central mechanism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Aouda&rsquo;s arc from rescued vulnerability to active company member must be tracked physically across the play; under-writing her is a common exam mistake.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Minor characters in the ensemble require precise gestus &mdash; a clear physical attitude that communicates social function and status instantly, readable from every angle in an in-the-round configuration.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">For 8-mark performer questions on this play, the most common error is writing about what the character is feeling without translating that into specific vocal and physical choices. An examiner cannot give marks for &ldquo;I would show that Passepartout is frightened.&rdquo; You must say: how would you show it? Name the vocal quality (e.g. a raised pitch, a faster pace, a mid-phrase pause as if catching breath), the physical choice (e.g. a backward step towards the edge of the stage, slightly raised shoulders, a hand reaching towards Fogg before being withdrawn), and the spatial relationship (e.g. pulling away from Fix&rsquo;s position upstage to place more distance between them). For every character in this play, think in terms of contrast: how do your choices for Fogg differ from your choices for Passepartout? How do your choices for Fix differ from the choices for Fogg? Contrast and specificity are the keys to the higher bands.</p>
