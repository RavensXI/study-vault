--- DESCRIPTION ---
Scottish resistance, the First War of Scottish Independence and Edward I’s contested legacy.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Conquered Kingdom That Would Not Stay Conquered</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In August 1296, when Edward I rode south claiming Scotland as a conquered territory, he believed the war was over. Within twelve months it was clear it had only begun. The English administration left under John Warenne, Earl of Surrey, was harsh and unpopular: Scottish lords were forced to swear loyalty to Edward in person on the &lsquo;Ragman Roll&rsquo;, English officials taxed Scottish trade for the king&rsquo;s war chest, and an attempt to conscript Scottish men into the French campaign provoked outrage. By spring 1297 risings broke out in the south-west and the north. Two figures emerged to lead them &mdash; a young knight from Renfrewshire called William Wallace, and a Highland nobleman called Andrew Murray.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list the four major battles of the Anglo-Scottish war between 1297 and 1306 with the year and outcome of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four pivotal battles shaped the First War of Scottish Independence under Edward I: <dfn class="term" data-def="The Scottish victory of 11 September 1297 in which Wallace and Murray destroyed an English army crossing the narrow wooden bridge over the Forth at Stirling.">Stirling Bridge</dfn> (September 1297, Scottish victory), Falkirk (July 1298, English victory), the campaign of 1303&ndash;04 ending in Edward&rsquo;s acceptance of Scottish surrender at Strathord, and Methven (June 1306, defeat of the newly crowned Robert Bruce).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">William Wallace and Stirling Bridge, 1297</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">William Wallace was an obscure younger son of a Scottish knight; he killed an English sheriff at Lanark in May 1297, sparking a wider rising in the lowlands. Andrew Murray simultaneously raised the north. By September their armies had joined and were waiting at Stirling, the gateway between northern and southern Scotland. On 11 September 1297 Surrey&rsquo;s English army began crossing the narrow wooden bridge over the River Forth two abreast. When roughly half the army was over, Wallace and Murray charged. The English vanguard was cut to pieces, the bridge collapsed under the panicked retreat and as many as 5,000 English soldiers were killed or drowned. Murray died of his wounds soon after, leaving Wallace alone in command. He was knighted and named &lsquo;Guardian of Scotland&rsquo; in March 1298.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Falkirk and the Rise of the Longbow, 1298</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Edward returned in person and met Wallace&rsquo;s army outside Falkirk on 22 July 1298. Wallace deployed his spearmen in four <dfn class="term" data-def="A tightly packed circular formation of Scottish spearmen, with spear-points levelled outwards in every direction; a defence against cavalry but vulnerable to massed arrow fire.">schiltrons</dfn> &mdash; tight circles of spearmen with their points levelled outwards in every direction. The schiltrons broke the first English cavalry charges easily. Then Edward called up his Welsh and Cheshire <dfn class="term" data-def="The long English bow drawn to the ear, capable of launching armour-piercing arrows over 200 metres; recruited heavily from the Welsh borders by Edward I after the conquest of Wales.">longbowmen</dfn>, who poured arrows into the static formations until the schiltrons crumbled. The cavalry then charged the survivors. Wallace escaped but his army was destroyed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Falkirk marked the emergence of the longbow as the dominant English battlefield weapon. Edward had recruited massed archers from the Welsh borders during the conquest of Wales, and now he turned them on the Scots. The combination of disciplined spearmen broken by archery and finished by cavalry would shape English warfare for the next 150 years &mdash; through Cr&eacute;cy (1346) and Agincourt (1415).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">After Falkirk Wallace resigned the Guardianship and waged a guerrilla campaign for seven years, travelling to France to seek support from Philip IV. He was finally betrayed in August 1305 near Glasgow by John de Menteith, a Scottish knight loyal to Edward, and brought to London. He was tried in Westminster Hall on 23 August 1305 with a crown of laurels on his head &mdash; mocking his refusal to acknowledge Edward as king of Scotland. He was hanged, drawn and quartered the same day, his head set on a pike on London Bridge, his quarters sent to Newcastle, Berwick, Stirling and Perth. The brutality made Wallace a Scottish national martyr at the moment Edward intended to erase him.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Robert Bruce&rsquo;s Coup and Coronation, 1306</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In February 1306 the war broke open again. Robert Bruce, Earl of Carrick &mdash; grandson of the Bruce who had been a Competitor for the throne in 1291 &mdash; met John Comyn, his rival for leadership of Scottish resistance, in the Greyfriars church at Dumfries. The argument turned violent. Bruce stabbed Comyn at the high altar and rode out to seize the throne. On 25 March 1306 Bruce was crowned at Scone &mdash; without the captured Stone, but with a new circlet borrowed from the regalia.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Edward responded with cold fury. He extended the punishment of treason to families: Bruce&rsquo;s sister Mary and the Countess of Buchan, who had crowned him, were hung in iron cages outside Roxburgh and Berwick castles. Three of Bruce&rsquo;s brothers were hanged, drawn and quartered. The new English commander, Aymer de Valence, defeated Bruce at the Battle of Methven on 19 June 1306; the king of Scots fled into the Highlands and the western islands. By autumn 1306 he was a hunted fugitive.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Edward&rsquo;s Death at Burgh-by-Sands, 1307</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In May 1307 Bruce returned to Ayrshire and won small but symbolic engagements at Glentrool and Loudoun Hill. Edward, now nearly seventy and seriously ill, had himself carried north on a litter to renew the campaign in person. On 7 July 1307 he died at <dfn class="term" data-def="The village on the Solway Firth where Edward I died on 7 July 1307 while marching north to put down Robert Bruce&rsquo;s renewed Scottish rising.">Burgh-by-Sands</dfn> on the south shore of the Solway Firth, just inside the English border. His body was returned to Westminster and buried in a plain marble tomb. His son, the new Edward II, abandoned the campaign and turned south.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Historians divide on the legacy. Marc Morris portrays Edward as a &lsquo;great and terrible king&rsquo; who shaped Britain by the sword and the gallows; the Scottish writer Fiona Watson stresses that Edward&rsquo;s war ultimately welded Scotland into a fierce national consciousness against him. Michael Prestwich emphasises Edward&rsquo;s administrative achievements at home, arguing that the Scottish failure should not erase the rest of the reign. The contested label on the tomb captures all three views &mdash; an English Crown commemorating victory while admitting, by adding the inscription long after the event, that the title needed defending.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The &lsquo;Hammer of the Scots&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The famous title <dfn class="term" data-def="A Latin tomb inscription &mdash; <em>Edwardus Primus, Scottorum Malleus</em> &mdash; on Edward I&rsquo;s tomb at Westminster Abbey, added long after his death and not used by his contemporaries.">Hammer of the Scots</dfn> &mdash; in Latin, <em>Scottorum Malleus</em> &mdash; was not Edward&rsquo;s contemporary nickname. It was inscribed on his tomb at Westminster Abbey long after his death, possibly as late as the sixteenth century. The reality at his death was very different. Robert Bruce was a crowned king of Scots, raising new armies in the Highlands. Wallace had been turned into a martyr. The 1296 conquest had not held. Edward&rsquo;s son inherited a Scottish war he could not win, and within seven years of Edward&rsquo;s death the Scots would crush an English army at Bannockburn (1314) under Edward II.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the &lsquo;Hammer of the Scots&rsquo; tomb inscription is misleading evidence about the state of Scotland in 1307.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The &lsquo;Hammer of the Scots&rsquo; (<em>Scottorum Malleus</em>) is a tomb inscription at Westminster Abbey, added long after Edward&rsquo;s death and probably as late as the sixteenth century. It was not a contemporary nickname &mdash; at his death in July 1307 Robert Bruce was an anointed king of Scots and the Scottish war was very much unfinished.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">William Wallace and Andrew Murray crushed Surrey&rsquo;s army at Stirling Bridge (1297); Edward&rsquo;s longbowmen broke the schiltrons at Falkirk (1298); Wallace was captured and executed in 1305.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Robert Bruce killed his rival Comyn at the Greyfriars in Dumfries in February 1306, was crowned at Scone in March, defeated at Methven in June, but renewed his campaign in 1307.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Edward I died at Burgh-by-Sands on 7 July 1307 with Scotland still defiant; the &lsquo;Hammer of the Scots&rsquo; (<em>Scottorum Malleus</em>) tomb inscription is a posthumous label, not a contemporary verdict.</li>
</ul>
