--- DESCRIPTION ---
Edward I’s accession in 1272, Henry III’s troubled legacy and the problems facing the new king.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A King Crowned in Absence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Henry III died on 16 November 1272, his son Edward was thousands of miles away on crusade. Edward did not rush home. He was already recognised as king from the day his father died &mdash; the first English ruler to be treated as king before any coronation &mdash; and the realm was governed by a council of trusted men until he reached London almost two years later. He was finally crowned at Westminster Abbey on 19 August 1274. The kingdom Edward inherited was not the orderly realm a strong medieval king needed. It was a country still bruised from civil war, where great <dfn class="term" data-def="A powerful noble or great lord, especially one with land and influence over the king's government.">magnates</dfn> had grown used to challenging royal authority and where ordinary people had lost faith in royal justice.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: on what date did Edward become king, and on what date was he crowned?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Edward I became king on 16 November 1272 but was not crowned until 19 August 1274. He was on crusade when his father Henry III died and travelled home slowly through France and Gascony, confident that no one would challenge his right to rule.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Henry III&rsquo;s Damaged Crown</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Henry III had ruled for fifty-six years &mdash; the longest reign of any medieval English king &mdash; but his rule had been weak. He spent vast sums on foreign schemes, especially a doomed plan to put his second son Edmund on the throne of Sicily, and relied on unpopular foreign favourites at court. By the late 1250s the leading barons had had enough.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1258 a group of barons led by Simon de Montfort forced Henry to accept the <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of reforms imposed on Henry III in 1258 placing royal power under a council of barons.">Provisions of Oxford</dfn>. These placed the king under the supervision of a council of fifteen barons, demanded regular parliaments, and reformed local government. For the first time, an English king was effectively put on a leash by his nobility. Henry quickly tried to wriggle free, getting the pope to release him from his oath in 1261.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">When Henry rejected the Provisions, the kingdom slid into civil war. At the Battle of Lewes in 1264, Simon de Montfort captured both Henry III and the young Lord Edward, ruling England in the king&rsquo;s name for over a year. In January 1265 Montfort summoned a parliament that included not only barons and bishops but also two knights from each shire and two burgesses from selected towns &mdash; a wider assembly than England had ever seen.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Edward escaped his guards in May 1265 and led the royalist counter-attack. At the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1265 battle in which the royalists destroyed Simon de Montfort's army; Montfort himself was killed and his body mutilated.">Battle of Evesham</dfn> on 4 August 1265, royalist forces trapped Montfort&rsquo;s smaller army in a loop of the River Avon. Montfort was killed and his body hacked apart. Edward had won, but the war left England exhausted, scattered with armed bands of disinherited rebels and deeply suspicious of royal promises.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Edward the Crusader</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">After helping his father restore order through the Statute of Marlborough in 1267, Edward took the cross and left for the Holy Land in 1270. His <dfn class="term" data-def="A holy war fought by Christian Europeans, in this period most often to recover or defend Jerusalem and the Holy Land.">crusade</dfn> achieved little militarily but did a great deal for his reputation. He survived an assassination attempt by a Nizari Ismaili (sometimes called &lsquo;Assassins&rsquo;) at Acre in 1272 &mdash; a story retold across Christendom. By the time he returned home, Edward arrived not just as a king but as a famous warrior in his own right, with a reputation that quietened any thought of rebellion.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Problems Waiting for the New King</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">When Edward finally landed at Dover on 2 August 1274, the inheritance was difficult. Local government had drifted: sheriffs and other royal officers had taken bribes, seized land that did not belong to them, and let private courts swallow up royal jurisdiction. The royal income was too small for the king&rsquo;s ambitions, partly because earlier kings had granted away crown lands. Disputes between the king and the great <dfn class="term" data-def="A privilege or right granted by the crown, often the right to hold a private court or collect certain revenues.">liberties</dfn> of the magnates were unresolved. And in Wales, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd refused to attend the coronation or pay homage &mdash; an open challenge that would soon force a war.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Edward&rsquo;s Character as King</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Contemporaries called Edward &lsquo;Longshanks&rsquo; because he was unusually tall &mdash; over six feet, with long arms suited to swordsmanship. He was disciplined, hard-working and deeply religious, but he could also be quick-tempered and merciless to anyone he thought had betrayed him. His marriage to Eleanor of Castile (married 1254) was famously close; she travelled with him on crusade and on campaign, and bore at least fourteen children.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Edward saw kingship as a duty to deliver justice and recover royal rights that earlier kings had let slip away. The chronicler Walter of Guisborough recorded the king&rsquo;s personal motto: &lsquo;Keep faith&rsquo;. This combination of legal mind, military energy and confidence in his own authority shaped almost every act of his reign.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Relations with the Nobility</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Unlike Henry III, Edward had grown up among the great barons and fought beside many of them at Evesham. He worked with leading magnates such as the earls of Gloucester and Norfolk rather than relying on foreign favourites. Yet he also intended to claw back rights and revenues the nobles had absorbed during the chaos. The Hundred Rolls inquiry, launched within months of his coronation, would put that intention on paper. From the very start, Edward&rsquo;s reign would be a balancing act &mdash; respecting the nobility enough to keep their support, while pressing hard on their privileges.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three problems Edward faced on his accession in 1274 &mdash; one political, one financial, one in Wales.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The problems waiting for Edward in 1274 included corrupt local officials, a shrunken royal income, magnates holding privileges that should have belonged to the crown, and an unanswered challenge from Llywelyn ap Gruffudd in Wales.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Edward I became king on 16 November 1272 while on crusade and was crowned on 19 August 1274; the realm was governed by a council in his absence.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Henry III&rsquo;s reign produced the Provisions of Oxford (1258), Simon de Montfort&rsquo;s parliament (1265) and the Battle of Evesham (1265), leaving Edward with a damaged crown.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">In 1274 Edward inherited corrupt local government, weak royal finances, magnates holding lost royal rights, and an open challenge from Llywelyn of Wales.</li>
</ul>
