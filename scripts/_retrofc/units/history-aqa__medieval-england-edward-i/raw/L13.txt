--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Castle That Isn't One</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Acton Burnell Castle is your specified site for the 2028 exam, and the first thing to understand is that its name misleads. This is not a fortress like the great castles Edward built in Wales (Lesson 9). It is a fortified manor house in rural Shropshire &mdash; a rich man&rsquo;s residence wearing a light costume of battlements &mdash; and that is exactly why AQA chose it. The building shows you Edward&rsquo;s England from an unexpected angle: what power, wealth and royal favour looked like at home, in peacetime, in the reign of a king you have mostly met at war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Man Behind the House</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The house was built by Robert Burnell, the most powerful royal servant of the age. A Shropshire clerk who rose in Edward&rsquo;s service before the king came to the throne, Burnell became <dfn class="term" data-def="The king's chief minister, keeper of the great seal, head of the royal writing office and effectively the head of government administration.">Chancellor</dfn> of England in 1274 and Bishop of Bath and Wells in 1275, and he held the chancellorship until his death in 1292 &mdash; nearly the whole working reign. Wherever Edward was, government ran through Burnell. He was also spectacularly rich, holding dozens of manors, and in the 1280s he set about rebuilding the village he was born in: a grand house for himself, a fine parish church beside it, at the family manor of Acton Burnell.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who built Acton Burnell, what were his two great offices, and what royal permission did the house receive?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Robert Burnell &mdash; Chancellor of England from 1274 and Bishop of Bath and Wells from 1275 &mdash; began his grand house around the time of the 1284 licence, with work continuing to his death in 1292. In January 1284 Edward granted him a <dfn class="term" data-def="Royal permission to fortify a building with battlements; by Edward I's reign often granted as a mark of status rather than for real military need.">licence to crenellate</dfn>: royal permission to add battlements.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the Building: Comfort Wearing Armour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Walk round the roofless red sandstone shell today and the domestic truth shows through the military dress. The building is a compact rectangular block with a tower at each corner &mdash; castle-shaped at first glance. But look closer, as the examiner wants you to. The windows are large, elegant and low enough to climb through; a real fortress has arrow slits. There is no moat, no curtain wall of any strength, no gatehouse, no room for a garrison. Inside, the block held a first-floor great hall with private chambers &mdash; a design for entertaining, administration and comfortable living. The battlements are real, but they are jewellery. This is what historians call a fortified manor house, with the emphasis firmly on <em>manor house</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The licence to crenellate completes the point. By Edward&rsquo;s reign such licences were largely honours &mdash; the architectural equivalent of a knighthood. A licence said publicly: this man matters to the king. Burnell&rsquo;s house did not need battlements to survive Shropshire; it needed them to be <em>seen</em>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Parliament in the Barn</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Acton Burnell earned its place in national history in the autumn of 1283, when Edward &mdash; in the Marches for the aftermath of the second Welsh war &mdash; held a parliament at Burnell&rsquo;s manor. Tradition places its sessions in the great barn whose two massive gable ends still stand near the house. From that assembly came the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1283 law, made at the parliament held at Acton Burnell, giving merchants a fast legal process to recover debts registered before mayors.">Statute of Acton Burnell</dfn>, a law that let merchants register debts and recover them quickly through the courts. It seems a dry subject until you see what it shows: Edward legislating for traders and credit, parliament meeting wherever the king happened to be &mdash; including at the private house of his chief minister &mdash; and the reach of royal law-making extending into everyday commercial life, the theme you met in Lesson 3.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">What the Site Shows About Edward's England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Set Acton Burnell beside Caernarfon (Lesson 9) and the pair explain the reign. In conquered Wales, Edward built true war castles &mdash; concentric walls, garrisons, fortress-towns. In the English Midlands, his chancellor built a house with big windows and ornamental battlements. The same king, the same decade, opposite buildings &mdash; because England itself was at peace, governed by law, taxes and parliaments rather than by garrisons. The house also shows the rise of a new kind of power: Burnell was neither a great baron nor a warrior, but a professional administrator raised by royal service to wealth that built in stone. And the parliament of 1283 shows government travelling with the king, making national law for commerce from a Shropshire manor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the telling contrast between Acton Burnell and Caernarfon?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Acton Burnell and Caernarfon rose in the same decade for the same king&rsquo;s regime &mdash; a comfortable lightly-fortified house in a peaceful England, a war fortress in a conquered Wales. The contrast is the single most useful idea in your site answer.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Site Afterwards and in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Burnell died in 1292; his family's fortunes faded within a couple of generations; the house was abandoned by the early fifteenth century and has long stood a roofless shell, preserved as a romantic ruin. The red sandstone walls, corner towers, window openings and the barn&rsquo;s gable ends survive in English Heritage care, free to visit. In the 16-mark question, read features as evidence: large windows and thin defences &rarr; peace and comfort; the licence to crenellate &rarr; status culture around royal favour; the barn parliament and statute &rarr; law-making, credit and the reach of royal government; the builder&rsquo;s career &rarr; the administrator&rsquo;s rise. Feature, meaning, link to the statement &mdash; every paragraph.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Acton Burnell is a fortified manor house of the 1280s, built in red sandstone by Robert Burnell — Chancellor from 1274, Bishop of Bath and Wells from 1275, Edward's indispensable minister until 1292.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Its features argue peace: great windows, a first-floor hall and corner towers with ornamental battlements, licensed by the king in 1284 as a mark of favour rather than a preparation for war.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The 1283 parliament at the manor — traditionally in the barn whose gable ends still stand — produced the Statute of Acton Burnell, speeding debt recovery for merchants and tying the site to trade, credit and royal law-making.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Read against Caernarfon, the site completes the picture of the reign: war castles for conquered Wales, comfortable houses and parliaments for a governed England.</li>
</ul>
