--- DESCRIPTION ---
Edward’s first inquiry into the state of the kingdom and his challenge to noble privilege.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Kingdom Under Inspection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Within months of his coronation in 1274, Edward I launched the largest investigation of local government any English king had ever attempted. Royal commissioners were sent into every <dfn class="term" data-def="A subdivision of an English county used for local government and law, originally based on the area able to support a hundred households.">hundred</dfn> of every county to ask the same set of questions: what royal lands were missing, which sheriffs had taken bribes, which lords had set up private courts, and which liberties had been claimed without the king&rsquo;s permission. The replies were written down on long parchment rolls &mdash; the Hundred Rolls &mdash; and they gave Edward something no previous king had ever possessed: a detailed map of where royal authority had leaked away.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in what year did the Hundred Rolls inquiry begin, and what kind of evidence did it gather?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Hundred Rolls inquiry of 1274&ndash;75 sent royal commissioners into every county to gather sworn jury evidence about lost royal rights, corrupt officials and unauthorised baronial liberties. The surviving rolls fill several volumes and remain one of the richest sources for medieval English local life.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Robert Burnell, the Chancellor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The man behind the inquiry was <dfn class="term" data-def="Edward I's chief minister and Lord Chancellor 1274&ndash;1292; the architect of the Hundred Rolls inquiry and the great Edwardian statutes.">Robert Burnell</dfn>, Edward&rsquo;s closest adviser. Burnell had served Edward as a household clerk before the king&rsquo;s accession and was appointed Lord Chancellor in September 1274. As <dfn class="term" data-def="The senior royal officer who kept the king's seal, drafted writs and headed the chancery; Edward I's chief minister.">Lord Chancellor</dfn> he kept the great seal of England, drafted royal <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal written order issued in the king's name, often setting in motion a court case or a piece of administration.">writs</dfn>, and ran the chancery &mdash; the king&rsquo;s writing office. Contemporary chroniclers described him as wise, cautious and a brilliant lawyer, although critics noted he held many church offices at once and lived in the manner of a great lord. He became Bishop of Bath and Wells in 1275 but continued to dominate royal government until his death in 1292.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Burnell&rsquo;s genius was administrative. He organised the commissioners, framed the questions they asked, and turned the answers into the legislative programme of the next decade. Almost every great Edwardian statute carries his fingerprints.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Local juries reported corrupt sheriffs taking bribes to ignore crimes, bailiffs charging illegal fees, and great lords running their own courts as if they were kings. They also recorded lost royal lands &mdash; pieces of crown estate that had been quietly absorbed by neighbouring lords. In some places jurors were too frightened of their lord to give honest answers, but the picture was clear: royal authority had been thinning out for two generations.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The findings shaped the First Statute of Westminster (1275), the Statute of Gloucester (1278) and the Quo Warranto inquiries that followed. The Hundred Rolls were not just a complaint book &mdash; they became the working data behind reform.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">&lsquo;By What Warrant?&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Once the rolls revealed how many lords claimed private courts and other rights, Edward struck. From 1278, royal justices on circuit began to summon barons and ask them <dfn class="term" data-def="Latin for &lsquo;by what warrant?&rsquo; &mdash; the inquiries from 1278 in which Edward I&rsquo;s justices demanded barons prove their right to hold private liberties.">Quo Warranto</dfn>: by what authority do you hold this market, this gallows, this hundred court? The principle was that all jurisdiction belonged to the crown unless a lord could show a royal grant. Many barons had no charter to wave at the justices &mdash; the rights had simply been exercised for so long that everyone had stopped asking.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Rusty Sword</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The most famous Quo Warranto moment came when Earl Warenne was challenged about his liberties. According to the chronicler, the earl drew an old, rust-stained sword and declared, &lsquo;Look, my lords, here is my warrant. My ancestors came with William the Bastard and conquered their lands by the sword, and I will defend them by the sword against anyone who tries to take them.&rsquo; Whether or not the speech was made exactly as reported, it captured the mood of the older nobility: they would not let Edward dismantle their <dfn class="term" data-def="A privilege or right granted by the crown &mdash; for example to hold a private court, a market or a fair &mdash; sometimes called a franchise.">franchise</dfn> rights through paper inquiries.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Edward eventually compromised. The Statute of Quo Warranto (1290) accepted that any liberty exercised continuously since the coronation of Richard I in 1189 was lawful, even without a written charter. Continuous use had become its own warrant. This was a partial royal retreat &mdash; barons kept most of what they held &mdash; but the king had still asserted, on paper, that all jurisdiction flowed from the crown. The principle would matter when later kings tested the same boundary.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">A New Style of Government</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Hundred Rolls and Quo Warranto inquiries together showed how Edward planned to rule: not through dramatic confrontation but through paperwork, evidence and the systematic recovery of lost royal rights. Edward did not destroy the great liberties of the magnates &mdash; he could not afford a new civil war &mdash; but he established that the crown was now actively monitoring local government in a way that had never been seen before.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what does &lsquo;Quo Warranto&rsquo; mean, and what compromise did Edward accept in 1290?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Quo Warranto inquiries from 1278 demanded that barons prove their right to hold private liberties. The Statute of Quo Warranto in 1290 accepted continuous use since 1189 (the coronation of Richard I) as proof &mdash; a partial royal retreat that still asserted the principle that all jurisdiction came from the crown.</p>
</div>
