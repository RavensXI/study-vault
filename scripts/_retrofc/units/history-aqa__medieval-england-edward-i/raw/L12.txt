<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why This Site Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Battle of Stirling Bridge is your specified site for the 2027 exam. The 16-mark question on Paper 2 will name it, and it will reward one thing above all: showing how the <em>place</em> &mdash; a narrow bridge, a river loop, soft meadow and a crag &mdash; turned the finest heavy cavalry in Britain into casualties. Lessons 10 and 11 give you the story of Edward and Scotland; this lesson walks the ground of 11 September 1297.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">How Edward's Scotland Caught Fire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In 1296 Edward I had crushed Scotland in a single campaign: Berwick sacked, the Scots army beaten at Dunbar, King John Balliol stripped of his crown, and English officials installed to run the country. Chief among them was Hugh de Cressingham, Edward&rsquo;s treasurer, whose taxes made him the most hated man in Scotland. By the summer of 1297 revolt had broken out in two places at once &mdash; William Wallace in the south-west, Andrew Moray in the north-east. Their combined force met the English army at the one place every army marching north had to pass: Stirling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: why did every campaign in Scotland have to pass through Stirling?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stirling was called the gateway to the Highlands: its castle guarded the lowest crossing of the River Forth. An army moving between southern and northern Scotland had little practical choice but to cross here &mdash; which is why the battle happened where it did.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the Ground: Bridge, Loop and Crag</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The site has three features you must be able to describe. First, the bridge itself: a narrow wooden crossing over the Forth, so tight &mdash; chroniclers say &mdash; that only two horsemen could ride abreast. An army of thousands could only trickle across it. Second, the ground beyond the bridge: the crossing led onto a low <dfn class="term" data-def="Flat, low-lying land beside a river; at Stirling, soft alluvial meadow inside a loop of the Forth, poor ground for heavy cavalry.">carse</dfn> inside a tight loop of the river &mdash; soft, wet meadow threaded by a single raised causeway, with water curling round it on three sides. Third, Abbey Craig: a steep volcanic crag a mile north of the bridge, where the Wallace Monument stands today. From its slopes Wallace and Moray could watch the entire English crossing while their own men stayed hidden and rested.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Put the three together and the trap describes itself. Whatever crossed the bridge would be stuck on soft ground in a river loop, with no room to form up, no firm footing for a cavalry charge, and an enemy waiting on high ground above the only exit.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Armies and the Commanders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The English army was commanded by John de Warenne, Earl of Surrey, the victor of Dunbar &mdash; elderly, unwell and openly reluctant to be in Scotland at all &mdash; with Cressingham at his side urging speed and economy. Their force was built around armoured knights, supported by thousands of infantry including Welsh spearmen. The Scots had no comparable cavalry and no great lords present: Wallace and Moray led spearmen &mdash; ordinary freemen with twelve-foot spears &mdash; positioned on and behind Abbey Craig. On paper it was no contest. On that ground, the paper was wrong.</p>

Cressingham objected that a delay would waste the king&rsquo;s money, and Warenne ordered the direct crossing. The site question rewards you for knowing this: the English saw the danger of the ground and chose it anyway.</p></div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Battle: A Trap Sprung at Half-Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Early on 11 September 1297 the English began filing across the bridge two abreast &mdash; a crossing so slow that chroniclers claim it had already been started and recalled more than once, with Warenne at one point oversleeping while his vanguard stood on the wrong bank. Wallace and Moray watched from Abbey Craig and did nothing. They let perhaps half the English army &mdash; the vanguard of knights and infantry, Cressingham among them &mdash; assemble on the soft ground inside the loop.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Then the spearmen charged down from the crag. Their first target was not the vanguard but the bridgehead itself: seize the end of the bridge, and the army was cut in two. It worked. The knights inside the loop turned to charge and found their warhorses floundering in the carse; the causeway could hold no formation; and the river hemmed them in on three sides. Some fought and died where they stood, some drowned trying to swim the Forth in armour, one knight famously hacked his way back across the bridge. Warenne, watching from the south bank with half an army, could feed no help through a two-horse-wide bottleneck into a collapsing pocket. He ordered the bridge destroyed and retreated to Berwick.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: why exactly could Warenne not rescue his vanguard?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The bridge that fed the English army into the trap also stopped its rescue. Once the Scots held the northern bridgehead, the width of the bridge &mdash; two horsemen abreast &mdash; meant Warenne&rsquo;s remaining thousands might as well have been in England.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Cost and the Aftermath</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Chroniclers put the English dead in the thousands, including around a hundred knights &mdash; staggering losses for the class of men who were rarely killed in battle, because ransoming them was profitable. Spearmen on foot took no ransoms. Cressingham was killed and, the chronicles record with relish, flayed &mdash; pieces of his skin kept as trophies, one allegedly made into a belt for Wallace&rsquo;s sword. The victory made Wallace Guardian of Scotland and shattered the assumption that knights simply beat infantry. But it came at a price: Andrew Moray, the rising's ablest soldier, was mortally wounded and died within months. And it guaranteed the return of Edward himself, who took personal command and destroyed Wallace&rsquo;s army in the open field at Falkirk the following summer &mdash; flat, firm ground, no bridge, no loop &mdash; which is the sharpest possible proof of what the Stirling site had been worth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Site Today and in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The wooden bridge is gone &mdash; a later stone bridge stands close to the spot &mdash; but the loop of the Forth, the carse and Abbey Craig, now crowned by the Victorian Wallace Monument, still let you read the battle from the ground. In the 16-mark question, work feature by feature: the bridge as bottleneck explains why only half the army fought; the carse explains why cavalry was useless; the loop explains why there was no escape; Abbey Craig explains why the Scots could choose the perfect moment; and Stirling&rsquo;s position as the gateway crossing explains why the battle happened here at all. Feature, consequence, link to the statement &mdash; every time.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The battle happened at Stirling because geography made it the gateway to northern Scotland: the castle guarded the lowest practical crossing of the Forth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Three site features decided it: a bridge two horsemen wide, soft carse inside a river loop, and Abbey Craig giving the Scots observation and the choice of moment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Wallace and Moray sprang the trap at half-time: seizing the bridgehead cut the English army in two and left knights floundering on ground that cancelled cavalry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The consequences ran both ways — Wallace became Guardian and the myth of knightly invincibility died, but Moray was mortally wounded, and Falkirk in 1298 showed the same Scots army destroyed on ground that gave it no protection.</li>
</ul>
