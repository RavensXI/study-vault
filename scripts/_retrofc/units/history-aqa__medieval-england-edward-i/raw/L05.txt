--- DESCRIPTION ---
How royal justice reached every village through courts, juries and brutal medieval punishments.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Reach of the King&rsquo;s Justice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Edward I came to the throne in 1272, the king&rsquo;s justice was already the most centralised in Europe. Henry II had built a system of royal courts, royal writs and travelling judges that could pull cases out of local lords&rsquo; hands and into the king&rsquo;s. Edward inherited this machine and sharpened it. By the 1280s a peasant in a Lincolnshire village or a merchant in a Cornish port could expect a royal judge to ride into his county at least once every few years to hear major crimes. The result was a kingdom where the law of the land &mdash; what historians later called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of law shared across the whole kingdom and shaped by decisions in the king&rsquo;s courts, rather than the local custom of a particular lord or town.">common law</dfn> &mdash; really did reach into the localities.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three main royal courts at Westminster and one type of case heard in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three main royal courts sat at Westminster under Edward I: King&rsquo;s Bench (criminal cases involving the Crown and serious felonies), Common Pleas (civil disputes between subjects, especially over land), and the Exchequer of Pleas (cases concerning royal revenue and the king&rsquo;s debtors).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Royal Courts at Westminster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">By Edward&rsquo;s reign three permanent benches met at Westminster. The Court of <dfn class="term" data-def="The royal court that handled criminal cases involving the Crown and the most serious felonies, sitting wherever the king was; later fixed at Westminster.">King&rsquo;s Bench</dfn> handled major criminal cases and offences against the king. The Court of Common Pleas dealt with civil disputes between subjects, especially over land. The Exchequer of Pleas handled cases concerning royal revenue. Each had its own chief justice, its own clerks and its own books of pleadings, with judgements gradually building up the case law that lawyers cited for centuries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Most ordinary people, though, never travelled to Westminster. The king&rsquo;s justice came to them. Travelling judges &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="A general circuit of royal judges who heard all the pleas of the Crown in a county at once; held only every few years and feared as a heavy investigation.">eyre</dfn> &mdash; toured the counties in great circuits, hearing every Crown plea outstanding. The eyre was a major event: it pulled together every coroner&rsquo;s roll, every escaped prisoner, every claim of liberty. Because it was so heavy, by Edward&rsquo;s reign the eyre was being supplemented by faster <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of royal commission sending judges to a county to try a specific list of cases &mdash; faster and more frequent than the general eyre.">assize</dfn> commissions, which dealt with specific cases on a short list and could be issued more often.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">From Trial by Ordeal to Trial by Jury</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In 1215 the Pope had banned clergy from taking part in trial by ordeal, which had relied on a priest blessing the iron or the water. English judges responded by turning to the jury &mdash; twelve men of the neighbourhood who decided whether an accused was guilty. By Edward&rsquo;s reign trial by jury was the standard route for serious crime. There were two stages: a presenting jury (called the grand jury) listed those suspected of crimes, and a trial jury (the petit jury) decided whether each was guilty. Witnesses were sometimes called, but the jurors were expected to know local reputations themselves &mdash; a far more local kind of justice than today&rsquo;s.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A defendant who refused to plead at all could be sentenced to <em>peine forte et dure</em> &mdash; pressed under heavy stones until they pleaded or died. The system was harsh, but it was at least public, regular and (compared with the older ordeals) rational.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The most serious crimes were felonies &mdash; murder, robbery, rape, arson, treason &mdash; punished by death and forfeiture of all the convict&rsquo;s goods to the Crown. Less serious offences were trespasses, dealt with by fines, the pillory or the stocks. Treason was a special category that grew under Edward: the punishment of hanging, drawing and quartering used on Dafydd ap Gruffudd in 1283 was extended to a wider range of treasonable crimes by his grandson&rsquo;s Statute of Treasons in 1352, but its origin lies firmly in this reign. Most petty wrongs were tried in the manor court of the local lord, where the lord&rsquo;s steward and a jury of villagers fined offenders or set them in the stocks.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Statute of Winchester, 1285</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In October 1285 Edward issued the Statute of Winchester, the most significant piece of legislation on local policing in the medieval period. It built on the older Anglo-Saxon system of <dfn class="term" data-def="The mutual-suretyship system in which every adult man belonged to a group of ten who were collectively responsible for each other&rsquo;s good behaviour and for producing offenders.">frankpledge</dfn> &mdash; collective responsibility &mdash; and gave it teeth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Every man between fifteen and sixty had to keep arms suited to his rank, from a knight&rsquo;s sword and lance down to a peasant&rsquo;s bow and arrows. A formal watch was set in each town, with gates closed at sunset; strangers entering after dark could be arrested. When a crime was reported, anyone hearing the alarm had to raise the <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal duty to shout an alarm and chase a fleeing criminal; everyone in the neighbourhood had to join the pursuit until the offender was caught or the trail went cold.">hue and cry</dfn> and join the chase. If the criminal was not caught, the whole hundred &mdash; the local administrative district &mdash; was collectively fined. Crucially, hedges and undergrowth within 200 feet of major roads had to be cleared so that highwaymen had nowhere to hide.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Punishment as Public Spectacle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Medieval punishment was meant to be seen. Petty offenders &mdash; thieves, fraudsters and brewers selling short measures &mdash; were placed in the <dfn class="term" data-def="A wooden frame with holes for the head and hands, used to display offenders publicly so that the community could mock or pelt them.">pillory</dfn> in the market place, where neighbours could mock or pelt them with rotten food. The stocks held offenders by the feet. Felons sentenced to death were hanged at the town gallows, often on market day, so that the largest possible crowd would witness the sentence. Bodies were sometimes left to rot in chains as a warning to travellers.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">For the most extreme cases of treason, Edward devised a new punishment. In 1283 Dafydd ap Gruffudd, brother of Llywelyn, was the first English nobleman to be hanged, drawn and quartered: dragged on a hurdle to the gallows, hanged but cut down alive, disembowelled, beheaded and his body cut into four parts to be displayed on city gates. The brutality was deliberate. Edward wanted treason against the Crown to look qualitatively different from any other crime, and the punishment was extended in later reigns to a wider list of treasonable offences.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down what the Statute of Winchester required of (a) every adult man and (b) the local hundred when a crime was committed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Statute of Winchester (1285) required every man aged 15 to 60 to keep arms suitable to his rank, organised the watch in towns and on highways, and made the local hundred collectively liable for unsolved offences. It remained the framework of English policing until the creation of professional police forces in the nineteenth century.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Three royal courts &mdash; King&rsquo;s Bench, Common Pleas and Exchequer of Pleas &mdash; sat at Westminster, while the eyre and the assize judges took the king&rsquo;s justice into the counties.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Trial by jury had replaced trial by ordeal after 1215; juries presented suspects (grand jury) and decided guilt (petit jury) using local knowledge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Statute of Winchester (1285) organised local policing through frankpledge, the watch and the hue and cry; punishment was a public spectacle, with felons hanged and traitors hanged, drawn and quartered.</li>
</ul>
