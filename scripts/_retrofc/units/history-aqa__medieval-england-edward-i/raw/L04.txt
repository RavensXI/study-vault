--- DESCRIPTION ---
How agriculture, the wool trade and Italian bankers funded Edward I’s ambitions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Kingdom Built on Wool</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Thirteenth-century England was a farming country, and its richest export by far was wool. The damp grasslands of the Cotswolds, Lincolnshire, the Welsh borders and the Yorkshire moors fed flocks that fleeced some of the finest wool in Europe. Most was shipped through London, Boston and Hull to Flemish weavers in Bruges and Ghent, who turned it into cloth and sold it back across Europe. Edward I understood that whoever controlled this trade controlled the kingdom&rsquo;s wealth. His financial system was built on it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three main pillars of Edward I&rsquo;s royal finance and one detail about each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Edward I&rsquo;s royal finance rested on three pillars: the wool customs (&lsquo;maltolt&rsquo;) introduced in 1275, loans from Italian banking houses (the Riccardi of Lucca, then the Frescobaldi of Florence), and parliamentary grants of taxation. Together they paid for the Welsh wars, castle programme and Scottish campaigns.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Wool Customs of 1275</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">At his first parliament in April 1275, Edward asked the merchants and barons to grant him a customs duty on wool exports. They agreed: half a mark (six shillings and eightpence) on every sack leaving an English port, with a smaller duty on hides. This was the &lsquo;Ancient Custom&rsquo; &mdash; a steady, predictable income paid by exporters at the dockside. The duty was light enough that wool merchants tolerated it, and it brought in roughly &pound;10,000 a year &mdash; a transformative addition to royal revenue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1294, with war breaking out against France and rebellion in Wales, Edward demanded a far heavier <dfn class="term" data-def="The heavy emergency wool customs duty levied by Edward I in 1294, six times the ordinary rate, deeply unpopular with merchants who called it &lsquo;the bad tax&rsquo;.">maltolt</dfn> &mdash; literally &lsquo;the bad tax&rsquo; in Anglo-French &mdash; of forty shillings on every sack, six times the ordinary rate. Merchants protested, calling the levy crippling and ruinous, and the maltolt became a flashpoint in the political crisis of 1297 that forced Edward to confirm Magna Carta and accept new limits on his powers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Riccardi of Lucca</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Customs revenue was steady but not enough on its own to fund a war. For credit, Edward turned to Italy. From 1272 the <dfn class="term" data-def="A banking house from the Italian city of Lucca that lent the English Crown around &pound;400,000 between 1272 and 1294, secured against the wool customs.">Riccardi</dfn> of Lucca became royal bankers. They lent on a far larger scale than English moneylenders could, and they were paid back through the wool customs &mdash; the king redirected the duty straight to them at the ports until the loans were cleared. Between 1272 and 1294 the Riccardi advanced around &pound;400,000 to the crown, financing the first Welsh war, the early Welsh castle programme and Edward&rsquo;s diplomatic missions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The system collapsed in 1294. War with France meant French royal officers seized Riccardi assets in Paris; at the same moment Edward froze their English accounts to fund his own campaigns. The bank could not survive losing both ends and went bankrupt. Edward turned almost immediately to a new house from Florence, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Florentine banking house that took over royal lending in England after the collapse of the Riccardi in 1294, financing the Scottish campaigns until their own crash in 1311.">Frescobaldi</dfn>, who took over royal lending until they too crashed in 1311 under Edward II.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Medieval Christian teaching forbade Christians to lend at interest, calling the practice usury. Italian banking houses found ways around this through bills of exchange, partnership contracts and currency-conversion fees that produced profit without naming it as interest. They also operated on a continental scale &mdash; the Riccardi could collect debts in France, Flanders and the papal court &mdash; which English moneylenders could not match. Once Edward&rsquo;s Jewish lenders had been pushed out of moneylending by the 1275 Statute of the Jewry, the Italians were the only credit network big enough to finance a king at war.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Great Re-Coinage, 1279&ndash;80</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The English silver penny had been the only coin in regular use since the eleventh century, and after generations of clipping, sweating and counterfeiting most pennies in circulation were short of their proper weight. In 1279 Edward ordered a complete <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1279&ndash;80 reissue of the entire English coinage in heavier, harder-to-clip pennies, with new groats (4d) and farthings, restoring confidence in royal money.">re-coinage</dfn>. Old coins were called in and melted down. New pennies were struck on a heavier flan with a larger inscription that made clipping obvious; he also introduced a four-penny groat and a quarter-penny farthing for the first time. The scheme paid for itself through the seigniorage &mdash; the small profit the mint took from each coin &mdash; and gave merchants a trustworthy currency that supported the wool trade.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Statute of Merchants, 1285</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Edward&rsquo;s government also wrote rules to make trade safer for creditors. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1285 statute that gave merchants a fast legal procedure to register debts before a mayor and seize a debtor&rsquo;s land or goods if they were not paid on time.">Statute of Merchants</dfn> of 1285 (an extension of an earlier 1283 Statute of Acton Burnell) let creditors register a debt before the mayor of a chartered town. If the debt was not paid by the agreed date, the creditor could seize the debtor&rsquo;s movable goods and, after three months, his lands as well. For a wool merchant in Boston or a draper in Lincoln, the new procedure was revolutionary: it turned credit, the engine of commerce, into a faster, more enforceable contract. Foreign merchants gained too, encouraging Italian and Flemish buyers to pay English producers up front for next year&rsquo;s wool clip.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Most wool changed hands at the great fairs &mdash; St Ives, Stamford, Boston, Winchester and Northampton &mdash; where Italian and Flemish buyers met English producers. Towns chartered by the Crown to hold weekly markets and annual fairs paid the king for the privilege; in return their citizens enjoyed legal protections under the borough customs. Edward extended this network throughout his reign, granting hundreds of new market charters. Each one widened the kingdom&rsquo;s tax base.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences how the wool tax connected to the Welsh wars, naming one specific year.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1275 wool customs of half a mark per sack brought in around &pound;10,000 a year and were used to repay loans from the Riccardi of Lucca. The much heavier maltolt of 1294 &mdash; six times the ordinary rate &mdash; helped pay for the Madog rising in Wales and the start of the Scottish campaigns, but provoked a serious political crisis in 1297.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">English wool was the kingdom&rsquo;s main export; the 1275 wool customs of half a mark per sack gave Edward roughly &pound;10,000 a year and the heavier 1294 maltolt provoked a major political crisis.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Riccardi of Lucca lent around &pound;400,000 to the crown between 1272 and 1294, secured against the wool customs; the Frescobaldi of Florence took over after the Riccardi collapsed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The 1279&ndash;80 re-coinage produced a trustworthy currency, while the Statute of Merchants (1285) gave creditors a fast procedure to seize debtors&rsquo; goods and lands.</li>
</ul>
