--- DESCRIPTION ---
The disputed Scottish succession, Balliol’s brief reign and the sack of Berwick.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Throne Without an Heir</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In March 1286 the Scottish king Alexander III rode home through a stormy night to his new young queen at Kinghorn in Fife. His horse stumbled in the dark; he was thrown from a cliff and killed. With Alexander dead, all his children dead before him, Scotland was left without an adult heir. The throne passed to his three-year-old granddaughter, the &lsquo;Maid of Norway&rsquo;, Margaret &mdash; daughter of his daughter Margaret and Eric II of Norway. A council of six Scottish &lsquo;Guardians&rsquo; took over the daily government and looked for a long-term solution.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who died in 1286, who in 1290, and what dynastic problem each death produced for Scotland?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Scottish royal line collapsed in two stages. Alexander III died in March 1286 leaving no adult heir; his granddaughter <dfn class="term" data-def="Margaret of Norway, granddaughter of Alexander III, recognised as queen of Scots from 1286 until her death aged seven on the voyage from Norway to Scotland in autumn 1290.">Maid of Norway</dfn> succeeded as queen of Scots but died aged seven in autumn 1290 on the voyage to Scotland, leaving thirteen rival candidates for the throne.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Treaty of Birgham, 1290</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In July 1290 the Treaty of Birgham agreed that the Maid of Norway would marry Edward I&rsquo;s six-year-old heir, Edward of Caernarfon. The treaty insisted that Scotland would remain &lsquo;separate and divided&rsquo; from England, with its own laws, courts and customs &mdash; the two kingdoms united only in the persons of the future king and queen. The arrangement promised a peaceful merger of the two crowns. Then came catastrophe. In late September 1290 the seven-year-old Maid sickened and died in Orkney before reaching the Scottish mainland. The proposed marriage was over; the throne stood vacant.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Great Cause, 1291&ndash;92</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Thirteen claimants came forward for the empty throne. The two strongest were John Balliol, Lord of Galloway, descended through the elder daughter of Earl David of Huntingdon, and Robert Bruce the Competitor (grandfather of the future Robert I), descended through the younger daughter but a generation closer. Civil war between their factions looked likely. The Guardians invited Edward I to arbitrate. Edward agreed &mdash; on the condition that the candidates first acknowledge him as overlord of Scotland.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In May 1291, on a meadow outside Norham on the English bank of the Tweed, the candidates and the Guardians swore that Edward was &lsquo;Lord Paramount&rsquo; of Scotland. The hearings then opened. Over eighteen months &mdash; what Scottish writers later called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The arbitration of the disputed Scottish royal succession in 1291&ndash;92, in which Edward I judged between thirteen competitors and chose John Balliol, on condition that he himself was recognised as overlord of Scotland.">Great Cause</dfn> &mdash; English and Scottish lawyers heard the rival claims. On 17 November 1292 Edward gave judgement at Berwick: the throne went to John Balliol. The decision was good law &mdash; primogeniture through the elder line &mdash; but the price was Scottish acceptance of an English overlord with a foot inside the kingdom.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Earlier English kings had received occasional acts of homage from Scottish rulers, but Edward took these scattered precedents and turned them into a doctrine. By forcing the candidates to accept his overlordship before they were heard, he locked the new king into a permanent legal relationship. The principle Edward applied was simple: a king of Scots who held his crown by Edward&rsquo;s judgement could be summoned to English courts, asked for military service, and overruled in his own kingdom. This is what set Scottish nobles against him within four years.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Balliol&rsquo;s Brief Reign, 1292&ndash;96</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">John Balliol was crowned at Scone on 30 November 1292. His reign lasted barely three years and was disastrous. Edward repeatedly humiliated him &mdash; allowing Scottish appellants to bring lawsuits against the king of Scots in the English court at Westminster, treating Balliol as just another vassal. In 1294 Edward summoned Balliol with a feudal demand for Scottish troops to fight in his French war. The Scottish nobles refused. In July 1295 they appointed a council of twelve to govern in Balliol&rsquo;s name and signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1295 alliance between Scotland and France, agreed by John Balliol&rsquo;s council of twelve against Edward I; renewed many times over the next 250 years until the Scottish Reformation.">auld alliance</dfn> with Philip IV of France &mdash; the first formal treaty between Scotland and France. From an English perspective this was open rebellion.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Sack of Berwick, March 1296</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Edward responded with overwhelming force. In late March 1296 his army crossed the Tweed and besieged Berwick-upon-Tweed, then Scotland&rsquo;s richest port and a major centre of Flemish trade. The town&rsquo;s defences were poor and on 30 March Edward stormed it. What followed was a deliberate massacre. English chroniclers admitted that the slaughter went on for two days; some Scottish accounts put the dead at 7,000 men, women and children. The Flemish merchants of the Red Hall fought to the last and were burned alive when the building collapsed. Berwick&rsquo;s prosperity was destroyed; the town would never again be the trading capital of Scotland.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Dunbar and the Deposition of Balliol</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A Scottish army moved to relieve the border. On 27 April 1296 Edward&rsquo;s cavalry crushed it at the Battle of Dunbar; the Scottish leadership surrendered en masse. Edward marched north almost without resistance. At Montrose on 8 July 1296 he ritually stripped Balliol of his royal regalia &mdash; the empty surcoat earned him the nickname <dfn class="term" data-def="Scots for &lsquo;empty coat&rsquo; &mdash; the nickname given to John Balliol after Edward I publicly stripped him of his royal arms at Montrose on 8 July 1296.">Toom Tabard</dfn>, &lsquo;empty coat&rsquo;. Balliol was sent to the Tower of London. Edward took the great seal of Scotland, broke it into four pieces, and sent the symbols of Scottish kingship south &mdash; including the <dfn class="term" data-def="The ancient block of red sandstone on which Scottish kings were inaugurated; seized by Edward I in 1296 and placed in the Coronation Chair at Westminster Abbey, where it remained until 1996.">Stone of Scone</dfn>, the ancient inauguration stone of Scottish kings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">By August 1296 Edward had returned south claiming Scotland as a conquered territory. He left an English administration in place under John Warenne, Earl of Surrey. To Edward, the kingdom of Scotland had ceased to exist; it was now simply a province of his realm. To Scots, he had carried out a hostile occupation. The seeds of the long war had been sown.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Edward placed the Stone of Scone beneath a special oak chair, the Coronation Chair, in Westminster Abbey. Every English monarch since Edward II has been crowned over it. The stone became one of the most loaded political objects in British history: stolen briefly by Scottish nationalists in 1950 and finally returned to Scotland in 1996, seven hundred years after Edward had taken it. It is an exact measure of how powerful a symbol Edward had captured.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down four steps in chronological order between Alexander III&rsquo;s death and Balliol&rsquo;s deposition, with the year for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Great Cause (1291&ndash;92) made Edward I overlord of Scotland and chose John Balliol as king. Balliol&rsquo;s 1295 alliance with France triggered Edward&rsquo;s invasion of 1296: the sack of Berwick (30 March), the victory at Dunbar (27 April), and the public deposition of &lsquo;Toom Tabard&rsquo; at Montrose (8 July) ended the Scottish kingdom for the moment.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The deaths of Alexander III (1286) and the Maid of Norway (1290) left Scotland without an adult heir; the Great Cause of 1291&ndash;92 made Edward I the arbiter and recognised him as &lsquo;Lord Paramount&rsquo; of Scotland.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">John Balliol was chosen king in November 1292 but humiliated repeatedly by Edward; in 1295 his council of twelve signed the &lsquo;auld alliance&rsquo; with France, the first formal Scottish&ndash;French treaty.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The 1296 invasion saw the sack of Berwick, the victory at Dunbar, the deposition of &lsquo;Toom Tabard&rsquo; at Montrose and the seizure of the Stone of Scone &mdash; placed in Westminster Abbey until 1996.</li>
</ul>
