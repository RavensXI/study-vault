--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Community in Royal Hands</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Jewish community of medieval England arrived after the Norman Conquest of 1066 and lived under direct royal protection. Successive kings used Jews as a financial resource: only Jews were legally permitted to lend money at interest, a practice the Church called <dfn class="term" data-def="The lending of money at interest, forbidden to Christians by the medieval Church but permitted to Jews under royal licence.">usury</dfn>. In return for this protection the king could tax Jews directly, without parliamentary consent, through a special levy called <dfn class="term" data-def="A tax demanded by the king from his own subjects, especially towns and the Jewish community, without needing parliamentary approval.">tallage</dfn>. By 1272 the Jewish population was perhaps 2,000 to 3,000 people, concentrated in towns such as London, York, Lincoln, Norwich and Oxford.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how was the medieval English Jewish community legally different from Christian subjects, and who controlled them?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>England&rsquo;s Jews lived under direct royal protection. The king could tax them through tallage without consulting Parliament, and they were the only group legally permitted to charge interest on loans &mdash; making them indispensable to royal finance until alternatives appeared.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Centuries of Persecution Before 1290</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Persecution did not begin with Edward I. In 1144 the murder of a boy in Norwich produced the first <dfn class="term" data-def="The false accusation that Jews murdered Christian children to use their blood in religious rituals; sometimes called the &lsquo;blood libel&rsquo;.">blood libel</dfn> in Europe &mdash; the false accusation that Jews killed Christian children for ritual purposes. The cult of &lsquo;Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln&rsquo; in 1255 followed the same pattern, and nineteen Jews were hanged. In 1190 a violent <dfn class="term" data-def="An organised mob attack against a religious minority, especially against Jews; the word later came to describe pre-modern and modern antisemitic riots.">pogrom</dfn> at Clifford&rsquo;s Tower in York saw 150 Jews killed or driven to suicide. From 1218 English Jews were forced to wear a yellow badge, and after 1255 Henry III sold his rights over the Jewish community to his brother Richard of Cornwall to settle royal debts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Statute of the Jewry, 1275</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1275 Edward I issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1275 royal law that banned Jews from lending money at interest, forced them to wear identifying badges and pushed them into trade or farming.">Statute of the Jewry</dfn>. Its terms transformed Jewish life. Lending money at interest was now banned outright; existing debts were to be reduced. Jews were ordered to take up trade, farming or skilled crafts &mdash; occupations from which Christian guilds had long excluded them. They had to wear a larger yellow felt badge in the shape of the Tablets of the Law, and they were taxed at three pence a head from age twelve. Synagogues were restricted to a small list of named towns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Statute placed an impossible burden on the community. Cut off from their traditional livelihood and locked out of Christian craft guilds, many Jews fell into poverty. Some turned to coin clipping &mdash; shaving silver from the edges of pennies &mdash; for which 269 Jews were hanged in London in 1278. The community that remained was poor, vulnerable and visibly marked.</p>

Once the king had a richer source of credit, the Jewish community lost the practical value that had previously protected it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Edict of Expulsion, 18 July 1290</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">On 18 July 1290 Edward issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="The royal order of 18 July 1290 commanding all Jews to leave England by 1 November or face death; not formally repealed until 1656.">Edict of Expulsion</dfn>. Every Jew in England was ordered to leave the country by 1 November, the feast of All Saints. Those who remained could be killed without penalty. Royal officials were instructed to allow the exiles to take portable goods and small sums of money, but their houses, debts and synagogues passed to the Crown. Around 2,000 to 3,000 people were forced into ships at the Cinque Ports; some captains, paid by the head, robbed and drowned passengers in the Thames estuary.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The expulsion was not a sudden whim. It was announced in Parliament alongside a grant of taxation worth roughly &pound;116,000 &mdash; the largest single grant of Edward&rsquo;s reign. The grateful Commons were trading money for the removal of a community their leaders had long resented. Edward had also been pressed by his mother Eleanor of Provence and by the friars, and had practised the policy first in his lands in Gascony, expelling Jews there in 1287.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the 1290 expulsion was both a religious and a financial decision &mdash; quote one figure to support each side.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1290 expulsion was both an act of religious intolerance and a financial deal. Edward gained a parliamentary grant of around &pound;116,000 plus the seized property of expelled Jews, while Parliament secured a popular policy. Roughly 2,000&ndash;3,000 Jews left England by 1 November 1290.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Aftermath and Significance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">England was the first European kingdom to expel its Jewish community. France followed in 1306 and Spain in 1492. The English ban lasted 366 years &mdash; Jews were not formally readmitted until Oliver Cromwell allowed their return in 1656. For Edward&rsquo;s reign the immediate effect was financial: a one-off windfall, a popular taxation grant and the symbolic image of a Christian king cleansing his realm. The longer effect was a country with no openly practising Jewish community for over three centuries, which shaped English religious culture into the modern period.</p>

Michael Prestwich emphasises the political bargain with Parliament and the king&rsquo;s shift to Italian credit. Modern Jewish historians such as Robin Mundill see deeper cultural prejudice running through the previous century, of which the Statute of 1275 and the expulsion of 1290 were the climax. All three views agree that the decision was permanent in its effect on English religious life.</p>
