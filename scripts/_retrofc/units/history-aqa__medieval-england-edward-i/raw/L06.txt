--- DESCRIPTION ---
The thirteenth-century church, Oxford and Cambridge, and the rise of medieval English learning.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Church at the Heart of Daily Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every parish in Edward I&rsquo;s England gathered in a stone church on Sunday. The medieval Church taught children, kept the calendar, recorded births and deaths, fed the poor, and offered the sacraments through which souls reached heaven. The Pope in Rome appointed England&rsquo;s archbishops and bishops, and English kings sometimes clashed with him over taxation and appointments. By Edward&rsquo;s reign two old religious orders &mdash; the Benedictines and Cistercians &mdash; were being challenged from within by a new and more austere kind of clergy: the friars.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two main orders of friars in thirteenth-century England and one distinguishing feature of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two main orders of <dfn class="term" data-def="A member of a religious order who lived by begging and preaching among ordinary people, rather than withdrawing into a monastery; the most important were the Franciscans (Greyfriars) and Dominicans (Blackfriars).">friars</dfn> arrived in England in the 1220s: the Franciscans (founded by St Francis of Assisi, called Greyfriars from their grey habit) preached poverty and humility; the Dominicans (founded by St Dominic, called Blackfriars from their black cloak) emphasised study and preaching against heresy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Friars in Edward&rsquo;s England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Unlike monks, who lived enclosed in monasteries, friars lived among lay people. They went barefoot through town streets, begged for their bread and preached in the open air. They settled deliberately in the rough, growing parts of England&rsquo;s cities &mdash; Greyfriars in Newgate in London, Blackfriars next to the Thames at Ludgate, similar houses at Oxford, Cambridge, York and Norwich. The Franciscans were valued for their simplicity; the Dominicans, often university trained, became the leading preachers and confessors of their day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Edward used the friars too. They preached the cause of the crusades, served as ambassadors and were among the voices urging him toward the 1290 expulsion of the Jews. By the end of his reign, every major town in England had at least one friary and the friars had become a familiar part of the urban landscape.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Rise of the Universities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Oxford and Cambridge had grown out of cathedral schools in the twelfth century. By Edward&rsquo;s reign both were established <dfn class="term" data-def="In the medieval sense, a community of masters and scholars who taught and studied together under a recognised charter, awarding degrees in the arts, theology, law and medicine.">universities</dfn>. Students arrived as young as thirteen and worked through the seven liberal arts (grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) before moving on to higher faculties of theology, canon law, civil law or medicine. Most teaching was in Latin and almost all students were in minor orders &mdash; clerks of the Church &mdash; even if they never became full priests.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Lectures were free; what students paid for was lodging and food in the masters&rsquo; halls. To bring order, wealthy patrons began endowing halls of residence with their own statutes &mdash; the first medieval colleges. <dfn class="term" data-def="An academic and pastoral community of masters and students at a university, with its own buildings, statutes and endowed income; Oxford and Cambridge are the only English universities to keep this medieval structure.">Colleges</dfn> at Oxford grew quickly: Merton (1264, refounded by Walter de Merton 1274), Balliol (founded by John de Balliol&rsquo;s widow Dervorguilla in the 1260s, statutes 1282), and University College (which traced itself to a 1249 endowment). At Cambridge, Peterhouse was founded in 1284 by the Bishop of Ely. These colleges fixed Oxford and Cambridge into stable institutions that would dominate English higher learning for centuries.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The undergraduate course took six or seven years. Students attended lectures, where a master read aloud from a set text such as Aristotle&rsquo;s logic, and disputations, where they argued formal questions on either side of a problem. The dominant intellectual method was <dfn class="term" data-def="The medieval university method of finding truth by setting authoritative texts (especially the Bible, Church Fathers and Aristotle) against each other and resolving the contradictions through careful reasoning.">scholasticism</dfn> &mdash; setting authoritative texts (the Bible, the Church Fathers, Aristotle) against each other and reconciling them through tight reasoning. A bachelor&rsquo;s degree required candidates to defend their answers in formal debate; a master&rsquo;s required them to lecture for two years.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Roger Bacon and Experimental Science</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The most famous English scholar of the age was the Franciscan Roger Bacon (c.1219&ndash;1292), known as &lsquo;Doctor Mirabilis&rsquo; or the &lsquo;wonderful doctor&rsquo;. Bacon studied at Oxford and Paris, joined the Franciscans in the 1250s, and produced a vast body of writing on mathematics, optics, astronomy and the philosophy of language. In 1267, at the request of Pope Clement IV, he produced his masterwork the <dfn class="term" data-def="Roger Bacon&rsquo;s &lsquo;Greater Work&rsquo;, written for Pope Clement IV in 1267, arguing for the reform of Christian learning by combining scripture, languages, mathematics and experimental science.">Opus Majus</dfn> &mdash; the &lsquo;Greater Work&rsquo; &mdash; arguing that Christian learning had to combine scripture, languages, mathematics and experimental science.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Bacon&rsquo;s emphasis on experiment and direct observation was unusual for his age and earned him a controversial reputation. He proposed magnifying lenses, theorised about flying machines and gunpowder, and corrected the calendar long before his ideas were accepted. His Franciscan order distrusted his independence and at one point placed him under restriction, but his writings became a foundation reference for the experimental tradition centuries later.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Duns Scotus and the &lsquo;Subtle Doctor&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A second towering figure of the late thirteenth century was the Scotsman John Duns Scotus (c.1266&ndash;1308), who studied and lectured at Oxford in the 1290s before moving to Paris. He earned the title &lsquo;Doctor Subtilis&rsquo; &mdash; the Subtle Doctor &mdash; for the precision of his reasoning. Where Bacon emphasised experiment, Scotus refined philosophical theology, exploring how individual things could be known and arguing for the immaculate conception of the Virgin Mary &mdash; a doctrine the Church only formally accepted six centuries later. Scotus&rsquo;s work was so dominant in fifteenth- and sixteenth-century universities that those who rejected it were nicknamed &lsquo;Dunsmen&rsquo; &mdash; the origin of the modern word &lsquo;dunce&rsquo;.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Edward&rsquo;s relations with the Papacy were not always smooth. The 1279 Statute of Mortmain limited the Church&rsquo;s ability to acquire land in England without royal licence, asserting royal authority over a sphere previously dominated by canon law. In 1296, Pope Boniface VIII issued the bull <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal letter from the Pope, sealed with a leaden &lsquo;bulla&rsquo; bearing his name, used to issue authoritative decrees on doctrine, discipline or international affairs.">papal bull</dfn> Clericis Laicos forbidding lay rulers to tax the clergy without papal consent. Edward, deep in war with France and Scotland, simply outlawed any clergyman who refused to pay; many clergy quickly relented. The episode showed how willing Edward was to defy Rome when royal finance demanded it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences the difference between Roger Bacon&rsquo;s and Duns Scotus&rsquo;s contributions to thirteenth-century learning.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Roger Bacon (c.1219&ndash;1292) was the Franciscan author of the Opus Majus (1267), pushing for experiment and observation in Christian learning. John Duns Scotus (c.1266&ndash;1308), the &lsquo;Subtle Doctor&rsquo;, refined philosophical theology at Oxford and shaped university teaching for the following two centuries.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Franciscans (Greyfriars) and Dominicans (Blackfriars) brought a new, public form of religious life to thirteenth-century English towns and worked closely with the universities.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Oxford and Cambridge developed into stable universities under Edward I, with the first colleges &mdash; Merton, Balliol, University and Peterhouse &mdash; founded by 1284.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Roger Bacon&rsquo;s Opus Majus (1267) argued for experimental science within Christian learning, while Duns Scotus, the &lsquo;Subtle Doctor&rsquo;, dominated philosophical theology in the 1290s.</li>
</ul>
