--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why some places dry out more than others, and how communities respond.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Slow-Burning Hazard</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A long period of below-average rainfall that causes water shortages and damages crops, ecosystems and livelihoods.">drought</dfn> is unlike a cyclone or earthquake: there is no single moment of impact. It begins quietly with low rainfall, deepens as soils dry out, and only becomes a crisis after months or years — by which time crops have failed, reservoirs have shrunk and food prices have surged. Drought is the most expensive natural hazard for global agriculture and one of the hardest to define, because what counts as ‘dry’ depends on what a place is used to. The Lake District seeing 600 mm of rain a year is normal; East Anglia seeing 600 mm is generous; the Sahel seeing 600 mm is a disaster only if it usually receives 800 mm.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Arid Environments vs Drought</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A region with persistently low rainfall (under 250 mm/year) where dry conditions are the long-term norm.">arid environment</dfn> is one that is permanently dry — the Sahara, Atacama and central Australia receive less than 250 mm of rainfall a year as a baseline. Plants, animals and people adapt to that baseline. <strong>Drought</strong> is different: it is a temporary shortfall below normal rainfall, and it can hit even rainy places. The 2018 UK drought saw lawns brown, reservoirs drop and harvests fall by a quarter despite rainfall returning later that year. Confusing the two leads to weak exam answers — arid is a climate type, drought is an event.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in one sentence the difference between an arid environment and a drought.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Arid = permanently dry climate (under 250 mm rain/year). Drought = a temporary shortfall in rainfall below the local norm. Both can co-exist, but they are separate concepts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Causes of Drought</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Drought has three families of cause. <strong>Meteorological</strong> droughts begin when rainfall drops far below normal — typically because of shifts in the global circulation, sinking dry air at 30° latitude (where the Hadley cell descends), or warm-water/cool-water flips in the Pacific known as <dfn class="term" data-def="A periodic warming of surface waters in the central and eastern Pacific that disrupts global rainfall patterns.">El Niño</dfn> and <strong>La Niña</strong>. El Niño years drought-stress eastern Australia and southern Africa; La Niña tends to bring drought to the southern USA. <strong>Hydrological</strong> drought follows when rivers, reservoirs and groundwater stay low even after rain returns — aquifers can take decades to recharge. <strong>Human</strong> causes include over-extraction for agriculture (the Aral Sea has shrunk to 10% of its 1960 size from cotton irrigation), unsustainable dam-building upstream (the Mekong’s lower flow has been hit by Chinese dams), and large-scale deforestation that breaks the cycle of evaporation and rainfall (the Amazon basin sees longer dry seasons after forest loss).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Hadley cell pushes dry sinking air down at 30° latitude, creating the world’s great deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Australian, Atacama). Areas just outside that band — the Sahel south of the Sahara, the south-west USA, north-east Brazil — have rainfall that is fragile to begin with and easily tipped into drought by El Niño or by climate change shifting the cell polewards. Climate change is making sinking-air zones drier and rising-air zones wetter, intensifying the natural pattern.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Why Droughts Are Hazardous</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Droughts kill mainly through <strong>food and water shortages</strong>, not directly. Crop failure cuts farmer incomes and triggers food price spikes that hit the urban poor. Livestock die when grassland fails. Water rationing closes industries that need cooling water (power stations, paper mills). Wildfires become routine in tinder-dry vegetation: the 2019–20 Australian Black Summer fires, fuelled by the worst drought in centuries, killed 33 people, three billion animals and burnt 18 million hectares. Long droughts can drive permanent migration, as the Dust Bowl did in 1930s America when 2.5 million people abandoned the Great Plains.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Australia 2017–19 (Developed-Country Drought)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Eastern Australia experienced its driest period on record between 2017 and 2019. The Murray-Darling river system — which feeds 40% of Australia’s farms — ran near-dry; some towns reported reservoirs below 5% capacity. Sheep numbers crashed; wheat exports halved. The economic loss was estimated at AU$8 billion. Yet death tolls remained low because Australia is wealthy: water trucks were sent to towns; emergency loans propped up farms; psychological support services treated rural depression. The federal government released AU$7 billion in drought funding. The drought also triggered the Black Summer wildfires that followed in late 2019.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Ethiopia 2015–16 (Developing-Country Drought)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A severe El Niño triggered Ethiopia’s worst drought in 50 years. Around 10.2 million people needed food aid; over 400,000 children faced acute malnutrition; livestock losses ran into millions. The economic loss was harder to absorb than Australia’s: Ethiopia’s GDP per capita is about $1,000, agriculture employs 70% of the workforce, and many rural households had no insurance or savings. International appeals raised $700 million in emergency aid — less than half what the UN said was needed. Long-term responses included a Productive Safety Net Programme that pays the rural poor to build small dams, terraces and water-harvesting systems.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Top-down responses are large-scale and expensive: dams, desalination plants (Australia built six major plants after the Millennium Drought), inter-basin water transfers, drip irrigation subsidies, and crop insurance. Bottom-up responses are local and low-cost: rainwater harvesting from roofs into household tanks, drought-tolerant crop varieties (millet and sorghum instead of maize), terracing to slow runoff, and women’s cooperatives sharing wells. The most resilient countries combine both. Charity-led projects in Ethiopia (e.g. WaterAid’s sand dams) deliver water for under £30 per person reached.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the death toll, GDP loss and one response for both the Australian and Ethiopian droughts.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Australia 2017–19: AU$8 billion loss, near-empty Murray-Darling, low death toll, federal aid programmes. Ethiopia 2015–16: 10.2 million needed food aid, 400,000 children malnourished, $700m international appeal.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Drought is a temporary shortfall below normal rainfall — different from arid climate, which is permanently dry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Causes are meteorological (El Niño, Hadley cell), hydrological (low rivers/groundwater) and human (over-extraction, deforestation).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Wealth shapes the response: Australia (2017–19) absorbed AU$8 billion in losses with state aid; Ethiopia (2015–16) needed $700 million in international support to feed 10.2 million people.</li>
</ul>
