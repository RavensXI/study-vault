--- DESCRIPTION ---
How circulation cells distribute heat, and the UK's place within the system.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Planet Spinning & Heating Unevenly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Sun heats Earth’s surface unevenly. The equator receives roughly twice as much energy per square metre as the poles, because the Sun’s rays strike the equator almost vertically while striking the poles at a glancing angle. If nothing redistributed that heat, the tropics would be unbearably hot and the poles even colder. Instead, the atmosphere and oceans constantly transfer heat from low to high latitudes. The mechanism is <dfn class="term" data-def="The large-scale movement of air around the Earth that redistributes heat from the equator to the poles.">global atmospheric circulation</dfn> — six giant convection cells that drive everything from tropical storms to UK rainfall.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Three Cells in Each Hemisphere</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Each hemisphere has three cells stacked between the equator and the pole. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The atmospheric circulation cell between the equator and roughly 30° latitude, where rising air at the equator descends in the subtropics.">Hadley cell</dfn> runs from 0° to 30°. Warm equatorial air rises, cools, releases heavy rainfall (giving us tropical rainforests), then sinks at around 30° as dry air (giving us the Sahara, Kalahari and Australian deserts). The <strong>Ferrel cell</strong> runs from 30° to 60° — the UK sits inside this cell. Air rises again at around 60°, releasing rain over north-west Europe, then heads back down at the poles in the <strong>polar cell</strong>. Where cells meet, you find consistent climate signatures: rising air = wet, sinking air = dry.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch a north hemisphere cross-section showing the three cells, with rising/sinking arrows and the latitudes 0°, 30°, 60°, 90°.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Earth has six atmospheric cells — three in each hemisphere. Hot air rises at the equator and 60°; cool air sinks at 30° and the poles. Where air rises = wet; where air sinks = dry.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Ocean Currents: The Other Conveyor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Oceans transfer heat too. Surface currents driven by prevailing winds carry warm water polewards and cold water back to the equator. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A warm ocean current that flows from the Caribbean across the North Atlantic to north-west Europe, keeping the UK climate mild.">Gulf Stream</dfn>, part of the North Atlantic Drift, brings tropical-warm water northwards past the UK. Without it, Britain at 55°N would have a climate more like Canada at the same latitude — cold winters with sea ice. Instead, the Gulf Stream gives the UK its famous mild winters. Deep-ocean circulation, the ‘global conveyor belt’, takes thousands of years to complete one cycle but quietly redistributes heat across the planet.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The UK’s Climate Today</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The UK has a <strong>temperate maritime</strong> climate. Average winter temperatures sit around 4–7°C across most of England; summers average 16–19°C. Annual rainfall ranges from over 4,000 mm in the Lake District to under 600 mm in East Anglia. Three factors create that variation. <strong>Latitude</strong> — northern Scotland is cooler than southern England because it receives less solar energy. <strong>Altitude</strong> — temperatures fall by around 1°C for every 100 m of elevation, so the Highlands are colder than the Lowlands. <strong>Distance from the sea</strong> — coastal areas have milder winters but cooler summers because oceans warm and cool slowly compared with land.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The UK’s prevailing south-westerly winds blow off the Atlantic carrying moist air. When that air hits western mountains (the Highlands, Snowdonia, the Lake District) it is forced to rise. Rising air cools, water vapour condenses and rain falls as <strong>relief rainfall</strong>. By the time the air descends on the eastern side of the country, it has lost most of its moisture — the <strong>rain-shadow effect</strong>. This is why Cumbria gets over 3,000 mm a year while Cambridgeshire receives less than 600 mm.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK’s Position Inside the Cell</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The UK sits at the boundary between the Ferrel and polar cells, where rising air encourages low-pressure weather systems known as <strong>depressions</strong>. These are the rotating storms that bring most of Britain’s rainfall and grey skies. They form along the polar front, where warm subtropical air meets cold polar air; the contrast in temperature and density forces air upwards, creating cloud and precipitation. UK rainfall is therefore mostly <strong>frontal</strong>, with depressions tracking eastwards across the Atlantic, often two or three a week in winter.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">UK climate has shifted noticeably even within human history. The <strong>Medieval Warm Period</strong> (c.950–1250) was warm enough that vineyards grew across England as far north as Yorkshire. The <strong>Little Ice Age</strong> (c.1300–1850) was so cold that the Thames regularly froze and frost fairs were held on the ice in central London. Since 1850, average UK temperatures have risen by around 1°C, with the rate accelerating since 1980. Recent records: 40.3°C at Coningsby in July 2022 broke the all-time UK temperature record; six of the warmest UK years on record have happened since 2003.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK’s mild, wet climate is the product of: latitude (mid 50s °N); the Gulf Stream warming Atlantic air; and the prevailing south-westerly winds bringing moisture across western uplands.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Six atmospheric cells redistribute heat from equator to pole; rising air = wet, sinking air = dry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Gulf Stream and prevailing south-westerlies make the UK warmer and wetter than its latitude alone would suggest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Latitude, altitude and distance from sea explain regional variations within the UK’s temperate maritime climate.</li>
</ul>
