--- DESCRIPTION ---
How rivers reshape themselves from upper course to lower course, and the landforms produced.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A River’s Three Lives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A river changes character along its course. Geographers split the journey from source to mouth into three sections: <strong>upper course</strong> (high energy, narrow valley, vertical erosion); <strong>middle course</strong> (more discharge, gentler gradient, lateral erosion); <strong>lower course</strong> (lots of water, almost flat, deposition dominates). Each section produces its own landforms, but the same processes — <dfn class="term" data-def="The wearing away of the river bed and banks by the moving water and its sediment load.">erosion</dfn>, transport and <dfn class="term" data-def="The dropping of sediment when the river loses energy.">deposition</dfn> — do all the work, just in different proportions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Four Erosion Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Rivers erode in four ways. <strong>Hydraulic action</strong> uses the sheer force of the water to dislodge material, especially in cracks. <strong>Abrasion</strong> uses the river’s sediment as sandpaper, scraping at the bed. <strong>Attrition</strong> rounds and smashes the load itself as pebbles collide. <strong>Solution</strong> dissolves slightly soluble rocks like limestone. The mix changes downstream: hydraulic action and abrasion dominate in the upper course where boulders are tumbled by fast flow; attrition is most visible in the middle course where rounded pebbles signal long transport; solution operates everywhere but is invisible to the eye.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down what changes downstream: width, depth, gradient, velocity, sediment size. State what each does (increase/decrease).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Going downstream: width increases, depth increases, gradient decreases, velocity actually <em>increases</em> (less friction in a wider, deeper channel), discharge increases, sediment size decreases and rounding increases.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Upper-Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In the steep upper course, the river has limited water but high energy because of gradient. Vertical erosion cuts down hard, producing a narrow V-shaped valley with <dfn class="term" data-def="Ridges of higher land that project alternately into a valley, forcing the river to weave around them.">interlocking spurs</dfn> — ridges that the young river weaves around because it lacks the power to cut through them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <strong>waterfall</strong> forms where a band of hard rock lies above softer rock. The softer rock erodes faster and is undercut, leaving the hard cap unsupported. Eventually the cap collapses; the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep-sided <strong>gorge</strong> behind. High Force on the River Tees and Aysgarth Falls in Yorkshire are classic UK examples. <strong>River cliffs</strong> — small, steep banks — appear at the outside of bends even in upper courses.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Middle-Course Landforms: Meanders & Floodplains Begin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">As the gradient eases, the river begins to swing sideways. Where flow is slightly stronger on the outside of a bend (deeper, faster water), erosion creates a <strong>river cliff</strong>; where flow is slower on the inside (shallower, less energy), deposition builds a <strong>point bar</strong> of sediment. Repeated cycles deepen the curve into a full <dfn class="term" data-def="A pronounced bend in a river formed by erosion on the outside of the curve and deposition on the inside.">meander</dfn>. Over decades the meander migrates across the valley floor, widening the floodplain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Lower-Course Landforms: Floodplains, Levees & Oxbow Lakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the lower course the river is wide, deep and full of fine sediment. Each flood spreads water across the broad <strong>floodplain</strong>, and as that water slows on the flat ground it drops its load — coarsest first, then finer particles. This builds raised banks called <strong>levees</strong> immediately next to the channel and gradually deepens the fertile floodplain soil beyond. <strong>Oxbow lakes</strong> form when a meander becomes so pronounced that the river cuts through its own narrow neck during a flood; the abandoned curve becomes a crescent-shaped lake that slowly silts up.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">The <strong>Bradshaw model</strong> shows how channel and discharge variables change downstream. As you move from source to mouth: discharge, width, depth, average velocity and load quantity all rise; gradient, sediment size and channel roughness fall. Many textbooks then add: load roundness rises (from angular boulders to smooth pebbles); valley shape changes from V to broad U; cross-profile shifts from steep banks to wide floodplain. Edexcel may show you part of the model and ask you to predict the missing variable — learn the directions, not the exact numbers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A storm hydrograph plots rainfall (bars) and river discharge (line) over time after a storm. The <strong>lag time</strong> is the gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge — short lag = flashy river, long lag = slower response. UK upland catchments with steep slopes and impermeable rock have short lag times; lowland catchments with permeable chalk, vegetation and large floodplains have long lag times. Urbanisation shortens lag times because impermeable concrete sends rainwater straight into drains.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: sketch a meander showing the river cliff on the outside, point bar on the inside, and label the direction of fastest flow.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meanders form because flow is fastest on the outside of a bend (eroding) and slowest on the inside (depositing). Repeating this widens the curve until a flood breaks across the neck, creating an oxbow lake.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Rivers vertically erode in the upper course, laterally erode in the middle, and deposit in the lower course.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Waterfalls and gorges form where hard rock caps softer rock; meanders evolve into oxbow lakes by neck cut-off.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Bradshaw model summarises how channel variables change downstream — learn the directions of change.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">For Edexcel, river-landform questions almost always want process plus location. Saying “a waterfall is where a river falls down” gets nothing. Saying “at High Force, hard whinstone caps softer limestone; abrasion undercuts the limestone, the cap collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream leaving a gorge” scores fully. For 4-mark describe questions on landforms, name the section of the river too — markers are looking for ‘upper course’, ‘middle course’ or ‘lower course’ signposting.</p>
