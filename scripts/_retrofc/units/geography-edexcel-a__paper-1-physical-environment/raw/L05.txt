--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Rivers Flood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An overflow of water from a river channel onto land that is normally dry.">flood</dfn> happens when the volume of water in a river exceeds the capacity of its channel. Both natural and human factors push rivers over the edge. Natural causes include heavy or prolonged rainfall, snowmelt on saturated ground, low-permeability rocks like clay that send water straight into the river, and steep upland slopes that speed runoff. Human causes include urbanisation (concrete prevents infiltration), deforestation (no roots to soak up water), and agriculture (compacted soil and field drains). All of these shorten <strong>lag time</strong> on the hydrograph — meaning the river peaks faster after a storm.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three natural and three human causes of UK flooding. Test yourself again the next day.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK flooding is rarely one cause. Major events combine prolonged frontal rainfall, saturated catchments, urban runoff and historical changes to land use. Always discuss multiple causes in extended writing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Hard Engineering: Containing the River</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Large engineered structures used to control river flow, such as dams, channelisation and flood walls.">Hard engineering</dfn> tries to contain or speed up the river. <strong>Dams and reservoirs</strong> store flood water and release it slowly, also providing drinking water and hydropower. The Kielder dam in Northumberland holds 200 million cubic metres of water but cost £167 million in the 1980s and drowned a Northumbrian valley. <strong>Channelisation</strong> — straightening and widening a river so flood water flows away faster — works for the protected stretch but pushes higher peaks downstream and damages river ecology by removing riffles, pools and bankside habitat. Even simple <strong>flood walls</strong> count as hard engineering: the Thames Barrier (1982) protects central London from tidal surge floods, and the Foss Barrier in York holds back surge water from the Ouse during winter floods.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Soft Engineering: Working With the Catchment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="River management that works with natural processes, such as floodplain zoning, washlands and restoration.">Soft engineering</dfn> takes a different approach — it works with the catchment rather than fighting it. <strong>Floodplain zoning</strong> uses land-use planning instead of concrete: fields and parks beside the river are accepted as places where water will spread; housing and infrastructure are kept on higher ground. <strong>Washlands</strong> are deliberately maintained low areas next to the river that flood naturally, like the Ouse Washes in East Anglia where 24 km<sup>2</sup> of farmland take up flood water in winter. <strong>River restoration</strong> removes old channelisation, allowing rivers to meander again. The River Skerne in Darlington was famously re-meandered in the 1990s; flood peaks downstream fell measurably and wildlife returned. Combined with <strong>upland peat rewetting</strong> in the Pennines and <strong>tree planting</strong> in upper catchments, soft engineering is increasingly the preferred long-term strategy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">A Distinctive UK River: The River Tees</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The River Tees flows 137 km from Cross Fell in the North Pennines to the North Sea at Middlesbrough. Its journey runs through every classic landform. In the upper course at <strong>High Force</strong>, the river drops 22 metres over a layer of hard whinstone capping softer carboniferous limestone, retreating slowly upstream and leaving a steep wooded gorge. The middle course swings through a wide-floored valley with classic meanders near Yarm. The lower course flows across a broad floodplain to a large tidal estuary at Teesside.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Human activity has reshaped the Tees throughout its course. Cow Green Reservoir (built 1970) regulates flow upstream. Channel improvements at Yarm and a flood-relief storage area at Hurworth Burn protect riverside towns. The estuary has been heavily industrialised since the 1850s — reclaimed for steelworks, chemical plants and the modern port — reducing the natural mudflats and salt marsh that once buffered storm surges.</p>

Saturated ground from earlier rainfall reduces infiltration; tidal locking at the estuary — where high tide blocks river outflow — can amplify upstream peaks. The Met Office has linked record-breaking 2015 (Cumbria) and 2019–20 (Yorkshire, South Wales) floods to climate change. Future-proofing rivers therefore requires both grey infrastructure (defences) and green infrastructure (rewetted upland peat, replanted catchments, washlands) so the catchment as a whole holds more water before it reaches the channel.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The River Tees rises at Cross Fell (~893 m), flows 137 km through the High Force waterfall, meanders near Yarm, and enters the North Sea at Middlesbrough. Each course has been shaped by both physical and human change.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">UK flooding is a mix of natural (rainfall, geology, slope) and human (urbanisation, deforestation, drainage) causes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Hard engineering (dams, channelisation) protects but moves the problem; soft engineering (zoning, washlands, restoration) reduces peaks naturally.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The River Tees illustrates every river course and the trade-offs of human management.</li>
</ul>
