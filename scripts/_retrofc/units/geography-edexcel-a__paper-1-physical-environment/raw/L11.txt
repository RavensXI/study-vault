--- DESCRIPTION ---
Distribution of biomes, the importance of the biosphere, and the UK’s distinctive ecosystems.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Living Planet, Mapped</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">From space, Earth’s land surface looks like a patchwork of greens, browns and yellows. Each colour reflects a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large-scale ecosystem defined by climate and the plants and animals that live in it, such as tropical rainforest or tundra.">biome</dfn> — a continent-sized ecosystem shaped above all by climate. Tropical rainforests cluster around the equator. Hot deserts sit at 30° latitude where the Hadley cell descends. Temperate grasslands and deciduous forests fill the mid-latitudes. Boreal coniferous forests (the taiga) form a giant belt across northern Canada, Scandinavia and Russia. Tundra fringes the Arctic. The pattern is not random — it is the global circulation made visible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What Climate & Local Factors Do</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Two climate variables decide which biome forms where. <strong>Temperature</strong> controls how long a growing season is and which plants can survive winter; <strong>rainfall</strong> controls how lush vegetation can grow before water runs short. Plot any spot on Earth on a graph of mean temperature versus rainfall and you can predict its biome to within one band. <strong>Soil</strong> and <strong>altitude</strong> add finer detail. Tropical soils are deep but nutrient-poor (because heavy rain leaches them); temperate soils are deep and fertile; boreal soils are thin and acidic. Every 100 m of altitude is roughly equivalent to moving 100 km polewards — which is why mountainsides reproduce the global pattern in miniature, with rainforest below cloud forest below alpine meadow.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch a world map labelling rainforest, hot desert, savanna, temperate forest, taiga and tundra at the right latitudes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Climate (temperature and rainfall) sets the global pattern of biomes. Hot wet near the equator = rainforest. Hot dry at 30° = desert. Cool wet at 50–60° = boreal forest. Cold dry near the poles = tundra.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Biosphere as a Resource</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total of all living things on Earth, considered as a system that supplies resources and regulates the environment.">biosphere</dfn> is the global sum of all ecosystems — everywhere life exists. People depend on it for four main things. <strong>Food</strong> — over 7,000 plant species are eaten and 75% of global food comes from just 12 of them. <strong>Medicine</strong> — a quarter of modern drugs (including aspirin from willow and the cancer drug Taxol from Pacific yew) are biosphere-derived. <strong>Building materials</strong> — timber, bamboo, thatch, and indirectly limestone (from ancient sea creatures). <strong>Fuel</strong> — firewood still cooks the meals of 2.4 billion people; modern bioenergy adds 6% of UK electricity. The biosphere is also exploited <strong>commercially</strong> for energy (oil from former forests), water (irrigation extraction) and minerals (open-cast mining), each of which can damage the very ecosystems we depend on.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Plants take CO<sub>2</sub> out of the atmosphere through photosynthesis and release oxygen; they also pump water back into the air through transpiration. Tropical forests alone store an estimated 250 gigatonnes of carbon — more than humans have emitted from fossil fuels since 1850. Soils store an additional 2,500 gigatonnes. Damage to ecosystems doesn’t just lose biodiversity; it releases stored carbon and reduces the planet’s ability to recycle gases. The biosphere is therefore a regulator as much as a resource.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">UK Terrestrial Ecosystems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The UK is small but ecologically varied. <strong>Moorlands</strong> (about 12% of UK land area) cover upland Britain — peaty soils, heather, grouse and pipits dominate; the North York Moors and the Pennines are classic examples. <strong>Heathlands</strong> are lower-altitude open habitats on poor sandy soils — the New Forest and Dorset heaths support reptiles such as the smooth snake and Dartford warbler. <strong>Woodlands</strong> are mainly deciduous (oak, ash, beech) in the south and coniferous (Scots pine in the Caledonian forest) in northern Scotland. <strong>Wetlands</strong> — reed beds, fens, bogs — cover the Norfolk Broads, the Somerset Levels and Caerlaverock; they are migratory bird hotspots. Each ecosystem evolved with farming over millennia, so most is ‘semi-natural’ rather than truly wild.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK Marine Ecosystems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">UK seas are productive because cool Atlantic water mixes with shallow continental shelves. <strong>Cod, herring, mackerel</strong> and <strong>shellfish</strong> support a fishing industry worth around £1 billion at the dockside. <dfn class="term" data-def="Coastal wetlands flooded by tides where salt-tolerant plants grow on muddy ground.">Salt marshes</dfn> on coasts like Morecambe Bay buffer storms and store carbon. <strong>Kelp forests</strong> off western Scotland host octopus, lobster and seal. But human pressure is severe: industrial trawling has cut North Sea cod stocks to a fraction of 1970s levels; sewage discharges have made many beaches unsafe; offshore oil and gas extraction has degraded seabed habitats. The 2009 Marine and Coastal Access Act created Marine Protected Areas to slow the damage; today, around 38% of UK seas have some level of protection on paper, though enforcement varies.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">The <strong>Caledonian Forest</strong> in the Scottish Highlands is the UK’s only fragment of true boreal woodland — a relict of the post-Ice Age forest that once covered most of Scotland. The <strong>chalk grasslands</strong> of the South Downs (Sussex, Hampshire) are some of the richest grasslands in Europe, with up to 40 plant species per square metre. The <strong>Flow Country</strong> in northern Scotland is the world’s largest blanket bog, storing 400 million tonnes of carbon — designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024. Each is irreplaceable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name four UK terrestrial ecosystem types with one place example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK terrestrial ecosystems include moorland (Pennines), heathland (New Forest), woodland (Caledonian Forest, Sussex Weald) and wetland (Norfolk Broads). Marine ecosystems support a £1 billion fishing industry but face pressure from trawling, pollution and oil and gas extraction.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Biome distribution is set by climate (temperature + rainfall) with soil and altitude as local modifiers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The biosphere supplies food, medicine, building materials and fuel — and regulates atmosphere, water and carbon cycles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">UK ecosystems include moorlands, heaths, woodlands, wetlands and productive seas — all under pressure from farming, fishing and pollution.</li>
</ul>
