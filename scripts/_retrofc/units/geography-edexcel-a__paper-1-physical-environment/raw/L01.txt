--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why upland and lowland landscapes form where they do, and the rocks that shape them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A UK Built From Three Rock Types</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Stand on Ben Nevis and you are on hard, ancient rock. Stand in the Fens and you are on soft sediment laid down by rivers. The UK’s scenery makes sense once you understand its <dfn class="term" data-def="The study of the rocks that make up the Earth's crust and the structures they form.">geology</dfn>. Three rock families do most of the work. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock formed in layers from compressed sediment, such as chalk, sandstone or clay.">Sedimentary</dfn> rocks like chalk, sandstone and clay formed in layers under ancient seas, deserts or river deltas, with each layer pressed into stone over millions of years. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock formed when magma or lava cools and solidifies, such as granite or basalt.">Igneous</dfn> rocks like granite and basalt formed when molten rock cooled, either deep underground (granite) or after erupting onto the surface (basalt). <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock changed by extreme heat or pressure, such as schist or slate.">Metamorphic</dfn> rocks like slate and schist were once other rocks but have been altered, deep underground, by heat and crushing pressure during mountain building.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three rock families with one example of each. Then state which family dominates UK uplands.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK uplands are dominated by hard igneous and metamorphic rocks (granite, slate). UK lowlands are dominated by softer sedimentary rocks (chalk, sandstone, clay). Resistance to erosion controls relief.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Uplands Are Where They Are</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Look at a relief map of Britain and the high ground sits firmly in the north and west — the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, Snowdonia, the Pennines, Dartmoor. These are not random locations. They sit on resistant rocks formed during ancient mountain-building periods. The original mountains were Himalayan in scale; weathering and glaciation have ground them down, but the toughest cores remain. Granite intrusions on Dartmoor and the Cairngorms, and metamorphic schists across the Highlands, resist erosion better than the rocks around them. Slowly, over hundreds of millions of years, the surrounding softer rocks have been worn away while the granite and slate have stayed standing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">This explains the famous “Tees-Exe line” that geographers draw across England. To the north and west of that line, hard rock and high ground; to the south and east, soft rock and lower ground. The line is not a perfect boundary — the chalk Wolds in Yorkshire and the granite of north-east Scotland complicate it — but as a rule of thumb it is remarkably useful.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Why Lowlands Are Where They Are</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Most of the south and east of the UK lies on younger sedimentary rocks. The chalk of the South Downs, the limestone of the Cotswolds and the clay of the East Anglian Fens were all laid down in shallow tropical seas, deserts or estuaries when, hundreds of millions of years ago, the UK was much closer to the equator. Rivers and ice sheets later eroded these softer rocks into the gentle, rolling lowlands students see today. Where harder bands of chalk or sandstone outcrop, you get <strong>escarpments</strong> — one steep scarp face and one gentle dip slope — like the North and South Downs framing the Weald.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Around 400 million years ago the Caledonian orogeny squeezed and folded the rocks of Scotland and northern England, producing the metamorphic rocks of the Highlands and Lake District and lifting the original Caledonian mountains. Around 300 million years ago the Variscan orogeny did the same to the south west, producing the granite that today forms the tors of Dartmoor and Bodmin Moor. These ancient mountain roots are why upland Britain runs roughly NW-SE: the geological ‘backbone’ lies on that line. Younger rocks were never folded as severely, so they sit flatter and form the lowlands.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Physical & Human Processes Shape Today’s Landscapes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Geology supplies the raw material; processes shape it. <dfn class="term" data-def="Erosion and deposition by ice during the most recent glacial period (around 18,000 years ago).">Glacial action</dfn> carved out the U-shaped valleys of Snowdonia, the corries of the Cairngorms and the ribbon lakes of Cumbria during the last Ice Age. <dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of rock by physical, chemical or biological action.">Weathering</dfn> — especially freeze-thaw on exposed peaks — continues to chip away at upland rock today, supplying scree to slopes. Post-glacial rivers carry that sediment downhill, and slow slope processes such as soil creep gradually smooth hillsides.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Humans have left their mark too. Centuries of sheep farming have kept the Lake District and the Pennines treeless, even though the natural cover would be deciduous woodland. Forestry plantations of conifers replace native species in many uplands. In the lowlands, settlement and agriculture have drained and reshaped the East Anglian Fens. Even the ‘wild’ uplands are therefore cultural landscapes — the outcome of physical and human processes acting together on the underlying geology.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A geological cross-section is a side-on slice through the ground showing how rock layers stack. To read one: identify the rock types from the key, look at whether layers are flat (lowland-forming) or tilted and folded (often upland-forming), and note where harder bands stand proud of softer ones. The link is direct: harder rock at the surface tends to mean higher ground. Cross-sections often appear in exam questions paired with a geological map — practise switching between the two.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two physical processes and two human processes that have shaped UK landscapes since the last Ice Age.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK landscapes today are shaped by past glaciation, ongoing weathering and slope processes, and centuries of human activity (farming, forestry, settlement). All four work on top of the underlying geology.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">UK uplands sit on resistant igneous and metamorphic rocks; lowlands sit on softer sedimentary rocks.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Two ancient mountain-building periods (Caledonian, Variscan) explain the NW-SE pattern of upland Britain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Today’s landscapes reflect physical processes (glaciation, weathering, slope) layered with human use (farming, forestry, settlement).</li>
</ul>
