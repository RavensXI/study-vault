Two contrasting biomes: their structure, biodiversity, threats and sustainable management.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Forests, Two Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tropical rainforests and deciduous woodlands lie at opposite ends of the global forest spectrum. Rainforests grow at the equator under year-round heat and 2,000–3,000 mm of rain; they hold over half of Earth’s known species. Deciduous woodlands grow across the temperate mid-latitudes — the UK, France, eastern USA, China — where four distinct seasons mean <dfn class="term" data-def="Trees that shed their leaves seasonally, typically in autumn, to survive cold or dry conditions.">deciduous</dfn> trees shed their leaves each autumn to survive winter. Both biomes are <dfn class="term" data-def="A community of plants, animals and the non-living environment that links them, where each part depends on the others.">interdependent ecosystems</dfn>: every change to climate, soil or species ripples through the rest.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Tropical Rainforest Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A rainforest has a layered, vertical structure. The <strong>emergent layer</strong> reaches over 50 m high — tall trees like the kapok poke through the canopy to catch full sunlight. The <strong>main canopy</strong> at 25–45 m is a continuous green roof; here most photosynthesis happens, and most species live. The <strong>understorey</strong> at 5–20 m is darker, with smaller trees, ferns and palms competing for filtered light. The <strong>forest floor</strong> sees just 2% of incoming light, so it is open and easy to walk through, with leaf litter that decomposes within weeks in the warm wet conditions. Trees adapt with <strong>buttress roots</strong> to support their height, <strong>drip tips</strong> to shed heavy rain, and <strong>lianas</strong> — woody vines that climb up to the canopy without growing trunks of their own. Animals adapt with strong limbs (sloths, monkeys), modified beaks (toucans), and camouflage (jaguars, leaf insects).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sketch the four rainforest layers labelling at least one example tree, one adaptation and one animal in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tropical rainforests have four layers: emergent, canopy, understorey, forest floor. Plants adapt with buttress roots, drip tips and lianas; animals with camouflage, strong limbs and modified beaks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Nutrient Cycle in Both Biomes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing how nutrients move between biomass, soil and litter in an ecosystem, with circle sizes indicating storage and arrows indicating flows.">Gersmehl nutrient cycle</dfn> shows three stores — biomass (living matter), soil and litter — with arrows for flows between them. In <strong>tropical rainforests</strong>, biomass is huge but soil and litter stores are tiny: rapid decomposition by termites and fungi means nutrients are cycled almost immediately back into the trees. Cut the trees down and the soil quickly becomes infertile. In <strong>deciduous woodlands</strong>, all three stores are more balanced: leaf fall builds a deep litter layer in autumn that decomposes slowly through the cold winter, releasing nutrients each spring. This is why temperate soils are deep and fertile (and why so much of the UK was forested before farming).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Why Rainforests Are Under Threat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Roughly 17% of the Amazon has already been cleared. Drivers include <strong>conversion to agriculture</strong> (cattle ranching for beef, soya plantations for animal feed) — together these two activities account for most Amazon loss. <strong>Resource extraction</strong> (logging hardwoods, illegal gold mining, oil drilling in Ecuador) cuts roads and access through previously remote forest. <strong>Population pressure</strong> brings smallholder farmers along those roads. Climate change adds another pressure: rising temperatures and longer dry seasons stress trees, increasing fire risk — the Amazon has burnt at record rates in 2019, 2020 and 2023. Lose the forest and you lose biodiversity, indigenous livelihoods, the rainforest carbon sink, and even the rainfall it generates for southern Brazilian agriculture.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Costa Rica reversed the steepest deforestation in Latin America. In 1983 only 21% of the country was forested; by 2024 over 60% was. The strategy combined: <strong>governance</strong> — banning new deforestation in 1996; <strong>economic instruments</strong> — the world’s first national Payments for Ecosystem Services scheme paying landowners to keep forest standing; and <strong>ecotourism</strong> — over 3 million visitors a year now generate $4 billion of annual revenue, providing better incomes than cattle ranching. Costa Rica is small and democratic; replicating its model in the Amazon is harder, but the principle — pay people more to keep the forest than to cut it — is widely copied.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">UK Deciduous Woodland: Structure & Threats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">UK deciduous woodlands have three layers: a canopy of oak, ash and beech (15–30 m); an understorey of holly, hazel and field maple; and a ground layer of bluebells, ferns and brambles. Animals include badgers, foxes, woodpeckers and hibernating dormice. Adaptations differ from rainforest: leaves are <strong>broad and flat</strong> to maximise photosynthesis in summer but <strong>shed in autumn</strong> before frost can damage them; many animals <strong>migrate</strong> (cuckoos, swallows), <strong>hibernate</strong> (hedgehogs, dormice) or <strong>store food</strong> (squirrels) for winter. Threats are different too: <strong>urbanisation and population growth</strong> have cleared woodland for housing (the UK is now only 13% forested, well below European average); <strong>timber extraction</strong> still occurs but largely from managed plantations; <strong>agricultural change</strong> — particularly the post-war drive for arable land — has fragmented ancient woodlands into isolated patches.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The New Forest in Hampshire (designated a National Park in 2005) preserves 580 km<sup>2</sup> of woodland, heath and bog. Management blends modern conservation with practices going back a thousand years: <strong>commoners’ rights</strong> let local people graze ponies and pigs (which keeps glades open); <strong>coppicing</strong> rotates the cutting back of hazel and chestnut on a 7-year cycle, regenerating the woodland and producing wood; <strong>visitor management</strong> uses fenced paths and car-park caps to handle 13 million annual visitors without trampling the bluebells. Funding comes from a mix of National Park budgets, EU/UK agri-environment schemes and Forestry Commission timber sales.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <p>Costa Rica restored forest from 21% (1983) to over 60% (2024) using government bans, payments for ecosystem services and ecotourism. The New Forest preserves UK woodland through commoners’ grazing, coppicing and visitor management.</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Rainforests have four layers; deciduous woodlands have three. Both rely on tightly interdependent biotic and abiotic features.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Rainforests have huge biomass but tiny soil stores; deciduous woodlands have balanced biomass, soil and litter.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Costa Rica (forest cover 21% &rarr; 60%) and the New Forest illustrate that economic incentives plus governance can keep forests standing.</li>
