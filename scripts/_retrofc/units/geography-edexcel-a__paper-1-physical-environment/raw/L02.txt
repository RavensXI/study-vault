--- DESCRIPTION ---
Erosion, transport and deposition shaping cliffs, stacks, beaches and spits.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Coast as a System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A coastline is a working system. Energy from waves, wind and tides arrives at the shore and reshapes it constantly. The geographer’s job is to track where the energy comes from, what it does to the rock and sediment, and where the material ends up. Three families of process do almost all the work: <dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of rock in place by physical, chemical or biological processes.">weathering</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The wearing away of rock and sediment by moving water, wind or ice.">erosion</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The dropping of sediment when water, wind or ice loses energy and can no longer carry it.">deposition</dfn>. Between erosion and deposition sits transport, which simply moves sediment from one place to another.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Waves: Constructive and Destructive</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Waves are the engine of the coast. <strong>Constructive waves</strong> are low and long. Their swash (the rush up the beach) is strong, while the backwash is weak, so they push sediment up the beach and build it up. <strong>Destructive waves</strong> are tall and steep. Their backwash is stronger than their swash, so they drag sediment back out to sea and tear into cliffs. The UK’s storm-prone west coast sees more destructive waves; sheltered east-coast bays see more constructive ones.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four types of erosion with one sentence on each. Test yourself again tomorrow.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four marine erosion processes are: hydraulic action (wave force compresses air in cracks), abrasion (rocks scrape the cliff), attrition (rocks collide and round), and solution (slightly acidic seawater dissolves limestone or chalk).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">How Sediment Travels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Once eroded, material has to go somewhere. Four transport processes carry it along. <strong>Traction</strong> rolls heavy pebbles along the seabed; <strong>saltation</strong> bounces smaller pebbles in short hops; <strong>suspension</strong> carries fine sand and silt within the water; and <strong>solution</strong> dissolves chemicals like calcium carbonate that travel invisibly. Most importantly, <dfn class="term" data-def="The zigzag movement of sediment along a coast caused by waves arriving at an angle.">longshore drift</dfn> moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind. Waves push sand up the beach at an angle (swash) but pull it straight back down (backwash), so each grain shifts slightly along the coast every cycle.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Erosional Landforms: Headlands, Bays, Cliffs & Stacks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the sea at right angles, the soft rock erodes faster, leaving the hard rock sticking out as a <strong>headland</strong> with <strong>bays</strong> between. The Dorset coast at Swanage shows this perfectly: chalk and limestone form headlands; soft clay forms the bays.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">On a single headland, weaknesses in the rock evolve into a sequence: a small <strong>crack</strong> widens into a <strong>cave</strong>; two caves on opposite sides of a headland meet to form an <strong>arch</strong>; the arch’s roof eventually collapses, leaving a <strong>stack</strong> isolated in the sea; the stack is undercut and falls, becoming a shorter <strong>stump</strong>. The Old Harry Rocks on the Jurassic Coast and the stacks at Flamborough Head are textbook examples. Cliffs themselves retreat by the same processes: undercutting at the wave-cut notch causes the rock above to collapse, leaving a flat <dfn class="term" data-def="A flat, gently sloping rock platform exposed at low tide, formed as a cliff retreats.">wave-cut platform</dfn>.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">A <strong>concordant</strong> coastline runs parallel to bands of rock — the sea meets one rock type along its length. A <strong>discordant</strong> coastline cuts across alternating hard and soft bands, producing the headland-and-bay pattern. Joints, faults and bedding planes are weaknesses where erosion bites first; that is why caves and arches form along visible cracks rather than across solid rock.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Depositional Landforms: Beaches, Spits & Bars</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Where waves lose energy — in sheltered bays, behind headlands, or where the coast suddenly changes direction — sediment piles up. <strong>Beaches</strong> form between the high and low tide marks. Sandy beaches are gentle (low-energy waves); pebble beaches are steeper (higher-energy storms). A <strong>spit</strong> forms where longshore drift carries sediment beyond a sudden bend in the coast and deposits it into open water; the end of the spit often hooks back, sheltering a salt marsh behind it. Spurn Head on the Yorkshire coast is a classic UK spit. A <strong>bar</strong> forms when a spit grows all the way across a bay, trapping a lagoon behind it.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The UK’s prevailing south-westerly winds drive bigger waves onto the south and west coasts — so erosion rates are higher there. Storm frequency in autumn and winter intensifies destructive-wave activity. The Holderness coast in Yorkshire, made of soft boulder clay, retreats at around 2 metres per year — one of the fastest rates in Europe. Hard-rock Cornish coasts retreat by only millimetres per year over the same period.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and sketch the headland-cave-arch-stack-stump sequence with arrows. Label what the sea does at each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Erosional landforms evolve in a sequence: crack → cave → arch → stack → stump. Hydraulic action and abrasion drive each stage; weathering above weakens the cliff so collapse follows.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Constructive waves build beaches; destructive waves erode cliffs.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Erosional sequences (crack&rarr;cave&rarr;arch&rarr;stack) develop where hard rock and weaknesses meet wave attack.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Depositional landforms (beaches, spits, bars) form where waves lose energy and longshore drift drops sediment.</li>
</ul>
