--- DESCRIPTION ---
Investigating change in city centres or rural settlements using quantitative and qualitative methods.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="lesson-notice" data-notice-title="Your school&rsquo;s fieldwork" hidden><p>This lesson covers the general fieldwork skills, terminology, and exam-paper expectations that apply to every student. The specific sites, data, and methods your class actually used will be different &mdash; for the case study you&rsquo;ll write about in the exam, refer to your teacher&rsquo;s notes and the data you collected on the day.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Choices, Same Skill Set</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Edexcel asks every student to carry out a second investigation in a <strong>human</strong> environment. You either looked at change in a <strong>central or inner urban area</strong> — the kind of place that shifts from week to week as shops close, flats are built and footfall changes — or you looked at <strong>change in rural settlements</strong>, where the question is more about counter-urbanisation, second homes, or thinning bus services. The skills are the same: ask a focused question, choose appropriate methods, collect data fairly, present and analyse it, then reflect on what went wrong.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the exact enquiry question your own human investigation tested. Then list the two methods you used.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For human fieldwork Edexcel requires at least one quantitative method, one qualitative method and at least two secondary sources, including <strong>Census data from the Office for National Statistics</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Option 7B: Central or Inner Urban Areas</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A typical urban question is ‘How is the central business district changing?’ or ‘How does environmental quality vary across the inner urban area?’ Two methods cover the brief.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">First, a <strong>quantitative <dfn class="term" data-def="A survey that records what each shop, office or building is used for, classified into categories such as comparison retail, convenience retail, services and vacancy.">land-use survey</dfn></strong>: walking each street and ticking off whether a building is comparison retail (e.g. clothes), convenience retail (e.g. supermarket), services (e.g. hairdresser), leisure or vacant. Compared with old maps or business directories, this shows how the area has changed — for example, the rise of independent coffee shops or the loss of department stores. Second, a <strong>qualitative <dfn class="term" data-def="A scoring sheet used to record subjective judgements about a place, such as litter, noise or graffiti, on a scale of 1 to 5.">environmental quality survey</dfn> (EQS)</strong>, where the same set of scores (1–5) is recorded at sample sites — usually for litter, noise, graffiti, building condition and green space. Bipolar scales (e.g. clean ↔ dirty) reduce, but do not remove, observer bias.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Subjective judgement is the EQS’s big weakness: one student scores rust on a railing as a 2, another scores it as a 4. Three fixes work in practice. <strong>Calibration</strong> — the whole class scores one site together before splitting up, agreeing what a 3 looks like. <strong>Triple sampling</strong> — three students score the same site and the mean is taken. <strong>Photo logging</strong> — every score is paired with a photograph so judgements can be re-checked later. Examiners reward students who name a fix, not just complain about bias.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Option 7B: Rural Settlements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">If your school chose a rural settlement, the broad task is to investigate change. A typical question is ‘How is village A changing because of counter-urbanisation?’ Edexcel requires <strong>one quantitative method</strong> to measure flows of people within the settlement — usually <strong>pedestrian counts</strong> at four or five points (junctions, the bus stop, the village shop) for a fixed period (e.g. 10 minutes), repeated at different times of day. Counts can be displayed on flow lines on a map of the village.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The required <strong>qualitative method</strong> records the views of people on the quality of the rural environment — typically a <strong>questionnaire</strong> with both closed (tick-box) and open (free-text) questions, or recorded interviews with residents, second-home owners and shopkeepers. Open responses give context that numbers cannot — one resident’s comment that ‘the new estate killed the village pub’ tells you more about change than a footfall figure.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sampling Real People</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Sampling matters even more in human fieldwork because people are far less uniform than rocks. <strong>Random sampling</strong> means stopping every fifth passer-by; <strong>systematic sampling</strong> means standing at fixed sites; <dfn class="term" data-def="Sampling that deliberately includes a balance of different age, gender or ethnic groups so the sample reflects the whole community.">stratified sampling</dfn> deliberately balances ages, genders and tourists vs locals so the sample reflects the village as a whole. <strong>Sample size</strong> matters too: 50 questionnaires give a much more reliable picture than 10. The exam often shows you a small sample and asks how reliability could be improved — the obvious answers are ‘more questionnaires’ and ‘sample on different days/times’.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Required Secondary Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For both options Edexcel requires <strong>Census data from the ONS</strong> as one secondary source. The Census is taken every 10 years (most recently in 2021) and gives age structure, ethnicity, occupation, housing tenure and tens of other variables for every tiny output area. The second source can be chosen by the school: an old map showing land use 50 years ago; ONS Neighbourhood Statistics for a rural parish; police crime data; or estate agent data on house prices. Always state the source and date in the analysis.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Ethics & Risk in Human Fieldwork</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Stopping members of the public requires care. Approach in pairs, in a public place, in daylight; introduce yourself, give a name and the school; explain the survey is anonymous; never ask under-16s without an adult present. People can refuse, and you must record refusals as a non-response — it affects sample size. Photographing people, especially children, is not allowed without permission. These rules protect both you and your respondents, and a strong evaluation will mention them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list four ways to make an environmental quality survey more reliable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reliability of EQS scores improves through calibration before fieldwork, triple-sampling at each site, photo logging, and increasing sample size. Naming a specific fix in the exam scores higher than complaining about bias.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">For urban areas, a land-use survey (quantitative) and environmental quality survey (qualitative) measure change in central or inner districts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">For rural settlements, pedestrian counts (quantitative) and questionnaires or interviews (qualitative) capture flows and resident views.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">All human fieldwork must use ONS Census data plus one other secondary source, manage ethical risks and use sampling that reflects the community.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">Human fieldwork questions often look at sample size and bias. If the exam asks ‘Suggest one way the student could improve their data,’ never say ‘ask more people’ alone — develop it: ‘Increase the sample to at least 50 respondents and split them across weekday/weekend so the data is not biased to commuters or shoppers.’ The 8-mark questions reward chains of reasoning, so develop two clear improvements rather than listing six undeveloped ones.</p>
