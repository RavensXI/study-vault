<div class="lesson-notice" data-notice-title="Your school&rsquo;s fieldwork" hidden><p>This lesson covers the general fieldwork skills, terminology, and exam-paper expectations that apply to every student. The specific sites, data, and methods your class actually used will be different &mdash; for the case study you&rsquo;ll write about in the exam, refer to your teacher&rsquo;s notes and the data you collected on the day.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Physical Fieldwork Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Physical fieldwork forces geography out of the textbook and into the field. The exam will not ask you about your trip directly, but it will give you an unfamiliar fieldwork scenario in a UK river or coastal landscape and ask you to design, carry out, present or evaluate the kind of investigation you actually did. Examiners expect you to write about <em>your own</em> physical investigation by name — the river, the coast, the date, the conditions — and to apply the same logic to scenarios you have never seen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six stages of the enquiry process in order. Then write one sentence on what each stage looks like in your own fieldwork.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six stages of the geographical <dfn class="term" data-def="The six-stage process of asking a geographical question, collecting data to answer it, analysing the results and reflecting on how reliable they are.">enquiry process</dfn> are: 1) formulate questions, 2) choose methods, 3) collect data, 4) present and process, 5) analyse and conclude, 6) evaluate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Option 7A: River Landscapes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">If your school chose a river investigation, the broad task is the same: <strong>investigate change in a river channel</strong>. A typical question is ‘How does the river channel change downstream?’ or ‘Does the river fit the Bradshaw model?’ The Bradshaw model predicts that as you move downstream, the channel widens and deepens, velocity increases, and the load becomes smaller and rounder — you test these claims at three or more sites along the river.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Edexcel requires at least <strong>one quantitative method</strong> to measure river <dfn class="term" data-def="The volume of water passing a point in a river per second, measured in cubic metres per second (cumecs).">discharge</dfn> — the standard combination is width (tape measure across the channel), depth (metre rule at intervals across) and velocity (a flow meter or a floating object timed over 10 m). Discharge is then calculated as cross-sectional area × mean velocity. Edexcel also requires at least <strong>one qualitative method</strong> — usually annotated field sketches or labelled photographs that record the meanders, riffles, pools or human features at each site.</p>

Most fieldwork uses systematic with a stratified element. Whatever you choose, write the reason in your method — examiners reward justified choices.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Option 7A: Coastal Landscapes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">If your school chose the coast, the task is to investigate <strong>coastal processes through landscape evidence</strong>. Common questions are ‘Does longshore drift transport sediment along this beach?’ or ‘Does sediment become smaller and rounder along the beach?’ Edexcel requires <strong>one quantitative method</strong> to measure beach morphology and sediment characteristics — typically beach profiles taken with a clinometer and ranging poles, plus pebble samples measured with callipers and Powers’ roundness chart. A second quantitative option is timing oranges or table-tennis balls along the shore to measure longshore drift velocity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The <strong>qualitative method</strong> is usually labelled field sketches of cliffs, groynes, beach huts or sea walls, or photographs annotated with arrows showing wave direction and human structures. Both methods together let you connect process (waves and current) to landform (beach shape and sediment) — exactly what the exam expects.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Risks & Practical Constraints</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A risk assessment is part of the enquiry process. River fieldwork carries the risk of slippery banks, fast flows after rain and Weil’s disease from contact with rat urine in the water; coastal fieldwork carries cliff falls, rip currents, slippery rocks and tides cutting off access. Mitigations include checking river-level gauges and tide tables, working in groups, wearing wellies and gloves, and never wading above knee depth. Examiners may ask you to describe how risks were managed — vague answers (‘we were careful’) score zero; specific ones (‘we checked the Environment Agency river-level gauge that morning’) score full marks.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Secondary Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Edexcel requires <strong>secondary data</strong> alongside your primary measurements. For rivers, that includes an Environment Agency flood risk map and one other source — for example, a hydrograph showing recent rainfall and discharge, or historical Ordnance Survey maps to show channel change. For coasts, the British Geological Survey’s online Geology of Britain viewer gives the rock type at each site and one other source might be a Shoreline Management Plan or aerial photograph of cliff retreat. Secondary data lets you set your fieldwork in a wider context — one day’s data is rarely enough on its own.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Examiners often ask ‘Justify one method used in your fieldwork.’ The trick is to link the method back to the original question. If your question is ‘Does the channel widen downstream?’, measuring channel width with a tape directly answers it — you cannot tell from a sketch alone. If your question is ‘Does sediment become rounder downstream?’, callipers and a roundness chart give numerical evidence; a photograph cannot. Always write your justification in two parts: what the method measures, and how that answers your question.</p>

</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the exact question your own physical fieldwork tested, and the two methods you used to answer it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For physical fieldwork you must include at least one quantitative method (discharge or beach morphology), one qualitative method (sketches or annotated photographs), and at least two secondary sources. Always link methods back to the enquiry question.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Edexcel requires one river OR coastal investigation, with one quantitative method, one qualitative method and at least two secondary sources.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">River fieldwork measures discharge and channel change; coastal fieldwork measures beach morphology, sediment and longshore drift.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Sampling, risk assessment and method justification all link back to the original enquiry question and earn marks when you make that link explicit.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When the exam shows you a fieldwork scenario you have never visited, do not panic — the skills are the same. Read the question for the command word: ‘describe’ wants what you see, ‘explain’ wants why, ‘justify’ wants pros and cons of the method, ‘evaluate’ wants a judgement. For 8-mark questions, develop two clear chains of reasoning rather than listing five undeveloped points. Always reference your own investigation by name — named rivers and coasts score better than ‘our river’.</p>
