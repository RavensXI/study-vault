--- DESCRIPTION ---
The UK two-speed economy, brownfield versus greenfield, and migration debates that shape settlement.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Two-Speed Economy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For more than 30 years the UK has had a <dfn class="term" data-def="A pattern in which one part of a country grows much faster than another, with sharp gaps in jobs, wages and investment.">two-speed economy</dfn>: London and the South East grow much faster than the rest of the UK. London alone produces around 22% of UK economic output despite being home to only 13% of the population. By contrast, regions such as the North East, Wales and parts of Northern Ireland have GDP per head only 60–70% of the UK average. Wage gaps follow the same line: median weekly earnings in London were £853 in 2023, against £629 in the North East — a gap of more than £200 a week.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two figures that prove the UK has a two-speed economy. Compare London with one northern region.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>London produces around 22% of UK output with 13% of the population. Median weekly pay in London (£853, 2023) is over £200 above the North East (£629), and house prices average around 11× earnings in London versus 5× in the north.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Bridging the Gap</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">UK governments have tried several strategies to narrow the divide. The <strong>Northern Powerhouse</strong>, launched in 2014, aimed to link Manchester, Leeds, Liverpool, Sheffield and Newcastle into a single ‘super-region’ through better transport (HS2 phase 2 was originally part of this) and devolution of budgets to mayors. The <strong>Levelling Up Fund</strong>, announced in 2020, has poured around £4.8 billion into local regeneration projects since 2021 — new museums, station upgrades, school refurbishments. <strong>Freeports</strong> at Teesside, Liverpool, Plymouth and elsewhere offer tax breaks to attract manufacturing. Critics argue results are patchy: HS2 was scaled back in 2023 to a London-Birmingham line, the productivity gap with London barely budged through the 2010s, and money is often spread thinly across small projects.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Brownfield vs Greenfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Wherever the UK builds, the same argument repeats. <dfn class="term" data-def="Land that has previously been built on, often in towns and cities, that is being redeveloped for new uses.">Brownfield</dfn> sites — ex-factories, old gas works, derelict warehouses — sit close to existing infrastructure, jobs and shops. Building on them protects the countryside and cuts car use. London’s Olympic Park (built on contaminated industrial land in Stratford) and Manchester’s Salford Quays (the old docks) are textbook brownfield successes. Drawbacks include high costs to clean contaminated soil and complex demolition.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Land that has not previously been built on, usually farmland, woodland or open countryside on the edge of towns.">Greenfield</dfn> sites are cheaper, easier and quicker to build on. Most large-scale housing estates in the UK — e.g. on the edge of Cambridge, York or Milton Keynes — sit on what used to be fields. The drawback is irreversible loss of countryside, increased car dependency, and pressure on water and drainage. The Campaign to Protect Rural England estimates 290,000 homes are planned on Green Belt land by 2026.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Salford Quays shows the brownfield model at full scale. From the 1980s, ITV, the BBC, hotels, restaurants and over 8,000 new homes turned the derelict Manchester Ship Canal docks into a working media district. Jobs grew from near-zero in 1985 to over 17,000 by 2018. But locals point out new flats are unaffordable to long-time residents, and tower blocks now dominate skylines that were industrial. Green-field equivalents do not face this gentrification pressure but consume open land. The exam often asks ‘which is better?’ — a strong answer says ‘it depends on context’ and gives one example each.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Migration: The Numbers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between the number of people moving into a country and the number leaving over the same period.">Net migration</dfn> to the UK was around 685,000 in 2023 (the latest full year), a record level driven partly by Ukrainian and Hong Kong resettlement schemes, partly by international students, and partly by NHS and care worker visas. The Office for National Statistics revises these figures — a 2024 update raised earlier estimates by around 100,000 — reminding us that migration data is uncertain. Large UK cities (especially London, Birmingham, Manchester) absorb most arrivals, although smaller cities such as Boston in Lincolnshire and Peterborough have seen the biggest <em>percentage</em> changes in their populations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Stakeholder Views on Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Stakeholders differ sharply. <strong>Business groups</strong> argue migration fills shortages in care homes, agriculture and IT — British farmers have warned that crops have been left unpicked since seasonal worker visas were tightened. <strong>The NHS</strong> relies on overseas doctors and nurses for around 17% of its workforce. <strong>Concerned residents</strong> point to pressure on housing, school places and GP appointments — especially in towns where housing supply has not kept up. <strong>Government</strong> tries to balance these by adjusting the points-based visa system — raising the minimum salary to £38,700 in 2024, which cut some migration but worsened care-sector shortages.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">UK migration figures rely on several overlapping sources: the International Passenger Survey, Home Office visa data, NHS GP registrations and the Census. The IPS sample was tiny — around 700,000 passengers a year — and was suspended in 2020 due to COVID, so 2020–22 estimates are particularly uncertain. The ONS now uses Home Office and HMRC administrative data instead, which is more accurate for non-EU arrivals but slower to release. Always treat any single migration headline with caution — figures are routinely revised, and the political debate often gets ahead of the data.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <p>UK net migration was around 685,000 in 2023. Stakeholders disagree: business and the NHS argue it fills skills gaps, residents point to housing and service pressure, and government tries to balance the two through visa rules.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The UK two-speed economy persists despite Northern Powerhouse, Levelling Up and Freeports — London still leads on output, wages and house prices.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Brownfield (Salford Quays, Olympic Park) protects countryside but costs more and risks gentrification; greenfield is cheaper but loses countryside permanently.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">UK net migration of around 685,000 in 2023 fills skills gaps but pressures local services; stakeholders read the same numbers very differently.</li>
</ul>
