--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="lesson-notice" data-notice-title="Your school&rsquo;s fieldwork" hidden><p>This lesson covers the general fieldwork skills, terminology, and exam-paper expectations that apply to every student. The specific sites, data, and methods your class actually used will be different &mdash; for the case study you&rsquo;ll write about in the exam, refer to your teacher&rsquo;s notes and the data you collected on the day.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Presentation Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Raw fieldwork data is just numbers and notes. To answer a geographical question you need to present it in a form that lets you see patterns — and the form has to fit the data. Choosing the wrong graph or map throws marks away. The exam often shows you data in a table or graph and asks you to describe a trend, calculate a statistic, or pick a better way of presenting it. The same logic applies to your own investigation: every piece of data you collected should appear in your write-up in the form most suited to its type.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and pair each graph type with the kind of data it suits best (continuous, categorical, percentage, paired).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Match the graph to the data: <strong>line graphs</strong> for continuous change, <strong>bar charts</strong> for categories, <strong>pie charts</strong> for percentages of a total, <strong>scatter graphs</strong> for paired variables, and <strong>flow lines</strong> or <strong>choropleth maps</strong> for spatial data.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Common Graph Choices</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <strong>line graph</strong> shows continuous change — river velocity at sites 1–5 along a course, or pedestrian count over the day. A <strong>bar chart</strong> compares categories — types of land use in a CBD, or visitor age groups in a National Park. A <strong>pie chart</strong> shows shares of a whole — the proportion of vacant vs occupied shops — but only when categories add to 100%. A <strong>scatter graph</strong> tests a relationship between two variables — pebble size against distance downstream. A <strong>compound (stacked) bar</strong> shows two layers at once, e.g. occupied vs vacant shops at five sites.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Maps for Spatial Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Maps are essential when location matters. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A map that uses shading or colour to show the value of a variable across areas, such as population density per district.">choropleth map</dfn> shades areas by value — perfect for population density across a city or footfall categories across village zones. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A map showing movement using arrows whose width is proportional to the size of the flow.">flow-line map</dfn> uses thick arrows to show movement — ideal for showing pedestrian flows along high streets, or river discharge at survey points. <strong>Proportional symbols</strong> place a circle or square at each site, sized to match the value — for example, footfall counts at five village locations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Describing & Analysing Patterns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When the exam says ‘describe the pattern shown,’ you must do three things: state the <strong>overall trend</strong>, quote <strong>specific figures</strong> from the data, and identify any <strong>anomalies</strong> — sites or values that go against the pattern. Examiners use the formula TEA: <strong>T</strong>rend, <strong>E</strong>xamples, <strong>A</strong>nomalies. So instead of writing ‘footfall increases from morning to lunchtime,’ write ‘footfall rose from 32/min at 9 a.m. to a peak of 78/min at 12:30, before falling to 41/min at 4 p.m.; site 4 was an anomaly with only 12/min, possibly because it lay on a back street.’</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Statistical Tools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Edexcel expects you to be able to calculate basic statistics. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The middle value of a data set when arranged in size order; if there are two middle values, take the mean of them.">median</dfn> is a useful average for skewed data such as pebble size, where one boulder can pull the mean. The <strong>interquartile range</strong> shows how spread out the data is. <strong>Percentage change</strong> = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; this often appears in change-over-time questions. Always show your working in the exam — calculations marks are easy to lose by missing units or not rounding clearly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Conclusions That Answer the Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A conclusion is not a description — it is a direct answer to the original enquiry question, supported by your own data and any wider geographical theory. The structure that scores well is: <strong>(1) restate the question, (2) state your overall finding, (3) quote two pieces of evidence, (4) link to a relevant model or case study, (5) acknowledge the most important limitation</strong>. For example: ‘Our investigation asked whether the river fits the Bradshaw model. Site 5 had three times the discharge of site 1, and pebble roundness rose from 1.2 to 3.4. This broadly supports the model, although the steep, narrow channel at site 3 was an anomaly — perhaps because of a recently engineered weir.’</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Stage 6 of the enquiry process is reflection: critically reviewing methods, data and conclusions. Examiners distinguish between <strong>describing</strong> (what happened) and <strong>evaluating</strong> (whether it was reliable). A strong evaluation names a specific weakness (small sample, weather on the day, observer bias) and explains how it affected the result, then suggests a realistic improvement. Vague comments (‘the data was not very reliable’) score nothing without a reason and a fix.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write a TEA-style description of a graph from your own fieldwork. Include trend, two figures and one anomaly.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For describing data, use TEA: <strong>T</strong>rend, <strong>E</strong>xamples (two figures), <strong>A</strong>nomalies. For conclusions, restate the question, give your finding, support with two pieces of evidence and end with one acknowledged limitation.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Pick the graph for the data: line for continuous change, bar for categories, pie for shares, scatter for relationships, flow lines or choropleth for spatial patterns.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Describe data using the TEA structure — trend, examples with figures, anomalies — and back conclusions with named evidence.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Reflection is the sixth stage of the enquiry process: name a specific weakness, explain its effect, and suggest a realistic improvement.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">Calculation marks are the easiest marks on the paper to lose — show working line by line, double-check units (m/s, %, m<sup>3</sup>), and round only at the final step. For describe questions, examiners watch for at least two pieces of data and one anomaly — if you don’t reference figures, the maximum is half marks. For conclusion or evaluation questions, always tie your answer back to the original investigation question rather than just summarising your data.</p>
