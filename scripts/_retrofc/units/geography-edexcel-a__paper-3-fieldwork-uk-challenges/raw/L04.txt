--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a growing UK population stresses ecosystems and resources, plus sustainable transport responses.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country Under Pressure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK’s population reached around 68 million in 2025 and is forecast to pass 73 million by 2050, driven mainly by net migration of around 300,000 a year and slowly rising life expectancy. Each extra person needs food, water, energy, housing and transport — and that adds up fast on an island the size of Britain. Geographers ask three connected questions about this challenge: how is consumption changing, how is the environment coping, and how can we live better without exhausting what is left?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the projected UK population for 2050 along with two ways extra people put pressure on resources.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK population is projected to grow from around 68 million in 2025 to 73 million by 2050. Each extra person consumes about 500 m<sup>3</sup> of water and 4–5 tonnes of CO<sub>2</sub> per year, raising pressure on water supply, food production and the climate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Changing Consumption Patterns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Population is only half the story. Per-person consumption is also shifting. The average UK home in 2025 contains over 30 electrical devices, more than double the figure 25 years ago. Online shopping has multiplied delivery van traffic and packaging waste — <dfn class="term" data-def="The total amount of greenhouse gases produced directly and indirectly by the activities of an individual, household or country.">carbon footprint</dfn> calculations show UK households throw away around 100 kg of plastic each year. Diets, however, have become slightly less meat-heavy than in the 2000s, and electricity use per home has fallen due to LED lighting and efficient appliances. The picture is mixed: some pressures are easing, others are intensifying.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Pressure on UK Ecosystems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">UK <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural community of plants, animals and the physical environment they inhabit, all interacting with each other.">ecosystems</dfn> face several growing pressures. <strong>Habitat loss</strong> from new housing has fragmented hedgerows and wetlands — the State of Nature 2023 report found that 1 in 6 UK species is now at risk of extinction. <strong>Air and water pollution</strong>: only 14% of English rivers reach ‘good ecological status’ under the Water Framework Directive, harmed by farm fertiliser run-off and untreated sewage. <strong>Climate change</strong> is shifting species ranges; insects such as the Essex skipper butterfly are spreading north faster than birds can adapt. <strong>Tourism pressure</strong> on National Parks erodes footpaths and disturbs wildlife.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Case Study: The Lake District National Park</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The Lake District welcomes around 18 million visitors a year, generating £1.4 billion of tourism income. But footpaths erode at popular peaks like Helvellyn (the National Trust spent £1.5 million repairing paths in the 2010s). Lake water quality declines as boats churn sediment and run-off from farms enters tributaries. Second-home ownership pushes house prices beyond local wages — in 2023 the average Lake District home cost 11.6 times average local earnings. The Park Authority responds with footpath repair, sustainable farming grants under Environmental Land Management schemes, and limits on second-home conversions in some parishes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Sustainable Transport: National Options</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Transport produces about 27% of UK carbon emissions, with cars and vans alone responsible for over half of that. <dfn class="term" data-def="Transport that meets people's needs without locking in long-term harm to the environment, often through electrification, fuel efficiency or modal shift.">Sustainable transport</dfn> aims to reduce that footprint while still moving people and freight. The UK government’s 2030 ban on new petrol and diesel car sales pushes drivers toward electric vehicles — over 30% of new car sales were electric in 2024. <strong>Rail electrification</strong>, including the Great Western route to Cardiff, replaces diesel locomotives. <strong>Active travel</strong> — walking and cycling — is being invested in through schemes like the £200 million National Cycle Network expansion. <strong>Bus franchising</strong> in Greater Manchester (Bee Network, 2024) gives the mayor power to set routes and fares, encouraging modal shift away from cars.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Tensions in the Sustainable Transport Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Sustainable transport sounds neat but creates genuine conflict. EVs cut tailpipe emissions but their batteries need lithium and cobalt mined elsewhere — sometimes with severe local impacts. <strong>HS2</strong>, originally a flagship sustainable rail project, was scaled back in 2023 to a London-Birmingham line, leaving northern England feeling short-changed. Cycle lanes shrink road space for cars and have triggered fierce local opposition (e.g. London’s Low Traffic Neighbourhoods). Rural areas, where over 90% of journeys are by car, find it hard to switch — bus services have fallen by a third since 2010 outside cities.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Where Top-Down and Bottom-Up Meet</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">National policy alone is not enough. <strong>Bottom-up schemes</strong> — community energy projects, town-scale 20 mph zones, lift-share apps — cut emissions while bringing residents on board. Bristol’s Clean Air Zone (2022) charges higher-polluting vehicles £9 to enter the city centre, and revenue funds local cycling. The combination of top-down rules (zero-emission deadlines, electrification) with local action (bike hire, car-free streets, EV charging in supermarket car parks) is what makes sustainability politically possible. The exam will reward students who can name both scales.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three sustainable transport options the UK is using and one drawback of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sustainable UK transport options include electric vehicles, rail electrification, active travel infrastructure and bus franchising. Each cuts emissions but creates new conflicts — mining for batteries, lost road space, or unequal benefits between cities and rural areas.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">UK population growth and rising consumption put pressure on water, food, energy and ecosystems — only 14% of English rivers reach good ecological status.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Tourism pressures National Parks (Lake District footpaths and second-home prices); planning and farming reforms try to manage this without removing access.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Sustainable transport relies on a mix of electrification, rail, active travel and franchising — but each option creates winners, losers and rural/urban tensions.</li>
</ul>
