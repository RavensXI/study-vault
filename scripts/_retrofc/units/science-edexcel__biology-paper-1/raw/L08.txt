<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Defining Health</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The World Health Organization (WHO) defines <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (WHO definition).">health</dfn> as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being — not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. This means that being healthy involves more than just not being ill; it includes emotional wellbeing and having good social relationships.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Diseases fall into two broad categories. <dfn class="term" data-def="A disease that can be spread from one organism to another, caused by a pathogen such as a bacterium, virus, fungus or protist.">Communicable disease</dfn>s can be spread from one organism to another and are caused by pathogens. <dfn class="term" data-def="A disease that cannot be transmitted between organisms, such as cancer, cardiovascular disease or type 2 diabetes.">Non-communicable disease</dfn>s cannot be passed on and are often linked to lifestyle or genetic factors. The presence of one disease can increase susceptibility to others — for example, a weakened immune system from HIV can make a person more vulnerable to tuberculosis.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Pathogens and Common Infections</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A microorganism that causes disease. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists.">pathogen</dfn> is a disease-causing organism. There are four types of pathogen: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists. The Edexcel specification requires you to know several named infections:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Cholera</strong> (caused by bacteria) — causes severe diarrhoea and dehydration. Spread through contaminated <strong>water</strong>. Prevented by clean water supplies and sanitation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Tuberculosis</strong> (TB, caused by bacteria) — causes lung damage, persistent cough and weight loss. Spread by <strong>airborne</strong> droplets when an infected person coughs or sneezes. Prevented by vaccination (BCG) and treating infected people with antibiotics.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Chalara ash dieback</strong> (caused by a fungus) — causes leaf loss and bark lesions in ash trees. Spread by <strong>airborne</strong> spores carried by wind. Difficult to prevent; infected trees are often removed to slow the spread.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Malaria</strong> (caused by a protist) — damages blood cells and the liver, causing fever and chills. Spread by <strong>animal vectors</strong> (female <em>Anopheles</em> mosquitoes). Prevented by mosquito nets, insecticides and draining stagnant water where mosquitoes breed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>HIV</strong> (a virus) — destroys white blood cells, weakening the immune system. If untreated, it leads to <dfn class="term" data-def="Acquired immune deficiency syndrome — a condition caused by HIV that severely weakens the immune system.">AIDS</dfn>. HIV is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An infection spread through sexual contact, such as chlamydia or HIV.">sexually transmitted infection</dfn> (STI) and can also be spread through blood-to-blood contact (e.g. shared needles). Prevented by using condoms and screening blood donations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Chlamydia</strong> (caused by bacteria) — the most common STI in the UK. Often has no symptoms but can cause infertility if untreated. Spread by unprotected sexual contact. Prevented by using condoms and treated with antibiotics. Screening programmes help identify and treat cases early.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Create a table from memory with columns: disease, pathogen type, symptoms, how spread, how prevented. Include cholera, TB, Chalara, malaria, HIV and chlamydia.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Learn the six named infections: cholera (bacteria, water), TB (bacteria, airborne), Chalara (fungus, airborne), malaria (protist, mosquito vector), HIV (virus, sexual/blood), chlamydia (bacteria, sexual).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Body’s Defences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The human body has <strong>physical barriers</strong> to prevent pathogens from entering: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The outer layer of the body that acts as a physical barrier to pathogens.">skin</dfn> provides a continuous barrier; <dfn class="term" data-def="A sticky substance produced by goblet cells in the respiratory tract that traps pathogens and particles.">mucus</dfn> in the respiratory tract traps pathogens; and <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny hair-like structures on cells lining the airways that sweep mucus and trapped pathogens up towards the throat.">cilia</dfn> (tiny hair-like structures) sweep mucus and trapped pathogens towards the throat to be swallowed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The body also has <strong>chemical defences</strong>: <dfn class="term" data-def="Enzymes found in tears, saliva and nasal secretions that destroy bacteria by breaking down their cell walls.">lysozyme</dfn>s in tears, saliva and nasal secretions destroy bacteria by breaking down their cell walls. <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong acid (pH 1-2) in the stomach that kills most pathogens in food and drink.">Hydrochloric acid</dfn> in the stomach (pH 1–2) kills most pathogens that are swallowed with food or drink.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Immune System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">If pathogens get past the body’s physical and chemical defences, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The body's specific defence system that identifies and destroys particular pathogens using white blood cells, antibodies and memory cells.">immune system</dfn> responds. Pathogens have unique molecules on their surface called <dfn class="term" data-def="Proteins on the surface of a pathogen that are recognised as foreign by the immune system, triggering an immune response.">antigen</dfn>s. White blood cells called <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of white blood cell that produces antibodies to target specific antigens on pathogens.">lymphocyte</dfn>s detect these antigens and produce specific <dfn class="term" data-def="Proteins produced by lymphocytes that bind to specific antigens on pathogens, marking them for destruction.">antibodies</dfn> that bind to the antigens and mark the pathogen for destruction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">After the infection is cleared, <dfn class="term" data-def="Lymphocytes that remain in the blood after an infection, allowing a faster and stronger response if the same pathogen is encountered again.">memory lymphocyte</dfn>s remain in the blood. If the same pathogen enters the body again, memory lymphocytes trigger a <strong>secondary response</strong> — a much faster and stronger production of antibodies, destroying the pathogen before symptoms develop.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Draw the immune response in four steps from memory: 1) pathogen enters, 2) antigens detected, 3) lymphocytes produce antibodies, 4) memory cells remain for secondary response.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Immune response: pathogen enters → antigens trigger lymphocytes → antibodies produced → pathogen destroyed → memory lymphocytes remain for faster secondary response.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Vaccination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="Injecting a dead or inactive form of a pathogen to stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease.">Vaccination</dfn> (immunisation) involves injecting a dead or inactive form of a pathogen into the body. The antigens on the inactive pathogen still trigger an immune response — lymphocytes produce antibodies and memory lymphocytes are formed. The person does not become ill because the pathogen is inactive. If the real pathogen enters the body later, memory lymphocytes enable a rapid secondary response, preventing the disease.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Antibiotics and Drug Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="Medicines that kill bacteria or stop them reproducing by inhibiting cell processes. They have no effect on viruses.">Antibiotic</dfn>s are medicines that kill bacteria or prevent them from reproducing. They work by inhibiting specific cell processes in the bacterium — for example, preventing cell wall formation. <strong>Antibiotics cannot treat viral infections</strong> because viruses reproduce inside host cells and do not have their own cell machinery.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Non-Communicable Diseases and Lifestyle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Many non-communicable diseases are caused by the interaction of several factors, including genetics, lifestyle and environment. Examples include <dfn class="term" data-def="Diseases affecting the heart and blood vessels, such as coronary heart disease, often linked to smoking, diet and lack of exercise.">cardiovascular disease</dfn>s, many cancers, and some lung and liver diseases.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Lifestyle factors that increase the risk of non-communicable diseases include: poor diet and lack of exercise leading to <dfn class="term" data-def="Having a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or above, which increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers.">obesity</dfn> (measured using BMI = mass in kg ÷ height in m², and waist-to-hip ratio); excessive <strong>alcohol</strong> consumption leading to liver disease and cirrhosis; and <strong>smoking</strong> which damages blood vessels and increases the risk of coronary heart disease, stroke and lung cancer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Treatments for cardiovascular disease include <strong>lifestyle changes</strong> (improved diet, more exercise, stopping smoking), <strong>life-long medication</strong> (statins to lower cholesterol, blood-thinning drugs) and <strong>surgical procedures</strong> (stents to open blocked arteries, bypass surgery). Each treatment has advantages and disadvantages — for example, medication must be taken daily and may have side effects, while surgery carries risks but can be immediately effective.</p>
