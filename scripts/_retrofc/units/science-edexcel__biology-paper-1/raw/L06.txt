<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Darwin’s Theory of Evolution by Natural Selection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charles <dfn class="term" data-def="Charles Darwin proposed the theory of evolution by natural selection in 1859, explaining how species change over time through survival of the fittest.">Darwin</dfn> proposed the theory of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which organisms with characteristics best suited to their environment are more likely to survive, reproduce and pass on their alleles.">natural selection</dfn>. His theory can be summarised in four steps:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">First, within any population there is <strong>variation</strong> — individuals have different characteristics due to differences in their alleles. Second, organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support, leading to a <strong>struggle for survival</strong>. Third, individuals with characteristics best suited to the environment are more likely to <strong>survive and reproduce</strong>. Fourth, these advantageous alleles are <strong>passed on</strong> to the next generation, so over time the population changes.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Practise writing out the four steps of natural selection from memory: variation, competition, survival of the fittest, inheritance. Then apply them to a named example such as antibiotic resistance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Natural selection: variation in a population → struggle for survival → best-adapted individuals survive and reproduce → advantageous alleles passed to offspring → population changes over time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Antibiotic Resistance as Evidence for Evolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The emergence of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of bacteria to survive exposure to an antibiotic that would normally kill them, due to a mutation that is passed on through natural selection.">antibiotic-resistant</dfn> bacteria supports Darwin’s theory. Within a bacterial population, random mutations may produce an individual that is resistant to an antibiotic. When the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria are killed, but the resistant bacterium survives. It reproduces rapidly, passing the resistance allele to its offspring. Over time, the population becomes dominated by resistant bacteria. This is natural selection in action.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">MRSA (methicillin-resistant <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>) is an example of a bacterium that has evolved resistance to several antibiotics. This is why doctors are encouraged to prescribe antibiotics only when necessary and patients must complete the full course.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Evidence for Human Evolution — Fossils</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The preserved remains or traces of organisms that lived in the past, found in rocks, ice or other materials.">Fossil</dfn>s provide evidence for human evolution. The Edexcel specification requires you to know three key fossil discoveries:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Ardi</strong> (Ardipithecus ramidus) dates from about 4.4 million years ago. Ardi had both ape-like features (long arms, an opposable big toe for gripping branches) and human-like features (she could walk upright on two legs, at least some of the time). This suggests she was an early stage in the evolution from apes to humans.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Lucy</strong> (Australopithecus afarensis) dates from about 3.2 million years ago. Lucy had a smaller brain than modern humans but her skeleton shows she walked upright more efficiently than Ardi. Her knee and hip joints were adapted for bipedal walking, showing that walking upright evolved before large brains.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Richard <dfn class="term" data-def="Richard Leakey discovered fossils of Homo erectus (Turkana Boy) from 1.6 million years ago, showing a more human-like body plan.">Leakey</dfn>’s discovery from about 1.6 million years ago (often called Turkana Boy) was a nearly complete skeleton of <em>Homo erectus</em>. This individual was tall, had a relatively large brain, and had a body shape much more like modern humans. These three fossils show a gradual change over millions of years from ape-like ancestors to modern humans.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Create a timeline from memory: Ardi (4.4 mya) → Lucy (3.2 mya) → Leakey's Homo erectus (1.6 mya). For each, note one ape-like and one human-like feature.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three key fossils: Ardi (4.4 million years ago), Lucy (3.2 mya) and Leakey’s Homo erectus (1.6 mya). They show a gradual progression from ape-like to human-like features.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Evidence from Stone Tools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Stone tools also provide evidence for human evolution. The earliest tools were simple pebble tools, used for cutting and scraping. Over time, tools became more sophisticated — hand axes, blades and eventually finely crafted arrowheads. This development shows increasing brain capacity and problem-solving ability. Stone tools can be dated from the rock layers (strata) in which they are found.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Classification — From Five Kingdoms to Three Domains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Traditionally, living organisms were classified into <dfn class="term" data-def="The five broad groups (Animals, Plants, Fungi, Protists, Prokaryotes) into which organisms were traditionally sorted based on observable features.">five kingdoms</dfn>: Animals, Plants, Fungi, Protists and Prokaryotes. This system was based on observable features such as cell structure and how organisms obtained food.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">More recently, <dfn class="term" data-def="Analysis of the DNA or RNA sequences of organisms to determine how closely related they are.">genetic analysis</dfn> has shown that the five-kingdom system does not accurately reflect evolutionary relationships. Scientists now use the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three-domain classification system (Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya) based on genetic analysis, replacing the five-kingdom model.">three-domain</dfn> system instead:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Archaea</strong> — primitive prokaryotes, often found in extreme environments. <strong>Bacteria</strong> — true bacteria (prokaryotes). <strong>Eukarya</strong> — all organisms with eukaryotic cells (animals, plants, fungi and protists). Genetic analysis revealed that Archaea are more different from Bacteria than was previously thought, which is why they were separated into their own domain.</p>
