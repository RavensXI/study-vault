--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Eukaryotic and Prokaryotic Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All living organisms are made of cells. There are two fundamental types of cell: <dfn class="term" data-def="Cells that contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, including animal and plant cells.">eukaryotic cells</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Cells that do not have a true nucleus; their DNA floats freely in the cytoplasm. Bacteria are prokaryotic.">prokaryotic cells</dfn>. Eukaryotic cells are found in animals and plants. They are complex, with a nucleus that holds the cell's genetic material. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, are much smaller and simpler, with no true nucleus.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Animal cells contain the following sub-cellular structures:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nucleus</strong> — contains genetic material (DNA) that controls the cell's activities.</li>
<li><strong>Cell membrane</strong> — controls what enters and leaves the cell; it is selectively permeable.</li>
<li><strong>Mitochondria</strong> — the site of <dfn class="term" data-def="The chemical process in mitochondria that transfers energy from glucose, using oxygen.">aerobic respiration</dfn>, where energy is transferred from glucose.</li>
<li><strong>Ribosomes</strong> — tiny structures where proteins are synthesised (made).</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Plant cells have all of these structures plus some additional ones:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Cell wall</strong> — a rigid outer layer made of cellulose that provides support and protection.</li>
<li><strong>Chloroplasts</strong> — contain chlorophyll, the green pigment that absorbs light energy for <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which plants use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen.">photosynthesis</dfn>.</li>
<li><strong>Vacuole</strong> — a large, permanent structure filled with cell sap that helps maintain the cell's shape.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5">
<h4>Key Fact</h4>
<p>Bacterial (prokaryotic) cells have chromosomal DNA that floats freely in the cytoplasm (no nucleus), plasmid DNA (small rings of extra DNA), a cell membrane, ribosomes, and some have a flagellum for movement. They do NOT have mitochondria or chloroplasts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Specialised Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Cells become specialised to carry out particular functions. This process is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a cell becomes specialised for a particular function.">differentiation</dfn>. The Edexcel specification requires you to know three examples of specialised cells.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sperm cells</strong> are adapted for fertilisation:</p>
<ul>
<li>An <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure at the tip of a sperm cell that contains enzymes to digest the outer layer of the egg cell.">acrosome</dfn> at the tip releases enzymes to penetrate the egg cell.</li>
<li>A <dfn class="term" data-def="A nucleus containing half the normal number of chromosomes (23 in humans).">haploid nucleus</dfn> carries half the genetic information so a full set is restored at fertilisation.</li>
<li>Many mitochondria in the middle section provide energy for the tail to swim.</li>
<li>A long tail (flagellum) enables the sperm to move towards the egg.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Egg cells (ova)</strong> are adapted for fertilisation and early development:</p>
<ul>
<li>They contain nutrients in the cytoplasm to nourish the early embryo after fertilisation.</li>
<li>They have a haploid nucleus, so the full diploid number is restored when the sperm fuses with the egg.</li>
<li>After fertilisation, the cell membrane changes structure to prevent more than one sperm from entering. This is essential to maintain the correct chromosome number.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Ciliated epithelial cells</strong> line the airways (trachea and bronchi). They have tiny hair-like projections called <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny hair-like structures on the surface of epithelial cells that beat rhythmically to move mucus.">cilia</dfn> on their surface that beat rhythmically. This sweeps mucus (which has trapped dust and microorganisms) upwards towards the throat, keeping the lungs clean.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11">
<h4>Key Fact</h4>
<p>Cells become specialised through differentiation, so their structure is adapted to suit their function: sperm cells have a tail and many mitochondria for swimming, egg cells store nutrients for the embryo, and ciliated epithelial cells use cilia to sweep mucus out of the airways.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Microscopy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Cells are far too small to see with the naked eye, so scientists use microscopes to study them. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A microscope that uses light and glass lenses to magnify a specimen. It is cheap and can show living cells in colour, but has limited magnification and resolution.">light microscope</dfn> uses light and glass lenses. Light microscopes are cheap, portable and can show living cells in colour, but they only magnify up to about &times;2000 and cannot show small sub-cellular structures clearly.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A microscope that uses a beam of electrons instead of light, giving much higher magnification and resolution than a light microscope.">electron microscope</dfn> uses a beam of electrons instead of light. This gives a much higher magnification (over &times;1,000,000) and a much higher resolution, so scientists can see tiny sub-cellular structures such as ribosomes and the internal detail of mitochondria. However, electron microscopes are large, very expensive, and the specimen must be dead.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a microscope to distinguish between two points that are very close together. Higher resolution gives a clearer, more detailed image.">Resolution</dfn> is the ability to tell apart two points that are very close together. Because electron microscopes have a much higher resolution than light microscopes, they reveal far more detail &mdash; this is what allowed scientists to discover sub-cellular structures.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Magnification tells you how many times larger an image is than the real object. It is calculated using the formula:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">\(\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{real size}}\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">You can rearrange this to find the real size or the image size. Always make sure both measurements are in the <strong>same units</strong> before calculating &mdash; for example, convert millimetres (mm) to micrometres (&micro;m) by multiplying by 1000.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">For example, if a cell is 50&nbsp;&micro;m wide in real life and appears 100&nbsp;mm wide in a photograph, first convert 100&nbsp;mm to 100,000&nbsp;&micro;m, then magnification = 100,000 &divide; 50 = &times;2000.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20">
<h4>Key Fact</h4>
<p>Magnification = image size &divide; real size. Electron microscopes have much higher magnification <em>and</em> resolution than light microscopes, so they can show sub-cellular structures. Always convert both measurements to the same units before you calculate.</p>
</div>
