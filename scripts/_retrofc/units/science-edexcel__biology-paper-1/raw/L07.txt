<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Selective Breeding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of choosing organisms with desirable characteristics and breeding them together over many generations to enhance those traits.">Selective breeding</dfn> (also called artificial selection) is the process by which humans choose organisms with desirable characteristics and breed them together. This is repeated over many generations until the desired trait is strongly expressed in the population.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Selective breeding has been used for thousands of years. Examples include breeding <strong>food plants</strong> for higher yield, disease resistance or improved taste, and breeding <strong>domesticated animals</strong> for traits such as increased milk production in cows, faster growth in chickens or tameness in dogs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The process works because the parents with the best characteristics are selected, so their alleles are more likely to be passed on. Over many generations, the frequency of the desirable alleles increases in the population.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Write out the steps of selective breeding from memory: choose organisms with desired trait, breed them, select best offspring, repeat over many generations.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Selective breeding: choose parents with desirable traits → breed them → select the best offspring → repeat over many generations. Used to improve food plants and domesticated animals.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n6">A major risk of selective breeding is <dfn class="term" data-def="A reduction in genetic variation within a population, which can make the population more vulnerable to disease or environmental change.">inbreeding</dfn>. Because only organisms with similar desirable traits are bred, the gene pool narrows. This reduces genetic variation, making the population more vulnerable to new diseases or environmental changes. In animals, inbreeding can also lead to health problems — for example, breathing difficulties in flat-faced dog breeds.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">What Is Genetic Engineering?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of modifying the genome of an organism by inserting a gene from another organism, giving it a new desirable characteristic.">Genetic engineering</dfn> is the process of modifying the <dfn class="term" data-def="The entire set of DNA in an organism, including all of its genes.">genome</dfn> of an organism to introduce desirable characteristics. Unlike selective breeding, which works within a species, genetic engineering can transfer genes between completely different species. An organism that contains a gene from a different species is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="An organism that has had a gene from another species inserted into its genome using genetic engineering.">genetically modified organism</dfn> (GMO) or transgenic organism.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Main Stages of Genetic Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Edexcel specification requires you to describe the main stages of genetic engineering, including the use of restriction enzymes, ligase, sticky ends and vectors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Step 1 — Identify and cut out the desired gene.</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="Enzymes that cut DNA at specific base sequences, leaving short unpaired stretches called sticky ends.">Restriction enzyme</dfn>s are used to cut the desired gene out of the DNA of the donor organism. These enzymes cut at specific base sequences, leaving short unpaired stretches of bases called <dfn class="term" data-def="Short unpaired sections of bases left on cut DNA ends by restriction enzymes, allowing complementary sticky ends to pair up.">sticky ends</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Step 2 — Open the vector.</strong> The same restriction enzyme is used to cut open a <dfn class="term" data-def="A carrier used to transfer a gene into a host organism. Common vectors include bacterial plasmids and viruses.">vector</dfn> — a carrier molecule that will transport the gene into the host organism. Common vectors include bacterial <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, circular loop of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosomal DNA. Often used as a vector in genetic engineering.">plasmid</dfn>s and viruses. Because the same restriction enzyme is used, the vector has complementary sticky ends.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Step 3 — Insert the gene.</strong> The desired gene is inserted into the vector. The complementary sticky ends of the gene and the vector pair up. <dfn class="term" data-def="An enzyme that joins together two pieces of DNA by forming bonds between their sticky ends.">Ligase</dfn> enzyme is then used to seal the gene into the vector by joining the sugar-phosphate backbone, creating <dfn class="term" data-def="DNA that has been created by combining genetic material from two different organisms.">recombinant DNA</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Step 4 — Transfer to host organism.</strong> The vector carries the gene into the host organism’s cells. If the gene is successfully taken up, the host organism will produce the protein coded for by the inserted gene, giving it the desired characteristic.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Draw a flow diagram of genetic engineering from memory: restriction enzyme cuts gene + cuts plasmid → sticky ends match → ligase seals → vector transfers gene to host.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Genetic engineering: restriction enzymes cut out the gene (leaving sticky ends) → same enzyme opens the vector → gene inserted, sticky ends pair up → ligase seals the join → vector transfers gene to host organism.</p>
</div>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Examples of Genetic Engineering</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>GM Crops and Medicine</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">In agriculture, crops such as soya and maize have been genetically modified to be resistant to herbicides or to produce their own insecticide (Bt crops). Golden rice has been engineered to produce beta-carotene (vitamin A) to help prevent deficiency in developing countries.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">In medicine, the gene for human insulin has been inserted into bacteria. These bacteria are grown in fermenters and produce large quantities of human insulin to treat diabetes. This replaced insulin extracted from pig or cow pancreases, which sometimes caused allergic reactions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Benefits and Risks of Genetic Engineering and Selective Breeding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The Edexcel specification requires you to evaluate the benefits and risks of both genetic engineering and selective breeding in modern agriculture and medicine, including practical and ethical implications.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Benefits of genetic engineering:</strong> genes can be transferred between species that could never breed naturally; crops can be made pest-resistant, reducing pesticide use; medicines like insulin can be produced cheaply and reliably; Golden Rice could reduce vitamin A deficiency in developing countries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Risks and concerns:</strong> GM crops could cross-pollinate with wild plants, spreading modified genes into the environment in unpredictable ways; there are concerns about the long-term effects of eating GM food (though no evidence of harm has been found); some people have ethical objections to ‘playing God’ with organisms’ DNA; GM seeds are often patented, making farmers dependent on seed companies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Selective breeding</strong> is considered more ‘natural’ but reduces genetic diversity, which can cause health problems and makes populations vulnerable to disease. Genetic engineering is faster and more precise, but raises more ethical and environmental concerns.</p>
