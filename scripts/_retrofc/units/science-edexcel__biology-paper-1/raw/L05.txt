<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Structure of DNA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Deoxyribonucleic acid — a large polymer that carries the genetic instructions for building and running an organism.">DNA</dfn> (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a polymer made of two strands coiled together to form a <strong>double helix</strong>. The two strands are linked by pairs of complementary <dfn class="term" data-def="The four molecules (adenine, thymine, cytosine, guanine) that pair up across the two strands of DNA.">bases</dfn> joined by weak hydrogen bonds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Each strand is built from repeating units called <dfn class="term" data-def="The monomer of DNA, consisting of a sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases (A, T, C or G).">nucleotide</dfn>s. Every nucleotide has three parts: a sugar, a phosphate group and one of four bases — adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) or guanine (G). The bases always pair in a fixed pattern: A with T and C with G. This is called <strong>complementary base pairing</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Sketch a short DNA ladder from memory. Label the sugar-phosphate backbone and write in 5 base pairs using A-T and C-G rules.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>DNA is a double helix of nucleotides. Each nucleotide = sugar + phosphate + base. Bases pair A–T and C–G, joined by weak hydrogen bonds.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Genomes and Genes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The entire set of DNA in an organism, containing all of its genes.">genome</dfn> is the entire DNA of an organism. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A section of DNA that codes for a specific protein.">gene</dfn> is a small section of DNA on a <dfn class="term" data-def="A long, coiled molecule of DNA found in the nucleus. Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes.">chromosome</dfn> that codes for a specific protein. The order of bases along a gene acts as a code, telling the cell which <dfn class="term" data-def="Small molecules that are joined together in a specific order to build a protein.">amino acid</dfn>s to join together to make that protein. Different proteins give an organism its characteristics.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">There is usually extensive <dfn class="term" data-def="Differences in the characteristics of individuals within a population.">genetic variation</dfn> within a population of a species. This variation arises through mutations. Most genetic <dfn class="term" data-def="A random change in the base sequence of DNA. Most mutations have no effect on the phenotype.">mutation</dfn>s have no effect on the phenotype. Some have a small effect, and only rarely does a single mutation significantly change the phenotype.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Meiosis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of cell division that produces four genetically different haploid gametes, each with half the chromosome number.">Meiosis</dfn> is the type of cell division that produces <dfn class="term" data-def="Sex cells (sperm and egg in animals) that contain half the normal number of chromosomes.">gamete</dfn>s — sex cells. Unlike mitosis, meiosis produces four genetically different <dfn class="term" data-def="Having only one set of chromosomes — half the full number. Human gametes have 23 chromosomes.">haploid</dfn> daughter cells, each with half the chromosome number of the parent cell. The stages of meiosis are not required for this specification.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In humans, body cells are <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two complete sets of chromosomes. Human body cells are diploid with 46 chromosomes.">diploid</dfn> with 46 chromosomes. Meiosis halves this to 23 per gamete. At fertilisation, two gametes fuse and the full diploid number (46) is restored in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The cell formed when two gametes fuse at fertilisation. It is diploid and develops into a new organism.">zygote</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Compare mitosis and meiosis in a table: number of daughter cells, chromosome number, genetically identical or different, and where each occurs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meiosis produces 4 genetically different haploid gametes. Fertilisation restores the diploid number. This is why offspring from sexual reproduction show genetic variation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Alleles and Inheritance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A version of a gene. Different alleles produce different forms of a characteristic.">allele</dfn> is a version of a gene. Organisms inherit two alleles for each gene — one from each parent. If both alleles are the same the organism is <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two identical alleles for a gene, e.g. BB or bb.">homozygous</dfn>; if different it is <dfn class="term" data-def="Having two different alleles for a gene, e.g. Bb.">heterozygous</dfn>. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An allele that is always expressed when present, even if only one copy is inherited.">dominant</dfn> allele is expressed whenever it is present. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An allele only expressed when two copies are present (homozygous recessive).">recessive</dfn> allele is only expressed in the homozygous state.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The combination of alleles is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The genetic makeup of an organism — the alleles it carries for a gene.">genotype</dfn>. The observable characteristic it produces is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The observable characteristics of an organism, determined by its genotype and environment.">phenotype</dfn>. Most phenotypic features result from multiple genes, not single-gene inheritance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Monohybrid Crosses and Punnett Squares</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A monohybrid cross tracks one gene with two alleles. A Punnett square shows all possible offspring genotypes. For example, crossing two heterozygous parents (Bb × Bb) gives genotypes BB, Bb, Bb and bb — a 3:1 phenotype ratio (3 dominant : 1 recessive).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">You must be able to calculate probabilities, ratios and percentages from these crosses. In the example above there is a 75% (\(\frac{3}{4}\)) chance of the dominant phenotype and a 25% (\(\frac{1}{4}\)) chance of the recessive phenotype. Family pedigree diagrams can also be used to trace the inheritance of alleles across generations.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Draw a Punnett square from memory for Bb x Bb. Write out the genotype ratio (1 BB : 2 Bb : 1 bb) and the phenotype ratio (3:1). Then do the same for Bb x bb.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Use Punnett squares to predict offspring genotypes and phenotypes. Bb × Bb gives 3:1 dominant : recessive. Calculate outcomes as ratios, fractions or percentages.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Sex Determination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The 23rd pair of chromosomes determines biological sex. Females have two X chromosomes (XX); males have one X and one Y (XY). Each egg carries an X chromosome. Sperm carry either X or Y. If an X-carrying sperm fertilises the egg the offspring is female; if a Y-carrying sperm fertilises the egg the offspring is male. This gives a 50:50 chance, which you can show with a Punnett square.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Variation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Variation in phenotype has two causes. <strong>Genetic variation</strong> arises from mutations and from sexual reproduction (meiosis shuffles alleles, and fertilisation creates new combinations). <strong>Environmental variation</strong> comes from an organism’s surroundings — for example, diet, climate or exercise. Most phenotypic features are influenced by both.</p>
