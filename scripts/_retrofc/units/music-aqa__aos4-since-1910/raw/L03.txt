--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Adams, Reich and Riley build music from a steady pulse and short repeated cells through the additive process, phasing and layering, and how to spot minimalism in an unfamiliar extract.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Minimalism: process instead of themes</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Minimalism is one of the four listening strands in Area of Study 4. In the exam you might hear an unfamiliar extract and have to recognise it as <dfn class="term" data-def="Music built from short repeated patterns, a steady pulse and gradual, rule-based change, rather than from themes and development.">minimalism</dfn> within seconds, so you need to know what the technique actually sounds like, not just its name.</p><p data-narration-id="n3">Area of Study 1 trained you to listen for themes and their <dfn class="term" data-def="The reworking of a theme through fragmentation, change of key or change of instrumentation.">development</dfn> in <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure built on two contrasting themes that are stated, developed and restated.">sonata form</dfn>. Minimalism works the opposite way: there is no theme to develop. A composer sets up a short musical idea and a rule for changing it, then lets that rule run. The structure you hear is the process itself, not a plot of themes coming and going.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover the box and write from memory the three named minimalist composers plus one key technique (e.g. phasing) each used."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Minimalism builds pieces from a steady pulse and short repeated cells, changed by a process such as addition or phasing, instead of from themes that are stated and developed.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n5">Every minimalist extract you hear rests on two constants: a <dfn class="term" data-def="A steady, unchanging beat that runs underneath the music, often continuing unbroken from start to end.">pulse</dfn> that never lets up, and a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short musical pattern, often only a bar or two long, that repeats far more than an ordinary motif.">cell</dfn> &mdash; a short pattern repeated over and over. Everything else in this lesson is a way of changing that cell over time.</p><p data-narration-id="n6">Adams, Reich and Riley are all in copyright, so the links below open official recordings on the performers&rsquo; or label&rsquo;s own channels rather than audio hosted here.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n7">The additive process, and how In C differs &mdash; Terry Riley</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/qzmi7Wm10Y0" title="Terry Riley — In C, live at Millennium Park" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Watch: Riley &mdash; <em>In C</em>. Dozens of players move through the same 53 cells at their own pace.</figcaption></figure><p data-narration-id="n8">In an <dfn class="term" data-def="A process where notes are added to a repeating pattern one at a time, so the pattern grows longer with each repeat.">additive process</dfn>, a cell grows by one note at a time: play it, add a note, play the new version, add another note, and the pattern lengthens in front of you. Terry Riley&rsquo;s <em>In C</em> works differently &mdash; built not on note-by-note growth but on staggered repetition. It is not one fixed melody but 53 short numbered patterns; every player in the ensemble repeats each pattern as many times as they like before moving to the next, so the players drift in and out of step with each other and no two performances ever match.</p><p data-narration-id="n9">A performance of <em>In C</em> typically runs anywhere from forty-five to ninety minutes &mdash; far too long for a revision session. Play a few minutes instead, with the additive process in mind.</p><ul class="sv-listen-links" data-narration-id="n10"><li><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AVfAoNHjTHQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Terry Riley, In C (Bang on a Can All-Stars)</a> &mdash; each player moves through the 53 numbered patterns at their own pace, so listen for the ensemble sliding in and out of alignment as a result.</li></ul><h2 data-narration-id="n11">Phasing and gradual change &mdash; Steve Reich</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/liYkRarIDfo" title="Steve Reich — Clapping Music (London Sinfonietta)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Watch: Reich &mdash; <em>Clapping Music</em>. Two performers, one pattern, one of them shifting a quaver at a time.</figcaption></figure><p data-narration-id="n12">Steve Reich&rsquo;s signature technique is <dfn class="term" data-def="Two identical parts that start together in time, then one gradually shifts until they are out of step, creating new interlocking patterns.">phasing</dfn>: two players start an identical pattern together, then one very gradually pulls ahead until the two lines lock into a new relationship. Reich built entire pieces from this single idea. In large ensemble works such as <em>Music for 18 Musicians</em>, that same instinct for extremely gradual, almost imperceptible change is stretched across a much bigger canvas: pulsing chords, tuned percussion parts that interlock note against note in a technique called <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique where two parts interlock by alternating single notes, so neither part plays a full melody alone but the two together create one.">hocket</dfn>, and a very slow <dfn class="term" data-def="How often the underlying chord changes; slow harmonic rhythm means the same chord is held for a long time before it moves.">harmonic rhythm</dfn> that only shifts every few minutes.</p><p data-narration-id="n13"><em>Music for 18 Musicians</em> runs to roughly an hour, so do not attempt the whole piece. Pick any five minutes and listen for how little happens, and how gradually it happens &mdash; that slowness is the point, not a flaw.</p><ul class="sv-listen-links" data-narration-id="n14"><li><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1oOmUi4HGt0" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Steve Reich, Music for 18 Musicians</a> &mdash; sample any five minutes and notice how little changes, and how gradually it changes.</li></ul><h2 data-narration-id="n15">Layering and texture &mdash; John Adams</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/5LoUm_r7It8" title="John Adams — Short Ride in a Fast Machine (BBC Proms)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Watch: Adams &mdash; <em>Short Ride in a Fast Machine</em>. Four and a half minutes of layered, pulsing orchestra.</figcaption></figure><p data-narration-id="n16">A minimalist texture rarely arrives all at once. Layer after layer is added on top of the pulse, thickening the sound until a listener notices the piece has quietly built from a bare few instruments into a full ensemble. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Adding instrumental or vocal layers on top of an existing pattern one at a time, so the texture thickens gradually.">layering</dfn> is easiest to hear when a piece is short enough to take in whole. John Adams&rsquo; <em>Short Ride in a Fast Machine</em> lasts about four minutes, which makes it the most immediately graspable of the three pieces in this lesson and a good place to start.</p><ul class="sv-listen-links" data-narration-id="n17"><li><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bekh-sZ0-A8" target="_blank" rel="noopener">John Adams, Short Ride in a Fast Machine (Berliner Philharmoniker)</a> &mdash; at under four minutes this is the clearest introduction to the whole technique, so start here before the longer pieces.</li></ul><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Minimalism vs sonata form, side by side</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n18">Sonata form states a theme, develops it through fragmentation and key change, then restates it. Minimalism states a cell, changes it through a process such as addition or phasing, and lets that process &mdash; not a return to the opening idea &mdash; carry the structure. If an extract has a clear tune that comes back transformed, think sonata form. If it has a short pattern repeating and mutating with almost no melody to speak of, think minimalism.</p></div></div></div><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ZXJWO2FQ16c" title="Steve Reich — Music for 18 Musicians (full performance)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Go deeper: Reich &mdash; <em>Music for 18 Musicians</em>, a full hour of interlocking pulse and slowly breathing texture.</figcaption></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n19">Why this matters in the exam</h2><p data-narration-id="n20">Section A of the listening exam plays short unfamiliar extracts and asks you to identify and describe what you hear. Marks come from naming the element, giving the correct technical term, and saying where in the extract it happened &mdash; not from a vague impression that the music &ldquo;sounds repetitive&rdquo;. Write &ldquo;additive process&rdquo; or &ldquo;phasing&rdquo;, not &ldquo;it keeps going&rdquo;.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<p>Minimalism trades themes for process: a pulse that never stops, a cell that grows, drifts, or gathers layers around it. Adams, Reich and Riley each show a different side of that idea. Head to the Area of Study 4 listening drill for this lesson and practise spotting the additive process, phasing and layering against extracts you have not heard before.</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>Section A tests recognition, not memory of a whole piece. If you hear a short repeating pattern under a steady, unbroken pulse, say &ldquo;minimalism&rdquo; and name the specific process &mdash; additive process, phasing, or layering &mdash; rather than just saying the music &ldquo;repeats&rdquo;.</p>
