Bart&oacute;k&rsquo;s music is still in copyright.</figcaption></figure><div class="sv-listening"><section class="sv-card sv-card--cover" data-title="The collector"><h2 data-narration-id="n1">Bartók, the folk-song collector</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n2">Béla Bartók did not just compose from imagination. From 1906 he travelled through remote villages in Hungary and Romania, and later further afield, with a phonograph, recording peasants singing their oldest songs. He and his friend Zoltán Kodály wrote thousands of these melodies down by hand, comparing versions and sorting them by type. </p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Fieldwork"><h2>How fieldwork changed his music</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n3">That fieldwork changed his own composing. Village singers rarely used the plain major or minor scales of a piano lesson. Bartók heard old <dfn class="term" data-def="A scale built from a pattern of tones and semitones other than major or minor, often drawn from folk melody.">modal scales</dfn>, short phrases repeated and varied rather than developed at length, and rhythms locked to the steps of a dance. All three habits carried straight into his own concert music, including Hungarian Sketches.</p><p data-narration-id="n4">Bartók grouped the tunes he collected into an &ldquo;old style&rdquo; &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="A scale using only five notes, common in older Hungarian folk melody.">pentatonic</dfn>, freely sung, without regular bar lines &mdash; and a &ldquo;new style&rdquo; shaped by newer dance forms with a steadier beat. Hungarian Sketches draws on both: slow, speech-like <dfn class="term" data-def="A flexible, expressive stretching and easing of tempo, without a strict beat.">rubato</dfn> melody in one movement, driving dance rhythm in the next.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="The suite"><h2 data-narration-id="n5">Hungarian Sketches: five pictures, four for your exam</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n6">Hungarian Sketches, Magyar Képek, or &ldquo;Hungarian Pictures&rdquo;, is an orchestral suite Bartók put together in 1931 from five short piano pieces he had written roughly twenty years earlier. Each movement is a small &ldquo;picture&rdquo; of Hungarian rural life &mdash; a village at dusk, a dance, a drunk stumbling home.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="The four movements"><h2>The four movements you need</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/DQHa1YZzlDY" title="Bartók — Hungarian Sketches (complete)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Listen: Bart&oacute;k &mdash; <em>Hungarian Sketches</em>, complete. Follow the movements as you read about each one.</figcaption></figure><div class="sv-card-body"><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="List all five movement titles of Hungarian Pictures in order from memory, then check against your notes."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Your exam asks for four of the five movements: 1 Evening in the Village, 2 Bear Dance, 4 Slightly Tipsy, and 5 Swineherd&rsquo;s Dance. Movement 3, Melody, is not on the specification &mdash; do not spend revision time on it.</p></div></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Orchestration"><h2 data-narration-id="n9">Orchestration with a light touch</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n10">Hungarian Sketches is written for orchestra &mdash; woodwind including piccolo, bass clarinet and contrabassoon, horns, trumpets, trombones and tuba, timpani, percussion including side drums, triangle, cymbals and bass drum, and strings &mdash; but used with a light touch.</p><p data-narration-id="qs1">Evening in the Village uses only woodwind, two horns and strings. Bartók keeps much of the texture thin, often just a solo woodwind line over a handful of accompanying instruments, so each &ldquo;picture&rdquo; keeps the intimate feel of a folk tune rather than a big symphonic statement.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Sonority"><h2>Precision about sonority</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n11">What examiners reward here is Bartók&rsquo;s precision about sonority. The strings switch constantly between bowed playing and <dfn class="term" data-def="A string technique: plucking the string with a finger instead of drawing the bow across it.">pizzicato</dfn>, add fast <dfn class="term" data-def="A rapid repetition of a single note or quick alternation between two notes, used for shimmer or tension.">tremolo</dfn> for shimmer, play muted (con sordino) and are even asked to play <em>ruvido</em> &mdash; coarsely.</p><p data-narration-id="qs2">The brass mute and unmute; the triangle player is told to use a metal beater. Almost every note of the woodwind melodies carries an articulation instruction. That level of detailed instruction about instrumental colour and dynamics is a signature of the piece &mdash; and a ready-made Section B answer.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Snap rhythms"><h2 data-narration-id="n12">Snap rhythms and folk-dance patterns</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rhythmic figure of a very short, accented note followed by a longer note &mdash; the reverse of the usual long-short pattern.">snap rhythm</dfn> is a very short, accented note followed by a longer one. It gives a dance an off-balance, kicking feel.</p><p data-narration-id="qs3">It comes straight from the rhythm of spoken Hungarian and of Hungarian folk dance steps. The dance movements drive differently &mdash; on short repeated <dfn class="term" data-def="A short musical pattern repeated persistently, often in the bass or accompaniment.">ostinato</dfn> figures that keep the dance driving forward.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Evening in the Village" data-track="t1" data-chapters="t1c1,t1c2,t1c3,t1c4,t1c5"><h2 data-narration-id="n14">Evening in the Village (Movement 1)</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n15">The movement is in <dfn class="term" data-def="A symmetrical shape that goes out and comes back: A B A1 B1 A2.">arch form</dfn> &mdash; A, B, A1, B1, A2 &mdash; a symmetry Bart&oacute;k was known for. The A sections are marked Lento <dfn class="term" data-def="Freely stretching and easing the tempo.">rubato</dfn>, slow and speech-like<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t1">1</button>. The B sections are Allegretto, nearly twice the speed<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="43.7" data-track="t1">2</button>.</p><p data-narration-id="qs4">A1 brings the slow music back varied<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="66" data-track="t1">3</button>, B1 the quick music with trills added<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="98" data-track="t1">4</button>, and A2 closes the arch<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="121.1" data-track="t1">5</button>. It is mostly 4/4, but bars of 3/4 and 2/4 near the end unsettle the ending.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Evening: mode and melody" data-track="t1"><h2>Evening in the Village: mode, melody and texture</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs14">Both melodies come from the F sharp <dfn class="term" data-def="A five-note scale, common in folk melody.">minor pentatonic</dfn> scale, and the A section uses the <dfn class="term" data-def="The natural minor scale &mdash; here starting on F sharp.">Aeolian mode</dfn> on F sharp. Both were typical of the folk music Bart&oacute;k collected.</p><p data-narration-id="qs15">The A melody is mainly a descending <dfn class="term" data-def="Smoothly, notes joined together.">legato</dfn> phrase &mdash; the musical equivalent of a sigh at the end of the day. The B melody is the opposite: staccato, <dfn class="term" data-def="Moving by step, without leaps.">conjunct</dfn> quavers over short pizzicato, so not everyone&rsquo;s evening is calm.</p><p data-narration-id="qs16">Texture is melody and accompaniment, with different woodwind taking the solo line over chordal accompaniment &mdash; either long held notes or pizzicato crotchets. Dynamics run from ppp to forte. Bart&oacute;k avoids traditional cadences and uses non-functional chords, so the key stays deliberately unclear.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Bear Dance" data-track="t2" data-chapters="t2c1,t2c2,t2c3,t2c4,t2c5"><h2 data-narration-id="n17">Bear Dance (Movement 2)</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n18">Bart&oacute;k described this movement as the impression of a bear dancing to its leader&rsquo;s song, growling to a drum. It is in varied <dfn class="term" data-def="The same music repeated with variations, verse by verse.">strophic form</dfn>, and the easiest way to hear the sections is to follow the <dfn class="term" data-def="A note held or repeated underneath while the music changes above it.">pedal note</dfn>.</p><p data-narration-id="qs5">A sits on a D pedal in cellos, basses, horns and tuba<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t2">1</button>. B moves the pedal to A flat in the second violins<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="16" data-track="t2">2</button> &mdash; D to A flat is a <dfn class="term" data-def="The most dissonant interval: three whole tones apart.">tritone</dfn>. Then A1<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="33" data-track="t2">3</button>, B1<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="52" data-track="t2">4</button> and A2<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="71.7" data-track="t2">5</button> complete the symmetry. The movement ends on a D major chord.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Bear: mode and forces" data-track="t2"><h2>Bear Dance: mode, forces and rhythm</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs17">The melody of A is based on the Aeolian mode on D sharp; B uses the Lydian dominant scale on D. Phrases are mostly four bars long and deliberately dissonant, which is what gives the dance its excitement.</p><p data-narration-id="qs18">Bart&oacute;k adds two trumpets, two trombones, tuba, contrabassoon, timpani and two side drums to the forces of the first movement. The side drums start without snares and switch them on for B<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="16" data-track="t2">2</button>. A triangle appears in A1<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="33" data-track="t2">3</button>, the brass sometimes play muted, and the strings both bow and pluck.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Bear: texture" data-track="t2"><h2>Bear Dance: texture and rhythm</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs19">Texture revolves around the pedal notes, which act as a rhythmic drone. The melody is harmonised in thirds, sixths and octaves and moves in block chords. Repeated quavers in the pedal and snare drum build the anticipation, while the melody moves mainly in crotchets &mdash; the rhythm of the Hungarian <em>kan&aacute;sz</em> folk dance. Dynamics run from piano to forte with <dfn class="term" data-def="A sudden forced accent.">sforzando</dfn> stabs, and there is a great deal of staccato.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Slightly Tipsy" data-track="t3" data-chapters="t3c1,t3c2,t3c3"><h2 data-narration-id="n21">Slightly Tipsy (Movement 4)</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/oY3lGUOvi2A" title="Bartók — Slightly Tipsy (Chicago Symphony Orchestra)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Listen: &lsquo;Slightly Tipsy&rsquo; on its own &mdash; snap rhythms and a deliberately unsteady pulse.</figcaption></figure><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n22">True to its title, this movement portrays someone stumbling rather than walking in a straight line. It is in <dfn class="term" data-def="Three sections: A, a contrasting B, then a return of A.">ternary form</dfn>: an opening A section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t3">1</button>, a contrasting B section<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="39" data-track="t3">2</button>, then a return of A that is significantly developed rather than simply repeated<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="97" data-track="t3">3</button>.</p><p data-narration-id="qs6">Almost every note of the opening melody is decorated with <dfn class="term" data-def="Crushed grace notes &mdash; a very quick note squeezed in before the main one.">acciaccaturas</dfn> &mdash; the single feature that most creates the tipsy feel<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t3">1</button>. The opening melody is built on the interval of a perfect fourth; the second motif descends by step.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Tipsy: the sound" data-track="t3"><h2>Slightly Tipsy: sound and texture</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs8">Sonority is mostly woodwind and strings. The strings sometimes play <dfn class="term" data-def="Striking the string with the wood of the bow instead of the hair.">col legno</dfn>, and at the start they are muted and told to play at the tip of the bow for a thin, quiet sound.</p><p data-narration-id="qs9">Tonality is deliberately ambiguous. It begins major, and the B section moves to G minor<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="39" data-track="t3">2</button>, with parallel chords, chromatic harmony and dissonant added-note chords.</p><p data-narration-id="qs10">The marking is Allegretto <dfn class="term" data-def="Freely stretching and easing the tempo.">rubato</dfn>, so the music speeds up and slows down. It is mostly 4/4, but a few bars of 2/4 and one bar of 5/4 unsettle the phrasing. Dynamics run from pianissimo to forte with sudden changes, and slur-staccato articulation makes the music sway.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Swineherd's Dance" data-track="t4" data-chapters="t4c1,t4c2,t4c3,t4c4,t4c5"><h2 data-narration-id="n24">Swineherd&rsquo;s Dance (Movement 5)</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n25">The suite ends with the only genuine collected folk tune in it. Bart&oacute;k recorded it in Tolna County in 1907. This movement has more sections than any other here. It opens with an introduction over a <dfn class="term" data-def="A sustained note held under the music, like a bagpipe.">drone</dfn>, with fragments of the tune appearing before it properly arrives<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="0" data-track="t4">1</button>.</p><p data-narration-id="qs7">The first half of the folk melody enters<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="12" data-track="t4">2</button>, then the second half<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="32" data-track="t4">3</button>. Both halves come back changed: a reworked second half, a four-bar link, then the first half again<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="68" data-track="t4">4</button>. After a three-bar link the second half is fragmented and developed<button type="button" class="sv-ap-ref" data-t="86" data-track="t4">5</button>, with its final two bars held back &mdash; the coda is built on exactly those two bars.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Swineherd: the sound" data-track="t4"><h2>Swineherd&rsquo;s Dance: mode and sound</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs11">The tonal centre is A, set by the drone, and the mode is <dfn class="term" data-def="A major-sounding mode with a flattened seventh &mdash; A major with G natural.">mixolydian</dfn> on A. The B section shifts its drone to D before returning to A, and G sharps in its second half lean towards G major.</p><p data-narration-id="qs12">The melody opens with leaps of fourths and fifths, then moves by step, and sits quite high. The woodwind carry it &mdash; piccolo, flute, oboe and clarinet &mdash; over strings playing both bowed and pizzicato, muted and unmuted. At the very end two solo violins play <dfn class="term" data-def="Light, glassy notes made by touching the string rather than pressing it down.">harmonics</dfn>.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Swineherd: rhythm" data-track="t4"><h2>Swineherd&rsquo;s Dance: metre and rhythm</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="qs13">The metre is 2/4 throughout and the marking is Allegro molto: quick and lively, with moments of calm and changes of tempo. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A short pattern repeated persistently.">ostinato</dfn> rhythm &mdash; quaver, crotchet, quaver &mdash; drives many sections, and the horns and trumpets have <dfn class="term" data-def="Accented off the main beat.">syncopated</dfn> melodies. Dynamics run from pianissimo to fortissimo with many crescendos and diminuendos.</p></div></section><section class="sv-card" data-title="Section B"><h2 data-narration-id="n27">Writing about Hungarian Sketches in Section B</h2><div class="sv-card-body"><p data-narration-id="n28">Section B marks come from three things, in this order: name the musical element or technique, give the correct technical term for it, and say exactly where in the movement it happens. &ldquo;Bartók uses folk music&rdquo; earns nothing on its own. &ldquo;Bartók builds the Allegretto dance tune of Evening in the Village on scotch-snap rhythms&rdquo; earns marks, because it names the feature, uses the right term, and locates it.</p><p data-narration-id="n29">Practise building answers in that order for each of the four required movements, and keep the ostinato and scotch-snap vocabulary ready &mdash; and make sure each one is attached to the right movement.</p></div></section></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<p>Bartók&rsquo;s years collecting folk song from Hungarian and Romanian villagers reshaped his musical language: modal scales instead of major and minor, short repeated phrases instead of long development, and dance rhythms built into the music itself. Hungarian Sketches puts all of this on display across the four movements you need &mdash; Evening in the Village, Bear Dance, Slightly Tipsy and Swineherd&rsquo;s Dance &mdash; with the scotch snaps of Evening in the Village&rsquo;s dance tune and the driving ostinati of the dance movements as the features examiners come back to most often. Now work through the matching listening drill for Hungarian Sketches and test yourself against short extracts under exam conditions.</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>In Section B, never just say &ldquo;Bartók uses folk music.&rdquo; Name the actual feature &mdash; modal scale, snap rhythm, cimbalom-effect, ostinato &mdash; give it its correct term, and say which movement it is in. Marks come from the term and the location, not from a general impression.</p>
