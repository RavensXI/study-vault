--- DESCRIPTION ---
How composers writing after 1910 used tone colour, spacing, long-range dynamics, jazz influence and the wind band to build a new twentieth-century sound world.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A New Sound World</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Before 1910, most classical music built its effect from melody, harmony and rhythm, in that order. Tone colour mattered, but it served the tune underneath it. After 1910, many composers began treating <dfn class="term" data-def="The characteristic sound quality of an instrument or voice, separate from its pitch or volume.">timbre</dfn> itself as a subject worth composing with, not just a wrapper around a melody. The choice of instrument became part of the musical idea, not an afterthought.</p><p data-narration-id="n3">This shows up first in <dfn class="term" data-def="The art of choosing which instruments play which musical material, and how they combine.">orchestration</dfn>. Composers picked unusual instrument pairings, extreme registers and exposed solo colours precisely because of how they sounded, not just because of the notes they played. A phrase given to a bassoon high in its range, or to a solo flute over hushed strings, means something different from the same phrase given to a full string section &mdash; even if every pitch is identical.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover the box, then write from memory the three focus areas: orchestration, dynamics, and space."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The same melody can sound completely different depending on which instrument plays it, how loudly, and how many other parts surround it &mdash; twentieth-century composers built entire pieces around exploiting this.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n5">Open Space versus Dense Texture</h2><figure class="sv-embed"><div class="sv-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/Q3aUMKrCh8Q" title="Copland — Appalachian Spring (score video)" loading="lazy" allow="fullscreen" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption class="sv-embed-cap">Watch with the score: Copland &mdash; <em>Appalachian Spring</em>. Hear how much air sits between the parts.</figcaption></figure><p data-narration-id="n6">Composers also began treating <dfn class="term" data-def="How many musical layers are sounding at once, and how they relate to each other.">texture</dfn> as an expressive choice in its own right. A texture can be widely spaced and open &mdash; a single melodic line with only a few sustained notes underneath, and a wide gap between the highest and lowest parts &mdash; or dense and layered, with many independent lines crowding the same registers at once. Neither approach is better than the other; each creates a completely different sound world, and composers moved between them for contrast.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n7"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Open, Widely Spaced Texture</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/aos-listening/aos4_pastoral_orchestral.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> the string writing is widely spaced, open and quiet, and the main melody is a simple solo woodwind line. The overall character is slow, spacious and calm.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">StudyVault study extract &mdash; written to demonstrate the style.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n8">Long-Range Control of Dynamics</h2><p data-narration-id="n9">Twentieth-century composers also stretched <dfn class="term" data-def="The range between the quietest and loudest sound a piece uses.">dynamics</dfn> to extremes, and controlled them over long spans of time rather than just from phrase to phrase. Instead of one sudden forte, a passage might begin at the edge of audibility and build, bar by bar, across several minutes, in one continuous <dfn class="term" data-def="A gradual increase in volume.">crescendo</dfn> towards a huge climax. That long-range shape is itself a structural device, not just a passing effect &mdash; it can hold an entire movement together.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n10"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; A Long-Range Crescendo</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/aos-listening/respighi_catacomb.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> a very slow tempo, long sustained melodic lines, and dynamics that begin very quietly and grow louder across the extract.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">Respighi, <em>Pines of Rome</em> (1924), II. Pines Near a Catacomb &mdash; United States Army Band. Public domain.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n11">Jazz and Popular Idioms Enter the Concert Hall</h2><p data-narration-id="n12">Jazz and blues, both new popular styles in the early twentieth century, fed directly into concert music. Composers borrowed sliding pitches, bent notes and syncopated rhythms from jazz players and wrote them straight into orchestral scores. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A note bent or flattened for expressive effect, borrowed from blues and jazz.">blue note</dfn> or a smooth upward slide can bring a whole jazz atmosphere into a symphony orchestra without a single jazz musician on stage.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n13"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Jazz Influence in the Concert Hall</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/aos-listening/gershwin_rhapsody.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> a clarinet plays the famous opening solo, sliding smoothly upwards. The whole passage is clearly influenced by jazz and blues.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">Gershwin, <em>Rhapsody in Blue</em> (1924) &mdash; &ldquo;The President&rsquo;s Own&rdquo; United States Marine Band. Public domain.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Wind Band as a Serious Concert Medium</h2><p data-narration-id="n15">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An ensemble of woodwind, brass and percussion instruments, without strings.">wind band</dfn> had long existed for marches and military occasions. In the twentieth century, composers began writing original concert works for it, treating it as a serious medium with its own palette of colours &mdash; bright massed brass, reedy woodwind choirs, a full percussion section &mdash; rather than as a cheaper substitute for a full orchestra.</p><figure class="sv-listen" data-narration-id="n16"><figcaption class="sv-listen-label">Listen &mdash; Wind Band Writing</figcaption><audio class="sv-listen-player" controls preload="none" src="https://pub-f7b76d81365b4b2f954567763694a24e.r2.dev/music-aqa/aos-listening/prokofiev_march.mp3"></audio><p class="sv-listen-for"><strong>Listen for:</strong> the performing forces are a wind and percussion band, playing a steady march rhythm at mostly loud, bold dynamics.</p><p class="sv-listen-credit">Prokofiev, <em>March, Op. 99</em> (1943&ndash;44) &mdash; &ldquo;The President&rsquo;s Own&rdquo; United States Marine Band. Public domain.</p></figure><h2 data-narration-id="n17">Loosening Tonality</h2><p data-narration-id="n18">Traditional tonal music treats dissonance as tension that must resolve to consonance. After 1910, many composers loosened this rule. Harmony often still points to a home key, but it can be <dfn class="term" data-def="Harmony that keeps some sense of a home key but stretches beyond simple major and minor chords and their normal resolutions.">extended tonality</dfn>: chords built from added and stacked notes, not just plain triads. Others turned to <dfn class="term" data-def="A scale pattern, often folk-derived, used instead of standard major or minor for a different, older-sounding colour.">modal</dfn> scales borrowed from folk music or earlier eras for a different flavour entirely. Crucially, <dfn class="term" data-def="A combination of notes that sounds unstable or clashing.">dissonance</dfn> itself became a colour to enjoy, not a problem that always needed fixing.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Is this the same as atonal music?</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n19">Not quite. Fully atonal music, including twelve-note serialism, abandons a home key altogether. Extended tonality and modality are gentler: the music still points towards a tonal centre, but reaches it by a wider, more colourful route than strict major-minor harmony allows.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n20">Putting the Picture Together</h2><p data-narration-id="n21">When an unfamiliar extract from this period comes up in the exam, run through these five clues. Is timbre doing more than decorating a tune? Is the texture wide open or densely packed? Are the dynamics building over a long span rather than jumping suddenly? Is there any trace of jazz or blues? Could the forces be a wind band rather than a full orchestra? And does the harmony still feel tonal, even though it does not behave like a textbook major or minor key? Naming two or three of these, with the correct technical term, is worth more marks than a general impression.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<p>Twentieth-century composers turned colour, space, dynamics and even genre-crossing into compositional subjects, not decoration. Once you can hear these five features &mdash; timbre as subject, open versus dense texture, long-range dynamics, jazz influence, and wind-band writing &mdash; alongside a looser sense of tonality, you are ready to identify unfamiliar extracts from this era. Move on to the AoS4 listening drill to practise spotting them under exam conditions.</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>In Section A, name the element, give the correct technical term, and say exactly where in the extract you heard it &mdash; for example &ldquo;the dynamics build gradually from very quiet to loud across the whole extract&rdquo;, not just &ldquo;it gets louder&rdquo;. Vague impressions score no marks; precise vocabulary does.</p>
