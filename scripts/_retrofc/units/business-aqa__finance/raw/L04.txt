--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reading break-even charts, understanding margin of safety and judging investment using ARR.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Break-Even Actually Means</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before a business can make a profit, it has to cover all its costs — both fixed and variable. <dfn class="term" data-def="The level of output where total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither profit nor loss.">Break-even</dfn> is the exact level of sales where total revenue equals total costs. One unit fewer and the business is in loss; one unit more and it starts making profit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For managers, break-even is not abstract theory — it’s a target. A coffee shop owner who knows she needs to sell 220 lattes a week to break even can plan opening hours, staffing and marketing around that number. If she reliably beats that figure, she’s profitable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Two Break-Even Formulae</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">AQA requires interpretation of break-even charts, not drawing them from scratch. But the underlying numbers come from two formulae. The first gives the break-even point in units:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$\text{Break-even (units)} = \frac{\text{Fixed costs}}{\text{Contribution per unit}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between selling price per unit and variable cost per unit; what each unit contributes towards covering fixed costs.">Contribution per unit</dfn> is the selling price minus the variable cost per unit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$\text{Contribution} = \text{Selling price} - \text{Variable cost per unit}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">To convert break-even units into a revenue figure:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$\text{Break-even (revenue)} = \text{Break-even units} \times \text{Selling price}$$</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Worked Example — Oakleaf Coffee Again</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Fictional Oakleaf Coffee sells lattes at £3.50. Variable cost per latte is £1.10, and fixed costs are £1,200 a week. How many lattes does Oakleaf need to sell to break even?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Step 1 — contribution per latte:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$\text{Contribution} = \pounds3.50 - \pounds1.10 = \pounds2.40$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Step 2 — break-even (units):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$\text{Break-even} = \frac{\pounds1{,}200}{\pounds2.40} = 500 \text{ lattes per week}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Oakleaf needs to sell 500 lattes per week to cover every penny of fixed and variable costs. Anything above 500 is profit. Anything below is loss.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the break-even formula from memory, then explain what contribution per unit means in one sentence.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Break-even (units) = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit. Contribution = Selling price − Variable cost per unit. Every unit sold above the break-even point is pure profit.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Margin of Safety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between actual (or forecast) sales and the break-even level of sales.">margin of safety</dfn> shows how far a business can afford its sales to fall before it starts making a loss. It’s calculated as:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$\text{Margin of safety} = \text{Actual sales} - \text{Break-even sales}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">If Oakleaf actually sells 650 lattes a week (break-even is 500), the margin of safety is 150 lattes. Sales could drop by up to 150 before the shop starts losing money. A healthy margin of safety gives the business a cushion against bad weather, rivals opening nearby, or a general dip in demand.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>What Happens When Variables Change</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>If fixed costs rise</strong> — say rent increases by £300 a week — the break-even point rises because the numerator in the formula is bigger. Oakleaf would now need to sell £1,500 ÷ £2.40 = 625 lattes per week, not 500.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>If selling price rises</strong> — say the latte goes up to £4.00 — contribution becomes £4.00 − £1.10 = £2.90. Break-even falls to £1,200 ÷ £2.90 = 414 lattes. The shop has to sell fewer units because each one contributes more.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>If variable costs rise</strong> — say milk prices go up, pushing variable cost to £1.40 — contribution falls to £2.10. Break-even rises to £1,200 ÷ £2.10 = 572 lattes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Investment Decisions: Average Rate of Return</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Businesses don’t only use break-even. When weighing up a one-off investment project — a new delivery van, a factory extension, a coffee roaster — managers calculate the <dfn class="term" data-def="The average annual profit from an investment expressed as a percentage of the initial cost.">average rate of return</dfn> (ARR). ARR turns a total profit figure into an annual percentage so it can be compared to savings rates or other investments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Total profit from project} \div \text{years}}{\text{Initial investment}} \times 100$$</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">Worked Example — Buying a New Coffee Roaster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Oakleaf spends £20,000 on a new roaster. Over five years it generates £30,000 of profit in total. The ARR is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">$$\text{Average annual profit} = \frac{\pounds30{,}000}{5} = \pounds6{,}000$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\pounds6{,}000}{\pounds20{,}000} \times 100 = 30\%$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">A 30% ARR comfortably beats bank savings (around 4-5% currently). The investment looks strong. If Oakleaf had a rival project offering 45% ARR, she would pick that instead — assuming the risk was similar.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n35" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the ARR formula from memory. Then list the two things ARR does NOT take into account.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>ARR = (Total profit ÷ years) ÷ Initial investment × 100. It gives the average annual return as a percentage. It does NOT take timing of returns or the risk of the project into account — these have to be considered separately.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Strengths and Weaknesses of ARR</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Strengths:</strong> ARR is simple to calculate and easy to explain to non-finance staff. It produces a percentage, which can be compared directly with interest rates on savings, loans or alternative investments. It uses the full life of a project, not just the first year or two.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Weaknesses:</strong> ARR averages profits over the whole project, ignoring <em>when</em> the returns arrive — a project paying big profits in year 1 is treated the same as one paying them in year 5, even though money now is worth more than money later. ARR is also based on forecasts that may be wildly wrong, and it ignores the level of risk attached to a project. A 30% ARR on a reckless venture is worse than a 15% ARR on a safe one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n39">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n40">Break-even = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit. Every unit sold above this point is pure profit; every unit below is loss.</li><li data-narration-id="n41">Margin of safety = Actual sales − Break-even sales. It measures how much sales can fall before the business starts losing money.</li><li data-narration-id="n42">ARR = (Total profit ÷ years) ÷ Initial investment × 100. It turns a project’s total profit into an annual percentage for comparison with alternatives.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n38">AQA will not give you the formulae for break-even or ARR — memorise them. For break-even questions, always calculate contribution per unit first, then divide fixed costs by that figure. For ARR, the two-step order matters: find average annual profit first, then divide by initial investment and multiply by 100. Add the % sign — forgetting it loses a mark. For ‘Evaluate’ questions on break-even, acknowledge its weaknesses: it assumes all units sell at the same price and ignores changes in variable cost per unit as output scales.</p>
