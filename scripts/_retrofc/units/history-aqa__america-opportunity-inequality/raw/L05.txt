--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country at War with Itself</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The 1920s did not just bring jazz and assembly lines. It brought the most ambitious social-engineering experiment in American history (Prohibition), the largest white supremacist movement the country had ever seen (the revived Klan), the tightest immigration laws in its history, and a panic about communism that produced one of its most controversial trials. These were all responses by older, white, Protestant, rural America to the changes covered in Lessons 1&ndash;4. The Boom split the country.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the four divisions covered in this lesson and one event for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four divisions defined 1920s America: <strong>Prohibition</strong> (1920&ndash;33, the 18th Amendment), <strong>the revived Ku Klux Klan</strong> (membership reached around 4&ndash;5 million by 1925), <strong>immigration restriction</strong> (Emergency Quota Act 1921, Immigration Act 1924) and the <strong>Red Scare</strong>, which culminated in the Sacco and Vanzetti trial of 1921.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Prohibition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The period between 1920 and 1933 when the manufacture, sale and transport of alcohol were illegal in the USA, introduced by the 18th Amendment.">Prohibition</dfn> began in January 1920 with the 18th Amendment and the enforcing Volstead Act. Campaigners &mdash; the Anti-Saloon League, the Women&rsquo;s Christian Temperance Union, evangelical Protestant churches &mdash; argued that alcohol was destroying families, encouraging crime and ruining workers&rsquo; productivity. Their core support was rural, Protestant and white; they distrusted the saloons of the Catholic immigrant cities. Within months it was clear most Americans did not want to give up alcohol. The result was an enormous illegal economy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Illegal bars known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Illegal bars during Prohibition where alcohol was sold; New York alone is estimated to have had over 30,000 by 1929.">speakeasies</dfn> opened in every major city &mdash; New York alone had over 30,000 by 1929, more than the city had had legal saloons before Prohibition. <dfn class="term" data-def="The illegal making, selling and smuggling of alcohol during Prohibition.">Bootlegging</dfn> stretched from rum-runners off the Florida coast to backyard stills in West Virginia. Federal Prohibition agents (only about 1,500 nationally) could not police a country that did not want to be policed.</p>

He bribed the mayor, the police chief and many federal agents; estimates put his gang&rsquo;s 1927 income at around $60 million. Rival gangs fought over distribution &mdash; the St Valentine&rsquo;s Day Massacre of 1929 saw Capone&rsquo;s men gun down seven Bugs Moran gangsters. Federal agents could not catch Capone for murder; in 1931 he was finally jailed for tax evasion. He had, however, demonstrated that Prohibition had handed an entire industry to organised crime.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Ku Klux Klan Revived</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The original Klan had been crushed in the 1870s. A new <dfn class="term" data-def="A white supremacist organisation revived in 1915; by the mid-1920s it claimed around 4-5 million members and targeted African-Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.">Ku Klux Klan</dfn> was launched in Georgia in 1915, inspired in part by the Hollywood film <em>The Birth of a Nation</em>. Through the early 1920s it grew into a mass movement, its membership peaking at perhaps 4&ndash;5 million by 1925. Its targets were not just African-Americans but also Catholics, Jews and recent immigrants &mdash; anyone who did not fit the WASP (White Anglo-Saxon Protestant) ideal. It dominated state politics in Indiana, Oregon and Colorado. Its hooded marches, cross-burnings and lynchings spread fear across the South and into the Midwest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Klan&rsquo;s influence collapsed almost as fast as it grew. In 1925 D. C. Stephenson, the Grand Dragon of Indiana, was convicted of the rape and murder of a young woman, Madge Oberholtzer. The trial exposed the Klan&rsquo;s violence and corruption, and membership halved in two years. By 1929 the Klan was a fraction of its 1925 size &mdash; though the prejudice that had powered it remained.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Immigration Restriction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">For most of its history the USA had welcomed mass immigration. Between 1900 and 1914 around 13 million Europeans arrived. After the First World War, fear of revolution, the new Red Scare (below) and racism towards southern and eastern Europeans changed the politics. The <dfn class="term" data-def="1921 law that limited immigration to 3% of the foreign-born of each nationality counted in the 1910 census; aimed at reducing southern and eastern European arrivals.">Emergency Quota Act</dfn> of 1921 capped overall immigration at around 357,000 a year and set quotas favouring northern Europeans. The <dfn class="term" data-def="1924 law that cut total immigration to 165,000 a year and based national quotas on the 1890 census, almost eliminating arrivals from Italy, Poland and the Russian Empire.">Immigration Act</dfn> of 1924 went further: it cut overall immigration to 165,000 a year and pegged the quotas to the 1890 census &mdash; before most southern and eastern Europeans had arrived. Asian immigration was banned outright. The 1924 Act was the basis of US immigration policy for the next 41 years.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The Klan, Prohibition and the immigration Acts shared a base of supporters and a shared anxiety. By 1920 over half of all Americans lived in cities for the first time; the cities were full of southern and eastern Europeans, Catholics, Jews and African-Americans up from the South. The 1920 census made the change visible to anyone who read the figures. Older WASP (White Anglo-Saxon Protestant) America &mdash; rural, Protestant, north-western European in origin &mdash; saw its dominance slipping and reached for laws to reverse it. The 1924 Immigration Act was drafted by Albert Johnson, who openly cited &lsquo;eugenic&rsquo; arguments about race; the Klan&rsquo;s growth was strongest in midwestern states like Indiana whose populations had been shifting fastest; Prohibition aimed at the saloons of Catholic immigrant cities. Treat these as one connected backlash, not three separate stories &mdash; that is how examiners reward an answer.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Red Scare and Sacco and Vanzetti</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The 1917 Russian Revolution had terrified American business and government. The 1919 strike wave (over 4 million workers walked out, including the Boston police) and a series of anarchist letter bombs convinced Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer that communism was an immediate threat. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A period of intense fear of communism and anarchism in the USA after 1919, marked by raids and deportations under Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer.">Red Scare</dfn> peaked in the 1919&ndash;20 Palmer Raids: thousands were arrested, 556 immigrants were deported without trial. Civil-liberties campaigners protested, but the political climate had shifted decisively against the foreign-born and the political left.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In April 1920 a paymaster and a guard were shot dead in a robbery at a shoe factory in Massachusetts. Two Italian immigrants &mdash; Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti &mdash; were arrested. Both were known anarchists who had dodged the wartime draft. The evidence was thin and contradictory; the trial judge, Webster Thayer, openly described the men as &lsquo;anarchist bastards&rsquo;. They were convicted in 1921 and, after seven years of appeals, executed in August 1927. The case became internationally famous as a symbol of how the Red Scare and immigration prejudice could combine to deny justice. The spec names <dfn class="term" data-def="Italian-American anarchist immigrants whose 1921 conviction and 1927 execution for murder became an international symbol of Red Scare prejudice.">Sacco and Vanzetti</dfn> precisely because their case shows the intersection of three of this lesson&rsquo;s themes: communism panic, immigration restriction and ethnic prejudice.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the Sacco and Vanzetti case mattered beyond the men themselves.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Sacco and Vanzetti case (1920&ndash;27) is named in the spec because it captures three forces in one trial: anti-communist panic (Red Scare), prejudice against Italian-Americans, and the wider 1920s push to restrict immigration. Examiners reward students who explain its significance in those terms, not just retell the story.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Prohibition (1920&ndash;33) tried to eliminate alcohol but produced speakeasies, bootlegging and organised-crime empires like Al Capone&rsquo;s in Chicago.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The revived Ku Klux Klan reached 4&ndash;5 million members by 1925 before collapsing after the 1925 Stephenson scandal; immigration was sharply cut by the 1921 Emergency Quota Act and the 1924 Immigration Act.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Red Scare and the Sacco and Vanzetti case (1920&ndash;27) showed how fear of communism, immigrant prejudice and biased justice combined in one of the decade&rsquo;s most divisive episodes.</li>
</ul>
