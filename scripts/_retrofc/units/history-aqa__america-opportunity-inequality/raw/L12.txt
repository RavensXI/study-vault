--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Cold War fear created HUAC, the Hollywood blacklist and McCarthy’s 1950s witch hunts.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country in Fear</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1945 the United States was the only nuclear power. By 1949 the Soviet Union had its own atomic bomb, China had become Communist, and confidential American documents were appearing in Soviet archives. In June 1950 Communist North Korea invaded the South. To many ordinary Americans, the Cold War looked as if it were being lost from the inside &mdash; betrayed by spies, intellectuals and Hollywood writers. The result was the second great <dfn class="term" data-def="A period of intense fear of Communism in the United States after 1947, in which thousands of public servants, academics, actors and writers were investigated, blacklisted or jailed for suspected Communist sympathies.">Red Scare</dfn>, which dominated American politics from 1947 to 1954. Its central figure was a Republican senator from Wisconsin, Joseph McCarthy, but the machinery of fear was much wider than one man.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the four events between 1949 and 1950 that turned American Cold War anxiety into a domestic Red Scare.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Four events triggered the second Red Scare: the Soviet atomic bomb (August 1949), the Communist victory in China (October 1949), the conviction of Alger Hiss (January 1950), and the outbreak of the Korean War (June 1950). These convinced many Americans the country was losing the Cold War from the inside.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">HUAC and the Hollywood Ten</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The House Un-American Activities Committee &mdash; a US Congressional committee that from 1947 onwards investigated alleged Communist activity in government, education and entertainment, with a particular focus on Hollywood.">House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)</dfn>, originally founded in 1938, became a major political force in October 1947 when it began investigating alleged Communist influence in Hollywood. HUAC summoned 41 writers, directors and actors. Most cooperated; ten refused on First Amendment grounds to answer the question &lsquo;are you now or have you ever been a member of the Communist Party?&rsquo;. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Ten Hollywood screenwriters and directors who in October 1947 refused to answer HUAC&rsquo;s questions on Communist Party membership; they were jailed for contempt of Congress and added to a Hollywood blacklist that ended their careers.">Hollywood Ten</dfn> were jailed for contempt of Congress for between six and twelve months.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Hollywood studios, terrified of losing audiences, responded with the &lsquo;Waldorf Statement&rsquo; in November 1947 announcing they would not employ Communists. Around 320 entertainment-industry workers were eventually blacklisted; many never worked under their own names again. Charlie Chaplin lost his US re-entry permit in 1952 and never returned. Composer Hanns Eisler was deported. Actor Larry Parks confessed Communist Party membership and was blacklisted anyway. The blacklist demonstrated that fear of being seen to be soft on Communism could make private companies enforce political loyalty more harshly than the state itself.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Even before HUAC&rsquo;s Hollywood hearings, President Truman&rsquo;s Executive Order 9835 (March 1947) created the Federal Loyalty Programme. By 1953 around 4.7 million federal employees had been investigated; around 560 had been dismissed and around 6,000 had resigned rather than face investigation. The Internal Security Act of 1950 (the McCarran Act) required Communist organisations to register with the federal government and forbade Communists from working in defence industries. Truman vetoed it; Congress overrode him. State and local loyalty programmes spread further: by 1956 around 13 million Americans had been touched by some kind of loyalty test, often refusing to sign costing public-sector workers their jobs without due process.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Hiss and the Rosenbergs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Two espionage cases convinced wavering Americans that the threat was real. Alger Hiss, a former senior State Department official who had attended the 1945 Yalta Conference with Roosevelt, was accused by ex-Communist Whittaker Chambers in August 1948 of passing documents to the USSR in the 1930s. After two trials Hiss was convicted of perjury in January 1950 and sentenced to five years. The case was prosecuted by a young Republican congressman named Richard Nixon and made his career.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In June 1950 Julius Rosenberg, a New York electrical engineer, was arrested on charges of passing American atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. His wife Ethel was arrested two months later. Both were convicted in March 1951 and executed in the electric chair at Sing Sing in June 1953 &mdash; the only Americans executed for espionage in peacetime. Modern declassified Soviet cables (the Venona project) confirm Julius&rsquo;s espionage; Ethel&rsquo;s role is more disputed and historians broadly agree she was overcharged. At the time, the case appeared to confirm that Communist subversion reached into American homes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">McCarthy&rsquo;s Rise, 1950&ndash;54</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">On 9 February 1950 Senator <dfn class="term" data-def="Republican senator from Wisconsin (1947&ndash;57) whose unsubstantiated accusations of Communist subversion in government from 1950 onwards gave the Red Scare its name; censured by the Senate in December 1954.">Joseph McCarthy</dfn> of Wisconsin gave a speech at Wheeling, West Virginia. He waved a piece of paper and claimed it listed 205 known Communists working in the State Department. He never produced the list. The number changed in subsequent speeches (57, then 81). It did not matter. McCarthy&rsquo;s claim, made at the moment of maximum Cold War anxiety, dominated the news cycle and built him a national following.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Through 1950 to 1954 McCarthy used his Senate Committee on Government Operations to investigate alleged Communists in the State Department, the Voice of America, the Government Printing Office, the Army and even the libraries of US embassies abroad. Witnesses were summoned, careers ended, accusations broadcast. Few Senate colleagues challenged him in public &mdash; he had become electorally dangerous. Republican nominee Dwight Eisenhower, campaigning in Wisconsin in October 1952, dropped a planned defence of his friend General George Marshall (whom McCarthy had accused of pro-Communist sympathies) rather than confront McCarthy. The press largely repeated McCarthy&rsquo;s charges without checking them. Eisenhower privately disliked him but said publicly he &lsquo;would not get in the gutter with that guy&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Army-McCarthy Hearings and Decline</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">McCarthy&rsquo;s overreach was the Army. In 1954 he turned his accusations on the US Army for protecting an alleged Communist dentist. The Senate televised the hearings between April and June 1954. For 36 days, an audience of around 20 million Americans watched McCarthy bully witnesses, contradict himself and lose his temper. The decisive moment came on 9 June 1954, when Army counsel Joseph Welch confronted him with the now-famous question: &lsquo;Have you no sense of decency, sir, at long last?&rsquo;. Public approval of McCarthy collapsed from around 50% in January 1954 to around 35% by June.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In December 1954 the Senate voted 67&ndash;22 to formally censure McCarthy for &lsquo;conduct unbecoming&rsquo;. He remained a senator but his power was broken. He drank heavily and died of liver failure in May 1957 aged 48. His name became a byword for unsubstantiated political smear &mdash; &lsquo;McCarthyism&rsquo;. The Hollywood blacklist persisted into the 1960s, lifted gradually as Dalton Trumbo and others wrote under their own names again. Federal loyalty programmes were scaled back but not abolished.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The cost of the second Red Scare went well beyond the 320 entertainment-industry blacklisted figures. Universities sacked Marxist academics. Public libraries removed books. Trade unions purged left-wing officers. The State Department lost a generation of China experts who had warned that Chiang Kai-Shek would lose the Chinese civil war &mdash; their absence shaped American policy in Asia for years. Civil-rights and women&rsquo;s organisations were intimidated; the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) felt obliged to expel members it suspected of Communist links. Political dissent in general was narrowed. The phrase &lsquo;guilt by association&rsquo; entered everyday speech. Recovery from McCarthyism would take much of the 1960s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why the Army-McCarthy hearings finally broke McCarthy&rsquo;s power.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1954 televised Army-McCarthy hearings exposed McCarthy to around 20 million viewers as a bully and a liar. His approval fell from about 50% (January 1954) to 35% (June 1954). The Senate censured him 67&ndash;22 in December 1954. McCarthy died in 1957.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The second Red Scare was triggered by Cold War setbacks (Soviet bomb 1949, China 1949, Korea 1950) and the Hiss and Rosenberg cases, not just by Senator McCarthy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">HUAC&rsquo;s 1947 hearings produced the Hollywood Ten and a blacklist of around 320 entertainment-industry figures; Truman&rsquo;s 1947 federal loyalty programme investigated 4.7 million federal workers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">McCarthy&rsquo;s power peaked between 1950 and 1954 and was broken by the televised Army-McCarthy hearings; the Senate censured him in December 1954 and he died in 1957.</li>
</ul>
