--- DESCRIPTION ---
The stock-market climb, speculation on the margin and the Americans the Boom left behind.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Bull Market and a Divided Country</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">If Lesson 1 explained how the Boom worked for middle-class buyers, Lesson 2 looks at where their money went and who was left out. Across the 1920s the Dow Jones industrial average climbed from 100 points in 1924 to 381 points in September 1929. Newspapers ran daily share-price columns; shoeshine boys offered tips. But while one third of Americans bought their first refrigerator, another third lived in homes without electricity. The Boom was loud at the top of society and silent at the bottom.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: define 'buying on the margin' in one sentence and explain why it made the stock market unstable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1929 around three million Americans owned shares, and most paid only 10% of the price up front, borrowing the rest from their stockbroker. This was called <strong>buying on the margin</strong>. It worked while prices were rising &mdash; but if shares fell, the loan still had to be repaid in full.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Wall Street Boom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The financial district of New York City and the home of the New York Stock Exchange, used as shorthand for the American stock market.">Wall Street</dfn> stock market boomed because three things happened at once. Republican tax cuts (Lesson 1) left wealthy Americans with billions to invest. Mass production was generating record corporate profits, so company shares looked like a sure bet. And new buying rules let small investors join in. The result was a rising stock market in which prices ran far ahead of the real value of the companies underneath.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Brokers offered <dfn class="term" data-def="Buying shares by paying only a small deposit (often 10%) and borrowing the rest from a stockbroker, hoping to repay the loan from rising share prices.">buying on the margin</dfn> as a way for ordinary Americans to take part. A clerk who put down $100 could borrow $900 to buy $1,000 of shares. If the shares rose to $1,500, he sold, repaid the $900 loan, and walked away with a $500 profit on his original $100. Speculators &mdash; people who bought shares to make a quick profit rather than to invest in a business &mdash; piled into the market. By 1929 the volume of margin loans had reached over $9 billion.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Speculation: how the bubble inflated</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Buying shares hoping their price will rise quickly so they can be sold for a profit, rather than for long-term investment.">Speculation</dfn> drove prices upward in a self-feeding loop. People bought shares because they were rising; the buying made them rise more; rising prices attracted yet more buyers. Newspapers printed stories of secretaries and lift operators making fortunes. By 1929 the Radio Corporation of America&rsquo;s shares had risen 500% in two years, even though the company had paid no dividend. Investors were not buying a stake in a business; they were buying the chance to sell to someone else for more.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Most ordinary Americans never bought shares &mdash; only about 1.5 million of the country&rsquo;s 30 million households did. But banks invested customers&rsquo; deposits in the market, so when share prices wobbled, the banks themselves were exposed. The whole edifice rested on the assumption that prices would only ever climb.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Inequalities Beneath the Boom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The richest 5% of Americans took home one third of all national income in 1929. The poorest 40% received less than a sixth. The Boom benefited shareholders, business owners and skilled industrial workers. It largely bypassed the groups below.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Farmers and Sharecroppers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The First World War had been good for American farmers: European fighting wrecked French and German agriculture, so the USA exported huge surpluses of wheat and meat. After 1921 European production recovered and prices collapsed. Wheat prices fell from $2.50 a bushel in 1920 to under $1 by 1929. Six million American farmers were forced off their land across the decade. Mechanisation made matters worse &mdash; tractors meant fewer labourers were needed, and farm employment fell sharply.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Conditions were worst in the South. <dfn class="term" data-def="Tenant farmers, often African-American, who paid rent for land by giving the landlord a share (typically half) of the crop they grew.">Sharecroppers</dfn> &mdash; both white and black, but disproportionately African-American &mdash; rented land in exchange for half their crop. When cotton prices fell, sharecroppers could end the season owing the landlord more than they earned. Their incomes were a fraction of the national average and stayed there throughout the Boom.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">African-Americans, Native Americans and Immigrants</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In the southern states, <dfn class="term" data-def="State and local laws across the southern USA from the late nineteenth century that enforced racial segregation in schools, transport, housing and public spaces.">Jim Crow laws</dfn> kept African-Americans out of decent jobs, schools and voting booths. The Great Migration north to Chicago, Detroit and New York gave some families industrial wages, but they faced housing segregation and were the first to be sacked when work slowed. Native Americans, confined to reservations on poor land, lived in extreme poverty &mdash; the 1928 Meriam Report described conditions as a national disgrace. New immigrants from southern and eastern Europe were funnelled into the lowest-paid factory jobs and faced the immigration restrictions covered in Lesson 5.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Great Migration and the limits of northern jobs</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Between 1916 and 1929 around 1.3 million African-Americans left the rural South for industrial cities like Chicago, Detroit, New York and Cleveland. They escaped the worst of Jim Crow segregation and could earn factory wages well above sharecropping incomes. But the welcome was conditional. Estate agents enforced &lsquo;restrictive covenants&rsquo; that kept whole neighbourhoods white-only, so African-Americans were channelled into overcrowded districts like Harlem and Chicago&rsquo;s South Side. Trade unions in many industries refused to admit black members, leaving them in the lowest-paid roles. White workers in some cities rioted when black migrants arrived &mdash; Chicago in 1919, Tulsa (Oklahoma) in 1921, where a thriving African-American business district called Greenwood was burned to the ground. The Great Migration was an opportunity, but it was an opportunity inside a country that was still deeply hostile.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down four groups the Boom failed and one specific reason for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Boom left out farmers (collapsing crop prices), sharecroppers (debt to landlords), African-Americans (Jim Crow segregation), Native Americans (reservations) and unskilled industrial workers (whose wages rose far less than profits). For the unit&rsquo;s analytical question &mdash; opportunity for whom, inequality for whom? &mdash; this list is the answer.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why Two Halves of the Same Decade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Take the two halves of this lesson together. Wall Street was rising on borrowed money for a small minority. The countryside was sinking into debt for a much larger one. When the bubble burst in October 1929 (Lesson 6) it would not just hurt the speculators; it would hit a country whose foundations were already unequal. That is why historians remember the 1920s as a decade of opportunity AND inequality, not one or the other.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Wall Street boom climbed steeply because tax cuts, corporate profits and buying on the margin (10% deposit, 90% broker loan) drew small investors into the market.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Speculation pushed share prices far ahead of company values, while banks invested customer deposits in the same shares &mdash; concentrating risk in the financial system.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Farmers, sharecroppers, African-Americans, Native Americans and unskilled workers were largely excluded from the Boom &mdash; making the 1920s a decade of both opportunity and deep inequality.</li>
</ul>
