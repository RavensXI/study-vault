Who Homer was, oral epic poetry, and the Odyssey's place at the start of Greek literature.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Was Homer?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <em>Odyssey</em> is one of the oldest surviving works of Western literature, and tradition attributes it to a poet called <dfn class="term" data-def="The ancient Greek poet to whom the Iliad and the Odyssey are traditionally attributed; his identity and even his existence as a single historical person remain debated.">Homer</dfn>. Ancient writers generally believed Homer was a blind bard from Ionia &mdash; the western coast of what is now Turkey &mdash; who composed both the <em>Iliad</em> and the <em>Odyssey</em>. Modern scholars, however, debate whether a single poet called Homer ever existed, whether the two epics were composed by the same person, and whether the poems we have today were the work of one genius or the result of centuries of collaborative oral tradition. What matters for the exam is understanding the nature of the tradition that produced these poems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The ancient Greeks themselves held Homer in extraordinary esteem. They called him simply &ldquo;the Poet,&rdquo; as though no other poet needed naming. His works were the foundation of Greek education: schoolchildren learnt passages by heart, rhapsodes (professional reciters) performed them at festivals, and philosophers quoted them to settle arguments about ethics and the gods.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the Greek term for a professional performer of oral epic, and what were the two main conventions of oral-formulaic composition?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <em>Odyssey</em> was composed and performed in an <dfn class="term" data-def="A tradition in which poetry is composed and transmitted through spoken performance rather than writing, relying on stock phrases, standard scenes and the performer&rsquo;s memory.">oral tradition</dfn>. The performer &mdash; a bard or <dfn class="term" data-def="A Greek professional singer who performed epic poetry from memory at festivals and public gatherings.">rhapsode</dfn> &mdash; used a stock repertoire of formulae, repeated phrases and set-piece scenes to compose the poem in performance, rather than reading from a written text.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">What Makes the Odyssey an Epic?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A long narrative poem on a grand scale, dealing with heroes, gods and momentous events, composed in formal verse and following traditional conventions.">epic poem</dfn> is a long narrative poem that deals with heroes and their deeds on a grand scale. The <em>Odyssey</em> has several defining features that mark it out as epic. It is composed in dactylic hexameter, the traditional metre of Greek epic &mdash; a rolling, stately rhythm that feels weightier than ordinary speech. It opens with an <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal appeal to a divine being at the start of a poem, asking for inspiration and assistance in telling the story.">invocation of the Muse</dfn>, asking the goddess of poetry to inspire the telling: the poem begins by asking the Muse to tell of &ldquo;the man of many ways&rdquo; &mdash; Odysseus. This signals immediately that the story is not merely entertainment but a divinely inspired account of great deeds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Another epic convention is beginning <dfn class="term" data-def="Latin for &lsquo;in the middle of things&rsquo;; a narrative technique where the story begins at a late point in the action, with earlier events revealed through flashback.">in medias res</dfn> &mdash; that is, starting the story in the middle of the action rather than at the beginning. When the <em>Odyssey</em> opens, Odysseus has already been wandering for ten years. We learn the earlier stages of his journey through his own narration to the Phaeacians in Books 9 and 10. This technique creates dramatic suspense and allows Homer to concentrate on the most significant episodes.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Three key <dfn class="term" data-def="The craft of composing and delivering poetry in the oral tradition, using standard phrases, scenes and structural patterns to aid composition and memory.">oral-formulaic technique</dfn>s appear throughout the <em>Odyssey</em>. First, <dfn class="term" data-def="A repeated descriptive phrase attached to a character or object in epic poetry, which fills a certain metrical slot in the line and acts as a shorthand identity tag.">epithets</dfn> are fixed labels attached to characters: Odysseus is repeatedly called &ldquo;resourceful Odysseus,&rdquo; &ldquo;godlike Odysseus,&rdquo; or &ldquo;the great tactician.&rdquo; The rosy-fingered Dawn appears at the start of many mornings. These tags are not decorative accidents &mdash; they serve a practical function, allowing the performer to fill a particular part of the line while also reinforcing a character&rsquo;s defining quality.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Second, formulae are repeated phrases used to describe recurring actions &mdash; drinking, eating, praying, greeting a guest. When Odysseus arrives at a new palace, Homer uses a set sequence of reception scenes that mirrors the rules of <dfn class="term" data-def="The Greek code of hospitality, which required hosts to welcome strangers with food and shelter before asking their name or business, and guests to behave respectfully in return.">xenia</dfn>. Third, <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended comparison in epic poetry that develops over several lines, comparing something in the main narrative to something from everyday life to help the audience visualise and understand it.">Homeric similes</dfn> &mdash; also called extended similes &mdash; pause the action to compare events to scenes from everyday life. When Odysseus finally kills the leading suitor, Homer compares the fallen man to an acrobat tumbling off a stool. These similes are vivid, surprising and ground the heroic world in the real world of the audience.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Structure of the Odyssey</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The <em>Odyssey</em> is divided into 24 books. The first four books follow Odysseus&rsquo;s son Telemachus as he searches for news of his father &mdash; a section often called the Telemachy. Books 5&ndash;8 introduce Odysseus, stranded on the island of Calypso and eventually reaching the Phaeacians. Books 9&ndash;12 are Odysseus&rsquo;s own first-person account of his adventures &mdash; a story within the story. Books 13&ndash;24 describe his return to Ithaca, his disguise as a beggar, the recognition scenes and the final bloody revenge on the suitors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">This structure is highly crafted. By placing Books 9&ndash;12 as a first-person flashback told by Odysseus to the Phaeacians, Homer allows the hero to be his own storyteller &mdash; which itself reveals something important about his character. Odysseus is a man of words as much as of deeds, and his ability to tell a compelling story is part of what makes him a great hero.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down three features that mark the Odyssey as an epic poem &mdash; include the technical term for each feature.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <em>Odyssey</em> consists of 24 books. The prescribed books for study are Books 9, 10, 19, 21 and 22. Book 9 contains the Cyclops episode; Book 10 the encounter with Circe; Book 19 the recognition of Odysseus by his nurse Eurycleia; Book 21 the contest of the bow; and Book 22 the slaughter of the suitors.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A common misconception is that oral poetry must be imprecise or low quality. In fact, the scholarship of Milman Parry and Albert Lord in the 20th century showed that oral poets in the Balkans could compose thousands of lines of technically accomplished verse in performance. The repetitions, formulae and epithets in Homer are not signs of laziness &mdash; they are the tools of a sophisticated oral craft. The question of when the poems were written down is separate from the question of when they were first composed: the standard scholarly view is that the poems reached roughly their current form around the 8th century BC, and were written down during the 6th or 7th century BC.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">It is also a misconception to think of Homer&rsquo;s poems as simple myths invented for entertainment. The Greeks regarded them as a record of a real heroic age, a time when great men interacted directly with the gods. Modern archaeology has confirmed that a wealthy Bronze Age civilisation &mdash; the Mycenaean world &mdash; did exist, though the poems blend historical memory with legend and imagination in ways we cannot fully separate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Themes and the Odyssey&rsquo;s Appeal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The <em>Odyssey</em> is built around a series of powerful, interlocking themes. <dfn class="term" data-def="The Greek concept of the longing or drive to return home, a central motivation for Odysseus throughout the poem.">Nostos</dfn> &mdash; the desire to return home &mdash; drives every choice Odysseus makes. The poem asks: what is it that makes a place &ldquo;home&rdquo;? Is it a physical location, a wife and son, a kingdom, or an identity? Every obstacle Odysseus faces tests whether his longing for Ithaca is stronger than the temptations placed in his way.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The tension between civilised and barbaric behaviour runs through the poem. Every new land Odysseus visits is measured against Greek standards of civilised life: do they practise xenia? Do they have proper laws and agriculture? The Cyclops Polyphemus fails every test. Circe begins as a monster but comes to embody a kind of civilised hospitality. These contrasts are not just entertaining &mdash; they define the civilised world that Odysseus is fighting to return to.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The <em>Odyssey</em> is an oral epic attributed to Homer &mdash; composed in the oral tradition using epithets, formulae and extended similes, and structured to begin in medias res.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Epic conventions include the invocation of the Muse, dactylic hexameter, heroic subject matter and formal narrative scale; the five prescribed books (9, 10, 19, 21, 22) cover the adventures, the recognition and the revenge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Central themes are nostos (the drive to return home), xenia (hospitality), and the contrast between civilised and barbaric behaviour &mdash; all of which are tested through Odysseus&rsquo;s encounters on his journey.</li>
</ul>
