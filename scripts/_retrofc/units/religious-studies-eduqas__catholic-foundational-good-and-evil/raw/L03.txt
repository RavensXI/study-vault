<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sculpture and Statuary in Catholic Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Three-dimensional religious art, including carved or cast figures of Christ, Mary and the saints, used in Catholic devotion and worship.">Statuary</dfn> has been central to Catholic Christianity for centuries. Unlike some Protestant traditions that removed religious images during the Reformation, the Catholic Church affirmed in the Council of Trent (1545&ndash;1563) that sacred images &ldquo;should be placed and retained&rdquo; in churches, because they help believers direct their minds and hearts towards God. A statue or sculpture does not represent an idol &mdash; Catholics are clear that they venerate the person depicted, not the object itself. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (paragraph 2132) draws a sharp distinction between <dfn class="term" data-def="The honour given to God alone in worship.">latria</dfn> (worship owed to God alone) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The honour given to the saints and to Mary, a level below the worship due to God.">dulia</dfn> (honour given to saints), with a higher form &mdash; hyperdulia &mdash; reserved for Mary.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what distinction does the Catholic Church make between the veneration of statues and the worship of idols?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Catholic Church teaches that statues and sculptures serve as visual aids for prayer and meditation, helping believers focus on the holiness of Christ, Mary and the saints. The object is not worshipped &mdash; the figure represented is honoured. This is the Catholic response to accusations of idolatry.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Michelangelo&rsquo;s Piet&agrave;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Among the most celebrated examples of Catholic sacred sculpture is Michelangelo&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="A subject in Christian art depicting the Virgin Mary cradling the body of Jesus after the Crucifixion; also the name of Michelangelo's marble sculpture completed around 1499.">Piet&agrave;</dfn>, housed in St Peter&rsquo;s Basilica in Rome. Carved from a single block of white Carrara marble, the work shows the Virgin Mary holding the body of the crucified Christ across her lap. Michelangelo completed the sculpture around 1499, and it is generally considered one of the finest works of Renaissance art.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For Catholics, the Piet&agrave; carries profound theological meaning. It makes visible the central mystery of the faith: the Incarnation of the Son of God, who truly suffered and died in a human body. Mary&rsquo;s expression is one of serene grief &mdash; sorrowful, yet composed &mdash; which Catholic commentators read as a model of how a believer should receive suffering: with trust in God&rsquo;s providence rather than despair. The sculpture also mediates on the relationship between the Incarnation and redemption: Christ took on human flesh (the Incarnation) so that humanity could share in his resurrection.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The Piet&agrave; connects three key Catholic doctrines. First, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Christian doctrine that the Son of God took on human flesh, becoming fully human while remaining fully divine.">Incarnation</dfn>: the figure of Christ is clearly human, with real physical weight and vulnerability. Second, the Passion &mdash; Christ&rsquo;s suffering and death as the means of human salvation. Third, the role of Mary: Catholic theology presents Mary not merely as a biological mother but as a co-sufferer (the &ldquo;Sorrowful Mother&rdquo;) who participates in the redemptive mystery. The Catechism describes Mary as the model of the Church (CCC 967), whose perfect faith and willing surrender to God&rsquo;s plan make her the first and most complete disciple.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">In parish churches across Britain, smaller reproductions of the Piet&agrave; are commonly found in Lady chapels or near stations of the Cross. Parishioners may pause to pray before such images, especially during Lent, Good Friday and November (the month of the dead in the Catholic tradition). The statue thus functions as a focus for contemplative prayer at moments of personal suffering or bereavement.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Lourdes and Marian Pilgrimage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">One of the most important expressions of popular Catholic devotion is <dfn class="term" data-def="A pilgrimage town in southern France where, according to Catholic tradition, the Virgin Mary appeared to Bernadette Soubirous in 1858; now a major international Catholic pilgrimage site.">Lourdes</dfn>. According to Catholic tradition, in 1858 a young woman named Bernadette Soubirous reported a series of visions of a &ldquo;beautiful lady&rdquo; at the grotto of Massabielle in southern France. The Church eventually recognised these as authentic apparitions of the Virgin Mary, and Bernadette was canonised in 1933. A spring of water emerged from the site, and numerous accounts of physical healings have since been reported and investigated by the Church&rsquo;s Medical Bureau at Lourdes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For Catholics, Lourdes represents a meeting-point between the physical and the spiritual. Pilgrims bathe in or drink the spring water as an act of faith, attend candlelit processions in the evenings, celebrate Mass at the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary, and pray for healing &mdash; physical or spiritual. Britain sends one of the largest national pilgrimages to Lourdes each year, organised by the Catholic Bishops&rsquo; Conference of England and Wales. Many diocesan groups, schools and hospital chaplaincies include Lourdes pilgrimages as a significant part of their spiritual programme.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words why Lourdes matters to Catholics as a response to suffering. Include at least two reasons.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Lourdes is a major Marian pilgrimage site in France where the Virgin Mary is said to have appeared in 1858. Catholics visit to pray for healing, participate in communal worship, and draw strength from the shared experience of faith in the face of illness and suffering. British Catholic organisations, including HCPT &mdash; The Pilgrimage Trust, run annual pilgrimages to Lourdes for sick and disabled children and adults.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Devotional practices that arise from the faith of ordinary Catholic believers, such as pilgrimages, novenas, processions and the Rosary, distinct from but connected to the official liturgy.">Popular piety</dfn> refers to the devotional life of ordinary Catholic communities, which complements and grows out of the official liturgy (the Mass and sacraments). The Catholic Church has consistently encouraged authentic popular piety while cautioning against superstition. Pope Francis&rsquo;s apostolic exhortation <em>Evangelii Gaudium</em> (2013) acknowledged that popular expressions of faith &ldquo;manifest a thirst for God which only the poor and the simple can know.&rdquo; Such practices include candlelit Marian processions, novenas (nine days of prayer to a saint), wearing a miraculous medal, and making the Stations of the Cross.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">In British Catholic communities, popular piety is expressed through May devotions to Mary, parish pilgrimages to sites such as Holywell in north Wales (a historic Catholic shrine associated with St Winefride, one of the oldest continuously used pilgrimage sites in Britain), and the annual Assumption celebrations on 15 August. These practices root believers&rsquo; faith in embodied, communal experience rather than abstract doctrine alone.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Rosary and the Sorrowful Mysteries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Catholic form of prayer using beads to count a series of prayers, centred on meditation on the key events (Mysteries) of the lives of Jesus and Mary.">Rosary</dfn> is one of the most widely practised forms of Catholic devotion. It consists of a series of Hail Marys, Our Fathers and other prayers, said while meditating on a set of &ldquo;Mysteries&rdquo; &mdash; significant events in the lives of Jesus and Mary. The Mysteries are grouped into four sets: Joyful, Luminous, Sorrowful and Glorious. Pope John Paul II added the Luminous Mysteries in his apostolic letter <em>Rosarium Virginis Mariae</em> (2002).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The five Mysteries of the Rosary that meditate on the Passion of Jesus: the Agony in the Garden, the Scourging at the Pillar, the Crowning with Thorns, the Carrying of the Cross, and the Crucifixion.">Sorrowful Mysteries</dfn> are particularly relevant to the theme of suffering. They are: the Agony in the Garden (Gethsemane), the Scourging at the Pillar, the Crowning with Thorns, the Carrying of the Cross, and the Crucifixion and Death of Jesus. By meditating on Christ&rsquo;s own suffering during these decades of the Rosary, Catholics are encouraged to see their own pain in relation to Christ&rsquo;s &mdash; not as meaningless, but as a participation in his redeeming work.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Catholic tradition teaches that the Rosary is not merely repetitive recitation but an act of sustained contemplation. The verbal prayers create a background rhythm that frees the mind to dwell on the visual and theological content of each Mystery. Pope Paul VI&rsquo;s apostolic exhortation <em>Marialis Cultus</em> (1974) described the Rosary as a&nbsp;&ldquo;Gospel prayer&rdquo; because the Mysteries draw directly from the Gospel narratives of Jesus&rsquo; life, death and resurrection.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The Rosary as a Reflection on the Incarnation and Trinity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The Rosary links Marian devotion to the central doctrines of the Trinity and the Incarnation. When Catholics recite the Hail Mary (&ldquo;Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee&rdquo;), they are addressing Mary as the Theotokos &mdash; the God-bearer &mdash; who gave human flesh to the eternal Son of God. The prayer&rsquo;s opening echoes the angelic greeting in Luke 1:28. The Joyful Mysteries begin with the Annunciation, the moment when the Incarnation became possible through Mary&rsquo;s &ldquo;fiat&rdquo; (her &ldquo;yes&rdquo; to God). The Sorrowful Mysteries then show the cost of that Incarnation: a real human death. The Glorious Mysteries show its vindication: resurrection and the gift of the Holy Spirit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">In this way, the Rosary as a whole functions as a meditation on the entire Christian story, structured around the Trinity &mdash; the plan of the Father, carried out by the Son, celebrated in the Spirit. Catholics who pray the Rosary regularly describe it as a form of scripture contemplation that does not require literacy or theological training: it is, in the Church&rsquo;s own self-understanding, a prayer suited to the whole people of God.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Statuary such as Michelangelo&rsquo;s Piet&agrave; is used in Catholic worship as a visual aid for prayer and contemplation, not as an idol. The Piet&agrave; expresses the doctrines of the Incarnation, the Passion and Mary&rsquo;s role as model of the Church.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Lourdes is a Marian pilgrimage site in France, visited by millions of Catholics annually seeking physical or spiritual healing. British dioceses send large pilgrimage groups each year, and the site is central to popular Catholic piety.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The Rosary is a meditative prayer using beads, structured around Mysteries from the lives of Jesus and Mary. The Sorrowful Mysteries focus on the Passion of Christ, helping Catholics to understand and accept suffering in relation to Christ&rsquo;s own redemptive suffering.</li>
</ul>
