<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Catholics Make Moral Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Catholic moral teaching draws on three main approaches to ethics. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An ethical approach holding that moral rules apply absolutely in all situations, regardless of consequences, because they come from God's commands or natural law.">absolutist</dfn> (or divine command) approach holds that certain actions are always right or wrong, regardless of circumstances. A relativist approach accepts that context matters when applying moral principles. A third framework, especially important in Catholic tradition, is <dfn class="term" data-def="An ethical approach, developed by Aristotle and adopted by Aquinas, which focuses on cultivating good character traits (virtues) that lead a person to act well habitually.">virtue ethics</dfn>. Virtue ethics asks not &ldquo;what should I do?&rdquo; but &ldquo;what kind of person should I become?&rdquo; A virtuous person develops habits of acting rightly &mdash; courage, justice, prudence, temperance (the cardinal virtues), and faith, hope and love (the theological virtues) &mdash; until good action becomes second nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Aquinas built on Aristotle&rsquo;s virtue ethics by grounding it in Catholic theology. He argued that the virtues orient a person towards their true end: union with God. This synthesis means that for Catholics, growing in virtue is itself a form of spiritual development, not just moral self-improvement.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Teachings of Jesus on Morality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Jesus&rsquo; ethical teaching shapes Catholic moral life directly. The parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25&ndash;37) teaches that <dfn class="term" data-def="Unconditional, selfless love — the highest form of Christian love, understood as God's own love extended through and between humans.">agap&eacute;</dfn> must extend to everyone, regardless of background. Jesus gave his disciples a new commandment to love one another as he himself had loved them (John 13:34&ndash;35), making self-giving love the hallmark of the Christian community. Forgiveness is central: Jesus instructed his followers to forgive without limit (Matthew 18:21&ndash;22) and taught in the Lord&rsquo;s Prayer that forgiving others and receiving God&rsquo;s forgiveness are inseparably linked (Matthew 6:5&ndash;13). Concern for the poor is also a moral imperative: Matthew 6:19&ndash;21 warns against storing up earthly wealth at the expense of heavenly riches, and Luke 23:34 records Jesus praying for forgiveness for those who crucified him &mdash; the ultimate model of unconditional mercy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three ethical frameworks in Catholic moral teaching, giving one sentence about each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Catholic morality draws on three frameworks: absolutist (certain actions are always right or wrong), relativist (context matters), and virtue ethics (cultivating good character, developed by Aristotle and adopted by Aquinas). Love, forgiveness and concern for the poor &mdash; rooted in Jesus&rsquo; teachings &mdash; run through all three approaches.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Sacrament of Reconciliation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Also called Confession or Penance — the sacrament through which Catholics receive God's forgiveness for sins committed after baptism, through confession to a priest and absolution.">Sacrament of Reconciliation</dfn> (also known as Confession or Penance) is how Catholics receive forgiveness for serious sins committed after baptism. The person examines their conscience, confesses their sins to a priest, expresses genuine sorrow (<em>contrition</em>), receives a penance (a prayer or action to make amends) and is granted absolution &mdash; the priest, acting in the name of Christ, declares God&rsquo;s forgiveness. Catholics believe Jesus gave this authority to forgive sins to the Apostles: in John 20:22&ndash;23, he breathed the Holy Spirit on them and said that those whose sins they forgave were forgiven.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Reconciliation is understood not merely as a legal clearing of guilt but as a genuine healing of the relationship with God. It connects directly to Jesus&rsquo; teaching on forgiveness without limit (Matthew 18:21&ndash;22) and to the virtue ethics framework: regular confession is seen as a practice that supports growth in humility and honesty &mdash; two virtues essential for moral development.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The central act of Catholic worship, in which the sacrifice of Jesus is made present through the consecration of bread and wine into his body and blood.">Mass</dfn> is the central act of Catholic worship. Every Mass has the same two-part structure: the Liturgy of the Word (scripture readings, a psalm, a Gospel reading and a homily) and the Liturgy of the Eucharist (the offertory, consecration and communion). The <dfn class="term" data-def="The sacrament in which bread and wine are consecrated and become the body and blood of Christ; also called Holy Communion.">Eucharist</dfn> is at the heart of the Mass. Catholics believe in <em>transubstantiation</em>: while the outward appearance of bread and wine remains the same, the substance is transformed into the actual body and blood of Christ during the words of consecration. This belief is rooted in Paul&rsquo;s account of the last supper: Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it and said it was his body given for them; he did the same with the cup, calling it the new covenant in his blood (1 Corinthians 11:23&ndash;26). Paul adds that whenever Christians share this meal they proclaim the Lord&rsquo;s death until he comes again.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The Catholic diocese is the regional unit led by a bishop; within each diocese are many <dfn class="term" data-def="The local Catholic community gathered around a church, led by a parish priest.">parish</dfn> communities, each led by a parish priest. The parish church is where Catholics gather weekly for Mass, receive the sacraments, and form community. The liturgical calendar shapes parish life, moving through Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter and Ordinary Time each year.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">The practice of offering Masses for the dead is rooted in Catholic belief in purgatory &mdash; a state of purification after death in which souls are prepared for heaven. Catholics believe that the prayers of the living, and especially the offering of the Mass, can benefit those who have died. Requiem Masses (Masses offered specifically for the dead) are celebrated at funerals and on anniversaries, expressing belief in the communion of saints and the continuing bond between the living and the dead.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">&ldquo;Living the Mass&rdquo; describes the Catholic conviction that the Mass is not only something done inside a church building but a way of life. Pope Francis, in his apostolic exhortation <em>Evangelii Gaudium</em> (2013), called Catholics to go beyond the walls of the parish and carry the joy of the Gospel into the world &mdash; especially to the poor and marginalised. Catholic social teaching, grounded in the vision of human dignity (imago Dei), calls this the social dimension of the Mass: what is received at the altar must be lived in daily life, in justice and solidarity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Catholic Social Justice: CARJ, CAFOD and Gaudium et Spes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Catholic Church in Britain is actively engaged in social justice work. <strong>CAFOD</strong> (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development) is the official Catholic overseas aid agency for England and Wales, working to relieve poverty and promote development globally. <strong>CARJ</strong> (the Catholic Association for Racial Justice) works to promote racial equality within the Church and wider society. The <strong>St Vincent de Paul Society</strong> is a worldwide Catholic charity whose members visit the sick, homeless and marginalised in their local communities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">These organisations draw their mandate from <em>Gaudium et Spes</em> (Joy and Hope), the Second Vatican Council&rsquo;s pastoral constitution on the Church in the modern world. Paragraph 78 of <em>Gaudium et Spes</em> calls on Catholics to build peace through justice, and paragraph 29 affirms the fundamental equality of all human beings. Together, these teachings ground the Catholic commitment to social justice as an integral part of faith, not an optional extra.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: what CAFOD and CARJ do, and which document grounds Catholic social justice in paragraph 78 and 29.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>CAFOD is the Catholic overseas aid agency for England and Wales; CARJ promotes racial justice within the Church; the St Vincent de Paul Society serves the poor locally. <em>Gaudium et Spes</em> 78 (peace through justice) and 29 (fundamental human equality) provide the conciliar foundation for Catholic social action.</p>
</div>
