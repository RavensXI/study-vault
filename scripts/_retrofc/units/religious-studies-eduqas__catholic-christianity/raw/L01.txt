<h2 data-narration-id="n1">God as Creator</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Catholics believe that God created the universe from nothing &mdash; a doctrine expressed in Latin as <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that God created the universe out of nothing, with no pre-existing material. From the Latin meaning 'from nothing'.">creator ex nihilo</dfn>. This teaching distinguishes the Christian God from any idea of a craftsman who simply shapes existing material: God is the absolute origin of all that exists. The theologian Augustine of Hippo (354&ndash;430) reflected on this in his <em>Confessions</em> (Book XII, 7), arguing that when God created, there was no pre-existing formless matter &mdash; even unformed matter was itself God&rsquo;s creation. For Catholics, this underlines God&rsquo;s complete sovereignty: nothing exists independently of his will and creative act.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Catholic theology also affirms that God is not only Creator but sustainer &mdash; the universe depends on God&rsquo;s continued will for its ongoing existence. God&rsquo;s nature as Creator is described through four key attributes: <dfn class="term" data-def="All-powerful; able to do anything consistent with God's own nature.">omnipotence</dfn> (all-power), omnibenevolence (all-goodness), omniscience (all-knowledge) and omnipresence (present everywhere). These are understood as qualities that flow necessarily from being the source of all existence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words what 'creator ex nihilo' means, and where Augustine discusses this idea.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Creator ex nihilo means God created everything from nothing &mdash; there was no pre-existing matter. Augustine of Hippo explored this in his <em>Confessions</em> (Book XII, 7). For Catholics, this establishes God&rsquo;s absolute sovereignty over all creation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Trinity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Catholics believe in one God who exists as three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit &mdash; a mystery called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Christian doctrine that God is one being in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, co-equal and co-eternal.">Trinity</dfn>. This is not three separate gods but one God in three distinct modes of relation. The Father is understood through the parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:11&ndash;32), which reveals God as a loving, forgiving parent who runs to welcome back a wayward child. The Son is described in John&rsquo;s Gospel as the eternal Word who was with God from the beginning and through whom all things were made (John 1:1&ndash;3, 14). The Holy Spirit appears in John 14:25&ndash;26, where Jesus promises the Advocate who will teach and remind believers of all he said, and in Galatians 5:22&ndash;23, which lists the fruits of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Augustine&rsquo;s <em>De Trinitate</em> (On the Trinity), Book 8 section 10, reflects on how the Trinity can be understood through the model of love: lover, beloved, and the love between them. While all analogies for the Trinity fall short, this helps Catholics grasp the relational nature of God. The Council of Nicaea (325 CE) defined the Father and Son as &lsquo;of one substance&rsquo; (homoousios). The Council of Constantinople (381 CE) gave the Trinity its definitive doctrinal formulation by affirming the Holy Spirit&rsquo;s full divinity, completing what is now called the Nicene Creed.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Catholics believe Jesus is the Messiah &mdash; the anointed one sent by God to save humanity. Peter&rsquo;s declaration in Matthew 16:13&ndash;17 that Jesus is &ldquo;the Messiah, the Son of the living God&rdquo; is a foundational moment. Jesus is also called the Word of God: John 1:1&ndash;3 says the Word was with God and was God from before creation; John 1:14 says the Word became flesh and lived among people &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The doctrine that the eternal Son of God took on full human nature and was born as Jesus of Nazareth.">Incarnation</dfn>. Through this event, God entered human experience completely.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Salvation and atonement are central to Catholic understanding of Jesus. John 3:16 teaches that God gave his only Son so that everyone who believes in him can have eternal life. John 14:6 records Jesus describing himself as the way, the truth and the life, and the only path to the Father. The crucifixion, resurrection and ascension together form the Paschal Mystery &mdash; the heart of Catholic theology and liturgy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Human Nature: Imago Dei and the Fall</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Genesis 1:26&ndash;28 records God saying: &ldquo;Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness.&rdquo; The Latin phrase for this is <dfn class="term" data-def="Latin for 'image of God' — the Christian teaching that every human being reflects something of the divine nature and therefore possesses inherent dignity.">imago Dei</dfn>. For Catholics, this means that every human person carries a spark of divine likeness &mdash; the capacity for reason, moral choice, creativity and relationship with God. This is the basis for the Catholic teaching on the inviolable dignity of every human being from conception to natural death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Genesis 2:15&ndash;17 describes God placing the first humans in the garden and warning them not to eat from one particular tree, warning that death will follow. The Genesis 3 account of disobedience &mdash; the fall of Adam and Eve &mdash; introduces the doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="The inherited condition of human nature damaged by the first act of disobedience, leaving all humans inclined towards sin and in need of redemption.">original sin</dfn>. Catholics believe that this first act of disobedience damaged human nature for all their descendants: not that each person is personally guilty of Adam&rsquo;s sin, but that all inherit a nature weakened by sin, inclined towards self-centredness and away from God. Baptism is understood to cleanse original sin.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write the key phrases: what 'imago Dei' means, which Genesis passage it comes from, and what original sin teaches.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Imago Dei (Genesis 1:26&ndash;28) &mdash; humans are made in the image of God, giving every person inherent dignity. Original sin &mdash; from Genesis 2&ndash;3 &mdash; teaches that human nature is damaged by the first disobedience, leaving all people inclined towards sin and in need of redemption through Christ.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274): a Dominican friar and theologian whose writings, especially the Summa Theologiae, remain foundational to Catholic theology.">Thomas Aquinas</dfn> (1225&ndash;1274) offered a nuanced account of human nature that the Catholic tradition has adopted. Drawing on Aristotle and Christian theology, Aquinas argued that human nature is essentially good &mdash; created by God with a natural orientation towards the good and towards God. Sin damages but does not destroy this orientation. Human beings possess both a rational soul (capable of knowing truth) and free will (the capacity to choose). Aquinas emphasised that reason and faith are complementary, not contradictory: natural reason can know something of God through creation, while divine revelation in scripture and the Church provides what reason alone cannot reach.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">This synthesis is important for Catholic ethics: because human nature is essentially rational and good, moral reasoning (natural law) is a genuine path to knowing God&rsquo;s will. Catholics thus engage confidently with philosophical and ethical debates, convinced that reason properly used leads towards truth.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">God is creator ex nihilo (from nothing) &mdash; no pre-existing matter (Augustine, <em>Confessions</em> XII, 7). God is one being in three persons &mdash; the Trinity (John 1:1&ndash;3, Luke 15, Galatians 5:22&ndash;23; Augustine, <em>De Trinitate</em> 8.10).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Humans are made in the image of God (imago Dei, Genesis 1:26&ndash;28) &mdash; giving each person inherent dignity. But original sin (Genesis 2&ndash;3) damages human nature, making all people in need of salvation through Christ.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Aquinas taught that human nature is essentially good, with reason capable of knowing moral truth. This underpins Catholic engagement with ethics through natural law and rational argument.</li>
</ul>
