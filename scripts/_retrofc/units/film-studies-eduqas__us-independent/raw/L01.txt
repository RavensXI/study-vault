<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Defining Independence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">American independent cinema is not simply a stylistic label &mdash; it is first and foremost an <em>industrial</em> category. A film is &ldquo;independent&rdquo; when it is produced without the financial control of a major Hollywood studio. That definition sounds simple, but the reality is more complicated: some of the most commercially successful &ldquo;indie&rdquo; films of the past thirty years were made by <dfn class="term" data-def="A distribution arm set up by a major Hollywood studio to handle smaller, more distinctive films aimed at niche audiences.">specialty divisions</dfn> of major conglomerates &mdash; Fox Searchlight (a division of 20th Century Fox), Focus Features (Universal), and Sony Pictures Classics (Sony). These films occupy an ambiguous middle ground: their production was independent in spirit but their distribution was backed by studio money.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Students sometimes treat &ldquo;indie&rdquo; as shorthand for a particular aesthetic &mdash; handheld cameras, mumbled dialogue, low colour saturation. Whilst these features do appear in some independent work, they are consequences of budget and intention, not a definition. The real test is this: who financed the film, and who controlled the creative decisions? If the answer is a small production company answering only to the director and a limited number of investors, the film is independent. If the answer is a studio with final cut approval, it is not &mdash; regardless of how &ldquo;indie&rdquo; it looks on screen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the difference between a true independent production and a specialty-division film. Name one example of each type.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An independent film is produced outside the financial control of a major Hollywood studio. Specialty divisions (Fox Searchlight, Focus Features, Sony Pictures Classics) are studio-owned arms that distribute indie-style films &mdash; they blur the line between independent and mainstream.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">A Short History: From Cassavetes to A24</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The roots of American independent cinema stretch back to the 1950s and 1960s. <dfn class="term" data-def="An influential American director (1929&ndash;1989) widely regarded as the father of US independent cinema, known for naturalistic performance and improvisational technique.">John Cassavetes</dfn> is often cited as the founding figure: films such as <em>Shadows</em> (1959), shot on 16mm for a tiny budget, proved that narrative features could be made entirely outside the studio system. Cassavetes financed his own work, cast non-professional actors alongside trained ones, and distributed prints himself to independent cinemas. His method established the template &mdash; personal filmmaking driven by artistic vision rather than commercial calculation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An independent film festival held annually in Park City, Utah, the leading showcase for American independent cinema since the mid-1980s.">Sundance Film Festival</dfn>, founded in 1978 and revitalised under Robert Redford in the mid-1980s, became the defining launchpad for the independent wave of the late twentieth century. Directors including Steven Soderbergh, Kevin Smith and Quentin Tarantino broke through via Sundance, attracting studio attention and eventually signing distribution deals. The festival model demonstrated that audiences would seek out challenging, personal films if given access to them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The 1990s saw <dfn class="term" data-def="A production and distribution company co-founded by Harvey and Bob Weinstein in 1979, instrumental in distributing US and international independent films during the 1990s and 2000s.">Miramax</dfn> become the dominant force in independent distribution. Its slate &mdash; including <em>Pulp Fiction</em> (1994), <em>Good Will Hunting</em> (1997) and countless others &mdash; proved that independent films could reach mainstream audiences and compete at the Academy Awards. Miramax&rsquo;s commercial ambition stretched the meaning of &ldquo;independent&rdquo; considerably; the company was acquired by Disney in 1993, making it technically a studio subsidiary. This moment crystallised the tension that runs through any discussion of American indie film: commercial success and genuine independence are difficult to sustain simultaneously.</p>

Its slate &mdash; <em>Moonlight</em> (2016 Best Picture winner), <em>Lady Bird</em> (2017), <em>The Florida Project</em> (2017), <em>Hereditary</em> (2018), <em>Everything Everywhere All at Once</em> (2022) &mdash; signals a consistent commitment to director-driven projects with distinctive visual styles and unconventional narrative structures. A24 is notable for genuinely remaining independent of major-studio ownership, funding its own acquisitions and productions without a studio parent.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">A24&rsquo;s marketing strategy is also instructive: the company built a strong identity with younger audiences through social media, merchandise and carefully cultivated word-of-mouth. This demonstrated that the distribution model for independent film had shifted. In the 1990s, a Miramax film found its audience through art-house cinemas. In the 2020s, an A24 film finds its audience online, often before it opens in theatres. The industrial conditions of independence change; the principle of artistic autonomy remains the constant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach to filmmaking in which the director is understood as the primary creative author of the film, expressing a personal artistic vision.">auteur</dfn> Sensibility and Budget Constraints</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Independent cinema tends to attract directors with strong personal visions precisely because the studio system, with its demand for broad commercial appeal, often cannot accommodate unusual artistic choices. The low-budget environment paradoxically enables creative risk: without the obligation to recoup a $200 million investment, an indie filmmaker can experiment with non-linear narrative structure, unconventional casting, or endings that resist resolution. This is the industrial explanation for why so many formally daring American films emerge from the independent sector.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Budget constraints also shape aesthetic choices in practical ways. <dfn class="term" data-def="A style of low-budget filmmaking emphasising naturalistic performance, improvised or semi-improvised dialogue, and a deliberately rough visual texture.">Mumblecore</dfn> &mdash; the micro-budget movement centred on directors including Andrew Bujalski and the Duplass brothers in the 2000s &mdash; developed its overlapping, naturalistic dialogue partly because recording clean sound in controlled studio conditions was beyond its means. What began as a practical workaround became an aesthetic signature. Many of the set films in this unit carry traces of that legacy: a preference for long, static dialogue scenes; a willingness to let characters ramble; an attention to domestic space that big-budget productions rarely afford.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down three key names and dates from the history of US independent cinema. Then explain in one sentence why Miramax complicates the simple definition of 'independence'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key figures and organisations: John Cassavetes (1950s&ndash;60s, founding independent), Sundance Film Festival (founded 1978, relaunched mid-1980s), Miramax (1979&ndash;present, acquired by Disney 1993), A24 (founded 2012, remains genuinely independent).</p>
</div>

In extended writing, this leads to answers that describe handheld cameras and muted palettes as if they <em>define</em> indie film, rather than treating them as possible consequences of low budgets and personal creative choices.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The distinction matters in the exam because the extended-writing question on the US independent film rewards AO1 knowledge of production context &mdash; who financed the film, how it was distributed, what industrial conditions shaped its making. A student who knows that <em>Juno</em> (2007) was distributed by Fox Searchlight (a specialty division of 20th Century Fox) and made for approximately $7 million is demonstrating knowledge of institutional context. A student who only describes the film&rsquo;s &ldquo;indie feel&rdquo; is not earning those marks.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A route by which independent films gain visibility, recognition and distribution deals through screenings at film festivals such as Sundance, Cannes or Toronto.">festival circuit</dfn> and the Distribution Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For genuinely low-budget independent productions, the festival circuit remains the primary route to distribution. A strong Sundance premiere can generate competing distribution offers within days; the 2014 short version of <em>Whiplash</em> followed exactly this path, securing its Sundance screening before director Damien Chazelle expanded it to the feature version distributed by Sony Pictures Classics. Understanding this industrial pathway &mdash; production company, festival premiere, specialty-division acquisition &mdash; is essential AO1 context for every set film in this unit.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">American independent cinema is an industrial category, not a style &mdash; a film is independent when it is produced outside the financial control of a major Hollywood studio, even if it is later picked up by a specialty division such as Fox Searchlight or Sony Pictures Classics.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The history runs from John Cassavetes in the 1950s&ndash;60s, through the Sundance festival circuit of the 1980s, the Miramax era of the 1990s (complicated by Disney ownership from 1993), to the A24 model of the 2010s &mdash; the conditions change, but artistic autonomy remains the defining value.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">In extended writing, demonstrate AO1 by citing the production company, distributor, and approximate budget of the set film &mdash; then connect that industrial context to a specific filmic choice the director made, linking micro to macro.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">The extended-writing question on the US independent film rewards AO2 (analysis) more heavily than AO1 (knowledge) &mdash; but AO1 is still a quarter of the marks, and the most reliable way to demonstrate it is through accurate production context: the name of the production company, the distributor, the approximate budget, and a clear distinction between &lsquo;independent&rsquo; and &lsquo;specialty-division studio indie&rsquo;. A common student error is to treat &lsquo;indie&rsquo; as purely a stylistic description and to write about handheld cameras or mumbled dialogue without grounding the answer in the industrial category first. To hit the top tier, your answer must show that you understand <em>why</em> a particular film was made the way it was &mdash; what industrial conditions enabled those creative choices. Always connect your AO1 context to a specific filmic choice: &ldquo;Because Chazelle was working with a $3 million budget and a tight shooting schedule, he relied on a locked-off camera during performance sequences, which creates the sense of watching a staged recital rather than a spontaneously captured moment.&rdquo;</p>
