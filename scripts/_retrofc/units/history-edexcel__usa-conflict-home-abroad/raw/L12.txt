<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Wars, Not One</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the spring of 1965 the United States had committed itself to a war it did not understand. Operation Rolling Thunder began on 2 March 1965, the first US combat marines waded ashore at Da Nang on 8 March, and by July President Johnson had authorised the deployment of 175,000 ground troops. American commanders measured the war in firepower, miles of road controlled and enemy bodies counted. The Vietcong measured it in patience, terrain and political legitimacy. The mismatch between the two ways of fighting &mdash; one industrial, the other rural and political &mdash; explains why the world&rsquo;s strongest military spent four years escalating without coming any closer to victory.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Operation Rolling Thunder</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The sustained US bombing campaign against North Vietnam, 2 March 1965 to 31 October 1968. Aimed to break Hanoi&rsquo;s will, cut supply lines down the Ho Chi Minh Trail and reassure Saigon; dropped roughly 643,000 tons of bombs without halting Northern infiltration of the South.">Operation Rolling Thunder</dfn> was the longest sustained bombing campaign in US history. It was meant to do three things at once: break Hanoi&rsquo;s political will to support the southern insurgency, cut the supply lines running south down the Ho Chi Minh Trail, and reassure the wobbling government in Saigon that Washington was serious. Over forty-three months B-52s, F-4 Phantoms and F-105 Thunderchiefs dropped roughly 643,000 tons of bombs on the North &mdash; more than the United States had dropped in the entire Pacific theatre in the Second World War. Targets included roads, railways, bridges, fuel depots and factories. American commanders argued that another six months of bombing would crack the regime; six months later they argued the same again.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Rolling Thunder failed for reasons that were structural, not technical. North Vietnam was an agricultural country with little industry to bomb; what industry it had was dispersed, often into Chinese border regions where US aircraft were forbidden to fly. Soviet anti-aircraft missiles and MiG fighters made the bombing increasingly costly: by the end of the campaign around 900 US aircraft had been lost. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Network of jungle paths, river crossings and truck roads running through Laos and Cambodia by which North Vietnam moved soldiers and supplies into South Vietnam. Repeatedly bombed by US aircraft from the mid-1960s without ever being closed.">Ho Chi Minh Trail</dfn>, which threaded through Laos and Cambodia, was repaired faster than US aircraft could destroy it &mdash; in places by tens of thousands of conscripted civilians moving by bicycle and shovel. North Vietnamese morale, far from cracking, hardened.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: when did Rolling Thunder begin and end, roughly how many tons of bombs did it drop, and why was the Ho Chi Minh Trail so hard to close?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Operation Rolling Thunder ran from 2 March 1965 to 31 October 1968 and dropped roughly 643,000 tons of bombs on North Vietnam &mdash; more than the United States dropped in the whole Pacific in the Second World War. It failed to halt the Vietcong because the North&rsquo;s industry was small and dispersed, the Ho Chi Minh Trail in Laos and Cambodia was repaired by hand as fast as it was bombed, and Soviet anti-aircraft missiles cost the US around 900 aircraft.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Search and Destroy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">On the ground General William Westmoreland chose <dfn class="term" data-def="US ground tactic in Vietnam under General Westmoreland from 1965. Patrols entered Vietcong-held territory to draw the enemy into open battle, then called in artillery, helicopter gunships and air strikes; success was measured by the body count rather than territory held.">search and destroy</dfn>. US battalions were lifted by helicopter into Vietcong-controlled countryside, drew the enemy into open contact and then called down artillery, gunships and tactical air strikes. The aim was attrition: to kill more Vietcong and North Vietnamese soldiers than Hanoi could replace. Success was measured not in ground held but in <dfn class="term" data-def="The metric used by US commanders in Vietnam to measure the success of search-and-destroy operations: enemy dead per US dead. Inflated by inclusion of civilians and double counting; rewarded officers for raising kill totals and corroded military discipline.">the body count</dfn> &mdash; the daily tally of enemy dead reported up the chain of command. Officers were promoted on rising kill totals. The metric corroded itself: dead civilians were entered as Vietcong, the same casualty was counted twice on different reports, and a brutal logic spread through some units that &lsquo;if it&rsquo;s dead and Vietnamese, it&rsquo;s VC&rsquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Search and destroy won most of its battles. In the Ia Drang valley in November 1965 the 7th Cavalry inflicted around ten times more casualties than they took. At the same time the tactic surrendered the political war. US troops moved through villages, fought through them, and then helicoptered out the next day; the Vietcong returned that night and asked the villagers what the Americans had done. By 1967 a US Defense Department study concluded that the war was producing roughly two new Vietcong recruits for every one killed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">A common misconception is that the US lost the Vietnam War because it lost battles. It did not. American firepower &mdash; B-52s, helicopters, napalm, half a million ground troops &mdash; produced victory in almost every direct engagement, including the great test of the Tet Offensive in 1968. The war was lost because the body-count metric measured the wrong thing. Holding ground and protecting villagers would have built South Vietnamese state legitimacy. Killing without holding produced refugees and resentment, and the Vietcong fed on both. Top-band evaluation answers should weigh the tactical question (firepower won most battles) against the strategic one (firepower could not generate political legitimacy for Saigon).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Chemical Weapons: Agent Orange and Napalm</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Search and destroy depended on being able to see the enemy in jungle terrain that Westmoreland&rsquo;s commanders called &lsquo;triple canopy&rsquo;. The US response was to remove the jungle. Between 1962 and 1971, Operation Ranch Hand sprayed roughly 19 million gallons of herbicide over South Vietnam, around 11 million of which were <dfn class="term" data-def="Herbicide sprayed by US aircraft in Operation Ranch Hand (1962-71) over around 4.5 million acres of South Vietnam to defoliate jungle and destroy Vietcong food crops. Contained the dioxin TCDD, which has since been linked to cancers and birth defects in Vietnamese civilians and US veterans.">Agent Orange</dfn>. The aim was to defoliate the canopy and to destroy Vietcong rice crops. The herbicide contained the dioxin TCDD, since linked to cancers and to birth defects in both Vietnamese civilians and US veterans. American forces also used <dfn class="term" data-def="Petrol thickened with a gelling agent so that it sticks to whatever it hits and burns at around 1,200 degrees Celsius. Used by US aircraft against Vietcong positions, bunkers and villages from 1965; photographs of napalmed civilians, especially Nick Ut&rsquo;s 1972 image of Phan Thi Kim Phuc, became defining anti-war evidence.">napalm</dfn> &mdash; petrol thickened with a gelling agent so that it sticks to whatever it hits and burns at around 1,200 degrees Celsius. Photographs of napalmed civilians, especially Nick Ut&rsquo;s 1972 image of nine-year-old Phan Thi Kim Phuc running naked from a strike, would become some of the war&rsquo;s defining anti-war evidence.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Vietcong Guerrilla Tactics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A way of fighting that avoids decisive battles and instead uses ambushes, raids, sabotage and harassment by small mobile units to wear down a stronger enemy and exhaust its political will. Vietcong tactics combined ambushes from jungle and tunnels, the use of booby traps, integration with the peasant population, and supply along the Ho Chi Minh Trail.">guerrilla warfare</dfn> the Vietcong fought against this firepower turned American strengths into liabilities. Where the Americans wanted decisive battles, the Vietcong refused them. Small mobile units ambushed patrols and then disappeared into the jungle. Roads, tracks and rice paddies were seeded with booby traps and punji-stick pits; one Pentagon study estimated that around eleven per cent of US casualties in Vietnam came from booby traps rather than direct fire. Tunnels &mdash; most famously the Cu Chi network north of Saigon, around 250 kilometres of underground passages with kitchens, hospitals and command rooms &mdash; allowed Vietcong fighters to vanish even from territory the Americans had &lsquo;cleared&rsquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Above all the Vietcong lived among the rural population. They wore peasant clothes, slept in peasant huts, and depended on peasant food and intelligence. The American principle &lsquo;don&rsquo;t fight the enemy you can see&rsquo; ran into the problem that the Vietcong were almost never visible until they fired. <dfn class="term" data-def="Network of around 250 kilometres of underground passages built by the Vietcong north of Saigon. Included kitchens, hospitals, command rooms and storage; allowed fighters to disappear from territory the US had cleared and resurface behind American lines.">Tunnel networks</dfn> and the Trail through Laos and Cambodia gave the insurgency strategic depth that no amount of US firepower could subtract.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four Vietcong tactics with one sentence each on how each one neutralised US firepower.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Vietcong neutralised US firepower with four interlocking tactics: ambush from jungle cover (refusing the decisive battle the US wanted), booby traps and punji pits (around eleven per cent of US casualties), tunnel networks such as the 250 km Cu Chi system (vanishing from cleared ground), and integration with the peasant population (depriving the US of an identifiable enemy). The Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia kept all four supplied.</p>
</div>

In strategic terms it deepened American failure. Destroyed rice crops produced refugees, and the refugee camps that ringed Saigon were Vietcong recruitment grounds. Photographs of napalm strikes &mdash; especially Nick Ut&rsquo;s 1972 image of Kim Phuc &mdash; gave the anti-war movement at home an image bank no government statement could counter. Top-band answers will read chemical weapons as a tactic that bought visibility on the ground at the cost of legitimacy in Saigon and political support in the United States.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Operation Rolling Thunder (1965-68) dropped roughly 643,000 tons of bombs on North Vietnam but failed to break Hanoi&rsquo;s will or close the Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Westmoreland&rsquo;s search-and-destroy tactics, measured by the body count, won most direct engagements but generated refugees and Vietcong recruitment; chemical weapons (Agent Orange, napalm) defoliated around 4.5 million acres at the cost of civilian casualties and a defining anti-war image bank.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Vietcong guerrilla tactics &mdash; ambushes, booby traps, tunnel networks such as the 250 km Cu Chi system, and integration with the peasant population &mdash; turned American firepower into a strategic liability.</li>
</ul>
