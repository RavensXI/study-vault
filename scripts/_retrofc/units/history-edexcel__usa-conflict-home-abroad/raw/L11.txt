--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Vietnam, and Why 1954?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In May 1954 a French army of 13,000 men surrendered at Dien Bien Phu in northern Vietnam after a fifty-six-day siege by the Vietminh, the communist-led independence movement under Ho Chi Minh. The defeat ended sixty-four years of French colonial rule in Indochina. The peace conference that followed in Geneva produced the <dfn class="term" data-def="July 1954 international agreement that ended French rule in Indochina; partitioned Vietnam at the seventeenth parallel pending nationwide reunification elections in 1956 that South Vietnam, with US backing, refused to hold.">Geneva Accords</dfn>: Vietnam was partitioned at the seventeenth parallel pending nationwide reunification elections in 1956. The communists held the north (the Democratic Republic of Vietnam under Ho Chi Minh in Hanoi); a US-backed government held the south (the Republic of Vietnam under Bao Dai in Saigon, and from 1955 under Ngo Dinh Diem).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">President Eisenhower&rsquo;s administration would not accept that Indochina might fall to communism. In April 1954, even before Dien Bien Phu, Eisenhower had explained the danger in a press conference using a metaphor that would shape American policy for fifteen years. &lsquo;You have a row of dominoes set up,&rsquo; he said, &lsquo;you knock over the first one, and what will happen to the last one is the certainty that it will go over very quickly.&rsquo; If Vietnam fell, Laos and Cambodia would follow; then Thailand, then Burma, then potentially India and Australasia. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Cold War theory, articulated by President Eisenhower in April 1954, that if one Southeast Asian country fell to communism the rest would follow in sequence; provided the strategic justification for fifteen years of US involvement in Vietnam.">domino theory</dfn> presented the war as a single piece of a global containment strategy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: who articulated the domino theory, when, and what specifically did it predict would happen if Vietnam fell?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The domino theory was articulated by President Eisenhower in an April 1954 press conference and shaped fifteen years of US policy in Vietnam. It predicted that if South Vietnam fell to communism, the other states of Southeast Asia &mdash; Laos, Cambodia, Thailand, Burma &mdash; would topple in sequence and that communism would advance towards India and Australasia. It made South Vietnam a global, not local, problem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Eisenhower&rsquo;s Man in Saigon: Ngo Dinh Diem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Catholic, anti-communist, Mandarin-class president of South Vietnam from 1955 until his assassination in a US-tolerated coup on 2 November 1963; refused the 1956 reunification elections; the Strategic Hamlets Programme alienated the rural population.">Ngo Dinh Diem</dfn> took power as prime minister of South Vietnam in 1954 and, after a rigged 1955 referendum, declared himself president of a republic. Diem was Catholic, French-educated, anti-communist and a member of the small Mandarin landlord class in a country roughly 80 per cent Buddhist and roughly 80 per cent peasant. Eisenhower committed the United States to keeping Diem in power. Between 1955 and 1961 around $1.6 billion in US aid flowed to Saigon.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The weaknesses of the Diem government were obvious to almost everyone except Diem himself. He refused to hold the 1956 reunification elections promised at Geneva (President Eisenhower privately conceded he would have lost them). His government was openly nepotistic: his brother Ngo Dinh Nhu ran a network of secret police, and Nhu&rsquo;s wife Madame Nhu acted as the regime&rsquo;s public spokeswoman. Land reform, which the Vietminh had used to win peasant support in the north, was reversed in the south &mdash; landlords who had fled were brought back and rents reimposed. Catholic refugees from the north received preferential treatment in the south, including land grants and government jobs. In 1963 the regime&rsquo;s repression of Buddhist demonstrations, including the live televised self-immolation of the monk Thich Quang Duc on a Saigon street in June 1963, destroyed Diem&rsquo;s remaining international standing.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Students sometimes describe Diem as a corrupt strongman whose mistakes a more competent leader could have avoided. The deeper problem was structural. The Republic of Vietnam was a state with no rural legitimacy. Most of the south&rsquo;s population were tenant farmers; reversing land reform handed the Vietminh a recruitment programme. The Catholic-Mandarin character of the regime alienated the Buddhist and peasant majority. The 1960 founding of the southern <dfn class="term" data-def="Communist insurgency in South Vietnam founded in 1960 from former Vietminh fighters, supplied via the Ho Chi Minh Trail; conducted guerrilla operations against the Diem and successor governments; American forces called them the Vietcong.">Vietcong</dfn> &mdash; the National Liberation Front, formed by southern communists left over from the anti-French war &mdash; gave a structure to peasant grievances that Diem could not address without giving up his class base. Top-band evaluation answers will read US escalation as forced by structural weaknesses, not just by Diem&rsquo;s personal failures.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Kennedy: Strategic Hamlets and 16,000 Advisers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When John F. Kennedy entered the White House in January 1961, around 800 US military &lsquo;advisers&rsquo; were stationed in South Vietnam. By the time of his assassination in November 1963 the figure was over 16,000. Kennedy expanded covert action, helicopter support and special forces, and signed off on a counter-insurgency project copied from British operations in Malaya: the <dfn class="term" data-def="Counter-insurgency programme launched in 1962 by South Vietnamese and US officials, copying British tactics in Malaya; forcibly relocated Vietnamese peasants into fortified hamlets to deny food, shelter and recruits to the Vietcong; alienated the rural population that the project was meant to win.">Strategic Hamlets Programme</dfn>. Vietnamese peasants were forcibly relocated into fortified hamlets ringed with barbed wire and ditches to deny the Vietcong food, shelter and recruits. Around 8 million people were moved into around 8,000 such hamlets between 1962 and 1963.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The programme was a disaster. Peasants resented being uprooted from ancestral village land, often at gunpoint. Hamlets were poorly supplied and frequently overrun; the Vietcong simply waited until the relocation was complete and then walked through the wire. By late 1963 most of the hamlets had been abandoned or had effectively become Vietcong recruiting bases. Kennedy&rsquo;s strategy of fighting an Asian guerrilla war on the cheap with American advisers and a Vietnamese client army had failed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The November 1963 Coup</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">By the autumn of 1963 the Kennedy administration concluded that the war could not be won with Diem in power. On 1 November 1963, Saigon generals supported by US ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge launched <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1-2 November 1963 overthrow of President Ngo Dinh Diem by South Vietnamese generals, with the prior knowledge of US ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge and the Kennedy White House; Diem and his brother Nhu were murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier on 2 November 1963.">a coup</dfn> against Diem. Diem and his brother Nhu were captured the following morning and murdered in the back of an armoured personnel carrier. Three weeks later Kennedy himself was assassinated in Dallas. The new Johnson administration inherited a South Vietnam that was now politically more unstable than it had been under Diem &mdash; nine successive juntas would govern it in the first eighteen months after the coup &mdash; and a war that the United States was visibly losing.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in three sentences: how did US troop numbers change between 1961 and 1963, and what major decision did Washington back on 1 November 1963?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under President Kennedy, the number of US military &lsquo;advisers&rsquo; in South Vietnam grew from around 800 to over 16,000 between 1961 and 1963. Kennedy approved the Strategic Hamlets Programme of 1962-63, which uprooted around 8 million peasants and accelerated the Vietcong&rsquo;s recruitment. On 1-2 November 1963 the Kennedy administration backed a Saigon coup that overthrew and killed President Diem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Gulf of Tonkin Incident, August 1964</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">On 2 August 1964, in international waters off the North Vietnamese coast, the destroyer USS Maddox exchanged fire with three North Vietnamese torpedo boats. The Maddox was supporting covert South Vietnamese commando raids on the North Vietnamese coast. Two days later, in heavy weather, the Maddox and the destroyer USS Turner Joy reported a second engagement. The second incident never happened. Sonar operators on the Turner Joy had picked up echoes off their own propellers; no North Vietnamese boats were ever within range. The Johnson administration, deciding the truth did not matter, presented the August 4 &lsquo;attack&rsquo; to Congress as unprovoked aggression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">On 7 August 1964 Congress passed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Joint resolution of the US Congress passed on 7 August 1964 in response to the disputed Gulf of Tonkin engagements; authorised the president &lsquo;to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack&rsquo; against US forces and was used by Johnson and Nixon to wage the Vietnam War without a formal declaration of war until its repeal in January 1971.">Gulf of Tonkin Resolution</dfn>. It authorised the president &lsquo;to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack&rsquo; against US forces and to assist any Southeast Asian state under attack. The resolution passed 416-0 in the House and 88-2 in the Senate. Without ever declaring war, Johnson now had the legal authority to fight one. Within seven months he had ordered the bombing of North Vietnam (Operation Rolling Thunder, March 1965) and committed the first US ground combat units to South Vietnam.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">Top-band 12-mark causation answers will weigh several factors against each other. The Tonkin Resolution provided the legal vehicle for escalation but was itself a symptom rather than a cause: by August 1964 South Vietnam&rsquo;s government had collapsed nine times in nine months, the Strategic Hamlets had failed, the Vietcong controlled most of the countryside, and the domino logic that had drawn the US into Indochina under Eisenhower made withdrawal politically impossible for Johnson in an election year. The August 4 incident was the trigger; the underlying causes were the structural weakness of South Vietnam, the failure of Kennedy-era counter-insurgency, the increasing strength of the Vietcong supplied via the Ho Chi Minh Trail, and the Cold War commitment to containment that no US president from 1954 to 1968 would walk away from.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">After French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 and the Geneva Accords, Eisenhower&rsquo;s domino theory committed the US to keeping South Vietnam non-communist; from 1955 the regime was Ngo Dinh Diem&rsquo;s, propped up by around $1.6 billion of US aid through 1961 despite his refusal to hold the 1956 elections.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Kennedy raised &lsquo;advisers&rsquo; from around 800 to over 16,000 by 1963 and approved the Strategic Hamlets Programme, which uprooted around 8 million peasants and accelerated Vietcong recruitment; on 1-2 November 1963 Washington backed a coup in which Diem was overthrown and killed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The disputed Gulf of Tonkin engagement of 4 August 1964, an incident that did not actually occur, produced the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of 7 August 1964; Johnson now had open-ended authority to wage war and within seven months had begun Operation Rolling Thunder and the deployment of US ground combat troops.</li>
</ul>
