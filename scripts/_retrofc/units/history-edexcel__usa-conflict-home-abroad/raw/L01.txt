Jim Crow segregation, voter suppression and how the NAACP and CORE began the legal fight.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Black America on the Eve of Brown</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1954, almost ninety years after the abolition of slavery, the daily life of a Black American in the South was shaped by a system called <dfn class="term" data-def="State and local laws passed across the Southern states from the 1870s that enforced racial segregation in public spaces.">Jim Crow</dfn>. Schools, hospitals, buses, parks, water fountains and even cemeteries were marked &lsquo;White&rsquo; or &lsquo;Colored&rsquo;. The Supreme Court&rsquo;s 1896 ruling in <em>Plessy v. Ferguson</em> had blessed this with the doctrine of &lsquo;separate but equal&rsquo;. In practice, &lsquo;separate&rsquo; was enforced by law and custom; &lsquo;equal&rsquo; was a fiction. Black schools in Mississippi spent roughly a quarter of what white schools spent per pupil, and many Black children walked past well-funded white schools to reach their own.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write three concrete examples of Jim Crow segregation a Black American might encounter in a single day in 1954.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1954, segregation in the South was both legal and customary. <dfn class="term" data-def="Segregation that is enforced by law.">De jure segregation</dfn> ran through state laws, while <dfn class="term" data-def="Segregation that exists in practice through housing patterns, custom and economics, even when no law requires it.">de facto segregation</dfn> shaped Northern cities through housing and employment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Voting: A Right on Paper Only</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Fifteenth Amendment had granted Black men the vote in 1870, but Southern states had spent eighty years stripping it back. By 1954, fewer than 20 per cent of eligible Black voters in the Deep South were registered. Mississippi was the worst, with under 5 per cent registered. The barriers were deliberate. <dfn class="term" data-def="Reading or writing tests that registrars used to prevent Black citizens registering to vote, applied unfairly and with impossible questions.">Literacy tests</dfn> demanded that applicants interpret obscure passages of the state constitution to a white registrar&rsquo;s satisfaction; the same registrar would wave through illiterate white applicants.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A fee that voters had to pay before registering, used in Southern states to exclude poor Black and some poor white voters.">Poll taxes</dfn> required citizens to pay a fee &mdash; sometimes cumulative across years &mdash; before they could register. White-only primaries, &lsquo;grandfather clauses&rsquo; (which exempted anyone whose grandfather had voted before 1867) and the threat of being fired, evicted or attacked completed the system. Registering to vote could cost a sharecropper his livelihood; voting could cost him his life.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Students often assume that segregation simply meant Black and white facilities sat side by side. The truth was sharper. In 1950 the average white teacher in South Carolina earned roughly twice what a Black teacher earned. Black hospital wards routinely lacked equipment that white wards took for granted. Buses required Black passengers to pay at the front, then re-board at the rear; if the white section filled up, Black passengers had to give up their seats and stand. The point of Jim Crow was not parallel facilities but daily reminders of inferior status.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The NAACP: Lawyers Building a Case</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Founded in 1909 by W. E. B. Du Bois and others, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (<dfn class="term" data-def="Civil rights organisation founded in 1909 that pursued legal challenges to segregation through the courts and lobbied for anti-lynching laws.">NAACP</dfn>) was the largest civil rights body of the era. By 1954 it had around 400,000 members. Its strategy was patient and legalistic: train Black lawyers, identify the right plaintiffs, take cases through the federal courts and chip away at <em>Plessy</em> piece by piece. Thurgood Marshall, head of the NAACP&rsquo;s Legal Defense Fund, had won a string of victories in higher education during the late 1940s before turning to the schools.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The NAACP also published <em>The Crisis</em> magazine, lobbied Congress for federal anti-lynching laws (which the Senate repeatedly blocked) and supported local branches investigating police brutality. White supremacists understood the threat: in many Southern states the NAACP was harassed by lawsuits and, by 1956, banned outright in Alabama. Membership records were demanded, and members lost jobs and homes for their affiliation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">CORE: Direct Action Before the Movement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A second organisation took a different approach. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Civil rights organisation founded in 1942 that pioneered nonviolent direct action including sit-ins and freedom rides.">CORE</dfn> (Congress of Racial Equality) was founded in Chicago in 1942 by James Farmer and an interracial group of pacifists influenced by Mahatma Gandhi. CORE pioneered the sit-in long before Greensboro &mdash; Chicago restaurants integrated under CORE pressure in the 1940s. Its 1947 &lsquo;Journey of Reconciliation&rsquo; sent integrated bus riders into the upper South and previewed the Freedom Rides of 1961.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Through the 1950s CORE remained smaller than the NAACP and concentrated in Northern cities, but its commitment to <em>nonviolent direct action</em> &mdash; deliberately breaking unjust laws and accepting arrest &mdash; gave the later movement a working playbook. Many famous campaigners, including Bayard Rustin who advised Martin Luther King, came from this CORE tradition.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The &lsquo;long civil rights movement&rsquo; idea</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Many students treat 1954 as the start of the civil rights story. Historians who write about the &lsquo;long movement&rsquo; argue that the foundations were laid much earlier &mdash; by Black newspapers, the NAACP&rsquo;s 1930s anti-lynching campaign, A. Philip Randolph&rsquo;s 1941 March on Washington threat which forced Roosevelt to ban segregation in defence industries, and the activism of Black women like Ella Baker and Septima Clark in adult literacy and citizenship schools. The 1954 ruling did not create the movement; the movement created the conditions for the ruling.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences how the NAACP and CORE differed in tactics in the early 1950s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two distinct strategies were already in place by 1954. The NAACP used the courts and lobbying; CORE used nonviolent direct action. The mass-protest movement that exploded after Montgomery drew on both, but neither organisation invented civil rights activism &mdash; both inherited decades of earlier struggle.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">A System Ready to Crack</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By the early 1950s, several pressures were converging on Jim Crow. Black soldiers had returned from a war against Nazi racism and refused to accept segregation at home. Northern migration during the 1940s had given Black voters new political weight in cities like Detroit and Chicago. The Cold War made American segregation a propaganda gift to the Soviet Union, embarrassing successive administrations. The NAACP&rsquo;s legal strategy was reaching the Supreme Court. The system that had governed Black life for half a century was about to face its first real legal test &mdash; <em>Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka</em>.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">In 1954 Jim Crow segregation was legal across the South, upheld by the 1896 <em>Plessy</em> ruling and policed by daily custom and violence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Voting rights existed on paper but literacy tests, poll taxes and economic threats kept Black registration below 5 per cent in Mississippi.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The NAACP fought through the courts, while CORE pioneered nonviolent direct action &mdash; both organisations laid the groundwork before mass protest erupted.</li>
</ul>
