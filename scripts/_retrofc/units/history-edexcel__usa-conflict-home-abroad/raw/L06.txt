--- DESCRIPTION ---
Birmingham’s fire hoses, the March on Washington and the path to the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Birmingham, 1963</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By spring 1963 the SCLC had concluded that the slow, court-by-court progress of the Kennedy administration would never produce a federal civil rights law. Martin Luther King and his lieutenant Wyatt Tee Walker chose Birmingham, Alabama, as the campaign target precisely because they expected its police commissioner <dfn class="term" data-def="Birmingham&rsquo;s segregationist police commissioner whose use of fire hoses and police dogs against children in May 1963 was televised worldwide.">Bull Connor</dfn> to react with public violence. Birmingham, nicknamed &lsquo;Bombingham&rsquo; for the dozens of unsolved bombings of Black homes and churches since 1945, was the most rigidly segregated large city in the South. The campaign would force the country to look at what segregation actually was.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The protest began in April 1963 with sit-ins, marches and a boycott of downtown stores. King was arrested on 12 April for marching without a permit. From his cell he wrote the famous <dfn class="term" data-def="King&rsquo;s 1963 open letter, written on newspaper margins, defending nonviolent direct action against criticism from white moderate clergy who urged patience.">Letter from Birmingham Jail</dfn>, replying to eight white moderate clergymen who had urged patience. &lsquo;For years now I have heard the word &ldquo;Wait!&rdquo;&rsquo; he wrote. &lsquo;This &ldquo;Wait&rdquo; has almost always meant &ldquo;Never&rdquo;.&rsquo; The letter became one of the founding documents of the modern civil rights canon.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Children&rsquo;s Crusade and the Fire Hoses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Adult arrests had nearly emptied the Birmingham movement&rsquo;s reserves. On 2 May 1963, James Bevel of the SCLC sent over a thousand schoolchildren onto the streets in what became known as the Children&rsquo;s Crusade. By the end of the day around 600 had been arrested, the youngest aged six. The next day Bull Connor ordered Birmingham police to turn high-pressure fire hoses, set strong enough to strip bark from trees, on schoolchildren, and to set police dogs on protestors. The photographs &mdash; a teenage boy in a cardigan being lunged at by a German Shepherd, children rolled along the pavement by water jets &mdash; ran on every front page in the world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why the Children&rsquo;s Crusade succeeded where the adult marches had stalled.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 2-3 May 1963, around 1,000 Birmingham schoolchildren were arrested and others were attacked with fire hoses and police dogs on Bull Connor&rsquo;s orders. Television images of the assault forced President Kennedy onto national television within days to call for federal civil rights legislation.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7">President Kennedy, who had moved cautiously on civil rights through 1961-62, said the photographs made him &lsquo;sick&rsquo;. On 11 June 1963 he gave a televised address to the nation in which he framed civil rights as &lsquo;a moral issue &hellip; as old as the scriptures and as clear as the American Constitution&rsquo;, and announced he would send a federal civil rights bill to Congress. He had been pushed there by Birmingham. Hours after the speech, the NAACP&rsquo;s Mississippi field secretary Medgar Evers was shot dead in his driveway in Jackson.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The March on Washington, 28 August 1963</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">To pressure Congress to pass the bill, the major civil rights organisations agreed to hold the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 28 August 1963 civil rights demonstration in Washington DC at which around 250,000 people gathered, and where Martin Luther King delivered his &lsquo;I Have a Dream&rsquo; speech.">March on Washington</dfn> for Jobs and Freedom. On 28 August 1963 around 250,000 people, roughly 60 per cent of them Black, gathered between the Washington Monument and the Lincoln Memorial. It was the largest political demonstration in American history to that point. King delivered the closing speech. The prepared text laid out specific legislative demands; the closing &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="The closing section of King&rsquo;s 28 August 1963 speech at the March on Washington, partly improvised, articulating a vision of racial equality.">I Have a Dream</dfn>&rsquo; section was largely improvised after gospel singer Mahalia Jackson called from behind him &lsquo;Tell them about the dream, Martin&rsquo;.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Popular memory reduces the March on Washington to King&rsquo;s seventeen-minute speech. The day&rsquo;s organisation belongs to A. Philip Randolph, the veteran Black trade unionist who had threatened a march on Washington in 1941 to force wartime fair-employment laws, and to Bayard Rustin, the gay socialist Quaker organiser who built the logistical operation of 250,000 marchers, 2,000 buses and 21 chartered trains in eight weeks. SNCC chairman John Lewis&rsquo;s prepared speech, which had threatened to march &lsquo;through the South like Sherman&rsquo;, was toned down at the request of Catholic church leaders to keep the march&rsquo;s ecumenical coalition intact. The day belonged to a movement, not a man.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">From Kennedy to Johnson: Passing the Bill</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas on 22 November 1963. His successor <dfn class="term" data-def="US president 1963-69 who used Kennedy&rsquo;s assassination as moral leverage on Congress to pass the 1964 Civil Rights Act and 1965 Voting Rights Act.">Lyndon B. Johnson</dfn>, a Texan who had spent twenty years in the Senate, used Kennedy&rsquo;s death as moral leverage. Five days after the assassination he told Congress that &lsquo;no memorial oration or eulogy could more eloquently honour President Kennedy&rsquo;s memory than the earliest possible passage of the civil rights bill&rsquo;. Through the spring of 1964 he applied the legislative skill that had earned him the nickname &lsquo;Master of the Senate&rsquo;, twisting Republican arms to overcome a 60-day Southern Democratic filibuster &mdash; the longest in Senate history. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1964 federal law that banned discrimination on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin in public accommodations, federally funded programmes and employment.">Civil Rights Act 1964</dfn>, signed on 2 July 1964, banned segregation in public accommodations, federally funded programmes and employment, and gave the federal government real enforcement power for the first time since Reconstruction.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Freedom Summer and the Mississippi Murders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Civil Rights Act did not address voting. In Mississippi only 6.7 per cent of eligible Black adults were registered to vote. In June 1964 SNCC, CORE and the NAACP launched <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1964 voter-registration project in Mississippi run by SNCC, CORE and the NAACP that recruited mainly white Northern student volunteers and ran freedom schools.">Freedom Summer</dfn>, sending hundreds of mainly white Northern students to register Black voters and run freedom schools. The choice of white volunteers was deliberate: organisers knew that violence against Black activists alone had been ignored, but violence against white students would generate national news.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">On 21 June 1964, the project&rsquo;s third day, three volunteers &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="The three Freedom Summer volunteers (a Black Mississippian and two white Northerners) murdered by Klansmen and local law officers in Neshoba County, Mississippi, on 21 June 1964.">James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner</dfn> &mdash; disappeared near Philadelphia, Mississippi. Six weeks later, after a search involving 200 FBI agents and US Navy divers (who in the process found the bodies of eight other murdered Black men whose disappearances had never been investigated), the three were found buried in an earthen dam. They had been beaten and shot by a mob of Klansmen led by the Neshoba County deputy sheriff. The murders made the front pages and the network news exactly as the organisers had reluctantly anticipated.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Freedom Summer mattered &mdash; even though no convictions came at the time</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Federal civil rights charges against eighteen suspects in 1967 produced seven convictions for conspiracy &mdash; not murder, since Mississippi refused to prosecute. Edgar Ray Killen, organiser of the killings, was finally convicted of manslaughter only in 2005, on the 41st anniversary of the murders. Yet Freedom Summer succeeded in two ways. It put the absence of Black voting rights at the centre of national news in summer 1964. And it produced the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, whose challenge at the 1964 Democratic Convention &mdash; led by Fannie Lou Hamer&rsquo;s televised testimony of being beaten in a Mississippi jail for trying to register to vote &mdash; embarrassed Johnson and prepared the political ground for the 1965 Voting Rights Act.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: which act was signed in 1964, what it banned, and which act of protest had pushed Kennedy to propose it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1964 Civil Rights Act outlawed segregation in public accommodations, federally funded programmes and employment. It was proposed by Kennedy after the Birmingham campaign in June 1963 and passed by Lyndon Johnson on 2 July 1964 after the longest filibuster in Senate history.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The Birmingham campaign of April-May 1963 was deliberately designed to provoke Bull Connor; the Children&rsquo;s Crusade and televised use of fire hoses and police dogs forced President Kennedy to propose federal civil rights legislation in June 1963.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The 28 August 1963 March on Washington brought 250,000 people to the capital and produced King&rsquo;s &lsquo;I Have a Dream&rsquo; speech; it was organised by A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin and broadened the bill&rsquo;s political coalition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">After Kennedy&rsquo;s assassination, Lyndon Johnson used the moral momentum to pass the 1964 Civil Rights Act on 2 July; Freedom Summer ran in parallel, exposing the violence behind Mississippi&rsquo;s exclusion of Black voters in the murders of Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner.</li>
</ul>
