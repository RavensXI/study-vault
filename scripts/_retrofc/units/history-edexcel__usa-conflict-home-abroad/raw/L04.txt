--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Emmett Till: A Death That Shook the Nation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In August 1955, three months after <em>Brown II</em>, a 14-year-old boy from Chicago travelled south to spend the summer with relatives in Money, Mississippi. <dfn class="term" data-def="Fourteen-year-old African-American boy from Chicago who was lynched in Mississippi in August 1955; his open-casket funeral galvanised the civil rights movement.">Emmett Till</dfn> stopped at Bryant&rsquo;s Grocery and was accused, on disputed grounds, of whistling at the white shopkeeper Carolyn Bryant. Four nights later her husband Roy Bryant and his half-brother J. W. Milam dragged Till from his uncle&rsquo;s home, beat him, shot him in the head and threw his body, weighted with a cotton-gin fan, into the Tallahatchie River. He was identified by a ring his father had owned.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Till&rsquo;s mother Mamie made a decision that changed the case: she insisted on an open-casket funeral in Chicago so that &lsquo;the whole world&rsquo; could see what had been done to her son. <em>Jet</em> magazine printed the photographs. Tens of thousands queued past the casket. The accused men were tried in Mississippi in September 1955; an all-white jury acquitted them in 67 minutes. Months later Bryant and Milam admitted the murder in <em>Look</em> magazine, protected by double-jeopardy laws. Rosa Parks said she was thinking of Till that December as she sat down on the Montgomery bus. The case became a generation&rsquo;s recruiting moment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why Mamie Till&rsquo;s open-casket decision mattered for the civil rights movement.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Emmett Till&rsquo;s lynching in August 1955 was a turning point because his mother&rsquo;s decision to hold an open-casket funeral, and the photographs in <em>Jet</em>, made Southern racial violence undeniable to the rest of the country. The all-white jury&rsquo;s acquittal of his killers exposed the legal protection that violence enjoyed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Little Rock 1957: Nine Students and a Constitutional Crisis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Three years after <em>Brown</em>, Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas was due to admit nine Black students in September 1957: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The nine African-American students who integrated Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas in September 1957.">Little Rock Nine</dfn>. Arkansas&rsquo;s governor, <dfn class="term" data-def="Democratic Governor of Arkansas (1955-67) who deployed the state National Guard in 1957 to block the integration of Central High School.">Orval Faubus</dfn>, ordered the Arkansas National Guard to surround the school and turn the students away. White mobs gathered. Photographs of 15-year-old Elizabeth Eckford walking alone through a screaming crowd ran on front pages worldwide and became a Cold War embarrassment for the United States.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">President Eisenhower had been reluctant to use federal force on civil rights, but Faubus had created a constitutional confrontation he could not ignore: a state governor was using state troops to defy a federal court order. On 24 September 1957 Eisenhower signed <dfn class="term" data-def="Executive Order issued by President Eisenhower on 24 September 1957 federalising the Arkansas National Guard and authorising the deployment of US Army troops to enforce school desegregation in Little Rock.">Executive Order 10730</dfn>, federalised the Arkansas National Guard, and sent 1,200 paratroopers from the <dfn class="term" data-def="Elite US Army airborne division deployed to Little Rock by President Eisenhower in 1957 to escort the Little Rock Nine into Central High School.">101st Airborne</dfn> Division to escort the students into school. Federal troops escorted the Nine to class for the rest of the academic year. Melba Pattillo, one of the Nine, later wrote that her bodyguard, a paratrooper called Danny, advised her to &lsquo;put on your warrior face&rsquo;.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Aftermath: Closing the Schools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Faubus had lost the constitutional battle but he was not finished. In autumn 1958, rather than admit Black students for a second year, he closed all of Little Rock&rsquo;s public high schools entirely. White children attended hastily organised private academies; many Black students went without education for the year. Federal courts struck down the closures in 1959, but the &lsquo;lost year&rsquo; signalled what white-majority opposition was prepared to sacrifice. Across the South similar &lsquo;school-closing&rsquo; statutes were prepared. Prince Edward County, Virginia, eventually shut its schools for five years (1959-64). Massive resistance was not just rhetoric; it was policy.</p>

He acted at Little Rock because Faubus had defied a federal court order using state troops &mdash; that was a constitutional issue, not a civil rights one. The 101st Airborne escorted the Nine into class, but Eisenhower did not deliver any wider promise of federal protection for Black students elsewhere. The lesson many activists drew was sobering: the federal government would intervene only when state defiance was so flagrant it threatened the authority of Washington itself. Without that level of provocation, Black families had to make their own bargains with massive resistance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Ku Klux Klan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="White-supremacist secret organisation, refounded in 1915 and revived after Brown, that used cross-burnings, beatings, bombings and lynchings to terrorise Black communities and civil rights activists.">Ku Klux Klan</dfn> (KKK) had been refounded in 1915 and went into a fresh wave of recruitment after <em>Brown</em>. Klan membership in the South rose sharply in 1956-57. The KKK&rsquo;s methods were terror &mdash; cross-burnings outside Black churches and homes, beatings of activists, and the bombings that became known as &lsquo;the Mississippi state flower&rsquo; in some Black communities for their grim regularity. NAACP field secretary Medgar Evers investigated Till&rsquo;s killing and the murders of other Black Mississippians; he would himself be assassinated by a Klan-aligned killer in 1963. The Klan&rsquo;s violence was not separate from formal politics &mdash; many local police officers, sheriffs and elected officials were members or sympathisers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Dixiecrats: Massive Resistance Goes Political</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Southern Democrats opposed to civil rights; the term originally referred to the 1948 splinter States&rsquo; Rights Democratic Party but became shorthand for segregationist Southern members of Congress.">Dixiecrats</dfn> were the segregationist wing of the Democratic Party. They had broken with the national party in 1948 to form the States&rsquo; Rights Democratic Party under Strom Thurmond, who carried four Deep South states. By the mid-1950s &lsquo;Dixiecrat&rsquo; was used loosely for any Southern Democrat opposed to civil rights. Their power lay in Congress: through committee chairmanships and the Senate filibuster they routinely blocked civil rights legislation. The 1957 Civil Rights Act survived only because Lyndon Johnson stripped it of meaningful enforcement to get Southern senators to allow a vote. Senator Thurmond delivered a 24-hour 18-minute filibuster &mdash; still the longest in Senate history &mdash; against the bill.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">White Citizens&rsquo; Councils</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Below the Klan and alongside the Dixiecrats sat a third tier of resistance: the <dfn class="term" data-def="Local segregationist organisations, founded in Mississippi in 1954 in response to the Brown ruling, that used economic coercion and political pressure rather than overt violence to oppose desegregation.">White Citizens&rsquo; Councils</dfn>. Founded in Indianola, Mississippi in July 1954 within weeks of the <em>Brown</em> ruling, they spread quickly through the Deep South to a peak of around 250,000 members. Where the Klan used violence, the Councils used economic coercion: they pressured banks to call in mortgages of Black activists, employers to dismiss them, suppliers to refuse them goods. Members were typically respectable middle-class whites &mdash; bankers, lawyers, school-board members &mdash; who saw themselves as the Klan&rsquo;s civilised alternative. The combination of Klan violence, Dixiecrat political obstruction, and Citizens&rsquo; Council economic intimidation made every act of integration costly. Many Black families who signed school-desegregation petitions lost their jobs within weeks.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the three forms of white opposition to desegregation, with one example of how each operated.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Massive resistance to <em>Brown</em> took three forms: violence (the KKK), political obstruction (Dixiecrats in Congress) and economic coercion (White Citizens&rsquo; Councils). The 1957 Civil Rights Act was watered down by the second; the Till case showed the lethal power of the first; the Councils targeted families who signed desegregation petitions.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The murder of Emmett Till in August 1955 and his mother&rsquo;s open-casket funeral made Southern racial violence undeniable to a national audience and inspired younger activists including Rosa Parks.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Little Rock 1957 forced Eisenhower to send the 101st Airborne to enforce <em>Brown</em>, but Faubus then closed Little Rock&rsquo;s high schools for 1958-59 &mdash; a pattern repeated by Prince Edward County, Virginia, for five years.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">White opposition to desegregation operated on three levels &mdash; the KKK&rsquo;s violence, the Dixiecrats&rsquo; political obstruction in Congress, and the White Citizens&rsquo; Councils&rsquo; economic coercion of Black families.</li>
</ul>
