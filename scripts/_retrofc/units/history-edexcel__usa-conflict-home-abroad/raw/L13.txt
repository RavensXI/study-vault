<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The War the United States Was Told It Was Winning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the end of 1967 General William Westmoreland was telling the American public that the Vietnam War had reached a turning point. There was, he said in a White House press conference in November, &lsquo;light at the end of the tunnel&rsquo;. The Vietcong were exhausted; the body count was rising; the South Vietnamese government was stabilising. Three months later that confidence collapsed. The events of 1968 to 1970 &mdash; the Tet Offensive, Walter Cronkite&rsquo;s CBS broadcast, the My Lai Massacre and the Kent State shootings &mdash; broke the credibility of the Johnson administration, divided American society into a war camp and an anti-war camp, and brought the home front and the battlefield together as a single political problem.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Tet Offensive</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">On the night of 30 January 1968, during the Vietnamese lunar new year holiday, around 84,000 Vietcong and North Vietnamese soldiers launched coordinated assaults on more than a hundred cities and military bases across South Vietnam. A Vietcong commando squad blew its way into the grounds of the US embassy in Saigon and held part of it for six hours. The ancient imperial city of <dfn class="term" data-def="Ancient imperial capital of Vietnam, around halfway between Hanoi and Saigon. Captured by the Vietcong and North Vietnamese Army on 31 January 1968 in the opening hours of the Tet Offensive; recaptured after twenty-five days of urban fighting that left around 80 per cent of the city destroyed and uncovered mass graves of around 2,800 civilians killed by communist forces.">Hue</dfn> was overrun in the first hours and held for twenty-five days. Footage of US marines fighting house to house in Hue, and of Vietcong gunmen inside the embassy compound in Saigon, ran on every American evening news bulletin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Coordinated Vietcong and North Vietnamese assault on more than a hundred South Vietnamese cities and military bases launched at the lunar new year on 30 January 1968. Tactically a US-South Vietnamese victory: around 40,000 Vietcong dead and almost all urban territory recaptured. Strategically it broke the Johnson administration&rsquo;s claim that the enemy was on the verge of defeat.">Tet Offensive</dfn> was, in narrow military terms, a US-South Vietnamese victory. The Vietcong took catastrophic casualties &mdash; around 40,000 dead, by some estimates &mdash; and lost almost all the urban territory they had briefly seized. The southern uprising that Hanoi had hoped to provoke did not materialise. Westmoreland told a press conference in early February that he had &lsquo;defeated the enemy&rsquo;s will to win&rsquo;. The political reality was the reverse. Tet contradicted the &lsquo;light at the end of the tunnel&rsquo; assurances of November 1967 so completely that no one in the United States believed the war was being won. The gap between official optimism and televised reality produced what journalists called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The widening gap between Johnson administration claims that the war in Vietnam was being won and the televised evidence to the contrary that reached US audiences from late 1967. Sharply opened by the Tet Offensive of 1968 and by Walter Cronkite&rsquo;s 27 February 1968 CBS broadcast that the war would &lsquo;end in stalemate&rsquo;.">credibility gap</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: when did the Tet Offensive begin, what was its tactical outcome, and why is it remembered as a strategic shock?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Tet Offensive began on 30 January 1968 with coordinated Vietcong attacks on more than a hundred targets across South Vietnam. Tactically the US and South Vietnamese drove the attackers back, killing around 40,000 Vietcong and recovering Hue after twenty-five days of urban fighting. Strategically Tet shattered the Johnson administration&rsquo;s &lsquo;light at the end of the tunnel&rsquo; line, opened the credibility gap, and turned American public opinion against the war.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">A common student error is to label Tet 1968 as the moment the United States &lsquo;lost&rsquo; Vietnam militarily. Tactically Tet was a US-South Vietnamese victory: the Vietcong lost almost all the urban territory they had briefly seized, took around 40,000 casualties and were so depleted they would never again play the leading role in the war. The strategic shock was political and televisual. The offensive contradicted Westmoreland&rsquo;s November 1967 &lsquo;light at the end of the tunnel&rsquo; assurances; Walter Cronkite&rsquo;s broadcast of 27 February 1968 that the war would &lsquo;end in stalemate&rsquo; eroded public support to the point that Johnson, on 31 March 1968, told the country he would not seek re-election. The most useful framing for top-band evaluation is to weigh the tactical victory (Vietcong losses, urban recapture) against the strategic defeat (credibility gap, Cronkite, Johnson&rsquo;s withdrawal).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Walter Cronkite and the &lsquo;Stalemate&rsquo; Broadcast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">On the evening of 27 February 1968, after a reporting trip to Vietnam, the CBS Evening News anchor <dfn class="term" data-def="CBS Evening News anchor and the most trusted television journalist in the United States in the late 1960s. His 27 February 1968 broadcast from Saigon, declaring the war would &lsquo;end in stalemate&rsquo;, became the defining example of TV media power in shaping US Vietnam opinion; Johnson reportedly said losing Cronkite meant losing &lsquo;Mr Average Citizen&rsquo;.">Walter Cronkite</dfn> closed his programme by telling viewers that the war would &lsquo;end in stalemate&rsquo;. Cronkite was the most trusted television journalist in the United States; his on-air judgement, made in his own voice rather than as part of a news report, registered as a verdict. President Johnson reportedly told an aide that if he had lost Cronkite, he had lost &lsquo;Mr Average Citizen&rsquo;. On 31 March Johnson made a televised address: he announced a pause in bombing of most of North Vietnam, opened the door to negotiations, and stated that he would not seek the Democratic nomination for re-election. By June, polls showed that for the first time more Americans called the war a mistake than supported it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">My Lai, March 1968</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">On the morning of 16 March 1968 American soldiers from Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, entered the South Vietnamese hamlet of <dfn class="term" data-def="Hamlet in South Vietnam where on 16 March 1968 American soldiers from Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry Regiment, killed between 347 and 504 unarmed Vietnamese civilians (mostly women, children and elderly). Covered up by the Army for over a year, exposed by journalist Seymour Hersh in November 1969; the trial of Lt William Calley split US opinion on the morality of the war.">My Lai</dfn> on a search-and-destroy mission. There was no enemy in the hamlet. Over four hours the soldiers killed between 347 and 504 unarmed Vietnamese civilians &mdash; mostly women, children and elderly. Some victims were shot in their homes; others were rounded up and machine-gunned in a drainage ditch. Helicopter pilot Hugh Thompson Jr landed his observation aircraft between American troops and a group of fleeing Vietnamese, ordered his crew to fire on his own side if they advanced, and rescued around eleven civilians.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Army covered up the massacre for over a year. The story broke in the American press in November 1969 in an investigation by the freelance journalist Seymour Hersh, illustrated by the colour photographs that combat photographer Ronald Haeberle had taken on the day. <dfn class="term" data-def="US Army platoon leader at My Lai on 16 March 1968. In March 1971 he was the only officer convicted of any My Lai-related charge: premeditated murder of twenty-two Vietnamese civilians; sentenced to life imprisonment. Within forty-eight hours President Nixon ordered him moved to house arrest at Fort Benning; he was paroled in 1974 after serving around three and a half years.">Lieutenant William Calley</dfn>, the platoon leader, went on trial in November 1970 charged with the premeditated murder of 109 Vietnamese civilians. He was convicted on 29 March 1971 of murdering twenty-two and sentenced to life imprisonment. Within forty-eight hours President Nixon ordered him moved from prison to house arrest at Fort Benning. He was paroled in 1974 after serving around three and a half years.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in three sentences: what happened at My Lai on 16 March 1968, who was the only officer convicted, and what did Nixon do to his sentence?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 16 March 1968 American soldiers killed between 347 and 504 unarmed Vietnamese civilians at My Lai. The story was broken by Seymour Hersh in November 1969, more than a year after the Army cover-up. Lt William Calley was the only officer convicted of any My Lai-related charge in March 1971; within forty-eight hours President Nixon ordered him moved from prison to house arrest, and he was paroled in 1974.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Kent State, May 1970</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">On 30 April 1970 President Nixon announced on television that US ground troops had crossed into Cambodia. Anti-war protests, which had thinned during the early withdrawals of the Nixon Doctrine, exploded across more than 350 college campuses in the days that followed. At Kent State University in Ohio the Reserve Officer Training Corps building was set on fire on the evening of 2 May; Governor James Rhodes deployed the Ohio National Guard. On the early afternoon of 4 May 1970 a contingent of around seventy-seven guardsmen, exhausted and inexperienced, opened fire on a crowd of student protesters and bystanders. They fired sixty-seven rounds in around thirteen seconds. <dfn class="term" data-def="Shooting on 4 May 1970 in which Ohio National Guardsmen opened fire on student protesters at Kent State University, killing four (Allison Krause, Jeffrey Miller, Sandra Scheuer, William Schroeder) and wounding nine. Triggered by Nixon&rsquo;s announcement of the Cambodia incursion four days earlier; produced the largest student strike in US history, with more than four million students walking out from over 700 campuses.">The Kent State shootings</dfn> killed four students &mdash; Allison Krause, Jeffrey Miller, Sandra Scheuer and William Schroeder &mdash; and wounded nine. Two of the dead were not protesters but students walking between classes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The killings produced the largest student strike in US history: more than four million students walked out from over 700 campuses, and around 450 universities and colleges shut down for the rest of the spring term. The Pulitzer Prize-winning photograph by John Filo of fourteen-year-old Mary Ann Vecchio kneeling over the body of Jeffrey Miller became the defining image of the home front war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Hard Hats and the Silent Majority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">It would be wrong to think the United States by 1970 was unanimously anti-war. President Nixon had won the 1968 election by appealing to what he would call in a televised address on 3 November 1969 <dfn class="term" data-def="Phrase used by President Nixon in a televised address on 3 November 1969 to describe the older, conservative-leaning, mostly white Americans whom he claimed supported his Vietnam policy and disapproved of the anti-war movement; framed the war debate as a contest between a respectable centre and a noisy student fringe.">the silent majority</dfn>: older, mostly white, conservative-leaning Americans who supported the war and disapproved of the protest movement. On 8 May 1970 &mdash; four days after Kent State &mdash; around two hundred New York construction workers in red, white and blue helmets attacked an anti-war demonstration in lower Manhattan, beating students and breaking up the rally. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Construction workers, mostly from New York and other industrial cities, identifiable by their hard hats. Symbolised pro-war working-class Americans; on 8 May 1970 around two hundred attacked an anti-war demonstration in lower Manhattan; later in the month around 100,000 marched in support of Nixon&rsquo;s Vietnam policy.">hard hats</dfn> later in the month organised a demonstration of around 100,000 marchers in support of Nixon&rsquo;s Vietnam policy. The war was now an argument among Americans, on Americans, as much as a war fought against a Vietnamese enemy.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">A textbook narrative sometimes presents the United States in 1968-70 as a society sliding uniformly into opposition. The picture was sharper and more divided. The student movement, draft resistance and TV coverage of Tet, My Lai and Kent State did pull middle-American opinion against the war. At the same time Nixon won the 1968 election on a silent-majority appeal, and the hard hats produced visible pro-war counter-protests in 1970. Top-band evaluation answers can use this divide to test interpretations: did the home front turn the war (an anti-war reading), or did it produce a divided politics that Nixon was able to ride for another five years (a silent-majority reading)?</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Tet Offensive of 30 January 1968 was a tactical US-South Vietnamese victory (around 40,000 Vietcong dead, Hue retaken after twenty-five days) but a strategic shock: it opened the credibility gap, prompted Cronkite&rsquo;s 27 February &lsquo;stalemate&rsquo; broadcast and led Johnson on 31 March to withdraw from the presidential race.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">My Lai (16 March 1968) saw American soldiers kill between 347 and 504 unarmed Vietnamese civilians; the story was broken by Seymour Hersh in November 1969; Lt William Calley was the only officer convicted (March 1971) and Nixon moved him to house arrest within forty-eight hours.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Kent State shootings of 4 May 1970, after Nixon&rsquo;s Cambodia announcement, killed four students and produced the largest student strike in US history; the silent majority and hard hats showed the home front was a divided politics, not a single anti-war movement.</li>
</ul>
