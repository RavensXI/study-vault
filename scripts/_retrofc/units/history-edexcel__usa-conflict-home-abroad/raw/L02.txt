--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Case Built Brick by Brick</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1950 a welder called Oliver Brown tried to enrol his eight-year-old daughter Linda at the all-white Sumner Elementary School, four blocks from the family home in Topeka, Kansas. The school district refused. Linda was bussed across town to the all-Black Monroe Elementary instead. Topeka had segregated elementary schools but integrated high schools, and the buildings were not glaringly unequal &mdash; the system itself was the injury. That made it the perfect test case for the <dfn class="term" data-def="Civil rights organisation founded in 1909 that pursued legal challenges to segregation through the federal courts.">NAACP</dfn> Legal Defense Fund and its director, <dfn class="term" data-def="NAACP lawyer who led the legal team in Brown v. Topeka and later became the first African American Supreme Court Justice in 1967.">Thurgood Marshall</dfn>, who was looking to overturn the doctrine of segregation itself, not just unequal funding.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Topeka suit was bundled with four others from Delaware, South Carolina, Virginia and Washington D.C. into a single appeal known as <em>Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka</em>. Marshall argued before the Supreme Court that segregation, however materially equal, branded Black children as inferior and damaged their development. He drew on social-science evidence including the doll experiments of psychologists Kenneth and Mamie Clark, who had shown Black children consistently identifying white dolls as &lsquo;nice&rsquo; and Black dolls as &lsquo;bad&rsquo; &mdash; evidence cited in the eventual ruling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the case Brown overturned, the year, and the key phrase from each ruling.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1954 ruling overturned the 1896 case <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1896 Supreme Court case that established the doctrine of &lsquo;separate but equal&rsquo;, allowing legal segregation across the Southern states.">Plessy v. Ferguson</dfn>, which had established the doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal doctrine that allowed segregation as long as the separate facilities were of equal quality &mdash; in practice rarely enforced.">&lsquo;separate but equal&rsquo;</dfn>. <em>Brown</em> declared that &lsquo;separate educational facilities are inherently unequal&rsquo;.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Ruling: 17 May 1954</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Chief Justice <dfn class="term" data-def="Chief Justice of the United States from 1953 to 1969 who lobbied his fellow justices to deliver a unanimous Brown ruling and led the liberal Warren Court.">Earl Warren</dfn>, recently appointed by Eisenhower, was determined to deliver the ruling unanimously. He spent months persuading reluctant Southern justices that a 9-0 decision would land with greater authority than a split court. On 17 May 1954 he read the opinion himself: &lsquo;Does segregation of children in public schools solely on the basis of race &hellip; deprive the children of the minority group of equal educational opportunities? We believe that it does.&rsquo; The court ruled school segregation a violation of the Fourteenth Amendment&rsquo;s equal-protection clause. Sixty years of <em>Plessy</em> were swept aside in a single sentence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The reaction was immediate. The Black press celebrated &mdash; the Pittsburgh Courier called it &lsquo;the second emancipation&rsquo;. President Eisenhower, by contrast, refused to endorse the ruling publicly and later told a friend that appointing Warren had been &lsquo;the biggest damn-fool mistake I ever made&rsquo;. White Southern leaders responded with what they called &lsquo;massive resistance&rsquo;. Within two years 101 Southern members of Congress had signed the <em>Southern Manifesto</em> denouncing the ruling and pledging to use &lsquo;all lawful means&rsquo; to reverse it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Brown II and &lsquo;All Deliberate Speed&rsquo;</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The first <em>Brown</em> decision said segregation was unconstitutional. It did not say when or how desegregation should happen. A year later, on 31 May 1955, the Supreme Court issued a second ruling known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1955 Supreme Court ruling that ordered Southern school districts to desegregate &lsquo;with all deliberate speed&rsquo;, leaving enforcement to local courts.">Brown II</dfn>, which ordered Southern districts to desegregate <dfn class="term" data-def="The deliberately vague phrase used in Brown II to describe the pace of school integration; in practice it gave Southern districts cover to delay.">&lsquo;with all deliberate speed&rsquo;</dfn> and left enforcement to local federal judges.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The phrase was a compromise &mdash; designed to win Southern justices&rsquo; support &mdash; and a disaster for desegregation. &lsquo;Deliberate speed&rsquo; was contradictory enough to mean almost anything, and Southern districts read it as &lsquo;take your time&rsquo;. Ten years after the original ruling, fewer than 2 per cent of Black children in the eleven Southern states attended integrated schools. Virginia closed Prince Edward County&rsquo;s public schools entirely from 1959 to 1964 rather than integrate, leaving Black children without education for five years.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A common mistake is to write that <em>Brown</em> &lsquo;ended segregation&rsquo;. It did not. The 1954 ruling created a legal opening; closing that opening took marshals, troops, repeated lawsuits and another decade of activism. Eisenhower had to federalise the Arkansas National Guard and send the 101st Airborne to Little Rock in 1957. Governor Faubus then closed all Little Rock&rsquo;s high schools for 1958-59 rather than integrate. Real desegregation in many Southern districts dates from the 1964 Civil Rights Act and the 1968 <em>Green v. New Kent County</em> ruling, not 1954. Top-band answers separate the legal change from the lived change.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Significance Beyond the Schools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Even with massive resistance, <em>Brown</em>&rsquo;s significance was huge. It signalled that the federal courts would no longer protect segregation. It encouraged activists to challenge other Jim Crow laws, knowing the legal terrain had shifted. It supplied a moral language &mdash; &lsquo;separate is inherently unequal&rsquo; &mdash; that movement leaders deployed for the next decade. Rosa Parks later said the ruling gave Black Southerners hope; it was, she argued, part of the climate that made Montgomery thinkable just eighteen months later.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">It also exposed the federal government&rsquo;s reluctance to enforce its own constitutional commitments. Without Eisenhower behind it, <em>Brown</em>&rsquo;s words sat on paper. Without the movement &mdash; without Emmett Till&rsquo;s funeral, the Montgomery boycott, the Little Rock Nine, and Birmingham &mdash; those words might have stayed there. The ruling was a beginning. The work belonged to ordinary people.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Some historians celebrate <em>Brown</em> as the watershed of the civil rights era &mdash; the moment America&rsquo;s highest court turned against legal racism. Others, including legal scholars who study what actually changed in Southern classrooms, argue that the ruling has been over-credited; without movement pressure and federal enforcement, integration on the ground was negligible until the 1960s. This is a productive split for interpretation answers: the same legal text can be read as a dramatic break or as words on paper depending on what evidence the historian foregrounds.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why &lsquo;with all deliberate speed&rsquo; weakened the Brown ruling.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The combination of <em>Brown I</em> (1954) and <em>Brown II</em> (1955) declared school segregation unconstitutional but left enforcement to local courts. Ten years on, fewer than 2 per cent of Black children in the South attended integrated schools.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">In May 1954 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in <em>Brown v. Topeka</em> that segregated schools were inherently unequal, overturning the 1896 <em>Plessy</em> doctrine of &lsquo;separate but equal&rsquo;.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><em>Brown II</em> in 1955 ordered desegregation &lsquo;with all deliberate speed&rsquo;, a phrase Southern districts used as cover to delay; ten years later fewer than 2 per cent of Black Southern children attended integrated schools.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The ruling&rsquo;s significance lay less in immediate desegregation and more in shifting the moral and legal ground &mdash; encouraging the activism that followed and forcing federal enforcement at Little Rock and beyond.</li>
</ul>
