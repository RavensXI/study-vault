The unfinished vote of 1964, Bloody Sunday at Selma, and the 1965 Voting Rights Act.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Vote: The Unfinished Business of 1964</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The 1964 Civil Rights Act had banned segregation in public accommodations, schools and federally funded programmes. It had not addressed voting. Across the Deep South, Black Americans were kept off the electoral roll by a network of devices that had grown up since the end of Reconstruction in the 1870s: <dfn class="term" data-def="Tests imposed by Southern registrars before voter registration; questions ranged from arbitrary (interpret a passage from the state constitution) to impossible (recite the entire constitution from memory).">literacy tests</dfn> set arbitrarily by white registrars, poll taxes, the requirement to bring a registered voter to vouch for the applicant, and the threat of being sacked, evicted or beaten if you tried to register. In Selma, Alabama, county seat of Dallas County, only 2 per cent of voting-age Black adults were on the register in 1965, despite Black people being a majority of the population.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: in Dallas County, what percentage of voting-age Black adults were on the register in 1965, even though Black people were a majority of the population?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1965 only 2 per cent of voting-age Black adults in Dallas County, Alabama were registered to vote, even though Black Americans were the majority of the county&rsquo;s population. Across Mississippi the figure was 6.7 per cent. The 1964 Civil Rights Act had not addressed voting.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Selma?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">SNCC organisers had been working in Selma since 1963 to register voters and to organise local people who had borne reprisals for trying. In January 1965 the SCLC, fresh from a difficult campaign in St Augustine, Florida, agreed to come to Selma at the invitation of the local Dallas County Voters League. Martin Luther King chose Selma for the same reason he had chosen Birmingham: he expected the local sheriff, Jim Clark, to react with public violence. Clark&rsquo;s deputies wore lapel pins reading &lsquo;Never&rsquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The campaign opened with mass attempts to register that ran straight into Clark&rsquo;s tactics &mdash; long queues kept standing for hours in the rain, arrests for &lsquo;loitering&rsquo; on courthouse steps. By the end of January 1965, 2,000 Black activists had been arrested, including King himself. On 18 February 1965, in nearby Marion, state troopers shot a 26-year-old church deacon called Jimmie Lee Jackson while he was trying to protect his mother and grandfather from a beating. Jackson died eight days later. Out of grief and anger, the SCLC announced a march from Selma to the state capital Montgomery to deliver a petition to Governor George Wallace.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Bloody Sunday, 7 March 1965</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">On the afternoon of Sunday 7 March 1965, around 600 marchers set out from Brown Chapel in Selma. They were led by SNCC chairman <dfn class="term" data-def="SNCC chairman 1963-66 who led marchers across the Edmund Pettus Bridge on Bloody Sunday in March 1965 and was severely beaten by Alabama state troopers.">John Lewis</dfn> and SCLC organiser Hosea Williams. They walked in pairs along the pavement of US Route 80, crossed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Selma steel-arch bridge over the Alabama River, named after a Confederate general and Ku Klux Klan leader, where state troopers attacked civil rights marchers on Bloody Sunday in March 1965.">Edmund Pettus Bridge</dfn> over the Alabama River, and stopped where the road descended on the far side. A line of around 200 state troopers and Sheriff Clark&rsquo;s mounted posse blocked the road. The troopers gave them two minutes to disperse. After ninety seconds the troopers attacked.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The marchers were charged with horses, beaten with billy clubs wrapped in barbed wire, and tear-gassed. John Lewis&rsquo;s skull was fractured. Around 60 marchers were hospitalised. ABC News interrupted its scheduled film &mdash; <em>Judgment at Nuremberg</em>, a drama about the Nazi war-crimes trials &mdash; to broadcast 15 minutes of footage from the bridge. Forty-eight million Americans watched. Telegrams of protest poured into the White House through Sunday night. The day became known as <dfn class="term" data-def="7 March 1965, when Alabama state troopers attacked civil rights marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma; the televised assault transformed national opinion on voting rights.">Bloody Sunday</dfn>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">King had not been on the bridge on Bloody Sunday. On Tuesday 9 March he led a second &lsquo;turnaround&rsquo; march to the bridge, knelt, prayed and turned back to honour a federal restraining order. That night three white Unitarian ministers who had come south for the march were attacked in a Selma street; one, James Reeb, died of his injuries. Reeb&rsquo;s death generated headlines and presidential condolences that Jimmie Lee Jackson&rsquo;s had not &mdash; a fact King later spoke about with characteristic honesty. Federal District Judge Frank Johnson lifted the restraining order, and on 21 March a third march, this time legally protected by 2,000 federalised Alabama National Guard and 1,800 US Army troops, set off from Selma. By the time the marchers reached the Alabama State Capitol on 25 March they were 25,000 strong. King addressed them: &lsquo;How long? Not long, because the arc of the moral universe is long but it bends toward justice.&rsquo;</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">President Johnson&rsquo;s &lsquo;We Shall Overcome&rsquo; Speech</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">On 15 March 1965, eight days after Bloody Sunday, President Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress and a national television audience. He announced he was sending Congress a federal voting rights bill, and named the cause as a moral one in language no white president had used before: &lsquo;Their cause must be our cause too. Because it is not just Negroes, but really it is all of us, who must overcome the crippling legacy of bigotry and injustice. And we shall overcome.&rsquo; The borrowing of the movement&rsquo;s anthem from the lips of a Texan president was, for many in the SNCC and SCLC offices watching, the moment they realised the bill would pass.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Voting Rights Act 1965</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1965 federal law that suspended literacy tests, authorised federal registrars to enrol voters in covered districts, and required Justice Department pre-clearance of changes to voting rules in states with a history of discrimination.">Voting Rights Act 1965</dfn> was signed by Johnson in the Capitol Rotunda on 6 August 1965. It suspended literacy tests in any county where less than 50 per cent of voting-age adults had registered or voted in 1964. It authorised <dfn class="term" data-def="Federal officials sent under the 1965 Voting Rights Act to enrol Black voters in counties where local registrars had blocked them.">federal registrars</dfn> to enrol voters directly in those counties &mdash; bypassing obstructive local registrars entirely. And under Section 5 it required state and local jurisdictions with a history of voting discrimination to submit any change to voting rules to the US Department of Justice for pre-clearance, to prevent the cycle of new exclusions replacing the old. The 24th Amendment of January 1964 had already abolished the federal poll tax; the 1966 Supreme Court ruling in <em>Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections</em> banned the state poll tax that remained.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In Mississippi, Black voter registration jumped from 6.7 per cent in 1964 to 59.8 per cent in 1968. Across the Deep South more than a quarter of a million Black voters were added to the rolls within five months of the Act being signed. Federal registrars enrolled half of them. The Act was the most effective single piece of civil rights legislation of the twentieth century. But the franchise alone did not address Northern de facto segregation, housing, policing, jobs, schools or wealth. The Watts riot in Los Angeles broke out five days after Johnson signed the Voting Rights Act, killing 34 and signalling that civil rights legislation had not addressed the urban North. The movement&rsquo;s next phase, and its splintering, would be over those questions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three things the 1965 Voting Rights Act did, and one thing it did not address.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1965 Voting Rights Act suspended literacy tests, sent federal registrars into counties with histories of discrimination, and required Justice Department pre-clearance of changes to voting rules. By 1968, Black voter registration in Mississippi had risen from 6.7 per cent to 59.8 per cent.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The 1964 Civil Rights Act had not addressed voting; in 1965 only 2 per cent of voting-age Black adults in Dallas County, Alabama, were registered, despite being a majority of the population.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">On Bloody Sunday (7 March 1965), Alabama state troopers attacked 600 marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge; ABC News broadcast 15 minutes of footage to 48 million Americans, transforming national opinion in 24 hours.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">President Johnson&rsquo;s &lsquo;We Shall Overcome&rsquo; speech of 15 March announced federal voting rights legislation; the Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 suspended literacy tests, sent federal registrars into the South, and lifted Mississippi&rsquo;s Black voter registration from 6.7 per cent to 59.8 per cent within three years.</li>
</ul>
