--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Montgomery&rsquo;s Buses Before December 1955</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Montgomery, Alabama, ran its city buses on the standard Southern model. Black passengers paid the driver at the front of the bus, then climbed off and re-entered through the rear door. They sat from the back forward; white passengers from the front backward. When the white section filled, Black passengers were ordered to surrender their seats and stand. Many drivers were openly hostile, slamming doors as Black customers walked round to the rear. By 1955 around 75 per cent of Montgomery&rsquo;s bus passengers were Black, but the drivers were entirely white.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The injustice had been documented and organised long before 1955. The Montgomery <dfn class="term" data-def="A Black women&rsquo;s political organisation in Montgomery, led by Jo Ann Robinson, that had been preparing a bus boycott since the early 1950s.">Women&rsquo;s Political Council</dfn> (WPC), led by Jo Ann Robinson, had been gathering complaints and drafting boycott plans since 1953. They were waiting for the right test case &mdash; a respected, unmistakably blameless Black woman whose arrest they could rally the city around. They had considered Claudette Colvin, a 15-year-old who had been arrested in March 1955 for refusing to give up her seat, but she was unmarried and pregnant, and the WPC feared opponents would use her circumstances to discredit the cause.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Rosa Parks: An Activist, Not a Tired Seamstress</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="NAACP secretary in Montgomery and trained civil rights activist whose December 1955 arrest sparked the Montgomery Bus Boycott.">Rosa Parks</dfn>, secretary of the Montgomery NAACP, was forty-two years old, married, and had spent much of 1955 at the Highlander Folk School in Tennessee studying nonviolent direct action. On the evening of 1 December 1955 she boarded a city bus on Cleveland Avenue, took a seat in the &lsquo;coloured&rsquo; section behind the white section, and refused the driver&rsquo;s order to give up her seat for a white passenger. She was arrested.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The popular memory of Parks &mdash; a tired seamstress acting on impulse &mdash; misses how prepared she was. She was an experienced organiser whose case the WPC was waiting to use. That night Jo Ann Robinson and three of her students mimeographed 35,000 leaflets calling for a one-day bus boycott on Monday 5 December. Parks&rsquo;s arrest had finally given them the &lsquo;right&rsquo; case.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why the WPC chose Rosa Parks as their test case rather than Claudette Colvin.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Montgomery Bus Boycott was not a spontaneous act. The Women&rsquo;s Political Council had been preparing for two years and chose Rosa Parks &mdash; a respected NAACP organiser &mdash; deliberately. Tens of thousands of leaflets were distributed within 36 hours.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">A New Voice: Martin Luther King Jr.</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">On the evening of 5 December, after a successful one-day boycott, Black community leaders met at Holt Street Baptist Church to decide what came next. They formed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The organisation set up to coordinate the Montgomery Bus Boycott from December 1955; its first president was Martin Luther King Jr.">Montgomery Improvement Association</dfn> (MIA) and elected as its president a 26-year-old newcomer who had only arrived in Montgomery the previous year: <dfn class="term" data-def="Baptist minister and civil rights leader who became president of the Montgomery Improvement Association in 1955 and of the SCLC from 1957.">Martin Luther King Jr.</dfn> He had a doctorate from Boston University, a strong preaching voice, and crucially &mdash; as a recent arrival &mdash; few enemies in the city. King had not chosen leadership; leadership chose him. His speech that night, broadcast over loudspeakers to a crowd that overflowed the church, set the rhetorical template for the next decade. &lsquo;There comes a time when people get tired of being trampled over by the iron feet of oppression,&rsquo; he told them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The MIA voted to continue the boycott until three demands were met: courteous treatment by drivers, first-come first-served seating with no white-only section, and the hiring of Black drivers on Black-majority routes. The city refused. The boycott ran for 381 days.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sustaining a 381-Day Walk</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Sustaining the boycott took organisation that the heroic-leader version of the story often hides. Black women, who made up the majority of bus passengers, walked, hitched, or used a complex car-pool of around 300 vehicles dispatched from 48 pickup points. Black-owned taxi firms charged the same 10-cent bus fare; when the city banned this, the MIA bought station wagons. White employers who relied on Black domestic labour pressured city hall to settle &mdash; and were ignored. The boycott&rsquo;s discipline was extraordinary: through Christmas, through winter rain, through indictments under a 1921 anti-boycott law, through the firebombing of King&rsquo;s house in January 1956.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Legal Win: Browder v. Gayle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The boycott&rsquo;s decisive victory was not a city-hall surrender but a federal court ruling. The MIA&rsquo;s lawyer Fred Gray filed <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1956 federal lawsuit, brought on behalf of four Black women in Montgomery, that ended in a Supreme Court ruling against bus segregation.">Browder v. Gayle</dfn> on behalf of four Black women including Claudette Colvin. On 13 November 1956 the Supreme Court affirmed a lower-court ruling that bus segregation was unconstitutional. The order reached Montgomery on 20 December 1956. King boarded an integrated bus at 5:55 a.m. the following morning. The boycott had lasted 381 days.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">From the MIA to the SCLC</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Out of Montgomery came a new national vehicle for the movement. In January 1957 King and other Southern Black ministers founded the <dfn class="term" data-def="Civil rights organisation founded by Martin Luther King and other Southern Black ministers in 1957, drawing on church networks to coordinate nonviolent direct action.">SCLC</dfn> (Southern Christian Leadership Conference) in Atlanta. Built around Black churches &mdash; Southern institutions outside white financial control &mdash; the SCLC made <dfn class="term" data-def="A protest method that uses peaceful confrontation &mdash; sit-ins, marches, boycotts &mdash; while accepting arrest and refusing to retaliate against violence.">nonviolent direct action</dfn> the central strategy of King&rsquo;s wing of the movement. Eisenhower also signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The first federal civil rights legislation since Reconstruction, which created a Civil Rights Commission and federal voting-rights authority but had limited enforcement powers.">Civil Rights Act 1957</dfn>, the first since Reconstruction.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The 1957 Act was the first federal civil rights legislation since Reconstruction &mdash; a real symbolic break. But Lyndon Johnson, then Senate Majority Leader, had stripped it of meaningful enforcement to get past Southern Democratic opposition; Senator Strom Thurmond filibustered it for over 24 hours even so. The Act created a Civil Rights Commission and gave federal courts limited authority over voting-rights violations, but Black voter registration in Mississippi did not move &mdash; transformative federal action had to wait until 1964 and 1965. The Act&rsquo;s real value was political: it put civil rights back on the federal agenda and tested the procedural levers that the 1964 and 1965 Acts would later use successfully.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three reasons the boycott succeeded, each in one short sentence.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Montgomery succeeded because Black passengers were the majority of bus customers, the MIA had the organisation to sustain a year-long boycott, and the federal courts &mdash; not local government &mdash; eventually ordered desegregation through <em>Browder v. Gayle</em> (1956).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The Montgomery Bus Boycott was a planned campaign, not a spontaneous protest &mdash; the Women&rsquo;s Political Council had been preparing since 1953, and Rosa Parks&rsquo;s arrest provided the right test case.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The 381-day boycott was sustained by tens of thousands of Black women walking to work, a 300-vehicle car pool, and the MIA&rsquo;s organisation; Martin Luther King provided national leadership for an existing local network.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The decisive victory came through the federal courts in <em>Browder v. Gayle</em> (1956). The boycott also produced the SCLC (1957) and contributed to the 1957 Civil Rights Act &mdash; though enforcement remained weak.</li>
</ul>
