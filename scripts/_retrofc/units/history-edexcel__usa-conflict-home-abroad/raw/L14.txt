--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Nixon Inherits the War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Richard Nixon took office on 20 January 1969 there were over 540,000 American troops in South Vietnam. The Tet Offensive had broken the Johnson administration politically; the Paris peace talks were stalled; the draft was running at over 30,000 men a month and the US military was taking around 200 dead a week. Nixon had won the 1968 election by promising the silent majority &lsquo;peace with honour&rsquo;. The strategy he and his National Security Adviser <dfn class="term" data-def="German-born US National Security Adviser to Nixon (1969-75) and Secretary of State (1973-77). Conducted secret talks with Le Duc Tho from 1970, signed the January 1973 Paris Peace Agreement and shared the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize with Le Duc Tho (who declined it). Architect of Vietnamisation, the secret bombing of Cambodia and the bombing of Laos.">Henry Kissinger</dfn> developed had two faces: troop withdrawal in front of the cameras, and a widening of the air war into Cambodia, Laos and the North in the hope of forcing Hanoi to negotiate.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Nixon Doctrine and Vietnamisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In a press conference on the Pacific island of Guam on 25 July 1969, Nixon set out what would become known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Foreign-policy framework set out by Nixon at Guam on 25 July 1969 and developed in his 3 November 1969 address. Asian allies must take primary responsibility for their own ground defence, with the United States providing equipment, training and an air-and-sea umbrella. Provided the strategic justification for Vietnamisation.">the Nixon Doctrine</dfn>: in future wars in Asia, the United States would supply air power, equipment and training, but Asian allies would have to fight the ground war themselves. The Vietnam application of the doctrine was <dfn class="term" data-def="US policy from 1969 of withdrawing American ground troops from South Vietnam while building up the Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN) to take over the fighting. Combined with continued US air support, secret bombing of Cambodia, the Cambodia incursion of 1970, and the bombing of Laos in 1971. Failed because the ARVN, despite its size, lacked political legitimacy and effective leadership.">Vietnamisation</dfn>: the South Vietnamese army would be expanded, equipped and trained to take over the ground war while US troops were withdrawn. The Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN) grew from around 825,000 men in 1968 to around 1.1 million by 1972, equipped with American helicopters, M16 rifles and tanks. US troop numbers fell from over 540,000 in 1969 to around 156,000 by the end of 1971 and around 24,000 by the end of 1972.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Vietnamisation reduced US casualties and bought Nixon political space at home, but it could not solve the strategic problem his predecessors had failed to solve: South Vietnam still had no rural legitimacy. The ARVN was riven by corruption, desertion and politically appointed officers. When tested in conventional combat &mdash; for example in the cross-border Operation Lam Son 719 into Laos in early 1971 &mdash; ARVN units broke. The American withdrawal was real; the South Vietnamese capacity it left behind was not.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: when did Nixon set out the Nixon Doctrine, what was Vietnamisation, and roughly how did US troop numbers change between 1969 and 1972?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Nixon Doctrine was set out at Guam on 25 July 1969 and applied in Vietnam as Vietnamisation: the United States would withdraw ground troops while training and equipping the South Vietnamese army to take over. US troop numbers fell from over 540,000 in 1969 to around 24,000 by the end of 1972; the Army of the Republic of Vietnam grew from around 825,000 to around 1.1 million by 1972.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Cambodia, Laos and the Linebacker Bombings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Behind the public face of withdrawal, Nixon and Kissinger widened the air war. Operation Menu, the secret bombing of North Vietnamese sanctuaries in Cambodia, ran from March 1969 to May 1970; over 3,800 sorties dropped roughly 100,000 tons of bombs on a country with which the United States was not at war. On 30 April 1970 Nixon announced on television that US ground troops, alongside ARVN units, had crossed into <dfn class="term" data-def="Joint US-South Vietnamese ground operation that began on 30 April 1970, when around 30,000 US and 50,000 ARVN troops crossed into Cambodia to attack North Vietnamese sanctuaries. Captured supplies but did not destroy the enemy headquarters. Triggered campus protests across the United States and the Kent State shootings of 4 May 1970.">Cambodia</dfn> in a sixty-day operation against North Vietnamese sanctuaries. The announcement triggered the campus protests that ended in the Kent State shootings on 4 May 1970.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In February 1971 the Nixon administration sent ARVN troops, with US air support, into Laos in Operation Lam Son 719 to cut the Ho Chi Minh Trail. The operation collapsed; television footage of ARVN soldiers clinging to the skids of departing US helicopters undermined Vietnamisation in front of the cameras. In December 1972, after the Paris talks again deadlocked, Nixon launched <dfn class="term" data-def="The most intense US bombing of North Vietnam of the war, 18-29 December 1972. Around 200 B-52s dropped roughly 20,000 tons of bombs on Hanoi and Haiphong over eleven days; aimed at forcing North Vietnam back to the Paris talks; 26 US aircraft were lost. Followed by the signing of the Paris Peace Agreement on 27 January 1973.">Operation Linebacker II</dfn>, the so-called &lsquo;Christmas bombings&rsquo;: around 200 B-52s dropped roughly 20,000 tons of bombs on Hanoi and Haiphong in eleven days, the most intense US bombing of the war.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Paris Peace Agreement, January 1973</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Secret talks between Kissinger and the North Vietnamese negotiator <dfn class="term" data-def="Politburo member of the Workers&rsquo; Party of Vietnam who led the North Vietnamese delegation in the secret Paris talks with Henry Kissinger from 1970. Signed the Paris Peace Agreement on 27 January 1973 and was awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize jointly with Kissinger; he refused it on the grounds that peace had not been achieved. Directed the conventional offensive that took Saigon in April 1975.">Le Duc Tho</dfn> had been running in parallel with the public Paris peace talks since 1970. After Linebacker II, Hanoi returned to the table, and on 27 January 1973 the four parties &mdash; the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Vietcong&rsquo;s Provisional Revolutionary Government &mdash; signed <dfn class="term" data-def="Agreement signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Vietcong&rsquo;s Provisional Revolutionary Government. Provided for a US troop withdrawal within sixty days, the return of American prisoners of war, a ceasefire in place and political talks among the Vietnamese parties; allowed around 150,000 North Vietnamese soldiers to remain in South Vietnam, which made eventual Northern victory close to inevitable.">the Paris Peace Agreement</dfn>. The terms required US forces to withdraw within sixty days; American prisoners of war (the POW/MIA) to be returned; a ceasefire in place; and political talks among the Vietnamese parties. The agreement allowed roughly 150,000 North Vietnamese soldiers to remain inside South Vietnam &mdash; a concession that made eventual Northern victory close to inevitable. The 1973 Nobel Peace Prize was awarded jointly to Kissinger and Le Duc Tho; Le Duc Tho refused it on the grounds that peace had not been achieved.</p>

The 1973 agreement allowed around 150,000 North Vietnamese troops to remain inside South Vietnam and made the South&rsquo;s independent survival highly unlikely. Some historians argue that Nixon and Kissinger were aiming for a &lsquo;decent interval&rsquo; between US withdrawal and Northern victory rather than a durable peace. <h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Fall of Saigon, April 1975</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">After Watergate forced Nixon&rsquo;s resignation in August 1974, Congress slashed military aid to Saigon. In March 1975 the North Vietnamese Army opened a conventional offensive in the central highlands. ARVN units collapsed; in many places officers abandoned their men. Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. The last American helicopters lifted around 7,000 people, the great majority Vietnamese, off rooftops including Tan Son Nhut airbase and a CIA villa near the embassy in <dfn class="term" data-def="The 30 April 1975 capture of Saigon by the North Vietnamese Army. Ended South Vietnam, reunified Vietnam under Hanoi&rsquo;s rule, and produced iconic televised images of US helicopters lifting evacuees from rooftops. Around 7,000 people, the great majority Vietnamese, were evacuated by helicopter in Operation Frequent Wind on 29-30 April 1975.">the fall of Saigon</dfn>. The world&rsquo;s strongest military had been defeated by a North Vietnamese army with a fraction of the firepower and a southern insurgency that had taken catastrophic losses at Tet.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Costs and the Reckoning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The economic and human costs of the war for the United States were severe. Around 58,200 American servicemen were killed and over 300,000 wounded. The financial cost ran to roughly $168 billion in 1973 dollars (around $1.2 trillion in 2020s prices); the war helped end the post-war economic boom and contributed to the inflation and dollar weakness of the early 1970s. The list of returning <dfn class="term" data-def="Acronym for Prisoners of War / Missing in Action. Around 591 American POWs were returned by North Vietnam under Operation Homecoming in February-April 1973 after the Paris Peace Agreement; around 2,500 servicemen remained classified as Missing in Action and the question of unaccounted-for personnel shaped US-Vietnam relations for decades.">POW/MIA</dfn> shaped US politics for two decades. The military lost the trust of much of the American public, and the &lsquo;Vietnam syndrome&rsquo; &mdash; reluctance to commit US ground forces overseas &mdash; would constrain American foreign policy until the 1991 Gulf War.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and list four reasons the United States lost the Vietnam War, with one supporting fact for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The United States lost the war for four overlapping reasons: the strengths of North Vietnam and the Vietcong (Russian and Chinese support, guerrilla tactics, the Ho Chi Minh Trail), the failure of US tactics (Rolling Thunder, search and destroy, the body count), the political weakness of South Vietnam (no rural legitimacy, ARVN corruption and collapse), and the impact of opposition at home (the credibility gap, Cronkite, Kent State, congressional cuts to aid after Watergate).</p>
</div>

The North Vietnamese case &mdash; Russian and Chinese supplies, the Ho Chi Minh Trail, Vietcong guerrilla tactics &mdash; matters but does not by itself explain the loss; the United States out-gunned its enemy throughout the war. The US-failure case &mdash; Rolling Thunder, search and destroy, Vietnamisation, the political collapse of South Vietnam, congressional aid cuts after Watergate &mdash; matters but understates the genuine effort and skill of the Northern campaign. The strongest answers weigh the strengths of the North against the weaknesses of the United States and South Vietnam, and reach a judgement on which mattered more, drawing precisely on dates, statistics and named operations.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Vietnamisation under the Nixon Doctrine cut US troop numbers from over 540,000 in 1969 to around 24,000 by the end of 1972, but the ARVN that was left behind lacked political legitimacy and broke in conventional combat (e.g. Lam Son 719, 1971).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The 1970 Cambodia incursion, the 1971 Laos operation and Operation Linebacker II in December 1972 widened the air war while withdrawing on the ground; the Paris Peace Agreement of 27 January 1973 returned American POWs but allowed around 150,000 North Vietnamese troops to remain inside South Vietnam.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Saigon fell on 30 April 1975; the war cost the United States around 58,200 dead, over 300,000 wounded and roughly $168 billion in 1973 dollars, and was lost through the combined strengths of North Vietnam and the Vietcong, the failure of US tactics, the political weakness of South Vietnam and the impact of opposition at home.</li>
</ul>
