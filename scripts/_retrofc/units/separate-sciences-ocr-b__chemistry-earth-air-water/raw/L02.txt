--- DESCRIPTION ---
Endothermic and exothermic reactions, energy profiles, and bond energies explained.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Is Always Transferred</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every chemical reaction involves an energy transfer. Energy is never created or destroyed &mdash; it is transferred between the reacting chemicals (the system) and the surroundings. Whether a reaction heats up or cools down its environment tells you which direction that transfer goes. This idea underpins everything from hand warmers to sports cold packs to the reactions that power living cells.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the definitions of exothermic and endothermic from memory, including what happens to the temperature of the surroundings in each case.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to increase.">exothermic reaction</dfn> releases energy to the surroundings &mdash; the temperature of the surroundings rises. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to decrease.">endothermic reaction</dfn> absorbs energy from the surroundings &mdash; the temperature of the surroundings falls. A useful memory hook: EXO = energy EXits; ENDO = energy ENters.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Exothermic Reactions in Everyday Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Combustion is the most familiar exothermic reaction &mdash; burning methane in a gas boiler, for example, transfers energy to the water in a central heating system. Neutralisation reactions (acid + alkali) are also exothermic; you can feel the flask warm up. Hand warmers that contain iron powder work by an exothermic oxidation reaction: iron slowly reacts with oxygen from the air and releases heat.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Endothermic Reactions in Everyday Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction in which a compound is broken down into simpler substances by heating; it is an endothermic process.">Thermal decomposition</dfn> reactions require continuous heating to proceed because they absorb energy from the surroundings. For example, heating copper carbonate causes it to decompose:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$CuCO_3 \rightarrow CuO + CO_2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Sports injury cold packs are a familiar endothermic application: when the pack is activated, ammonium nitrate dissolves in water in an endothermic process, absorbing heat from the surroundings and cooling the injured area.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Activation Energy and Energy Profile Diagrams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Even an exothermic reaction needs an initial energy input to get started. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur. It is shown as the energy barrier on an energy profile diagram.">activation energy</dfn> &mdash; the energy needed to break existing bonds in the reactants so that new bonds can form in the products. Once a few bonds have been broken, the energy released by bond-making can sustain the reaction.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing the relative energy of reactants and products, with a peak representing the activation energy (energy barrier) that must be overcome for the reaction to proceed.">energy profile diagram</dfn> shows this graphically. The y-axis is energy; the x-axis is the progress of the reaction (sometimes called the reaction coordinate). The curve rises to a peak &mdash; the activation energy &mdash; before falling to the energy level of the products.</p>

A very common error is to draw the arrow from the y-axis or from the products &mdash; this scores no marks. Check: does your arrow begin on the reactant plateau and rise to the highest point on the curve?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">For an <strong>exothermic</strong> reaction: the products are at a <em>lower</em> energy level than the reactants. The overall energy change (ΔH) is negative &mdash; energy has been released. For an <strong>endothermic</strong> reaction: the products are at a <em>higher</em> energy level than the reactants. The overall energy change is positive &mdash; energy has been absorbed.</p>
  </div></div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and draw a labelled energy profile for an exothermic reaction from memory, then check: reactants higher than products, activation energy arrow starting at reactant level, peak labelled.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On an energy profile diagram, the activation energy arrow always starts at the energy level of the <em>reactants</em> and points up to the peak. This is the most frequently penalised error in exams. Products below reactants = exothermic; products above reactants = endothermic.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Bond Breaking and Bond Making</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The energy changes in reactions are explained at the bond level. Breaking chemical bonds always requires energy input &mdash; it is an endothermic process. Forming new chemical bonds always releases energy &mdash; it is an exothermic process. The overall energy change of a reaction depends on the balance between these two.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">If the energy needed to break bonds in the reactants is <em>less</em> than the energy released when new bonds form in the products, the overall reaction is exothermic. If more energy is needed to break bonds than is released in making them, the reaction is endothermic.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Measuring Energy Changes in the Laboratory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A simple way to investigate whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic is to measure the temperature change of the solution. If you mix two chemicals in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A container made from expanded polystyrene foam used in school calorimetry experiments. It acts as a thermal insulator, reducing heat loss to the surroundings and giving more accurate temperature readings.">polystyrene cup</dfn>, the cup acts as a simple calorimeter. Recording the temperature before and after tells you the direction of energy transfer: a temperature rise confirms the reaction is exothermic; a fall confirms endothermic.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">In practice, some energy is always lost to the surroundings even with insulation. Using a lid on the cup, taking readings quickly, and using a digital thermometer all reduce this systematic error. Being aware of the limitations of your equipment and method is part of working scientifically &mdash; acknowledging sources of error does not undermine a result, but it does show you understand the difference between what you measured and the true value.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

    <span>Calculating Energy Changes Using Bond Energies (Higher)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy required to break one mole of a particular chemical bond, measured in kJ/mol.">bond energy</dfn> values are given in kJ/mol and represent the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond. To calculate the overall energy change:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">$$\Delta H = \text{Energy to break bonds in reactants} - \text{Energy released making bonds in products}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">A negative result means exothermic (energy released); a positive result means endothermic (energy absorbed). Always set out the calculation clearly: list each bond broken with its energy, total them; list each bond made with its energy, total them; then subtract.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Worked example:</strong> For \(H_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2HCl\). Bond energies: H&ndash;H = 436 kJ/mol; Cl&ndash;Cl = 242 kJ/mol; H&ndash;Cl = 432 kJ/mol.<br/>
    Energy to break bonds: 436 + 242 = 678 kJ/mol.<br/>
    Energy released making bonds: 2 &times; 432 = 864 kJ/mol.<br/>
    &Delta;H = 678 &minus; 864 = &minus;186 kJ/mol. Exothermic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings (temperature rises); endothermic reactions absorb energy from the surroundings (temperature falls). All reactions require an activation energy to begin.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">On an energy profile diagram, the activation energy arrow must start at the reactant energy level and rise to the peak of the curve &mdash; not from the y-axis or from the products.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Bond breaking always requires energy; bond making always releases energy. Whether a reaction is overall exothermic or endothermic depends on which process releases or absorbs more energy in total.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">The most common error on energy profile questions is drawing the activation energy arrow from the wrong starting point. Always start the arrow at the energy level of the <em>reactants</em>, not from the y-axis and not from the products. When asked to compare exothermic and endothermic profiles, comment explicitly on both the position of products relative to reactants <em>and</em> the size of the activation energy. In Higher-tier bond energy calculations, show every step: bonds broken (positive), bonds made (negative), then the difference &mdash; losing marks for missing units or skipped steps is avoidable.</p>
