--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the final scene delivers Orwell's devastating message and what the novella's cyclical structure means.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Pigs Walk on Two Legs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the final chapter, the pigs complete their transformation into the very creatures they overthrew. One morning, Clover neighs in alarm and the animals rush outside to see the pigs walking upright on their hind legs. Squealer walks on two legs. Napoleon emerges last, carrying a whip in his trotter. The sheep, retrained by Squealer, now bleat: <strong>“Four legs good, two legs better!”</strong></p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the exact transformation of the slogan: what did "Four legs good, two legs bad" become?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The transformation from “Four legs good, two legs bad” to “Four legs good, two legs <strong>better</strong>” is the novella’s single most powerful moment of political irony. The slogan that once defined the revolution is reversed to serve the new oppressors. Orwell shows that language itself can be weaponised to mean the exact opposite of its original intent.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The Seven Commandments have been erased entirely. In their place, a single commandment is painted on the barn wall: <strong>“All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others.”</strong> This <dfn class="term" data-def="A statement that contradicts itself, often revealing a deeper truth or absurdity.">paradox</dfn> encapsulates Orwell’s entire argument about how language is corrupted to justify inequality.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Final Scene — Pigs and Humans</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The novella ends with a dinner party in the farmhouse. Napoleon hosts a group of human farmers, including Mr Pilkington. They sit at the table, drink together, and congratulate each other. Pilkington praises Napoleon for running a farm where the animals do more work and eat less food than any other farm in the county.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Card Game — The Final Image</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Napoleon announces that Animal Farm will be renamed “Manor Farm” — its original name under Jones. Even the farm’s identity has been erased. The name change symbolises the <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where the story ends in a similar position to where it began, suggesting repetition and inevitability.">cyclical structure</dfn> of the novella — everything has returned to how it was before the revolution.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">During a game of cards, Napoleon and Pilkington both play an ace of spades simultaneously and accuse each other of cheating. A quarrel breaks out. The watching animals look from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again — <strong>“but already it was impossible to say which was which.”</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This final sentence is one of the most famous in English literature. Orwell delivers his thesis in a single image: the revolutionaries have become indistinguishable from the oppressors they replaced. The card game quarrel suggests that powerful figures, regardless of ideology, will always cheat and fight for dominance.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Cyclical Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Orwell deliberately structures the novella as a circle. The farm begins under human tyranny, passes through a brief period of hope, and returns to tyranny — only this time, the tyrants are pigs. This <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring sequence of events that returns to its starting point, suggesting that history repeats itself.">cyclical narrative</dfn> implies that revolutions are doomed to repeat the patterns they aimed to break, unless the underlying power structures are fundamentally changed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what Animal Farm was renamed to at the end and what this name change symbolized about the revolution.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The renaming of Animal Farm back to “Manor Farm” completes the cycle. The name “Animal Farm” represented the revolution’s promise of a new beginning. Its erasure means the revolution has been entirely undone — not by external enemies, but by the very animals who led it.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Orwell’s Moral — What Is the Lesson?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Orwell’s message is layered and can be read at several levels:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Political:</strong> The Russian Revolution was betrayed by Stalin, who replaced one form of tyranny with another. This is a specific critique of <dfn class="term" data-def="The political ideology and practices associated with Joseph Stalin, characterised by centralised authority, political repression, and a cult of personality.">Stalinism</dfn>, not of socialism itself.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Universal:</strong> Power corrupts. Any revolution that concentrates power in a small elite without democratic accountability will produce new oppressors. The cycle of tyranny, revolution, and new tyranny is a pattern throughout history.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>About language:</strong> Those who control language control thought. Propaganda, censorship, and the rewriting of history are the tools that enable oppression. Orwell believed that defending the integrity of language was a political act.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>About responsibility:</strong> The animals’ failure to resist — whether through blind loyalty (Boxer), cynical passivity (Benjamin), or inability to think critically (the sheep) — makes them complicit. Orwell suggests that ordinary people have a duty to stay informed and hold their leaders accountable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why the Ending Matters for the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The final scene is one of the most commonly examined passages. It works as a powerful conclusion to almost any essay question because it brings together all the novella’s major themes: power, corruption, propaganda, class, and the failure of revolution. Being able to analyse this scene in detail — with specific quotations and confident contextual links — will strengthen any response.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The pigs become indistinguishable from humans — the revolution has produced the very tyranny it was meant to destroy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The cyclical structure (Manor Farm → Animal Farm → Manor Farm) suggests that revolutions without democratic accountability are doomed to fail.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">“All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others” is Orwell’s devastating summary of how language is corrupted to justify inequality.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">The final line (“it was impossible to say which was which”) should appear in almost every essay you write on <em>Animal Farm</em>. It is the novella’s thesis statement. When quoting it, analyse the <strong>structure</strong> (why Orwell places it last), the <strong>language</strong> (the repetition of “pig to man, man to pig”), and the <strong>context</strong> (Stalin’s embrace of the same power structures as the Tsars). This single quotation can earn marks across all three Assessment Objectives.</p>
