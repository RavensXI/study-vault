--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Napoleon uses public executions and false confessions to terrorise the farm into submission.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Confessions and Executions (Chapter 7)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Chapter 7 is the darkest moment in <em>Animal Farm</em>. Napoleon orders all the animals to assemble in the yard, then calls forward four pigs who had previously questioned his decisions. With the dogs snarling beside him, Napoleon forces them to confess to being in secret contact with Snowball and plotting to sabotage the farm. The dogs immediately tear out their throats.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Then other animals step forward — hens, geese, a sheep — and confess to various crimes, all supposedly inspired by Snowball. Each <dfn class="term" data-def="A forced or fabricated admission of guilt, often obtained through threats, torture, or psychological pressure.">confession</dfn> is followed by execution. By the end, a pile of corpses lies at Napoleon’s feet, and the air is heavy with the smell of blood.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the three names for Stalin's purges, the years they occurred, and the estimated death toll.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The purges directly mirror Stalin’s Great Purge (1936–1938), also known as the Great Terror. Stalin held <dfn class="term" data-def="Public legal proceedings in which the verdict is predetermined and the accused are forced to confess, used by Stalin to eliminate political opponents.">show trials</dfn> where former allies were forced to confess to impossible crimes before being executed. An estimated 750,000 people were killed during the Great Purge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Sixth Commandment — Rewritten</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Sixth Commandment originally read: “No animal shall kill any other animal.” After the executions, the animals remember this rule and feel uneasy. When Clover asks Muriel to read the commandment from the barn wall, it now reads: “No animal shall kill any other animal <strong>without cause</strong>.” The two added words change the entire meaning.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Orwell uses the commandments as a structural device to track the corruption of Animalism. Each alteration marks a new betrayal:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">“No animal shall sleep in a bed” becomes “No animal shall sleep in a bed <strong>with sheets</strong>” — when the pigs move into the farmhouse.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">“No animal shall drink alcohol” becomes “No animal shall drink alcohol <strong>to excess</strong>” — when Napoleon discovers whisky.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">“No animal shall kill any other animal” becomes “No animal shall kill any other animal <strong>without cause</strong>” — after the purges.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The animals think they must have misremembered the original wording. Orwell shows how <dfn class="term" data-def="The psychological experience of holding two contradictory beliefs simultaneously and accepting both.">cognitive dissonance</dfn> enables oppression — rather than accept that their leaders are lying, the animals doubt their own memories.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Clover’s Silent Despair</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">After the executions, Clover climbs the hill overlooking the farm. Orwell describes her looking out over the landscape that “ought to have been a scene of tranquillity.” She tries to marshal her thoughts but cannot find the words to express what she feels. She knows this is not what Old Major envisioned, but she cannot articulate why or how things went wrong.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall Clover's exact words about protesting and explain what Orwell's point about language absence represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Clover’s scene on the hillside is one of the most poignant in the novella. Orwell deliberately denies her the ability to speak — she “would have protested if she could have found the right words.” This represents how ordinary people can sense injustice but, without education and political language, cannot challenge it. The absence of words is itself Orwell’s point about the power of language.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Do the Animals Confess?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The confessions are one of the most disturbing aspects of the novella. Some animals may genuinely believe they have done wrong, having internalised the propaganda. Others may confess out of terror — they have watched others torn apart by the dogs and know resistance is futile.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Orwell leaves this ambiguous, mirroring the genuine uncertainty about why people confessed during Stalin’s show trials. Historians debate whether victims were tortured, threatened, ideologically broken, or simply desperate. Orwell captures all of these possibilities.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The hens who confess had previously staged a small rebellion, refusing to hand over their eggs for trade. Napoleon starved them into submission. Their “crime” was resisting an unjust order — but under Napoleon’s regime, any dissent is punishable by death.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Banning of “Beasts of England”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">After the purges, Napoleon bans <em>“Beasts of England.”</em> Squealer explains that the song is no longer necessary because the Rebellion is complete and the better society it promised has been achieved. A new anthem praising Napoleon replaces it. This is deeply ironic — the song represented hope for liberation, and its removal signals that hope’s death. The revolution has consumed its own ideals.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The purges mirror Stalin’s Great Terror — forced confessions and public executions used to eliminate dissent and create fear.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The commandments are rewritten to justify the pigs’ actions — each change marks a new stage in the corruption of Animalism.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Clover’s inability to articulate her despair represents how the absence of political language prevents resistance.</li>
</ul>
