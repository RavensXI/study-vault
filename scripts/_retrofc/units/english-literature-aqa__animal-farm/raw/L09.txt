--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Boxer's fate represents the ultimate betrayal of the working class and Orwell's most emotional moment.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Boxer’s Collapse</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Boxer has worked himself to exhaustion rebuilding the windmill for the second time. He is now twelve years old — past the retirement age he was promised under Animalism. One day, while hauling stone, his lungs give out and he collapses. He cannot get up. The other animals are horrified, especially <dfn class="term" data-def="A gentle, maternal cart-horse who senses the revolution has been betrayed but cannot articulate her feelings.">Clover</dfn>, who has watched Boxer’s decline with growing concern.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Boxer is philosophical about his injury. He says he had hoped to see the windmill finished before his retirement. He looks forward to spending his remaining years in the pasture that Old Major had promised all retiring animals would enjoy. The reader, of course, already senses that this promise will be broken.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what age Boxer was promised retirement and what this promise represented about the social contract.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Boxer was promised retirement at the age of twelve — a pension for loyal service. This promise represents the social contract between workers and the state: work hard, and you will be looked after. Napoleon’s betrayal of this promise is Orwell’s most damning indictment of the Soviet system.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Knacker’s Van</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Napoleon announces that Boxer will be sent to a veterinary hospital in Willingdon. A van arrives to collect him. But Benjamin, the cynical donkey who can read, notices the writing on the side: <strong>“Alfred Simmonds, Horse Slaughterer and Glue Boiler.”</strong> For the first time in the novella, Benjamin breaks his silence and screams for the animals to stop the van.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Most Devastating Scene in the Novella</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Clover cries out: “Boxer! Boxer! Boxer! Get out! Get out quickly! They’re taking you to your death!” Boxer tries to kick his way out of the van, but his strength has gone. The van drives away while the animals chase it helplessly.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">This is the emotional climax of <em>Animal Farm</em>. Orwell strips away all allegory and political symbolism — what the reader experiences is raw grief and injustice. The most loyal, hardworking creature on the farm is sold to a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who buys old or injured horses and slaughters them to make glue, leather, and other products.">knacker</dfn> for profit.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Squealer later claims Boxer died peacefully at the hospital, with the words “Long live Comrade Napoleon” on his lips. He explains that the van had previously belonged to a knacker but was now owned by a vet who hadn’t repainted it. The animals, exhausted and defeated, accept this obvious lie.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what Napoleon bought with the money from selling Boxer and why this creates savage irony in the novella.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Orwell reveals that Napoleon used the money from selling Boxer to the knacker to buy a case of whisky. This detail is the novella’s most savage moment of irony — a lifetime of loyal service literally converted into alcohol for the pigs.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Benjamin’s Silence and Its Meaning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="An old donkey on the farm who is intelligent and literate but refuses to use his knowledge to resist, representing cynical intellectuals.">Benjamin</dfn> is the only animal besides the pigs who can read fluently, and the only one who has never been fooled by Squealer’s propaganda. His consistent refusal to act — his belief that “life would go on as it had always gone on — that is, badly” — makes him a deeply ambiguous figure.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Is Benjamin Guilty?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Benjamin only acts when it is too late to save Boxer. Orwell seems to suggest that his silence throughout the novella makes him complicit in the pigs’ crimes. He has the intelligence to understand what is happening and the literacy to prove the commandments are being changed, but he does nothing.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Orwell uses Benjamin to criticise <dfn class="term" data-def="A belief that events are determined by forces beyond one's control, leading to a passive acceptance of all outcomes.">fatalism</dfn> — the intellectual’s temptation to shrug and say “it was always going to happen.” By refusing to engage, Benjamin allows the pigs to act without challenge. His guilt is different from Boxer’s — Boxer is too trusting, but Benjamin is too cynical.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Together, Boxer and Benjamin represent two failures of the ordinary citizen: blind loyalty and passive cynicism. Orwell suggests that both enable tyranny, and that the only defence against dictatorship is informed, active engagement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Orwell’s Emotional Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Boxer’s death is designed to make readers feel genuine anger. Orwell has spent the entire novella building sympathy for Boxer — his loyalty, his gentleness, his simple decency — only to show that these qualities are rewarded with betrayal. The emotional impact forces readers to engage with the political argument: if this makes you angry, Orwell implies, then you should be angry about what happened to the Soviet working class under Stalin.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Boxer is sold to the knacker — his lifetime of loyalty is literally converted into whisky for the pigs, representing the ultimate betrayal of the working class.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Benjamin’s silence throughout the novella makes him complicit — Orwell criticises both blind loyalty (Boxer) and cynical passivity (Benjamin).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Boxer’s death is the emotional climax, designed to convert sympathy into political anger at the system that allowed it.</li>
</ul>

By this point, readers have seen every other betrayal — Boxer’s death is the culmination, not just an event. Linking this structural awareness to your analysis earns top AO2 marks.</p>
