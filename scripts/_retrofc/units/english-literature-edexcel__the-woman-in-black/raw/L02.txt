<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Frame Narrative: Christmas Eve</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with Arthur Kipps as an older man, sitting with his family on Christmas Eve. His stepchildren are telling ghost stories by the fire, enjoying the thrill of the supernatural as entertainment. But Kipps cannot join in. He knows that ghost stories are not games — he has experienced something so devastating that even decades later, the memory fills him with dread. Hill establishes from the very first page that this story will not end well. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique in which events yet to come are hinted at early in the story, creating tension and a sense of inevitability.">foreshadowing</dfn> is unmistakable: something terrible happened to Kipps, and the act of writing it down is both a confession and a warning.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Kipps decides to write his account rather than speak it aloud. This is significant. Writing gives him control over the narrative — he can choose what to reveal and when. But it also creates a distance between the reader and the events, filtering everything through the lens of a man shaped by decades of trauma. Hill uses the older Kipps’s voice to create a tone of quiet dread: he knows what is coming, even when his younger self does not.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what two versions of Kipps exist in the novel, and how does the gap between them create dramatic irony?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator who tells the story looking back on events from a later point in time, often with greater understanding but also shaped by the emotional impact of those events.">retrospective narrator</dfn> creates dramatic irony throughout the novel. The older Kipps knows the full horror of what happened, so his account of his younger self’s confidence and courage is saturated with the knowledge that it will all be destroyed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Arthur Kipps: The Young Solicitor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When we first meet the young Arthur Kipps, he is a junior solicitor sent by his firm to attend the funeral of Mrs Alice Drablow and sort through her papers at Eel Marsh House. He is rational, confident, and eager to prove himself: “I was young, I was sure of myself.” He treats the assignment as a routine professional task and is mildly irritated by the journey to the remote town of Crythin Gifford. Hill presents the young Kipps as the archetypal <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who approaches events with logic and reason, dismissing the supernatural, which makes their eventual encounter with the inexplicable more powerful.">rational protagonist</dfn> of the ghost story tradition — a sensible, educated man who does not believe in ghosts and is therefore completely unprepared for what he will face.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Kipps’s journey north from London is described in terms that gradually shift from comfort to unease. The train carries him away from the familiar world of the city into an increasingly bleak, flat, and featureless landscape. The marshes, the grey sky, and the biting wind all serve as <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique where elements of weather, landscape, or natural environment reflect or intensify the emotional state of characters or the mood of a scene.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>, foreshadowing the horror that awaits. By the time Kipps arrives in Crythin Gifford, the reader already senses that something is wrong, even though Kipps himself remains oblivious.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">From the moment Kipps arrives, the townspeople behave strangely. Mr Jerome, the local land agent, becomes visibly distressed when Kipps mentions Eel Marsh House. Sam Daily, a friendly local businessman, offers veiled warnings but refuses to be specific. The landlord at the inn changes the subject whenever Kipps asks about Mrs Drablow. Hill uses these reactions to build suspense: the reader knows the town is hiding something, but the young Kipps interprets their behaviour as mere superstition. “They have learned to live with it,” Sam Daily eventually tells Kipps — a chilling admission that the entire community knows about the supernatural threat but has collectively decided to endure it in silence rather than confront it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This wall of silence serves a dual purpose. Structurally, it delays the revelation and builds tension. Thematically, it reflects the idea that communities can be complicit in suffering by choosing not to speak. The people of Crythin Gifford are not evil — they are afraid. But their fear makes them complicit in every death the Woman in Black causes, because they never warn outsiders like Kipps.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Mrs Drablow’s Funeral</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The funeral of Mrs Alice Drablow is one of the novel’s most important early scenes. Kipps notices that almost nobody has come — the church is nearly empty, which tells the reader that Mrs Drablow was feared or avoided by the community. During the service, Kipps sees a woman he does not recognise: “a tall woman, dressed in deepest black,” with “a wasted face.” Her skin seems stretched over her bones, and her expression radiates a terrible mixture of suffering and malice.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where does Kipps first see the Woman in Black, and what specific words describe her appearance?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>At Mrs Drablow’s funeral, Kipps sees the Woman in Black for the first time: “a tall woman, dressed in deepest black” with “a wasted face.” This first sighting establishes the ghost’s appearance and the atmosphere of death and decay that surrounds her. The word “wasted” suggests both physical deterioration and a life consumed by grief and bitterness.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The First Sighting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">After the funeral, Kipps sees the woman again in the churchyard, standing among the graves. He is unsettled by her appearance but assumes she is simply a mourner. He mentions her to Mr Jerome, whose reaction is immediate and terrifying: Jerome’s face drains of colour, his hands shake, and he is unable to speak. This is the first moment where Kipps begins to realise that something is genuinely wrong. Jerome’s physical reaction — described in visceral detail by Hill — is more frightening than any ghost, because it shows a rational adult reduced to speechless terror by a single mention of what Kipps has seen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Hill’s handling of this scene demonstrates her skill with <dfn class="term" data-def="The technique of implying horror through other characters’ reactions, partial information, and what is NOT said, rather than showing the horror directly.">indirect horror</dfn>. The ghost herself does nothing violent or dramatic in this scene. The terror comes entirely from the reactions of the living — from Jerome’s speechless fear and the community’s refusal to explain. Hill understands that what the reader imagines is always more frightening than what is shown directly.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Kipps’s Rationality Under Threat</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Throughout these early chapters, Kipps attempts to rationalise everything he encounters. He explains away the townspeople’s behaviour as local superstition. He assumes the woman at the funeral is simply a reclusive mourner. He tells himself there is nothing to fear. This rational resistance is a classic feature of the ghost story genre — the protagonist must first exhaust every logical explanation before accepting the supernatural. Hill uses Kipps’s rationality to create tension: the reader can see the danger building, but Kipps refuses to acknowledge it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The dramatic irony is intensified by the retrospective narration. The older Kipps, writing years later, knows exactly how foolish his younger self’s confidence was. Phrases like “I had not the least idea” and “how little I understood” remind the reader that this confident young man is walking into a trap, and the narrator knows it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The retrospective narration creates dramatic irony: the older Kipps knows the horror that awaits, making every moment of his younger self’s confidence deeply unsettling.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The first sighting of the Woman in Black at the funeral — “a wasted face” and “deepest black” — establishes her as a figure of death, grief, and malice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The community’s silence (Jerome’s terror, Sam Daily’s veiled warnings) builds suspense through indirect horror — the reactions of the living are as terrifying as the ghost herself.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When analysing Kipps as a narrator, consider the gap between his younger and older selves. The younger Kipps is confident and rational; the older Kipps is traumatised and reflective. This dual perspective creates dramatic irony — always a strong AO1 point. For AO3, link Kipps’s rational confidence to Victorian values of reason and self-control, and show how Hill systematically destroys that confidence to make a point about the limits of rationality when confronted with the supernatural.</p>
