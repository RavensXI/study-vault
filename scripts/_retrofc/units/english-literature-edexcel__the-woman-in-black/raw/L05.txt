<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Arthur Kipps: Young and Old</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Arthur Kipps is the novel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The main character through whose eyes the story is told and experienced, and whose journey forms the central narrative arc.">protagonist</dfn> and narrator. Hill presents him in two distinct versions. The young Kipps is a junior solicitor: rational, confident, professionally ambitious, and dismissive of superstition. He approaches his assignment at Eel Marsh House as a routine task and is irritated rather than frightened by the townspeople’s strange behaviour. “I was young, I was sure of myself,” he tells the reader. This confidence is not arrogance — it is the natural assurance of a young man who believes the world operates according to reason and logic.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The older Kipps, who narrates the story decades later, is a fundamentally different man. He has been broken by his experience. His wife and child are dead. He cannot speak about what happened and can barely bring himself to write about it. The gap between the two versions of Kipps creates the novel’s pervasive <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience or reader knows something that a character does not, creating tension, sympathy, or a sense of tragic inevitability.">dramatic irony</dfn>: every moment of the young Kipps’s courage and confidence is undercut by the reader’s knowledge that it will all end in devastation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the two versions of Kipps and explain what the gap between them shows about the supernatural's impact.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Kipps functions as two characters: the confident young solicitor and the traumatised older narrator. Hill uses the gap between them to create dramatic irony and to show that the supernatural does not just frighten — it permanently destroys. Kipps’s transformation is the novel’s central tragedy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Jennet Humfrye: Victim and Villain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jennet Humfrye is the Woman in Black. She was an unmarried mother in Victorian England whose son Nathaniel was taken from her and given to her sister, Mrs Drablow. When Nathaniel was killed in the pony and trap accident on the marshes, Jennet’s grief transformed into a supernatural rage that persists beyond death. She is described as “a tall woman, dressed in deepest black” with “a wasted face” — her appearance embodies the extremity of her mourning and the consuming nature of her grief.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Hill presents Jennet with deliberate <dfn class="term" data-def="The inclusion of contradictory or conflicting qualities in a character, forcing the reader to hold multiple responses simultaneously rather than making a simple judgement.">moral complexity</dfn>. She is a genuine victim of Victorian injustice: a woman stripped of her child by a patriarchal society that punished her for having a baby outside marriage. Her letters reveal desperation, love, and fury. But her curse is monstrous — she kills innocent children who had nothing to do with her suffering. Hill refuses to let the reader simply condemn or pity Jennet. She is both at once: the wronged mother and the child-killer, the victim who became the monster.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Sam Daily is a local landowner and businessman who befriends Kipps. He is practical, kind, and fundamentally honest — the most sympathetic adult character in the novel apart from Kipps himself. Unlike the other townspeople, Sam does not completely avoid the subject of the Woman in Black. He offers veiled warnings, lends Kipps his dog Spider for company, and eventually reveals the full truth of the curse. But even Sam cannot protect Kipps from what is coming.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Sam’s character serves as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who highlights the qualities of another character by contrast, making those qualities more visible to the reader.">foil</dfn> to Mr Jerome. Where Jerome is paralysed by fear, Sam is pragmatic and courageous enough to speak. But Hill uses Sam to make a crucial point: knowledge of the danger is not enough to prevent it. Sam knows everything about the Woman in Black, yet he cannot stop the curse. This reinforces the novel’s bleak message that some evils cannot be reasoned with, understood, or defeated.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Mr Jerome: Fear Made Visible</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Mr Jerome is Mrs Drablow’s land agent in Crythin Gifford. His most significant moment comes when Kipps mentions the woman he saw at the funeral. Jerome’s reaction is extreme and physical: his face drains of colour, his body trembles, and he is unable to speak. Hill uses Jerome as a device to show the reader how deep the community’s terror runs. Jerome is not a coward — he is a competent professional reduced to speechless horror by the mere mention of what Kipps has seen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Jerome also demonstrates the way the community’s silence perpetuates the cycle of death. He knows the truth but cannot bring himself to tell Kipps. His fear prevents him from issuing the warning that might save lives. In this way, Jerome represents the wider community of Crythin Gifford: well-meaning people whose fear makes them complicit in the ongoing tragedy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Spider: Loyalty and Vulnerability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Spider, Sam Daily’s small terrier, is often overlooked in character analysis, but she plays a vital role. She provides Kipps with companionship during his terrifying nights at Eel Marsh House. Her instinctive reactions to the supernatural — cowering, whimpering, refusing to enter certain rooms — validate the haunting as objectively real rather than a product of Kipps’s imagination. Animals were traditionally believed to sense the supernatural, and Hill uses this convention to confirm the ghost’s existence.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Stella: The Innocent Victim</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Stella is Kipps’s wife. She appears only briefly in the novel, but her death — along with their baby’s — is the culmination of the entire narrative. Stella represents innocence, normality, and the possibility of a happy life after the trauma of Eel Marsh House. By killing her and the baby, Hill destroys every hope the reader might have held for Kipps’s recovery. Stella’s death also creates a devastating parallel with Jennet: Kipps, like Jennet, loses the person he loves most. The cycle of grief repeats.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Hill’s decision to keep Stella almost entirely off the page until the final chapter is a structural choice. The reader barely knows her, which might seem to reduce the emotional impact. But the effect is the opposite: because Stella represents the entire future Kipps will never have — the life, the family, the happiness — her loss is felt as the destruction of a possibility rather than a person. This is arguably more devastating because it is abstract: everything Kipps might have been is taken away in a single moment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: how are Jennet and Kipps structural parallels? What do they both lose, and how do they each respond?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jennet and Kipps are structural parallels. Both lose a child. Both are destroyed by the experience. The difference is that Jennet’s grief becomes a supernatural curse, while Kipps’s grief becomes a lifetime of silent suffering. Hill uses this parallel to show that grief, not the ghost, is the novel’s true subject.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Kipps exists as two characters — the confident young solicitor and the broken older narrator — and the gap between them creates the novel’s dramatic irony and central tragedy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Jennet Humfrye is presented with moral complexity: a genuine victim of Victorian injustice whose grief has transformed into a monstrous, indiscriminate curse against innocent children.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Jennet and Kipps are structural parallels — both lose a child and are destroyed by grief — showing that loss and its consequences are the novel’s true subject.</li></ul>
