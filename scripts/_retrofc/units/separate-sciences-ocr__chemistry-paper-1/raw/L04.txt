<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pure Substances in Chemistry</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In everyday language, &ldquo;pure&rdquo; often means natural or uncontaminated. In chemistry, the meaning is much more specific: a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that consists of only one type of element or compound, with nothing else mixed in.">pure substance</dfn> contains only a single element or a single compound, with nothing else mixed in. Pure water contains only \(H_2O\) molecules. Orange juice labelled &ldquo;pure&rdquo; in a supermarket is <em>not</em> pure in the chemical sense, because it is a mixture of water, sugars, citric acid and other compounds. This distinction between the everyday and scientific meanings of &ldquo;pure&rdquo; is regularly tested.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">You can test purity by measuring <strong>melting point</strong> or <strong>boiling point</strong>. A pure substance melts and boils at a single sharp temperature. An impure substance (a mixture) melts over a <em>range</em> of temperatures, and its measured melting point will be lower than the expected value for the pure substance. Similarly, mixtures boil over a range of temperatures rather than at a single point.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the scientific definition of a pure substance, then explain how you would use melting point data to determine whether a sample is pure.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In chemistry, <strong>pure</strong> = only one element or one compound. A pure substance has a sharp, fixed melting and boiling point. An impure substance melts and boils over a range of temperatures, and the melting point is depressed below the expected value.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Formulations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Many useful products are <dfn class="term" data-def="A mixture that has been designed as a useful product, with each component present in a carefully measured proportion to give the product specific properties.">formulations</dfn> &mdash; carefully designed mixtures where each component is present in a precise amount. Formulations include alloys (e.g. steel: iron with a small proportion of carbon and sometimes other metals), paints (pigment, binder and solvent in defined ratios), medicines (active ingredient combined with fillers and stabilisers), fuels (blends of hydrocarbons), and foods such as sauces and dressings. Each component contributes specific properties to the final product, and changing the proportions changes the product&rsquo;s behaviour.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Filtration and Crystallisation (PAG C2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. The mixture is poured through filter paper in a funnel. The liquid (the <strong>filtrate</strong>) passes through the tiny pores in the paper, while the insoluble solid (the <strong>residue</strong>) is trapped. For example, you could separate sand from salt water using filtration &mdash; the sand stays on the paper and the salt water collects in the flask below. This is a key technique in practical activity group PAG C2.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">Crystallisation separates a dissolved solid (solute) from a solution. Heat the solution gently to evaporate most of the solvent until crystals just begin to form. Remove from heat and allow to cool slowly &mdash; as the temperature drops, more solute comes out of solution and grows into larger, purer crystals. Filter the crystals and leave them to dry. Slow cooling produces larger, purer crystals than rapid cooling. This technique is used to produce pure, dry samples of soluble salts such as copper sulfate or potassium nitrate.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Distillation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A separation technique in which a liquid is heated to form a vapour, which is then cooled and condensed to collect a purer liquid.">Simple distillation</dfn> separates a solvent from a solution when the solvent alone is wanted as the pure product &mdash; for example, collecting pure water from salt water. The solution is heated until the solvent evaporates, the vapour passes through a condenser where it is cooled, and it collects as liquid in a separate container. The dissolved solid remains in the flask.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating a mixture of liquids with different boiling points, using a fractionating column to improve separation.">Fractional distillation</dfn> is used when the mixture contains two or more liquids with <strong>different boiling points</strong>. The mixture is heated in a flask fitted with a fractionating column. Liquids with lower boiling points vaporise first, rise to the top of the column, and are collected separately. As the temperature is increased, fractions with progressively higher boiling points are collected. A familiar application is the separation of crude oil into useful fractions (petrol, kerosene, diesel, etc.) in a refinery.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Chromatography (PAG C3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating dissolved substances based on how strongly each substance is attracted to the stationary phase compared to the mobile phase.">Chromatography</dfn> separates a mixture of dissolved substances. It works because different substances travel at different speeds through a <strong>stationary phase</strong> (such as chromatography paper) when carried by a <strong>mobile phase</strong> (a solvent). In paper chromatography (PAG C3):</p>
<ol data-narration-id="n15">
  <li>Draw a pencil baseline near the bottom of the chromatography paper (pencil is used because ink would dissolve and move up with the solvent).</li>
  <li>Place a small spot of the mixture on the baseline.</li>
  <li>Place the paper in a beaker of solvent, making sure the baseline is <em>above</em> the solvent level so the spots do not dissolve directly.</li>
  <li>Allow the solvent to rise up the paper, carrying the different substances with it.</li>
  <li>Mark the solvent front before it evaporates.</li>
</ol>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Substances that are more attracted to the mobile phase (or less attracted to the stationary phase) travel further up the paper. A pure substance produces a single spot; a mixture produces several spots at different heights. The chromatogram can be used to identify substances by comparing spot positions to those of known reference substances run alongside.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the formula for Rf value. What does an Rf value of 1 mean, and what does a value of 0 mean?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="A ratio used to identify a substance in chromatography, calculated as the distance moved by the substance divided by the distance moved by the solvent front.">R<sub>f</sub> value</dfn> identifies a substance and is calculated as:</p>
  <p>$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$</p>
  <p>R<sub>f</sub> values are always between 0 and 1. A pure substance gives a single spot with a characteristic R<sub>f</sub> value for a given solvent and stationary phase. A mixture gives multiple spots.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">A chromatography experiment produces a spot that travels 4.8 cm from the baseline. The solvent front travels 8.0 cm from the baseline. Calculate the R<sub>f</sub> value.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Identify the values. Distance moved by substance = 4.8 cm. Distance moved by solvent front = 8.0 cm.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Apply the formula. $$R_f = \frac{4.8}{8.0} = 0.60$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Check the answer is between 0 and 1. \(R_f = 0.60\) &mdash; this is correct. Note that R<sub>f</sub> values have no units. If you obtain a value greater than 1, you have divided the wrong way round.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">A pure substance (scientific definition) contains only one element or compound, and melts/boils at a single sharp temperature. Formulations are designed mixtures with components in precise proportions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Separation techniques depend on the type of mixture: filtration (insoluble solid from liquid), crystallisation (dissolved solid from solution), simple distillation (solvent from solution), fractional distillation (liquids with different boiling points), chromatography (dissolved substances with different affinities for mobile and stationary phases).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">In chromatography, the R<sub>f</sub> value = distance moved by substance &divide; distance moved by solvent front. It is always between 0 and 1 and identifies substances. A pure substance gives a single spot.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">In practical questions about chromatography, watch for two common errors: (1) saying the baseline is drawn in ink &mdash; it must be <em>pencil</em> so the mark does not move; (2) mixing up what travels furthest (substances most attracted to mobile phase, not the stationary phase). For R<sub>f</sub> calculations, always divide distance moved by substance by distance moved by solvent front &mdash; never the other way round. In &lsquo;describe&rsquo; questions about separation techniques, name the technique, state what state of matter it separates, and briefly explain the physical principle (e.g. &ldquo;different boiling points&rdquo; for distillation, &ldquo;insoluble solid trapped by filter paper&rdquo; for filtration).</p>
