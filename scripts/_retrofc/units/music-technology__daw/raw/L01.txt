<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Hardware for Digital Audio Workstations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Digital Audio Workstation — software used to record, edit and produce music on a computer.">DAW</dfn> is a software application for recording, editing and producing music. But the software is only part of the picture — it relies on a range of hardware components to function. This lesson covers every piece of hardware you need to know for the exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Computer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">At the centre of any DAW setup is a computer. The key specifications that affect performance are:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Operating System (OS)</strong> — Windows or macOS. Some DAWs are platform-specific (e.g. Logic Pro is macOS only).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>RAM</strong> (Random Access Memory) — more RAM allows you to run more tracks, plug-ins and virtual instruments simultaneously. 16 GB is a common minimum for music production.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Hard drives</strong> — SSDs (Solid State Drives) are faster than HDDs (Hard Disk Drives) and reduce load times. Audio projects can be very large, so storage capacity matters.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Processor (CPU)</strong> — a faster processor handles more real-time audio processing, effects and virtual instruments without stuttering.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Display</strong> — a large, high-resolution screen makes it easier to work with complex arrangements, mixer views and plug-in interfaces.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: what are the five key computer specifications for DAW use?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five key computer specifications for DAW work are: operating system, RAM, hard drives (SSD preferred), processor (CPU) and display. Each affects performance, speed and workflow.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Peripherals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Peripherals are external devices that connect to the computer to extend its capabilities.</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>External storage</strong> — USB drives, external SSDs, cloud storage and network drives provide additional space for backing up projects and storing sample libraries.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Data input devices</strong> — keyboard, mouse, trackpad and touchscreen are used to navigate the DAW, edit MIDI, draw automation and control parameters.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Audio output</strong> — studio <dfn class="term" data-def="Headphones or speakers designed to reproduce audio accurately without adding colouration, used for mixing and mastering.">monitors</dfn> (speakers) and headphones are essential for hearing the audio accurately. Studio monitors are designed for flat, uncoloured playback so you can make reliable mixing decisions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Portable devices</strong> — tablets and smartphones can control DAW functions remotely or run lightweight music apps.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">MIDI Controllers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="A hardware device that sends MIDI data to a DAW or synthesiser, allowing hands-on control of software instruments and parameters.">MIDI controllers</dfn> are hardware devices that send MIDI data to the DAW. They don’t produce sound themselves — they trigger sounds in software instruments. Types include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Keyboard controllers</strong> — piano-style keys for playing melodies, chords and bass lines.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Guitar controllers</strong> — guitar-shaped devices that send MIDI from string input.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Percussion controllers</strong> — pads that are struck like drums to trigger samples and beats.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Wind controllers</strong> — blown like woodwind instruments to control breath-sensitive virtual instruments.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Control surfaces</strong> — devices with <dfn class="term" data-def="Physical sliding controls on a mixing desk or control surface used to adjust volume levels.">faders</dfn>, knobs, pads and data wheels that mimic a mixing desk for hands-on control. <strong>DJ controllers</strong> include jog wheels, crossfaders and performance pads for live mixing.</li>
</ul>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A hardware device that converts analogue audio signals to digital data (and vice versa) so a computer can record and play back sound.">audio interface</dfn> is the bridge between the analogue world of microphones and instruments and the digital world of the DAW. It converts analogue signals to digital (for recording) and digital signals to analogue (for playback through speakers or headphones).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Input types:</strong></p>
    <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Line inputs</strong> — accept signals from devices like keyboards, drum machines and playback equipment. Connector types include RCA phono, quarter-inch jack, mini jack and stereo quarter-inch jack.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Microphone inputs</strong> — accept signals from microphones via XLR, USB or quarter-inch connectors.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Instrument input (DI)</strong> — a <dfn class="term" data-def="Direct Input — a high-impedance input designed for plugging instruments like electric guitars and basses directly into a recording device.">DI</dfn> (Direct Input) designed for instruments like electric guitar and bass, which have high-impedance signals.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Output types:</strong> Audio interfaces provide both balanced and unbalanced outputs. <dfn class="term" data-def="An audio connection that uses three conductors to cancel out noise, producing a cleaner signal over longer cable runs.">Balanced outputs</dfn> use three conductors to cancel electrical noise, making them essential for professional studio monitors.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Interfaces also provide <strong>phantom power</strong> (+48V) to power condenser microphones and <strong>gain control</strong> to adjust the input signal level.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>MIDI Interface & Mixing Desk</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A hardware device that routes MIDI data between instruments, controllers and computers via IN, OUT and THRU ports.">MIDI interface</dfn> connects MIDI devices to a computer. It has three port types: <strong>IN</strong> (receives data from a controller), <strong>OUT</strong> (sends data to a sound module) and <strong>THRU</strong> (passes through received data to chain devices). Connectors are typically 5-pin DIN or USB.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that combines, routes and processes multiple audio signals, allowing control of volume, panning and effects for each channel.">mixing desk</dfn> (also called a mixer or mixing console) combines and processes multiple audio signals. Mixing desks come in three architectures:</p>
    <ul>
    <li data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Analogue</strong> — processes audio entirely in the analogue domain using physical circuits.</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Digital</strong> — converts audio to digital and processes it using DSP (Digital Signal Processing).</li>
    <li data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Hybrid</strong> — combines analogue signal paths with digital control and recall.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n36">A mixing desk provides audio inputs and outputs, can serve as a DAW interface (sending audio to and from the computer) and doubles as a control surface with physical faders and knobs.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n37" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the three MIDI port types and explain what each does.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A MIDI interface has three ports: IN (receives data), OUT (sends data) and THRU (passes data through to chain devices). Connectors are 5-pin DIN or USB.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n39">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n40">A DAW computer needs adequate RAM, a fast CPU, SSD storage and a good display.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n41">MIDI controllers (keyboard, guitar, percussion, wind, control surface) send data to trigger software instruments — they don’t produce sound.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">An audio interface converts between analogue and digital signals. Key features include line/mic/DI inputs, balanced outputs, phantom power and gain control.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n38">Hardware questions often test whether you can identify the correct connector or device for a given scenario. For example, “What type of input would you use to connect an electric guitar directly to an audio interface?” (Answer: DI/instrument input). Learn the connector types (XLR for mics, quarter-inch for line/instruments, RCA for consumer audio, DIN for MIDI) and match them to their uses.</p>
