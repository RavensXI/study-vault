<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Films, Two Constrained Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Wadjda</em> (Haifaa Al-Mansour, 2012) and <em>Girlhood</em> (C&eacute;line Sciamma, 2014) share a central representational project: showing young women navigating worlds in which their freedom of movement, self-expression and identity are constrained by gender, culture and economic circumstance. Both films use specific filmic choices to represent their protagonists as subjects with agency rather than passive objects of social pressure. Understanding how each film constructs <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability or power of an individual to act independently, make choices, and influence their own circumstances, as represented through filmic choices.">agency</dfn> through framing, camera placement, music and editing is the core analytical task for this lesson.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe, from memory, one filmic choice that gives Wadjda agency in the film, and one that gives Marieme agency. Name the sequence each comes from.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>Wadjda</em> (2012): directed by Haifaa Al-Mansour; shot in Riyadh; cinematographer Lutz Reitemeier; score by Max Richter; made for approximately three million euros. <em>Girlhood</em> (2014): directed by C&eacute;line Sciamma; shot in the Paris suburbs; cinematographer Crystel Fournier; score by Para One. Both films were made by women directors, which is a significant AO1 context for their representation of young women.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Framing as Enclosure: <em>Wadjda</em></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Al-Mansour&rsquo;s most consistently used formal strategy is framing female characters through doorways, windows and corridors &mdash; architectural features that function as visual metaphors for the social restrictions placed on women and girls in the film&rsquo;s setting. In the film&rsquo;s early sequences, Wadjda&rsquo;s mother is frequently seen at the threshold of rooms rather than at their centre: standing in doorways, looking out through window grilles, positioned at the edges of frames. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A compositional technique in which the main subject is seen through or within another frame inside the shot, such as a doorway, window or archway, directing the spectator's attention and adding visual meaning.">frame-within-a-frame</dfn> composition makes enclosure visible. But Al-Mansour reserves a different visual treatment for Wadjda herself: the ten-year-old is often filmed in open space &mdash; the school corridor, the street &mdash; with room to move in the frame. Her <dfn class="term" data-def="A shot taken from a camera position close to the ground, looking upward, which typically makes the subject appear larger, more powerful or more significant.">low-angle shot</dfn> perspective, frequently adopted when the camera takes Wadjda&rsquo;s point of view, gives her visual authority over the spaces she occupies.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Named Sequence: The Toy-Shop Bicycle Reveal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In the toy-shop bicycle reveal, Wadjda first sees the green bicycle that becomes the film&rsquo;s central symbol. Al-Mansour cuts from a medium shot of Wadjda walking past the shop to a point-of-view shot through the shop window: the bicycle sits on a display stand, lit by the warm overhead shop lighting, a rack-focus pulling it from blur to crisp sharpness as Wadjda&rsquo;s gaze settles. The bicycle is introduced visually before it is named by any character. This is a precise micro choice: the spectator receives the visual information through Wadjda&rsquo;s looking &mdash; her subjectivity governs what we see and when we see it. The bicycle is not a scenic prop. It is the object of Wadjda&rsquo;s desire, and the point-of-view shot makes that desire ours too. The macro meaning is clear: Al-Mansour represents Wadjda as a seeing, desiring subject, not an object of observation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The bicycle in <em>Wadjda</em> is a visual object that carries representational weight beyond its function as a toy. Bicycles give children physical mobility &mdash; the ability to go further, faster, beyond the immediate neighbourhood. In the film&rsquo;s setting, girls are not supposed to ride bicycles in public spaces. When Wadjda pursues and eventually obtains the bicycle, the closing street-traversal sequence &mdash; in which she rides it freely through the neighbourhood while Abdullah races alongside her &mdash; represents more than a child getting what she wanted. The open space of the street and the physical freedom of movement stand against the indoor, threshold-framed enclosure of the film&rsquo;s earlier sequences. The camera follows rather than leads her: it does not know where she is going, and neither do we. This is the representational argument of the film&rsquo;s ending: agency is not just desired but enacted.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Named Sequence: The Hotel-Room Karaoke Set Piece in <em>Girlhood</em></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In the hotel-room karaoke set piece, Marieme and her three new friends rent a hotel room for the night and perform Rihanna&rsquo;s &ldquo;Diamonds&rdquo; together in blue-gelled light. Cinematographer Crystel Fournier frames the four girls in a wide Cinemascope shot: they fill the horizontal width of the frame together, their bodies in motion, the blue light making the scene feel removed from the grey social realism of the film&rsquo;s exterior sequences. This is a sustained set piece &mdash; it runs for several minutes without a cut to any outside observer. There is no adult authority figure in the room. There is no male gaze directing the spectator&rsquo;s attention: the camera is at the friends&rsquo; own level, and when it moves it follows their movement rather than surveilling them. Laura Mulvey&rsquo;s gaze theory is useful here at GCSE level: who is looking at whom in this frame? The answer is that the four girls are looking at themselves and each other. The gaze is internal to the group. The spectator is invited into a space of female self-creation, not into a position of surveillance over it.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A common error in answering representation questions about these two films is to assume that films about young women automatically empower them. Representation analysis asks how the film constructs its subjects through specific filmic choices, not just whether it has a female protagonist. <em>Girlhood</em> has also generated critical debate about whether a white French director (Sciamma) making a film about Black teenage girls in the <em>banlieues</em> carries an inherent representational tension, regardless of the film&rsquo;s formal choices. At GCSE level, it is appropriate to note this cultural-ethics question as part of the film&rsquo;s reception context (AO1), while focusing your analysis on the specific filmic choices and their effects on representation (AO2). The question is not whether the film is &ldquo;good&rdquo; representation, but how it constructs representation through the tools of cinema.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Costume and Music as Identity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Both films use costume and music as identity-construction tools. In <em>Girlhood</em>, the saturated colour of the friends&rsquo; clothing &mdash; vivid blues, pinks and purples worn in contrast to the greys and browns of the suburban exterior &mdash; functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A visual code in film in which the colours of costumes, set design and lighting signal character emotion, social status or thematic meaning without dialogue.">colour code</dfn> for identity: the girls&rsquo; brightness is an assertion of presence in a landscape that otherwise renders them invisible. Para One&rsquo;s score uses extended needle-drops (sustained songs played at length rather than as background) to give the friends&rsquo; interior scenes an anthem quality. In <em>Wadjda</em>, the green trainers Wadjda customises with painted stars are a micro-detail of non-conformity: they sit beneath the uniform abaya she is required to wear, visible only to her and to the spectator. The film frames these trainers in close-up twice &mdash; a deliberate editorial choice to give weight to a small act of self-expression.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for the hotel-room karaoke set piece in Girlhood, write down three micro choices and the representational effect of each. Name who holds the gaze in this sequence.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The hotel-room karaoke set piece in <em>Girlhood</em> is one of the most analysed sequences in recent European cinema. Its representational power comes from three micro choices working together: Cinemascope widescreen framing placing all four girls as equal subjects in the frame; blue-gel lighting separating the scene from social-realist exteriors; and the absence of any external observer, male or adult. The gaze belongs to the girls themselves.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><em>Wadjda</em> uses frame-within-a-frame compositions for female characters in constrained spaces, while reserving point-of-view low-angle shots for Wadjda herself &mdash; constructing her as a seeing, desiring subject rather than an object of observation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The hotel-room karaoke set piece in <em>Girlhood</em> places the gaze inside the group of friends through Cinemascope framing, blue-gel lighting and the absence of external observers &mdash; representing Black femininity as a space of self-creation, not surveillance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Costume and music function as identity codes in both films: <em>Girlhood</em>&rsquo;s saturated colours assert presence; <em>Wadjda</em>&rsquo;s customised trainers are a close-up detail of private non-conformity beneath visible restriction.</li>
</ul>

The most common error is spreading the answer across the whole film (&ldquo;throughout the film, Wadjda is shown as determined&rdquo;) rather than analysing how a specific moment constructs that representation. For each micro feature &mdash; a low-angle shot, a point-of-view cut, a colour choice, a music cue &mdash; state what it is, then say what it does: what representation does it construct and what effect does it have on the spectator&rsquo;s relationship to the character? For extended-essay questions, the gaze theory (Mulvey) is a useful framework but must be applied to a specific shot rather than mentioned in the abstract. Your thesis should make a claim about what the film argues through its representation, not just describe what the film contains.</p>
