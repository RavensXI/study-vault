<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Production Context: Gavin Hood&rsquo;s Tsotsi (2005)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A fictional or factual account presented through audio-visual choices to position the spectator in a particular way.">Tsotsi</dfn> was directed by Gavin Hood, adapted from Athol Fugard&rsquo;s 1980 novel of the same name, and filmed on location in Soweto township and central Johannesburg in 2004. Cinematographer Lance Gewer shot the film, and the score was composed by Mark Kilian and Paul Hepker, featuring South African kwaito artist Zola and the voice of Vusi Mahlasela. Produced by Industrial Development Corporation of South Africa, the UK Film and TV Production Company and Tsotsi Films, the film was distributed internationally by Miramax Films. At the 78th Academy Awards in 2006, Tsotsi won Best Foreign Language Film &mdash; the first South African film to take that category.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who directed Tsotsi, who was the cinematographer, who composed the score, and what award did the film win at the 2006 Academy Awards?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tsotsi (2005) was directed by Gavin Hood, shot by Lance Gewer, and scored by Mark Kilian and Paul Hepker featuring kwaito artist Zola. It won Best Foreign Language Film at the 78th Academy Awards (2006), the first South African film to win that category.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Representation Focus: Post-Apartheid Masculinity in the Johannesburg Township</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The written-paper question on global non-English-language film centres on <dfn class="term" data-def="The way in which people, groups, places or ideas are constructed and presented to the spectator through filmic choices.">representation</dfn> &mdash; specifically, how the film constructs identity, social groups and power relationships through its audio-visual choices. In Tsotsi, the dominant representational concern is post-apartheid Black South African masculinity: what it means to be a young man in a Johannesburg township where structural poverty, absent parents and gang life intersect. The film&rsquo;s Zulu title word &lsquo;tsotsi&rsquo; means &lsquo;thug&rsquo; &mdash; already a representation laden with stereotype and the film&rsquo;s central project is to interrogate that label rather than confirm it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Hood&rsquo;s representational strategy works at the micro level through specific cinematographic and sound choices, and at the macro level through a redemption narrative that insists on the protagonist&rsquo;s interiority against the social forces that would reduce him to a type. Students who simply describe what happens miss the analytical question: how do filmic choices construct this version of masculinity for the spectator?</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Micro Analysis: The Township-Arrival Opening on the Train Platform</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The film opens on the train platform in Soweto, and the micro choices here immediately establish the representational register. The camera operates in close, using a <dfn class="term" data-def="A camera positioned very close to the subject, typically showing only part of the face or body to signal intimacy, intensity or detail.">close-up</dfn> on the protagonist&rsquo;s face as he moves through the crowd: the shot is held for longer than conventional continuity editing would dictate, forcing the spectator to read a face that gives very little away. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Low-key lighting: a lighting design using a dominant key light and minimal fill, creating strong shadows and contrast across the frame.">low-key lighting</dfn> of the exterior scene carves the protagonist out from the crowd behind him without idealising or softening him. Gewer&rsquo;s hand-held camera introduces a <dfn class="term" data-def="A camera held by the operator rather than fixed on a tripod, producing a slight physical unsteadiness that signals naturalism or urgency.">handheld</dfn> instability that codes the township space as volatile and lived-in rather than aestheticised.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">At the macro level, this opening sequence refuses to exoticise the township setting. The crowd of workers, the noise of the train, the dust on clothes &mdash; all of these mise-en-sc&egrave;ne choices represent the township as an ordinary social world, not a spectacle for an outside audience. This is significant: post-apartheid South African cinema was under pressure to produce images that either recapitulated apartheid-era depictions (Black life as problem, violence, danger) or offered redemptive correctives that sanitised the same spaces. Hood navigates between these by anchoring the spectator inside the protagonist&rsquo;s perspective before the narrative action begins.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The film was shot on location in Soweto, a township south-west of Johannesburg, and in central Johannesburg itself. Hood and Gewer chose practical locations rather than studio reconstructions, a decision that feeds directly into the film&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="A filmmaking approach that prioritises realistic, unglamourised representation of ordinary life, often using non-professional actors and real locations.">naturalist</dfn> representational style. The shantytown geography &mdash; corrugated iron, chain-link fences, the recurring train line &mdash; is not backdrop; it is a set of recurring visual anchors that situate the protagonist within a specific social and spatial world. The critical and commercial success of the film abroad was partly a function of this specificity: international audiences and critics responded to a South African story told from inside its own geography rather than through a foreign lens.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The film was well received critically and commercially. Subsequent debate has engaged with whether Hood&rsquo;s redemption narrative resolves the protagonist&rsquo;s arc too quickly and with the tension between melodrama and social realism. This is a productive classroom discussion: does the film&rsquo;s representational honesty compromise its narrative neatness, or are both achievable simultaneously?</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Micro Analysis: The Childhood-Flashback Under the Concrete Pipes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The childhood-flashback sequence, in which the protagonist relives the traumatic memory of his mother&rsquo;s death beneath the concrete pipes, is the film&rsquo;s most formally distinctive passage. Hood uses child-perspective inserts &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Low-angle shot: a shot in which the camera is positioned below the eye line of the subject, making the subject appear larger, more powerful or more threatening.">low-angle shots</dfn> from the remembered child&rsquo;s physical position on the ground &mdash; to collapse the distance between the adult protagonist and the child he was. The lighting shifts here: where the township exterior scenes carry the naturalist low-key palette, the flashback sequences use a warmer, softly overexposed light that codes the memories as simultaneously desirable and painful.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">At the macro level, this sequence constructs the protagonist as a survivor of domestic violence and parental abandonment &mdash; a representational move that contextualises his gang identity not as innate criminality but as a learned response to specific conditions. The film is making a sociological argument through filmic form: the close-up on the child&rsquo;s face, the distorted sound design of raised voices, the sudden cut back to the adult protagonist&rsquo;s face in the present all position the spectator to understand rather than simply judge.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the three named sequences in Tsotsi most useful for representation analysis, and write down one micro feature from each that links to a representational idea.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In Tsotsi, the key sequences for micro/representation analysis are: (1) the township-arrival opening on the train platform; (2) the childhood-flashback under the concrete pipes; (3) the Miriam apartment-feeding sequence; (4) the closing house-return confrontation. Each anchors representational argument about post-apartheid masculinity, trauma and the possibility of social change.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Micro Analysis: The Miriam Apartment-Feeding Sequence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Miriam apartment-feeding sequence repositions the protagonist in a domestic space defined by care rather than violence. When the protagonist forces Miriam at gunpoint to nurse a baby he has taken from the carjacking scene, the framing shifts from the hand-held naturalism of the township to a more static, locked-off coverage that holds both characters in frame together. The lighting is brighter at Miriam&rsquo;s apartment than in the shantytown interiors: Gewer uses a softer key light that diffuses across the white walls, coding the space as aspirational even within post-apartheid urban poverty.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Mise-en-sc&egrave;ne: everything placed within the camera&rsquo;s field of view to create meaning, including set design, costume, lighting, props, blocking and performance.">mise-en-sc&egrave;ne</dfn> of the apartment &mdash; the few but carefully chosen possessions, the child&rsquo;s drawing on the wall, the modest domestic order &mdash; represents Miriam as a counter-figure to the world the protagonist comes from: self-sufficient, gentle, holding a kind of moral authority without institutional power. Laura Mulvey&rsquo;s gaze theory, adapted at GCSE level, is useful here: rather than constructing Miriam as an object of desire for the protagonist or the spectator, the shot-reverse-shot between them positions her as the film&rsquo;s moral pole &mdash; the sequence is about what the protagonist is looking at and what that reveals about the character he might become.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">A common error in representation answers is to describe what a character does rather than how the film constructs a group or identity through filmic choices. Writing &lsquo;Tsotsi is a gang leader who discovers a baby and learns to be kind&rsquo; is plot description &mdash; it earns almost no marks in a representation question. The analytical move is always to ask: what specific shot type, lighting state, sound choice or editing decision does the film use at this moment, and what does that choice argue about this group or identity? For example: &lsquo;The extended close-up held on the protagonist&rsquo;s face during the Miriam sequence constructs Black South African masculinity as capable of interiority and moral conflict &mdash; a representational counter-argument to the gang-as-type stereotype that the film&rsquo;s title invokes.&rsquo;</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Kwaito Score as Representational Choice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The score&rsquo;s use of kwaito &mdash; a South African genre blending house music with local jazz, soul and township sounds &mdash; is itself a representational statement. <dfn class="term" data-def="Non-diegetic sound: sound that exists on the soundtrack but not in the world of the film, such as a background score or a narrator&rsquo;s voice.">Non-diegetic</dfn> kwaito music positions the film within a contemporary Black South African cultural context rather than scoring the township as exotic or dangerous for an international art-house audience. The choice to feature Zola &mdash; a major figure in kwaito at the time of production and himself a resident of Soweto &mdash; anchors the film&rsquo;s representation of township life in authentic cultural specificity. This is a macro representational choice operating through a micro sound decision: every cue Zola&rsquo;s voice appears on, the score is refusing the patronising documentary register that township-set films had often adopted when made by or for outside audiences.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Tsotsi (2005), directed by Gavin Hood and shot by Lance Gewer, uses handheld cinematography, low-key naturalist lighting and a kwaito score to represent post-apartheid Black South African masculinity from the inside, refusing to exoticise the Soweto setting for an outside audience.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Representation analysis requires naming a specific sequence, identifying a specific micro choice, and explaining what that choice constructs for the spectator &mdash; plot description alone earns no credit in the written-paper representation question.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Key sequences for micro/representation analysis include the township-arrival opening, the childhood-flashback under the concrete pipes, the Miriam apartment-feeding sequence, and the closing house-return confrontation.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">Representation questions ask you to analyse how the film constructs people, groups or ideas through filmic choices &mdash; not to retell the story. The most common error is writing &lsquo;Tsotsi is shown as...&rsquo; followed by plot description with no micro analysis. Every paragraph must name a specific sequence, identify a specific filmic choice (a shot type, a lighting state, a sound cue, an editing decision), and then explain what that choice argues about the group being represented. For extended-essay questions, aim to cover at least two named sequences and connect the micro choices to a macro representational argument. Avoid labelling the film as &lsquo;realistic&rsquo; without explaining which specific choices create that realism effect and what representational work it does.</p>
