--- DESCRIPTION ---
Break any electronic product into input, processing and output sub-systems.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Thinking in Systems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every electronic product &mdash; from a smoke alarm to a traffic light controller &mdash; can be broken into three connected stages: an <dfn class="term" data-def="A sub-system that detects a physical quantity such as light, temperature or sound and converts it into an electrical signal.">input sub-system</dfn>, a processing sub-system and an output sub-system. Thinking in these three stages is one of the most powerful tools an electronics engineer has. It lets you design a complex product by solving one small section at a time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The signal flows in one direction: from input to processing to output. Each stage has a clear role. Once you can sort components and circuits into these three categories, you can read and sketch <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that represents an electronic system using labelled boxes for each sub-system, connected by arrows showing signal flow.">block diagrams</dfn> confidently &mdash; a skill tested on almost every past paper.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and draw a blank three-box block diagram. Fill in the name and one example component for each stage from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every electronic system follows the same pattern: <strong>Input &rarr; Processing &rarr; Output</strong>. Input sub-systems sense the physical world. Processing sub-systems make decisions. Output sub-systems act on those decisions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Input Sub-Systems &mdash; Sensing the World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Input sub-systems contain <dfn class="term" data-def="A component that converts a physical quantity (light, heat, sound, pressure) into an electrical signal.">transducers</dfn> that convert a physical quantity into an electrical signal. The specification requires you to know these sensing units:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Light sensor</strong> &mdash; a light-dependent resistor (LDR) whose resistance falls as light intensity increases. Used in street-light controllers and security systems.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Temperature sensor</strong> &mdash; an NTC thermistor whose resistance falls as temperature rises. Used in fire alarms and thermostat circuits.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Magnetic field sensor</strong> &mdash; a reed switch that closes when a magnet is brought close. Used in door alarms and bicycle speed sensors.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Pressure sensor</strong> &mdash; a force-sensitive resistor whose resistance changes with applied force. Used in electronic scales and car park barriers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Moisture sensor</strong> &mdash; a pair of conducting tracks whose resistance falls when wet. Used in plant-watering monitors and rain detectors.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Sound sensor (microphone)</strong> &mdash; a small electret microphone that converts sound pressure waves into a varying voltage. Used in clap-activated switches and intruder alarms.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Rotation sensor</strong> &mdash; an encoder or potentiometer that produces a voltage or pulse train proportional to angular position or speed. Used in robotic arms and motor controllers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Pulse generator</strong> &mdash; an oscillator that produces a regular stream of pulses. It acts as a timing input sub-system, providing a clock signal to processing circuits such as counters and timers. It is never placed where a sensing transducer is needed &mdash; its role is timing, not physical measurement.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Processing Sub-Systems &mdash; Making Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The processing stage receives electrical signals from the inputs and decides what to do. Common processing sub-systems include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Logic gates</strong> &mdash; digital circuits (AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR) that combine binary signals according to a truth table.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Latch (bistable)</strong> &mdash; a memory element that holds its output HIGH or LOW until told to change. Useful where the output must stay on after the trigger is removed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Time delay</strong> &mdash; a monostable circuit that produces a single output pulse of a set length when triggered. Used in burglar alarm hold-off timers and courtesy lights.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Comparator</strong> &mdash; a circuit that compares two voltages and outputs HIGH when one is larger than the other. Links the analogue sensor voltage to a digital decision.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down two examples of processing sub-systems. State what each one does to the input signal.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Processing sub-systems operate on signals &mdash; they do not interact directly with the physical world. A comparator, logic gate, latch or timer all sit in the processing stage, never in the input or output stage.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Output Sub-Systems &mdash; Acting on Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Output sub-systems convert the processed electrical signal back into something physically useful. The specification lists these output devices:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Lamp / LED</strong> &mdash; visual indicators. LEDs require a current-limiting resistor in series.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Buzzer</strong> &mdash; an audio output. Some are &ldquo;active&rdquo; (buzzes at a fixed frequency when supplied with DC); others are &ldquo;passive&rdquo; (require an AC or pulsed signal).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Solenoid</strong> &mdash; an electromagnetic coil that produces linear motion when energised. Used to release door locks or trigger valves.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Motor</strong> &mdash; converts electrical energy into continuous rotation. Used in fans, conveyor belts and robots.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Servo</strong> &mdash; a geared motor with a position feedback circuit, allowing controlled angular movement. Used in robotic arms and model aircraft control surfaces.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Loudspeaker</strong> &mdash; a high-power audio output that requires an amplifier stage between the processing circuit and the speaker coil.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Most output devices cannot be driven directly by a logic gate or comparator because they need more current than those circuits can supply. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit (transistor or MOSFET switch) placed between the processing sub-system and a high-current output device to provide the extra current needed.">transducer driver</dfn> &mdash; typically a transistor or MOSFET switch &mdash; sits between the processing output and the device to boost current capacity.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n31">A security light turns on when it is dark AND someone is moving nearby. Break this into three stages:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Input:</strong> Two sensors are needed &mdash; an LDR (detects darkness) and a PIR motion sensor (detects movement). Each is an input sub-system.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Processing:</strong> The light should only come on if it is dark AND movement is detected. An AND logic gate combines the two signals: output is HIGH only when both inputs are HIGH.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Output:</strong> The lamp (output device). Because the AND gate cannot supply enough current to drive the lamp directly, a transistor switch (transducer driver) is added between the gate output and the lamp.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35">Block diagram (left to right): [LDR sensor] &rarr; [AND gate] &larr; [PIR sensor] &rarr; [Transistor driver] &rarr; [Lamp]</p>

    <span>Common Exam Errors with Block Diagrams</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n36">Examiners have consistently penalised three types of error in block diagrams:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>1. Mixing up stages.</strong> Putting a logic gate in the input stage or a thermistor in the output stage. Remember: transducers are always inputs; decision circuits are always processing; actuators are always outputs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>2. Inventing blocks not needed by the specification.</strong> Adding a pulse generator where no timing is required (e.g. a simple light sensor to LED circuit needs no clock). Only include a block if the system description justifies it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39"><strong>3. Omitting the transducer driver.</strong> When the output is a motor, relay, solenoid or lamp, a transistor or MOSFET driver is almost always required. Logic gates and comparators cannot supply the current these devices need directly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n41">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n42">All electronic systems have three stages &mdash; input (sensing), processing (decision) and output (action). Signal flows from left to right in a block diagram.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n43">Input transducers include LDRs, thermistors, microphones, pressure sensors, moisture sensors and pulse generators. Each converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n44">High-current output devices (motors, lamps, solenoids) always need a transducer driver between the processing circuit and the device itself.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n40">Block diagram questions ask you to complete or design a system. Before writing anything, identify the physical quantity being sensed, then map it to the correct input transducer. Next, decide whether the processing stage needs a logic decision (AND/OR/NOT gate), a time delay (monostable) or a comparison (comparator). Finally, check whether the output device needs a transducer driver. Write the sub-system name in each box &mdash; never draw a full circuit where a block diagram is asked for. Two marks are typically awarded for correct stage identification and one for correct signal direction arrows.</p>
