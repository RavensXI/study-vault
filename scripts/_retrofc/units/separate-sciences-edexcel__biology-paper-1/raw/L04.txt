--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Neurones and the Nervous System</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The nervous system allows rapid communication across the body using electrical impulses. It has two main divisions: the <strong>central nervous system</strong> (CNS), consisting of the brain and spinal cord, and the <strong>peripheral nervous system</strong>, consisting of the nerves that connect the CNS to the body&rsquo;s receptors and effectors.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">There are three types of <dfn class="term" data-def="A specialised cell adapted to carry electrical impulses rapidly around the body. Different types connect receptors, the CNS and effectors.">neurone</dfn>:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A nerve cell that carries electrical impulses from sense receptors (e.g. in the skin or eye) towards the spinal cord and brain.">Sensory neurone</dfn></strong> &mdash; carries impulses from receptors (e.g. in the skin, eyes, ears) towards the CNS. Has a long <dfn class="term" data-def="The long fibre of a neurone along which electrical impulses travel away from the cell body.">axon</dfn> and a cell body located outside the spinal cord.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A nerve cell entirely within the CNS (brain or spinal cord) that connects sensory and motor neurones.">Relay neurone</dfn></strong> &mdash; found entirely within the CNS. Connects sensory neurones to motor neurones and processes information within the spinal cord and brain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A nerve cell that carries electrical impulses from the brain or spinal cord to effectors (muscles or glands).">Motor neurone</dfn></strong> &mdash; carries impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands), causing a response.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Each neurone has a long axon surrounded by a <dfn class="term" data-def="A fatty sheath around the axon of some neurones that acts as an electrical insulator, speeding up the transmission of nerve impulses.">myelin sheath</dfn> in many neurones. The myelin sheath acts as an electrical insulator and dramatically increases the speed of impulse transmission. Damage to the myelin sheath (as in multiple sclerosis) slows impulse conduction.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the three neurone types in order for the reflex arc, and write one function for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Reflex arc sequence: receptor &rarr; sensory neurone &rarr; relay neurone (in spinal cord) &rarr; motor neurone &rarr; effector (muscle or gland). The myelin sheath insulates the axon and speeds up impulse transmission.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Synapses</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Neurones do not physically touch each other. The tiny gap between two neurones is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="The tiny gap between two neurones (or between a neurone and an effector). Electrical signals cannot cross this gap directly; instead chemical neurotransmitters are released.">synapse</dfn>. When an electrical impulse reaches the end of a neurone, it triggers the release of chemical messengers called <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical released from the end of a neurone into the synapse. It diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the next neurone, restarting the electrical impulse.">neurotransmitters</dfn> from small vesicles. These diffuse across the synaptic cleft and bind to complementary receptor proteins on the next neurone&rsquo;s membrane. If enough neurotransmitter binds, a new electrical impulse is generated in the next neurone.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">Synapses only allow impulses to travel in <strong>one direction</strong> (because neurotransmitters are released from one side only). Many drugs and toxins work by interfering with neurotransmitter release or reception at synapses.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">A reflex is a fast, automatic response to a stimulus that does not require conscious thought. Reflexes bypass the brain, passing through the spinal cord instead, which is why they are so rapid.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Reflex arc pathway: <strong>stimulus</strong> (e.g. touching a hot surface) &rarr; <strong>receptor</strong> (pain receptors in the skin) &rarr; <strong>sensory neurone</strong> (impulse to spinal cord) &rarr; <strong>relay neurone</strong> (in the spinal cord) &rarr; <strong>motor neurone</strong> (impulse to effector) &rarr; <strong>effector</strong> (muscle contracts, hand withdrawn). The brain receives information about the reflex only after the response has already begun.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Eye (Triple Only)</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">The eye is a sense organ that detects light. You must know the function of each structure and how the eye focuses light on the retina.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Eye Structures and Their Functions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The transparent curved layer at the front of the eye. It refracts (bends) most of the incoming light, providing the majority of the eye&rsquo;s focusing power.">Cornea</dfn></strong> &mdash; the transparent curved cover at the front. It refracts (bends) most of the incoming light.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The coloured part of the eye surrounding the pupil. Circular and radial muscles in the iris control the size of the pupil and therefore the amount of light entering the eye.">Iris</dfn></strong> &mdash; the coloured ring surrounding the pupil. Circular muscles (contract to constrict the pupil in bright light) and radial muscles (contract to dilate the pupil in dim light) control the amount of light entering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The transparent, flexible disc behind the iris. It changes shape (accommodation) to fine-tune the focus of light on the retina.">Lens</dfn></strong> &mdash; a transparent flexible disc that fine-tunes the focus of light. It changes shape by the action of the ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, containing rod cells (detect light and dark) and cone cells (detect colour). It converts light into electrical impulses.">Retina</dfn></strong> &mdash; the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye. It contains rod cells (sensitive to dim light; detect black and white) and cone cells (sensitive to bright light; detect colour in three wavelengths: red, green, blue). Impulses travel along the optic nerve to the brain.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Accommodation</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Accommodation is the eye&rsquo;s ability to change focus between near and distant objects by altering the shape of the lens. When viewing a <strong>near object</strong>: the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments loosen and the lens becomes rounder (more curved), increasing its refracting power. When viewing a <strong>distant object</strong>: the ciliary muscles relax, the suspensory ligaments pull taut and the lens flattens (less curved), reducing its refracting power.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Vision Defects and Correction</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the eye cannot focus on distant objects clearly. Light from distant objects is focused in front of the retina, producing a blurred image. Corrected with a concave (diverging) lens.">Short-sightedness</dfn> (myopia) occurs when the eyeball is too long or the cornea is too curved. Light from distant objects is focused in front of the retina instead of on it. It is corrected with a <strong>concave (diverging) lens</strong> that spreads the light rays before they enter the eye.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the eye cannot focus on close objects clearly. Light from near objects is focused behind the retina. Corrected with a convex (converging) lens.">Long-sightedness</dfn> (hyperopia) occurs when the eyeball is too short or the cornea is too flat. Light from close objects is focused behind the retina. It is corrected with a <strong>convex (converging) lens</strong> that brings the light rays together before they enter the eye.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Cataracts</strong> form when the lens becomes cloudy, scattering light and reducing vision. The cloudy lens is surgically replaced with an artificial implant. <strong>Colour blindness</strong> results from a deficiency in one or more types of cone cell. It is sex-linked (carried on the X chromosome) and is much more common in males than females.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write one sentence each for short-sightedness and long-sightedness: where light is focused and which lens corrects it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Short-sightedness: distant objects blurred; light focused in front of retina; corrected by concave lens. Long-sightedness: near objects blurred; light focused behind retina; corrected by convex lens.</p>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Three neurone types: sensory (receptor to CNS), relay (within CNS), motor (CNS to effector). The myelin sheath insulates the axon and speeds conduction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Synapses use neurotransmitters to pass impulses chemically across the gap; they ensure one-directional transmission. Reflex arcs bypass the brain for fast, automatic responses.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The eye focuses light on the retina via the cornea (main refraction) and the lens (accommodation). Short-sightedness: concave lens. Long-sightedness: convex lens. Colour blindness is due to deficient cone cells.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">For synapse questions, always explain the <strong>direction</strong> of impulse travel and the <strong>mechanism</strong>: neurotransmitter is released, diffuses across the cleft, binds to receptors on the next neurone. Avoid saying the impulse &lsquo;jumps&rsquo; the synapse &mdash; it doesn&rsquo;t. For eye questions, the most common error is confusing concave and convex lenses. Remember: short-sightedness needs to <strong>diverge</strong> light &rarr; concave; long-sightedness needs to <strong>converge</strong> light &rarr; convex. When explaining accommodation, state what the ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments do and the resulting lens shape change.</p>
