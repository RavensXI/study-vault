<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Eukaryotic and Prokaryotic Cells</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">All living things are made of cells. Cells fall into two fundamental types: <dfn class="term" data-def="A complex cell with a true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane, and membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic.">eukaryotic</dfn> cells and <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, simple cell with no true nucleus. The genetic material is a single loop of DNA that floats freely in the cytoplasm. Bacteria are prokaryotic.">prokaryotic</dfn> cells. Knowing the differences between them &mdash; and being able to name sub-cellular structures &mdash; is tested across both papers of your Triple Biology exam.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Eukaryotic cells include animal and plant cells. They contain a <strong>nucleus</strong> that encloses their DNA, along with membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria and ribosomes. Prokaryotic cells &mdash; bacteria &mdash; are much smaller and simpler. They have no true nucleus; instead, their genetic material is a single loop of DNA floating freely in the cytoplasm. Prokaryotes may also carry small rings of extra DNA called <dfn class="term" data-def="Small rings of extra DNA found in prokaryotic cells. They replicate independently and often carry genes for antibiotic resistance.">plasmids</dfn>. Bacteria also have a cell wall, but it is made from a different material to plant cell walls.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three features do prokaryotic cells lack that eukaryotic cells have, and what extra DNA structure may bacteria contain?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eukaryotic cells have a true nucleus, membrane-bound organelles and are typically 10&ndash;100 &mu;m. Prokaryotic cells have no true nucleus, a single loop of DNA in the cytoplasm, and may contain plasmids. They are typically 1&ndash;5 &mu;m.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Animal and Plant Cell Organelles</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Both animal and plant cells are eukaryotic, but their organelle content differs. You must be able to name each <dfn class="term" data-def="A specialised structure within a cell that carries out a specific function, such as the nucleus, mitochondria or ribosomes.">organelle</dfn> and link its structure to its function.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Animal Cell Organelles and Their Functions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Nucleus</strong> &mdash; contains the cell&rsquo;s DNA and controls the cell&rsquo;s activities. It is enclosed by the nuclear envelope.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cytoplasm</strong> &mdash; a jelly-like fluid where most chemical reactions take place, controlled by enzymes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Cell membrane</strong> &mdash; a thin, selectively permeable layer that controls which substances enter and leave the cell.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Mitochondria</strong> &mdash; the site of <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of respiration that requires oxygen, producing carbon dioxide, water and a large amount of energy (ATP). It occurs in the mitochondria.">aerobic respiration</dfn>, where energy is transferred from glucose. Cells with high energy demands (e.g. muscle cells) contain more mitochondria.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Ribosomes</strong> &mdash; tiny structures where protein synthesis takes place. Found in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n12">Plant cells have all the organelles above, plus three additional structures:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Chloroplasts</strong> &mdash; contain the green pigment chlorophyll and are the site of <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which plants use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, occurring in chloroplasts.">photosynthesis</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Permanent vacuole</strong> &mdash; a large fluid-filled space containing cell sap (a dilute solution of sugars and salts). It keeps the cell rigid and supports the plant structure.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Cell wall</strong> &mdash; made of cellulose, it surrounds the cell membrane and provides structural strength. It prevents the cell from bursting when water enters by osmosis.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Write from memory the three organelles found in plant cells but not animal cells, stating one function for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts for photosynthesis and a permanent vacuole filled with cell sap. Animal cells have none of these. Both have a nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, cytoplasm and cell membrane.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Specialised Cells</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Most cells are <dfn class="term" data-def="A cell that has developed specific structural features to carry out a particular function, such as a sperm cell, egg cell or ciliated epithelial cell.">specialised</dfn> &mdash; they have distinct structures matched to a specific job. Your exam frequently asks you to explain how a cell&rsquo;s structure is adapted to its function.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sperm Cell, Egg Cell and Ciliated Epithelial Cell</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Sperm cell</strong> &mdash; has a streamlined head with a pointed acrosome containing enzymes to digest the egg&rsquo;s membrane. The midpiece is packed with mitochondria to supply energy for swimming. The long flagellum (tail) provides propulsion. The nucleus carries the male&rsquo;s haploid genetic information to the egg.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Egg cell (ovum)</strong> &mdash; is the largest human cell and carries a haploid nucleus. It contains large food reserves (cytoplasm rich in nutrients) to nourish the developing embryo. Once fertilised, the cell membrane changes to prevent additional sperm from entering.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Ciliated epithelial cell</strong> &mdash; lines the trachea and bronchi. The surface is covered with tiny hair-like structures called cilia, which beat rhythmically to sweep mucus (containing trapped particles and bacteria) up towards the throat, keeping the airways clean.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Microscopy and Units</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n23">Microscopes let us see cells and sub-cellular structures too small for the naked eye. You must know two types: <strong>light microscopes</strong> and <strong>electron microscopes</strong>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n24">Light microscopes use visible light focused through glass lenses. They can magnify up to around &times;1,500 with a <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to distinguish two points that are close together as separate objects. Higher resolution means finer detail can be seen.">resolution</dfn> of about 200 nm. Electron microscopes fire a beam of electrons at the specimen; their resolution is about 0.2 nm and magnification can reach &times;2,000,000. This revealed sub-cellular structures &mdash; such as ribosomes and the internal membranes of mitochondria &mdash; that light microscopes cannot resolve.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Magnification Calculation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">The magnification formula is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">$$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{size of image}}{\text{size of real object}}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">It can be rearranged to find real size or image size:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">$$\text{real size} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Example:</strong> A student draws a cell image that is 45 mm wide. The real cell is 0.045 mm (45 &mu;m). What is the magnification?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">$$\text{magnification} = \frac{45\,\text{mm}}{0.045\,\text{mm}} = \times 1{,}000$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Always check both values are in the <strong>same units</strong> before dividing. Convert millimetres to micrometres if needed (1 mm = 1,000 &mu;m).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Unit Conversions and Standard Form</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n33">Biology uses a range of units for measuring cells and structures. You must be confident with the following prefixes:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n34"><strong>milli (m)</strong> &mdash; one thousandth, \(10^{-3}\) of a metre. Typical scale for small organisms.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35"><strong>micro (&mu;)</strong> &mdash; one millionth, \(10^{-6}\) of a metre. Typical for cells (10&ndash;100 &mu;m).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36"><strong>nano (n)</strong> &mdash; one billionth, \(10^{-9}\) of a metre. Used for sub-cellular structures and molecules.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37"><strong>pico (p)</strong> &mdash; one trillionth, \(10^{-12}\) of a metre. Used for atoms and very small molecules.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n38">Standard form expresses very large or very small numbers as \(A \times 10^n\). For example, a bacterial cell of \(2\,\mu\text{m}\) is \(2 \times 10^{-6}\,\text{m}\). Always express answers in standard form if the question uses it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n39" data-revision-tip="Cover this and convert: write 0.000 003 m in standard form, then state whether this is a typical cell size in micrometres.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key unit chain: 1 m = 1,000 mm = 1,000,000 &mu;m = 1,000,000,000 nm. A typical animal cell is 10&ndash;30 &mu;m; a bacterium is 1&ndash;5 &mu;m; a ribosome is around 20 nm.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n41">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n42">Eukaryotic cells (animal and plant) have a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no nucleus, a single loop of DNA in the cytoplasm, and may contain plasmids.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n43">Plant cells also have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a permanent vacuole; bacteria have a cell wall but no mitochondria, nucleus or chloroplasts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n44">Specialised cells &mdash; sperm, egg, ciliated epithelial &mdash; each have structural adaptations that match their function precisely.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n45">The magnification formula is image size &divide; real size; always use the same units. Electron microscopes have far greater resolution than light microscopes.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n40">In questions on cell structure, always <strong>link each organelle to its function</strong> &mdash; for example, &ldquo;mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration, transferring energy for the cell.&rdquo; In magnification calculations, convert both measurements to the same unit <em>before</em> dividing &mdash; this is the most common error. For specialised-cell questions, use the cell&rsquo;s specific adaptations (e.g. &ldquo;packed with mitochondria to provide energy for swimming&rdquo;) rather than generic answers. When you see the command word <strong>explain</strong>, you must include the word &ldquo;because&rdquo; or a causal link &mdash; description alone will not score explanation marks.</p>
