<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Cell Cycle and Mitosis</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Cells divide to allow organisms to grow, to repair damaged tissue and to replace worn-out cells. This follows the <dfn class="term" data-def="The repeating sequence of cell growth, DNA replication and cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells.">cell cycle</dfn>. Most of the cycle is spent in <dfn class="term" data-def="The phase of the cell cycle during which the cell grows, produces more sub-cellular structures and replicates its DNA in preparation for division.">interphase</dfn>, when the cell grows, manufactures more organelles and replicates all of its DNA so that each daughter cell will receive a complete set of chromosomes.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">After interphase, the cell enters <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell. Used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.">mitosis</dfn>. During mitosis the chromosomes condense and become visible, then the copies are pulled to opposite ends of the cell. The cytoplasm and cell membrane then divide to produce two <strong>genetically identical</strong> daughter cells, each with the full chromosome complement.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Mitosis has four main phases:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Prophase</strong> &mdash; chromosomes condense and become visible; the nuclear envelope breaks down.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Metaphase</strong> &mdash; chromosomes line up along the centre (equator) of the cell, attached to spindle fibres.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Anaphase</strong> &mdash; spindle fibres contract and pull the chromosome copies to opposite poles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Telophase and cytokinesis</strong> &mdash; nuclear envelopes reform around each set of chromosomes; the cytoplasm divides, producing two cells.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the four stages of mitosis in order, with one key event for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mitosis: Prophase (chromosomes condense, nuclear envelope breaks down) &rarr; Metaphase (chromosomes align at equator) &rarr; Anaphase (copies pulled to poles) &rarr; Telophase &amp; cytokinesis (two identical daughter cells form).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Cancer</strong> results when the cell cycle is no longer controlled. Cells divide repeatedly and uncontrollably, forming a tumour. Tumours that spread to other parts of the body are called malignant.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Cell Differentiation and Growth</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n12">As cells divide and grow, they become <strong>specialised</strong> through a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a cell develops specific structural features to carry out a particular function. Once differentiated, most animal cells cannot change type.">differentiation</dfn>. Most animal cells differentiate during embryonic development and then lose the ability to become other cell types. Plant cells retain the ability to differentiate throughout life because they have <strong>meristem</strong> tissue at root and shoot tips.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Growth in organisms can be measured by mass, length or percentage change. In children, growth is monitored using <dfn class="term" data-def="A chart that shows the typical range of measurements (e.g. height or mass) for children of a given age. Lines on the chart represent percentiles, indicating what percentage of children fall below that measurement.">percentile charts</dfn>. A percentile line, such as the 50th percentile, means that 50% of children of that age have a measurement below that value. Percentile charts allow doctors to identify children whose growth is unusually slow or unusually fast, prompting further investigation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Stem Cells</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Undifferentiated cells that can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cell types. Found in embryos (embryonic stem cells) and in some adult tissues (adult stem cells).">Stem cells</dfn> are undifferentiated cells that can divide to produce more stem cells or differentiate into a range of specialised cells. <strong>Embryonic stem cells</strong> (from the inner cell mass of an early embryo) can differentiate into almost any cell type. <strong>Adult stem cells</strong> are more limited; bone marrow stem cells, for example, produce all types of blood cell.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Stem cells have potential therapeutic uses: replacing damaged nerve cells in spinal cord injuries, producing insulin-secreting cells to treat diabetes, and growing replacement tissue for burns patients. These therapies are at various stages of research and clinical trials.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Embryonic stem cells raise ethical concerns because extracting them destroys the embryo. Some people argue the embryo is a potential human life and should not be used for research. Others argue that the potential to relieve suffering outweighs this concern. Adult stem cells and stem cells from umbilical cord blood avoid this ethical issue. Plant <dfn class="term" data-def="A region of unspecialised, actively dividing cells in a plant, found at root and shoot tips. Meristem cells can differentiate into any type of plant cell.">meristem</dfn> cells can also be used in tissue culture to grow new plants from a single cell.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state one benefit and one ethical objection to using embryonic stem cells in medicine.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Embryonic stem cells can differentiate into any cell type (pluripotent) but their use is controversial because extracting them destroys the embryo. Adult stem cells are less versatile but raise fewer ethical concerns. Plant meristems can generate whole new plants in tissue culture.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">The Brain (Triple Only)</h2>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n20">The brain controls most of the body&rsquo;s functions and is made of billions of interconnected neurones. Three key regions you must know are:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The largest part of the brain, divided into two hemispheres. It is responsible for conscious thought, memory, language, personality and voluntary movement.">Cerebral hemispheres</dfn></strong> (cerebrum) &mdash; the largest region. Controls conscious thought, memory, language, personality and voluntary movement. The outer layer (cerebral cortex) is deeply folded to increase surface area.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A region at the back of the brain that coordinates movement, balance and posture.">Cerebellum</dfn></strong> &mdash; at the back of the brain. Coordinates balance, posture and precise muscle movements such as riding a bicycle or playing a musical instrument.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The part of the brain that connects to the spinal cord and controls automatic, vital functions such as breathing, heart rate and blood pressure.">Medulla oblongata</dfn></strong> (medulla) &mdash; connects the brain to the spinal cord. Controls automatic, life-sustaining functions: breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>CT and PET Scanning</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Brain research relies on scanning techniques that allow scientists to investigate brain structure and function without surgery.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>CT scanning</strong> (computed tomography) uses X-rays taken from multiple angles. A computer reconstructs these to produce detailed cross-sectional images of brain structure. CT scans are good for identifying physical abnormalities such as tumours, bleeds or structural damage.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>PET scanning</strong> (positron emission tomography) uses a radioactive tracer (usually a form of glucose) injected into the blood. Active brain regions use more glucose, so they emit more radiation. The PET scanner detects this and produces a colour-coded map showing which areas of the brain are most active during a particular task. PET scans reveal function rather than structure, making them useful for studying conditions such as Alzheimer&rsquo;s disease or mapping speech and memory regions before surgery.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells via four stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase. Cancer is uncontrolled cell division.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Stem cells are undifferentiated; embryonic stem cells are pluripotent but ethically controversial. Percentile charts monitor child growth against typical population ranges.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The cerebral hemispheres control conscious thought; the cerebellum coordinates movement and balance; the medulla controls automatic vital functions. CT scans show structure; PET scans show function.</li>
</ul>
