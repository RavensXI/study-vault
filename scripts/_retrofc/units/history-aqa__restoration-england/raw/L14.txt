<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Cathedral as a Statement of Recovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">St Paul&rsquo;s Cathedral is your specified site for the 2028 exam. The building you can visit is mostly later than 1685, so be clear about what the examiner wants: the <em>Restoration</em> story of the site &mdash; the old cathedral&rsquo;s destruction in the Great Fire of 1666, the argument over what should replace it, Christopher Wren&rsquo;s designs, and the start of construction in 1675, with the walls rising through the last decade of Charles II&rsquo;s reign. Read that story and the site becomes a summary of Restoration England: disaster, recovery, the new science, and the alliance of Crown and Church.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Old St Paul's: A Ruin Before the Fire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The medieval cathedral on Ludgate Hill &mdash; the highest ground in the City &mdash; was one of the largest churches in Europe, and by 1660 one of the shabbiest. Its great spire had been destroyed by lightning in 1561 and never rebuilt; under the Commonwealth the nave had been used as cavalry stables; the fabric was cracked and patched. In August 1666 the young Christopher Wren &mdash; an Oxford astronomer turned architect &mdash; submitted a report proposing to crown the crumbling crossing with something England had never seen: a dome. Days later, the question became academic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what happened to Old St Paul's in September 1666?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the Great Fire of September 1666 (Lesson 6) the cathedral burned with the City around it. Booksellers had stacked their stock in the crypt for safety; it burned with everything else. Falling masonry and melted roof-lead left the great church an unstable shell, and after attempts at patching, demolition was accepted as unavoidable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Wren and the Battle of the Designs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Rebuilding the cathedral of England&rsquo;s capital was an argument about what England now was, and it ran for nearly a decade. Wren&rsquo;s favourite scheme, preserved in the huge oak <dfn class="term" data-def="Wren's great oak model of 1673-4, showing his preferred domed design; rejected by the clergy as too unlike a traditional cathedral.">Great Model</dfn> of 1673&ndash;4, was radical: a centralised church under a vast dome &mdash; classical, geometric, continental. The clergy rejected it; a cathedral, they insisted, needed the long nave and choir of English tradition. The compromise came in 1675: the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The design for St Paul's approved by royal warrant in 1675 — a traditional long-plan cathedral, which Wren then transformed during construction using the king's permission to make variations.">Warrant Design</dfn>, a conventional long-plan church approved by royal warrant &mdash; to which Charles II added a quietly decisive clause, allowing Wren liberty to make &lsquo;variations, rather ornamental than essential&rsquo; as the work proceeded. Wren used that licence to transform the approved design, and what rose from 1675 was far closer to his own vision, crowned in the end by the dome the clergy had refused on paper.</p>

St Paul&rsquo;s is what the new science looked like in stone: structural problems attacked with geometry and experiment. <h2 data-narration-id="n8">Paying for It: The Coal Tax</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A bankrupt-ish Crown (Lesson 13) could not simply buy a cathedral. The money came instead from the Rebuilding Acts&rsquo; <dfn class="term" data-def="The duty charged on coal shipped into London under the Rebuilding Acts, which funded the rebuilt City churches and St Paul's.">coal tax</dfn> &mdash; a duty on every ton of coal landed in London, the fuel of the city&rsquo;s hearths and trades. It was a shrewd instrument: broad, steady, collected at the port, and paid in pennies by everyone who warmed themselves in the capital. London, in effect, rebuilt its own cathedral by heating itself. The same fund rebuilt dozens of Wren&rsquo;s parish churches, whose steeples rose around St Paul&rsquo;s in a single co-ordinated skyline &mdash; the most visible public-works programme of the age.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Building Rises</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The foundation stone was laid in June 1675, and by the end of Charles II&rsquo;s reign in 1685 the choir walls stood well advanced &mdash; a working answer, in Portland stone, to anyone who doubted the City would recover. The cathedral took thirty-five years in all: the choir opened for the thanksgiving service for the Peace of Ryswick in December 1697, and Parliament declared the work complete in 1711, with Wren still alive to see it &mdash; a cathedral begun and finished under one architect, something no medieval builder ever managed. The dome, rising some 365 feet, made a new crown for London&rsquo;s skyline on the site where the lightning-struck spire had once stood.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how was the rebuilding of St Paul's paid for, and what else did that fund build?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The coal tax under the Rebuilding Acts financed St Paul&rsquo;s and the rebuilt City churches together &mdash; a co-ordinated skyline of Wren steeples around one dome, paid for by a duty on the fuel Londoners burned.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">What the Site Shows About Restoration England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Each layer of the story is evidence. The decayed old cathedral shows the scars the Restoration inherited &mdash; Reformation, neglect and Civil War abuse written into the fabric. The Fire shows the fragility of the wooden city (Lesson 6). The design battle shows a nation negotiating between tradition and the new &mdash; clergy defending the English past, a scientist-architect importing classical Europe, a king umpiring with a clause. The coal tax shows practical, parliamentary finance doing what the royal purse could not. And the rising walls show confidence itself: a regime that had survived plague, fire and the Dutch in three consecutive years (Lessons 5, 6 and 13) declaring in Portland stone that London&rsquo;s future would outshine its past.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Using the Site in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Chain features to meanings. Old St Paul&rsquo;s stabled and spireless &rarr; the inheritance of civil war; the crypt of burned books &rarr; the Fire&rsquo;s cost to trade and learning; the Great Model &rarr; the ambition of the new science; the clergy&rsquo;s rejection and the Warrant &rarr; the pull of tradition; the &lsquo;ornamental&rsquo; clause &rarr; how Charles II managed rivals; the coal tax &rarr; recovery financed by ordinary Londoners; the walls rising by 1685 &rarr; confidence restored within your period. Feature, meaning, link to the statement &mdash; and keep your evidence anchored in 1660&ndash;1685, using the later completion only as the outcome the period made possible.</p>

<ul>

  <li data-narration-id="n20">The design battle ran from the rejected Great Model of 1673-4 to the 1675 Warrant Design, whose royal 'ornamental variations' clause let Wren build most of his own vision anyway.</li>

</ul>
