<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Navy's Darkest Week</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In June 1667 a Dutch fleet sailed into the mouth of the Thames, captured the fort guarding the River Medway, broke the chain protecting England&rsquo;s premier naval dockyard, burned warships at their moorings and towed away the flagship of the Royal Navy. It is often called the worst humiliation in the navy&rsquo;s history, and it is your specified site for 2027. The 16-mark question will name the Medway and ask how far a study of the site supports a statement &mdash; so this lesson works through the river, its defences, the six days of the raid, and why the environment let it happen.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why the Fleet Was Laid Up: The Money Ran Out</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The raid begins with a decision made months earlier. By 1667 the Second Anglo-Dutch War had drained a treasury already wrecked by the Plague of 1665, which had shut London&rsquo;s trade, and the Great Fire of 1666, which had burned its taxable wealth. Parliament&rsquo;s grants were spent, sailors were being paid in <dfn class="term" data-def="Paper promises of future payment issued to unpaid sailors; widely discounted, resented, and a cause of desertion.">tickets</dfn> instead of coin, and peace talks were already under way at Breda. So the government chose economy: the great ships of the battle fleet were <dfn class="term" data-def="Taken out of active service and moored without full crews, stores or rigging; the state of England's biggest warships at Chatham in 1667.">laid up</dfn> at their moorings in the Medway, guarded by forts and a chain, while smaller ships harried Dutch trade. England&rsquo;s main defence in the summer of 1667 was the assumption that the Dutch would not dare come up the river.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: why were England's biggest warships sitting unmanned at Chatham in June 1667?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Laying up the battle fleet was a deliberate policy of a bankrupt government &mdash; after Plague, Fire and two years of war there was no money to man it. The Medway raid was the price of that decision.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the River: Sheerness, the Chain and Chatham</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Medway joins the Thames estuary at Sheerness on the Isle of Sheppey, where a fort &mdash; still unfinished in 1667 &mdash; covered the entrance. From there the river winds a dozen miles inland to Chatham dockyard, England&rsquo;s greatest naval arsenal, through a channel that is narrow, shallow in places and banked with mud, navigable for big ships only on the tide and only with pilots who knew it. The defenders&rsquo; trump card lay across the river at Gillingham: a great iron <dfn class="term" data-def="A heavy iron boom stretched across the Medway at Gillingham to block ships from reaching Chatham dockyard; the Dutch broke it on 12 June 1667.">chain</dfn>, floated on stages, guarded by batteries and guardships. Upstream again stood Upnor Castle, an Elizabethan gun fort facing the dockyard moorings. On paper, the river was a gauntlet no enemy could run. In practice, almost every layer was underfunded, undermanned or unfinished.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Six Days in June</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Dutch fleet under Michiel de Ruyter &mdash; with Cornelis de Witt aboard carrying the government&rsquo;s orders &mdash; appeared in the Thames estuary in early June. On 10 June the Dutch landed at Sheerness, whose unfinished fort was held by a small garrison; it fell quickly, and with it the mouth of the Medway. While panic gathered, the Dutch felt their way upriver on the tide. On 12 June they came to the chain. Guns on the shore were short of crews and platforms; the guardships were undermanned skeletons. The Dutch forced the chain, the guardships behind it were burned or captured, and the way to the fleet lay open. That afternoon boarding parties took the <em>Royal Charles</em> &mdash; the eighty-gun flagship that had carried Charles II home at the Restoration &mdash; almost without a fight, her skeleton crew having fled.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">On 13 June the Dutch pushed to the moorings under Upnor Castle, whose guns, and hastily gathered shore batteries, at last put up real resistance. It was not enough to save the ships. Fireships burned three of the navy&rsquo;s greatest &mdash; <em>Royal Oak</em>, <em>Loyal London</em> and <em>Royal James</em> &mdash; at their moorings while their few defenders swam for shore. On 14 June the Dutch dropped back downriver with the tide, towing the <em>Royal Charles</em> as a prize, and stayed in the estuary for weeks, blockading London&rsquo;s trade. Around a dozen English warships had been burned, sunk or taken &mdash; several of them scuttled by the defenders themselves in the panic to block the channel.</p>

  <p>The captured flagship <em>Royal Charles</em> was towed to the Netherlands as a trophy. Her carved stern decoration survives in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam &mdash; the site&rsquo;s most famous relic, and a measure of the humiliation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Why the Defences Failed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Each layer of the gauntlet failed for a reason you can name. Sheerness fort was unfinished because the money to build it had run out. The chain&rsquo;s supporting batteries lacked platforms and gunners. The guardships were undermanned because the sailors were unpaid. The Duke of Albemarle, sent down when the alarm finally rose, found almost nothing ready and resorted to sinking ships to block the channel. Even the river itself &mdash; the shoals and tides that should have baffled an enemy &mdash; had been handed to the Dutch by expert pilots. The environment did not fail England; England failed to man the environment. That sentence, evidenced feature by feature, is close to a complete site answer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Panic, Blame and Peace</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In London the raid produced something close to invasion panic: guns audible downriver, rumours of French landings, runs on the goldsmiths. Samuel Pepys &mdash; navy administrator and diarist, and our best witness &mdash; sent his wife and his gold out of the city and expected the government to fall. Scapegoats were found quickly: Peter Pett, the resident Commissioner at Chatham, was sent to the Tower and blamed in Parliament for the loss of ships he had begged for men to move. The deeper consequences came within months. The Treaty of Breda in July ended the war on terms England was in no position to argue with. In the autumn the king&rsquo;s chief minister, Clarendon, was driven from office and into exile &mdash; the raid&rsquo;s political bill. And the navy&rsquo;s administration and the Medway&rsquo;s fortifications &mdash; Sheerness among them &mdash; were rebuilt in earnest, because no government intended to explain a second Medway.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Using the Site in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Chain each feature to a consequence. The winding shoal channel explains why pilots &mdash; and deserters &mdash; mattered; Sheerness&rsquo;s unfinished walls explain why the river mouth fell in a day; the broken chain at Gillingham explains why Chatham&rsquo;s moorings were reachable; the laid-up, unmanned fleet explains why first-rate warships died without a battle; and behind every feature stands the same cause &mdash; a treasury emptied by war, Plague and Fire. Whatever statement the question offers, that web of feature, failure and finance is your evidence.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The raid grew from a policy decision: a treasury broken by war, Plague and Fire laid up the battle fleet at Chatham and left the Medway's defences unfinished and unmanned.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The site was a layered gauntlet — Sheerness fort, the shoal channel, the Gillingham chain, Upnor Castle — and every layer failed for a nameable, mostly financial, reason.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Between 10 and 14 June 1667 the Dutch took Sheerness, broke the chain, burned great ships at their moorings and towed away the flagship Royal Charles, whose stern survives in the Rijksmuseum.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The consequences were swift: panic in London, Pett scapegoated, peace signed at Breda, Clarendon driven from office, and a rebuilt system of naval defence — the raid taught the lessons the site question asks about.</li>
</ul>
