--- DESCRIPTION ---
Three wars with the Dutch, the Navigation Acts, and what mercantilism meant in practice.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mercantilism: A Zero-Sum World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The seventeenth-century economic theory that the world&rsquo;s wealth was fixed; nations grew rich by exporting more than they imported, hoarding bullion and protecting colonial trade through monopoly companies.">Mercantilism</dfn> shaped Restoration economic policy. Its core assumption was that the world&rsquo;s wealth was fixed: one nation&rsquo;s gain in trade was another nation&rsquo;s loss. To prosper, a nation must export more than it imported, hoard precious metal (bullion), found colonies that supplied raw materials and bought back finished goods, and protect its trade with monopoly chartered companies. The Dutch Republic, with its tiny population and vast trading fleet, was the model and the rival: by 1660 the Dutch carried more freight by tonnage than every other European nation combined.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Navigation Acts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The first <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of seventeenth-century English laws restricting colonial trade to English and colonial ships, requiring listed goods to land in England before re-export, and protecting English fisheries.">Navigation Acts</dfn> had been passed in 1651 by the Commonwealth, but the Restoration Parliament strengthened them. The Act of 1660 required that goods carried to or from English colonies must travel in ships built and owned in England or its colonies, with English masters and three-quarters English crews. The Act listed &lsquo;enumerated&rsquo; goods &mdash; sugar, tobacco, cotton, ginger, dyestuffs &mdash; that could only be sent to England, even if their final market was elsewhere. The Staple Act of 1663 added that European goods bound for the colonies must first be landed in an English port and pay duties before re-export. The Act of 1673 added export duties on those colonial goods sent direct between colonies.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three main Navigation Acts of the 1660s and one effect of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Navigation Acts of 1660, 1663 and 1673 required colonial trade to use English or colonial ships, listed enumerated goods (sugar, tobacco, cotton) that must land in England, and forced European goods bound for the colonies through English ports. Their target was Dutch shipping.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Second Dutch War, 1665&ndash;67</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The first major conflict came in 1665. England declared war over trade rivalry, the seizure of New Amsterdam (renamed New York in honour of the Duke of York) the year before, and skirmishes off West Africa. Early English success at the Battle of Lowestoft on 3 June 1665 &mdash; in which the Dutch flagship blew up &mdash; gave way to disease and money problems on the English fleet through 1666. In June 1667 the war ended in humiliation: Admiral Michiel de Ruyter sailed up the river Medway, broke the chain at Chatham and burned much of the English fleet at anchor. The flagship &lsquo;Royal Charles&rsquo; was towed back to the Netherlands as a trophy. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Dutch raid in June 1667 in which Admiral De Ruyter sailed up the Medway, burned much of the English fleet at Chatham and towed away the flagship &lsquo;Royal Charles&rsquo;; the worst defeat in English naval history.">Medway raid</dfn> was the worst defeat in English naval history.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Treaty of Breda and Pepys&rsquo;s Reforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The war ended at the Treaty of Breda in July 1667. Under it England kept New York and New Jersey; the Dutch kept Suriname and the spice island of Run; both sides relaxed the Navigation Acts slightly to ease trade. The political fallout was severe: Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon, was impeached and fled to France in November 1667. Samuel Pepys, the naval administrator, used the post-war period to push through reforms of <dfn class="term" data-def="The supply of food, drink and clothing to the navy; reformed by Samuel Pepys after 1668 to fix the chronic crisis of supply that had crippled the Second Dutch War fleet.">naval victualling</dfn>, of pay and of shipbuilding contracts. New ships of the line were ordered, the dockyards at Chatham, Portsmouth and Plymouth were upgraded, and a coherent system of naval administration began to emerge.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Naval battles in the Restoration moved from chaotic mêlées to disciplined formations. Under <dfn class="term" data-def="Naval tactic in which a fleet sailed in a single column, each ship firing its broadside in turn at the enemy line; standard from the mid-seventeenth century after the Fighting Instructions of 1653.">line of battle</dfn> tactics, fleets sailed in a single column so that every ship&rsquo;s broadside cannon could fire on the enemy line in turn. The English Fighting Instructions of 1653 had codified the tactic, and the Restoration navy refined it. Ships were classified into six rates by gun count; the largest, the three-decked &lsquo;ship of the line&rsquo;, carried 90 to 100 guns. Pepys&rsquo;s shipbuilding programme of the 1670s and 1680s created the early ships of the line that would fight under William III against Louis XIV in the 1690s.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Third Dutch War, 1672&ndash;74</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Five years after the Medway humiliation, Charles II went to war on the Dutch again &mdash; this time as Louis XIV&rsquo;s ally under the secret Treaty of Dover. The Third Dutch War began in 1672. The English fleet under James, Duke of York fought De Ruyter at Solebay in May 1672 to a bloody draw. On land, the French overran the Dutch Republic, but the Dutch broke the dykes to flood the country and held out. Parliament was furious about both the Catholic alliance with France and the costs of the war. In 1674 it forced Charles to make a separate peace at the Second Treaty of Westminster, two years before France finished its own war. The political effect was significant: Parliament had used the power of the purse to override royal foreign policy, and Charles never again pushed an open military alliance with France.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Spain and France framed Charles II&rsquo;s naval policy throughout. Spain remained the old enemy of the Cromwellian period, but its empire was visibly declining and its main use to England was as the source of the privateering commissions that legitimised raids on its Caribbean colonies. France was the rising danger: Louis XIV&rsquo;s navy, built up by Colbert from 1661, grew from twenty ships to over 270 by the 1680s. Charles II danced between the two: he allied with France against the Dutch under the Treaty of Dover, but was forced by Parliament to sign the Triple Alliance with the Dutch and Sweden against France in 1668. By the end of the reign Parliament feared France far more than it feared Spain &mdash; a shift that would define William III&rsquo;s wars against Louis after 1689.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Effects of the Wars</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Despite the Medway disaster, English shipping grew strongly across the reign. By the 1680s the English merchant fleet was approaching the size of the Dutch and pulling ahead in colonial routes. The Navigation Acts shifted carrying-trade business from Dutch shippers to English ones. Sugar imports from the Caribbean, tobacco from Virginia and Maryland, and African gold and slaves from West Africa increasingly travelled in English bottoms. The customs revenue from this booming trade was what kept Charles II solvent during his rule without Parliament after 1681. Pepys&rsquo;s naval reforms left a stronger navy and a working dockyard system. The age of Dutch maritime dominance was ending.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: name the two Anglo-Dutch wars of the reign and the place each was famous for &mdash; one for English humiliation, one for an inconclusive battle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Second Dutch War (1665&ndash;67) ended in humiliation at the Medway, where De Ruyter burned the English fleet at Chatham. The Third Dutch War (1672&ndash;74) was fought as Louis XIV&rsquo;s ally; Parliament forced peace at Westminster in 1674.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Mercantilism saw trade as zero-sum, and the Navigation Acts of 1660, 1663 and 1673 were designed to capture colonial freight from the Dutch by requiring English ships and English ports.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Second Dutch War (1665&ndash;67) ended in disaster when De Ruyter burned the English fleet at the Medway in June 1667 and towed away the flagship &lsquo;Royal Charles&rsquo;.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Third Dutch War (1672&ndash;74), fought alongside France under the secret Treaty of Dover, ended early when Parliament forced Charles II to make peace; Pepys&rsquo;s reforms produced the first generation of English ships of the line.</li>
</ul>
