--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Closed Theatres to Royal Patents</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In 1642 the Puritan Parliament had ordered London&rsquo;s theatres closed; in 1660 Charles II reopened them. He did so by issuing two royal letters patent, granting a near-monopoly on professional drama in London to two companies: the King&rsquo;s Company under Thomas Killigrew, and the Duke&rsquo;s Company under William Davenant. For most of the reign, only these two licensed playhouses could perform new spoken drama. Theatre was therefore not an open market &mdash; it was a piece of court patronage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Women on the Stage from 1660&ndash;61</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A second royal letter, issued by Charles in 1662, made it explicit: women&rsquo;s parts were to be played by women. Before 1660, female roles had been performed by boys. The first known professional <dfn class="term" data-def="A woman who acts professionally for a paying audience &mdash; a profession new to England from 1660 when women began to be paid to play female roles on the public stage.">actress</dfn> on the public English stage was probably Margaret Hughes, playing Desdemona in &lsquo;Othello&rsquo; on 8 December 1660. Within a few years a generation of star actresses &mdash; including <dfn class="term" data-def="Eleanor Gwyn (1650&ndash;1687), one of the first English actresses, a star of the King&rsquo;s Company and a long-term mistress of Charles II.">Nell Gwyn</dfn>, who became Charles II&rsquo;s mistress, and Elizabeth Barry, the leading tragedienne of the next generation &mdash; was working full-time in London.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which two theatre companies received royal patents in 1660 and when did women first perform professionally on the English stage?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1660 Charles II granted patents to the King&rsquo;s Company (Killigrew) and the Duke&rsquo;s Company (Davenant), creating a two-theatre monopoly in London. From December 1660 women played female roles professionally for the first time in English history.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Restoration Comedy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of comic drama written for the London stage between 1660 and around 1700, marked by witty dialogue, sexual intrigue, social satire of the elite, and the use of professional actresses.">Restoration comedy</dfn> was the period&rsquo;s defining new genre. Plays such as Etherege&rsquo;s &lsquo;The Man of Mode&rsquo; (1676), Wycherley&rsquo;s &lsquo;The Country Wife&rsquo; (1675) and Congreve&rsquo;s &lsquo;The Way of the World&rsquo; (1700) used witty dialogue, sexual intrigue and satire of fashionable London society. The plots were often bawdy, the characters self-aware, and the audience overwhelmingly drawn from the elite. Theatres were small (around 700 seats), candlelit, and rowdy; the king himself often attended, sometimes accompanied by his mistresses.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Female playwrights also broke through. Aphra Behn became the first English woman to make a living from writing. Her plays &mdash; &lsquo;The Rover&rsquo; (1677) is the most famous &mdash; were box-office successes; her novel &lsquo;Oroonoko&rsquo; (1688), an early prose narrative about an enslaved African prince, has been read ever since. Behn was attacked for her sex (Pope later sneered that &lsquo;the stage how loosely does Astrea tread&rsquo;), but she earned, lived independently, and is buried in Westminster Abbey.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A typical performance began at 3:30 p.m. and lasted around three hours. Audiences ate oranges (sold by &lsquo;orange-girls&rsquo; &mdash; Nell Gwyn famously started as one) and chatted through the play. The pit, occupied by gentlemen and writers, faced raked seating in the boxes. New plays alternated with revivals of Shakespeare in heavily adapted forms: William Davenant rewrote &lsquo;Macbeth&rsquo; with singing witches, and Nahum Tate gave &lsquo;King Lear&rsquo; a happy ending in 1681 that held the stage for over 150 years. Spectacle and scenery &mdash; new movable flats, lit by candles &mdash; meant Restoration theatre looked very different from the bare Globe of Shakespeare&rsquo;s day.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Coffee Houses: London&rsquo;s Penny Universities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The first London coffee house had opened in 1652, before the Restoration; by the 1670s the city had over 80, by 1700 several hundred. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A public premises serving coffee and providing newspapers, chocolate and tobacco, used as informal meeting places for news, business and political talk &mdash; nicknamed &lsquo;penny universities&rsquo;.">coffee house</dfn> was open to any man who could pay a penny for a dish of coffee. Newspapers were laid out free, gossip and political news circulated, and many trades clustered around particular houses: Lloyd&rsquo;s in Lombard Street became the centre of marine insurance, Jonathan&rsquo;s the heart of stock-jobbing in the City. Contemporaries called them <dfn class="term" data-def="A nickname for coffee houses, reflecting the idea that for a penny a man could hear the latest political and intellectual debate.">penny universities</dfn> because the conversation was an education for the price of a drink.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Charles II disliked them. In December 1675 he issued a royal proclamation suppressing coffee houses on the grounds that they encouraged sedition and idleness. The reaction was immediate: shopkeepers, customers and judges objected, and the proclamation was withdrawn within days. The episode shows both the perceived political power of the coffee houses and the limits of royal control over public opinion.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Coffee houses were almost always male spaces: women normally entered only as servers or as the wives running the business. A 1674 satirical pamphlet, &lsquo;The Women&rsquo;s Petition Against Coffee&rsquo;, mocked husbands for spending all day talking politics rather than at home; an answering &lsquo;Men&rsquo;s Petition&rsquo; replied in kind. The exclusion of women from this central new public space contrasts sharply with the new visibility of actresses on the stage and shows that the &lsquo;public sphere&rsquo; opened up unevenly.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Status of Women</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">For most women the legal framework remained the doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="The English common-law principle that on marriage a woman&rsquo;s legal identity was absorbed into her husband&rsquo;s, so she could not own property, sign contracts or sue independently.">coverture</dfn>: a married woman could not own property, sign contracts or sue in her own right. But the visible gains were real. Actresses became celebrities; female playwrights and poets such as Aphra Behn and Katherine Philips earned by their writing; women published in print on religion (the Quaker Margaret Fell argued for women&rsquo;s right to preach); and royal mistresses such as Barbara Villiers and Louise de K&eacute;roualle exerted real political influence at court. Restoration culture is therefore a story of widened public visibility against unchanged underlying law &mdash; a tension worth holding in any answer about women&rsquo;s status.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why coffee houses were nicknamed &lsquo;penny universities&rsquo; and why Charles II tried to ban them in 1675.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Coffee houses charged a penny for entry and offered free newspapers, becoming the engine of news, business and political talk. Charles II tried to ban them in December 1675 but withdrew the proclamation within days under public pressure.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Charles II reopened the theatres in 1660, granting patents to two licensed companies and ordering that female parts be played by women &mdash; the start of the English actress as a profession.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Restoration comedy and women playwrights such as Aphra Behn made the new theatre culturally and politically central, while audiences were drawn from a narrow but powerful elite.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Coffee houses spread through London as &lsquo;penny universities&rsquo;, transforming the circulation of news and business; Charles II&rsquo;s failed ban of 1675 showed the limits of royal control.</li>
</ul>
