--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a bakery fire became a city-destroying inferno and what rose from the ashes under Wren.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pudding Lane, 2 September 1666</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Just after one o&rsquo;clock in the morning of Sunday 2 September 1666, a fire broke out in the bakery of Thomas Farriner in Pudding Lane, near London Bridge. London had survived dozens of small fires; this one became the <dfn class="term" data-def="The fire that destroyed about four-fifths of the City of London between 2 and 6 September 1666, beginning at Thomas Farriner&rsquo;s bakery in Pudding Lane.">Great Fire</dfn>. Three things made it different: a long dry summer that had baked the timber houses, an unusually strong easterly wind, and the densely packed warehouses near the Thames full of pitch, oil, hemp, brandy and coal &mdash; all highly flammable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Bloodworth&rsquo;s Fatal Indecision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">When the Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, was woken before dawn and asked to authorise the demolition of houses to make a firebreak, he reportedly snorted, &lsquo;A woman might piss it out.&rsquo; By the time he changed his mind on Sunday afternoon, the wind had carried the fire halfway up Cannon Street. Houses in seventeenth-century London were timber-framed, jettied (each storey overhanging the one below), and packed against each other with thatch in poorer streets. Once a row caught, the whole row went up.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where, when and how did the Great Fire start, and what did Lord Mayor Bloodworth do wrong?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Great Fire broke out at Thomas Farriner&rsquo;s bakery in Pudding Lane in the early hours of 2 September 1666. Lord Mayor Bloodworth refused to authorise demolitions early enough; by the time he agreed, an easterly wind had spread the flames across the City.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The King and the Duke of York Take Charge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">By Monday 3 September Charles II and his brother James, Duke of York, had taken personal command of firefighting from the Lord Mayor. They worked the leather-bucket lines themselves and authorised the use of <dfn class="term" data-def="Black powder used as a propellant and demolition explosive in the seventeenth century; ordered by Charles II during the Fire of 1666 to blow up houses and create firebreaks.">gunpowder</dfn> to blow up rows of houses ahead of the flames and create firebreaks. James personally directed gunpowder demolitions on Pudding Lane, Tower Street and elsewhere, and the king himself rode to Smithfield to organise water carts. Their visible leadership was politically important: Pepys recorded crowds cheering them, in marked contrast to royal absence during the Plague of the previous year.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Scale of Destruction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">By the time the fire died on Thursday 6 September, the cost was staggering. About four-fifths of the walled City was destroyed: 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, the Royal Exchange, the Guildhall, the Custom House, four of the City&rsquo;s gates and old St Paul&rsquo;s Cathedral. Officially only six people died (the real toll was probably higher among the poor and uncounted). Around 70,000 of the City&rsquo;s 80,000 inhabitants were made homeless overnight. They camped on Moorfields, Tower Hill and Lincoln&rsquo;s Inn Fields. Charles II personally rode out to reassure the refugees.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The Second Dutch War was being fought, and Catholic France had recently joined the Dutch side. Many Londoners refused to believe a city could burn by accident. Stories spread of French and Dutch agents firing the city deliberately. A French watchmaker, Robert Hubert, confessed under questioning to starting the fire and was hanged at Tyburn in October 1666; the privy council itself privately doubted the confession (Hubert had been at sea on 2 September). The Monument to the Fire, completed in 1677, originally carried a Latin inscription blaming &lsquo;the treachery and malice of the Popish faction&rsquo;; it was chiselled off in the reign of James II and recut after 1689. The rumour that the Fire had been Catholic arson endured, feeding the Popish Plot panic of 1678.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Rebuilding Acts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Within a fortnight Charles II had appointed a commission to plan reconstruction. <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1667 and 1670 Acts of Parliament that regulated the rebuilding of the City of London after the Great Fire, requiring brick and stone houses, wider streets and standardised storey heights.">Rebuilding Acts</dfn> were passed in 1667 and 1670. They required all new houses to be built in brick and stone, not timber. Streets were widened and pavements added. House heights were standardised by class of street, with four set types of dwelling. The hearth tax was redirected to fund firefighting equipment, and a new tax on coal coming into the port of London was used to pay for the rebuilding of public buildings and parish churches.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Wren&rsquo;s Plan and What Was Actually Built</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Within days of the fire, the architect <dfn class="term" data-def="Sir Christopher Wren (1632&ndash;1723), astronomer and architect; designed the rebuilt St Paul&rsquo;s Cathedral and 51 City churches after the Great Fire, and was Surveyor of the King&rsquo;s Works from 1669.">Sir Christopher Wren</dfn> presented Charles II with a sweeping plan for a baroque London of broad avenues, piazzas and rationalised land plots. So did John Evelyn, Robert Hooke and others. None of these plans was adopted. The reason was simple: too many private freeholders held the medieval plots and the Crown had no money to compensate them. London was rebuilt on its old street pattern. Wren&rsquo;s real triumph was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The new domed cathedral on Ludgate Hill, designed by Sir Christopher Wren, built between 1675 and 1710 to replace the medieval cathedral lost in the Great Fire.">St Paul&rsquo;s Cathedral</dfn> (1675&ndash;1710) and 51 of the 87 lost parish churches, designed in his office between 1670 and 1700. Funded largely by a coal tax voted by Parliament, they gave the rebuilt City a unique horizon of white-stone steeples crowned by Wren&rsquo;s dome.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: which two new types of building material did the 1667 Rebuilding Act require, and which 51 buildings did Christopher Wren design after the Fire?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1667 Rebuilding Act required houses to be built in brick or stone with standardised heights, on wider streets. Wren&rsquo;s grand street plan was rejected because of private property rights, but he designed 51 new City churches and the new St Paul&rsquo;s Cathedral, finished in 1710.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Samuel Pepys is the historian&rsquo;s gift on the Fire. His diary entry for 2 September 1666 records being woken at three in the morning by his maid Jane to see the glow over the river. Pepys took a boat downstream to inspect the spread, returned to inform Charles II in person at Whitehall, and recorded the king&rsquo;s order to demolish houses. Over the next four days he saved his own goods (including a parmesan cheese, famously buried in the garden), watched the fire from boats and rooftops and recorded conversations with refugees on Moorfields. Other eyewitnesses included John Evelyn, who walked through the smoking ruins on 7 September and recorded the heat of the stones rising through his shoes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Effects on London</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Fire&rsquo;s effects pulled in different directions. The trauma was real: thousands lost everything, and London paid for the reconstruction for forty years. But the rebuilt City was fireproof in a way the old one had not been; the Plague did not return on the 1665 scale, partly because brick and stone gave rats fewer hiding places; and Wren&rsquo;s churches and cathedral became one of the world&rsquo;s most admired urban skylines. The Fire also pushed wealthy Londoners west to Covent Garden, St James&rsquo;s and the developing West End, accelerating a long-term shift in the city&rsquo;s social geography. The Catholic arson rumour, meanwhile, refused to die, and would resurface in the Popish Plot panic of 1678.</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The Great Fire of 2&ndash;6 September 1666 began at Thomas Farriner&rsquo;s bakery in Pudding Lane, was worsened by Lord Mayor Bloodworth&rsquo;s indecision, and destroyed about four-fifths of the walled City.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Charles II and James, Duke of York personally commanded firefighting and gunpowder demolitions; rumours of Catholic arson endured and would feed the Popish Plot panic of 1678.</li>

</ul>
