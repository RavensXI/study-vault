--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Titus Oates’s lies and a real Whig assassination plan brought England to the edge of revolution.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country Primed for Panic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1678, English Protestants were jumpy. The fear of <dfn class="term" data-def="Hostility towards the Roman Catholic Church and its followers, often linked in this period to fear of foreign invasion and absolute monarchy.">popery</dfn> ran deep: France under Louis XIV was the dominant Catholic power, Charles II had signed the Treaty of Dover (1670) with French money attached, and his brother and heir &mdash; James, Duke of York &mdash; had openly become Catholic by 1673. Into that anxiety walked a disgraced Anglican clergyman called Titus Oates, with a story that would change the politics of the reign.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Popish Plot, 1678</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In the autumn of 1678 Oates and his collaborator, Israel Tonge, swore on oath before a London magistrate that <dfn class="term" data-def="Members of the Society of Jesus, a Catholic religious order founded in 1540, often associated in Protestant minds with secret political activity.">Jesuits</dfn> were planning to assassinate Charles II, replace him with his Catholic brother James, and re-impose Catholicism by force. Oates named priests, dates and meeting places; almost all of it was invented. But the plot acquired terrifying credibility on 17 October 1678, when the magistrate who had taken Oates&rsquo;s deposition, <dfn class="term" data-def="The London magistrate who took Titus Oates&rsquo;s sworn statement and was found murdered on Primrose Hill in October 1678.">Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey</dfn>, was found dead on Primrose Hill, run through with his own sword.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the two men who fabricated the Popish Plot and the magistrate whose murder made it credible.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Popish Plot of 1678 was invented by Titus Oates and Israel Tonge. Although the assassination conspiracy was a fabrication, the unsolved murder of Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey on 17 October 1678 turned a wild story into a national emergency.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Oates was believed</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Oates fitted his lies to existing fears. Anti-Catholic feeling was reinforced by living memory of the Gunpowder Plot of 1605 and the Great Fire of 1666 (still rumoured to be a Catholic arson). Letters written by James&rsquo;s secretary, Edward Coleman, to French Jesuits were genuinely incriminating about wider Catholic plans, even though Coleman knew nothing of the assassination story. When those letters were seized, they seemed to confirm Oates &mdash; and Coleman was hanged, drawn and quartered in December 1678.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Hysteria and Executions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Parliament passed the Second Test Act of 1678, barring Catholics from sitting in either House of Parliament &mdash; a clause aimed at James himself, who had to leave the Lords. Around 35 Catholics were executed between 1678 and 1681 on the basis of Oates&rsquo;s perjury. They included Oliver Plunkett, the Catholic Archbishop of Armagh, hanged at Tyburn in 1681 and the last Catholic martyr executed in England. Catholics were ordered to leave London, and a wave of trials and pamphlets swept the country.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">It was inside this hysteria that the Earl of Shaftesbury launched the Exclusion campaign to bar James from the throne &mdash; the subject of the next lesson. The Popish Plot is therefore the trigger for what historians now call the Exclusion Crisis: real political consequences flowed from a fictional conspiracy.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Rye House Plot, 1683</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The mood swung the other way after Charles II dissolved the Oxford Parliament in March 1681 and ruled without one. Frustrated Whig leaders began to consider violence. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A Whig conspiracy in 1683 to ambush and kill Charles II and James at Rye House in Hertfordshire as they returned from the Newmarket races; betrayed before it could be attempted.">Rye House Plot</dfn> was a real conspiracy: a group of radicals planned to ambush Charles and James at Rye House, a moated farmhouse in Hertfordshire, on their journey home from the Newmarket races. A fire at Newmarket forced the king to leave early, the attack never happened, and one of the plotters &mdash; Josiah Keeling &mdash; informed the government in June 1683.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The reaction was severe. Lord William Russell was beheaded in July 1683, Algernon Sidney in December 1683; the Earl of Essex died in the Tower (officially suicide). The Duke of Monmouth fled to the Continent. Charles used the plot to purge corporations of Whig sympathisers, force boroughs to surrender their charters and rebuild their governments under Tory loyalists. By 1684 the Crown looked stronger than at any point since 1660.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Tory reaction in the corporations</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Borough corporations elected MPs and ran local justice, so controlling them mattered for any future Parliament. Beginning with London in 1683, the government used quo warranto writs &mdash; legal challenges to a corporation&rsquo;s charter &mdash; to force boroughs to surrender and accept new charters that gave the Crown a veto over key officers. By the time Charles died, more than fifty corporations had been remodelled. James II inherited a Parliament of constituencies stacked with Tories.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: who was executed for the Rye House Plot, and what did Charles II do to corporations afterwards?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Rye House Plot of 1683 was a real Whig conspiracy to assassinate Charles II and James, betrayed before it could be carried out. Russell and Sidney were executed, Monmouth fled abroad, and Charles used the plot to purge Whig corporations and govern without Parliament until his death in 1685.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Aftermath: A Reign Saved by a Lie and a Plot</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The two episodes pull in opposite directions. The Popish Plot was an invention that nearly destroyed the monarchy; the Rye House Plot was real and ended up rescuing it. Together they show how raw fear of Catholic succession and republican conspiracy could be exploited by either side. Titus Oates was finally tried for perjury in 1685 under the new King James II, pilloried, whipped and imprisoned for life &mdash; though William of Orange would release him after the Glorious Revolution of 1688.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Popish Plot of 1678 was a fabrication by Titus Oates and Israel Tonge, but the murder of Sir Edmund Berry Godfrey gave it terrifying credibility, leading to around 35 Catholic executions and the Second Test Act.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">The Rye House Plot of 1683 was a real Whig conspiracy to assassinate Charles and James; its discovery led to the executions of Russell and Sidney and the flight of Monmouth.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Charles II used the Rye House revelations to purge corporations and rule without Parliament until his death in 1685, leaving the Crown stronger but the succession unresolved.</li>
</ul>
