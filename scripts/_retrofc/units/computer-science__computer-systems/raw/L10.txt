--- DESCRIPTION ---
How networks are attacked — malware, phishing, DoS — and the defences that stop them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Threats Facing a Network</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Almost every computer is now connected to a network, and that connection is exactly what attackers try to exploit. Keeping a network secure means understanding two things together: the threats that criminals use, and the defences that block them. Those threats aim at two very different weak spots &mdash; the technology itself, and the people who use it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six common forms of attack a network faces.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There are six common forms of attack you should be able to describe: malware, social engineering (such as phishing), brute-force attacks, denial of service attacks, data interception and theft, and SQL injection.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Malware</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The word malware is short for &lsquo;malicious software&rsquo;. <dfn class="term" data-def="Malicious software written to damage, disrupt or gain unauthorised access to a computer system.">Malware</dfn> is any program written to damage, disrupt or gain unauthorised access to a computer system. It usually reaches a machine when a user opens an infected email attachment, downloads a dodgy file, or plugs in an unknown USB stick.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Common types of malware</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">A virus hides inside a file or program and spreads to other files when that program is run, often corrupting data as it goes. A worm is similar but more dangerous on a network, because it can copy itself and spread between computers on its own, without anyone opening a file.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A Trojan is disguised as a useful or harmless program, so the user installs it themselves, only for it to open a back door for the attacker. Ransomware encrypts the user&rsquo;s files and demands a payment to unlock them, while spyware quietly records what the user does, such as capturing passwords as they are typed.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Social Engineering and Phishing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Tricking or manipulating people into giving away confidential information or access, rather than attacking the technology.">Social engineering</dfn> attacks the human user rather than the machine. Instead of breaking through technical defences, the attacker tricks a person into handing over information or access. Because it targets people, staff are often called the &lsquo;weak point&rsquo; &mdash; even the strongest firewall is useless if an employee simply gives their password away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The most common example is <dfn class="term" data-def="Sending fake emails or messages that pretend to be from a trusted source to trick people into revealing private details.">phishing</dfn>, where fake emails or text messages pretend to come from a trusted source, such as a bank or the IT department. They usually contain a link to a fake login page designed to steal whatever the victim types in. A sense of urgency, like &lsquo;your account will be closed today&rsquo;, is a classic warning sign.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Brute-Force Attacks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Repeatedly trying different password combinations until the correct one is found, usually using software.">brute-force attack</dfn> is an attempt to crack a password by trying every possible combination in turn until the right one is found. Attackers use software to test thousands of guesses per second, which is why short, common passwords such as &lsquo;123456&rsquo; fall almost instantly, while long, unusual passwords take far longer to break.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Denial of Service Attacks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Flooding a server with so many requests that it cannot respond to genuine users.">denial of service attack</dfn>, or DoS, floods a server with so many requests at once that it becomes overwhelmed and cannot respond to genuine users. The aim is usually not to steal data but to make a website or service unavailable, which can cost a business money and damage its reputation. When the flood comes from many computers at the same time, it is called a distributed denial of service, or DDoS.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Data Interception and Theft</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Stealing data as it is transmitted across a network, for example by capturing data packets.">Data interception and theft</dfn> means capturing data while it travels across a network. Using software called a packet sniffer, an attacker on an unsecured network &mdash; such as free public Wi-Fi &mdash; can read the packets passing by and pick out useful information like usernames and passwords.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">SQL Injection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Entering malicious database commands into an input box to view, change or damage the data behind a website.">SQL injection</dfn> targets the databases that sit behind many websites. If an input box, such as a login field, is not properly protected, an attacker can type in database commands instead of a normal answer.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How SQL injection works</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">Databases are searched using a language called SQL. When a website builds its search out of text typed by the user without checking it first, a carefully written entry can change the meaning of that search. This can trick the database into revealing every record, skipping a password check, or even deleting a whole table. The fix is to validate and clean all input before it ever reaches the database.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why people are called the weak point in security.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Many successful attacks rely on human error rather than clever code. People are described as the &lsquo;weak point&rsquo; of security because they can be tricked by social engineering, choose weak passwords, or click a malicious link &mdash; which is why staff training matters as much as the technology.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Finding and Removing Vulnerabilities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A vulnerability is any weakness an attacker could exploit. One of the best ways to find them is <dfn class="term" data-def="Deliberately attacking a system to find weaknesses before real criminals can exploit them.">penetration testing</dfn>, where security experts are paid to attack a system on purpose, in a controlled way, so that weak points can be discovered and fixed before real criminals find them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Once weaknesses are known, several prevention methods work together to close them:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="A program that scans a computer for malicious software and blocks, quarantines or removes anything it finds.">Anti-malware software</dfn> scans files and memory for malicious programs, then blocks, quarantines or removes anything it finds; it must be updated regularly to recognise new threats.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Hardware or software that monitors and filters traffic between a network and the outside world, blocking anything that breaks its rules.">firewall</dfn> monitors and filters the traffic passing between a network and the outside world, blocking any connection that breaks its rules.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="Settings that control what each user is allowed to see, use or change on a system.">User access levels</dfn> control what each person is allowed to see or change, so an ordinary user cannot reach the sensitive files that only an administrator should touch.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Strong passwords &mdash; long, with a mix of letters, numbers and symbols &mdash; slow down brute-force attacks, especially when combined with a limit on the number of login attempts.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28"><dfn class="term" data-def="Scrambling data using a key so that it cannot be understood by anyone who intercepts it without the key.">Encryption</dfn> scrambles data using a key, so that even if it is intercepted it is useless to the attacker without that key.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29"><dfn class="term" data-def="Protecting the actual hardware with measures such as locked doors, keycards and CCTV.">Physical security</dfn> protects the hardware itself with measures such as locked server rooms, keycard entry and CCTV, stopping anyone from simply walking up to a machine.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Layering your defences</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n30">No single method is enough on its own, so organisations layer them, an idea known as defence in depth. A firewall might block an attack from the internet, anti-malware catches anything that slips through, encryption protects the data if it is stolen anyway, and access levels limit the damage a single stolen account can do. If one layer fails, the others still stand.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three prevention methods and say what each one stops.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Prevention methods work best in combination: penetration testing finds the weaknesses, then firewalls, anti-malware, user access levels, strong passwords, encryption and physical security each block a different kind of attack.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Networks face six main forms of attack: malware, social engineering such as phishing, brute-force attacks, denial of service attacks, data interception and theft, and SQL injection.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">People are often the weakest point, so tricking a user is frequently easier for an attacker than defeating the technology.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Prevention methods &mdash; penetration testing, anti-malware, firewalls, user access levels, strong passwords, encryption and physical security &mdash; work best when layered together.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n32">Watch the command word closely. &lsquo;State&rsquo; or &lsquo;identify&rsquo; needs only a short answer, &lsquo;describe&rsquo; wants you to say what the attack or method actually is, and &lsquo;explain&rsquo; wants how or why it works. A very common mistake is muddling threats with prevention methods &mdash; if a question asks how to prevent an attack, do not simply name another attack. Whenever you give a prevention method, link it to the specific threat it reduces.</p>
