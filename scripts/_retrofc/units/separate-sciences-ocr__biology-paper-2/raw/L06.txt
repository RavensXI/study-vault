<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Variation Within a Species</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Individuals within a species are not identical &mdash; they show <dfn class="term" data-def="Differences in characteristics between individuals of the same species, caused by genetic differences, environmental differences, or a combination of both.">variation</dfn>. Some variation is <strong>genetic</strong> (arising from differences in alleles, such as blood type), some is <strong>environmental</strong> (caused by living conditions, such as a scar or language spoken), and much is a combination of both (height depends on genes but also on diet). Genetic variation arises through three main routes: <strong>mutations</strong> (random changes in DNA sequence), <strong>meiosis</strong> (the shuffling and random assortment of chromosomes during gamete formation), and <strong>sexual reproduction</strong> (combining alleles from two different parents).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Variation within a population is essential for natural selection. Without variation, all individuals would respond identically to environmental pressures &mdash; selection would have nothing to act upon. It is variation that makes evolution possible.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three sources of genetic variation? Give one example each of purely genetic and purely environmental variation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Variation can be genetic, environmental or both. Genetic variation comes from mutations, meiosis and sexual reproduction. Without variation, natural selection cannot occur.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Natural Selection and Evolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which organisms with characteristics best suited to their environment survive, reproduce and pass on their advantageous alleles, changing the characteristics of a population over time.">Natural selection</dfn> was proposed by Charles Darwin (1809&ndash;1882) and, independently, by Alfred Russel Wallace (1823&ndash;1913). Their joint theory was presented to the Linnean Society in 1858. The theory works through four linked steps:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">1. Within a population, individuals show <strong>variation</strong> in their characteristics.<br>
2. More offspring are produced than the environment can support, so individuals <strong>compete</strong> for limited resources (food, water, mates, space).<br>
3. Those with characteristics best suited to the environment are more likely to <strong>survive and reproduce</strong> &mdash; survival of the fittest.<br>
4. Survivors pass the advantageous <strong>alleles</strong> to their offspring, so over many generations the proportion of individuals with the beneficial trait increases.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">This cumulative change across generations is <dfn class="term" data-def="The gradual change in the inherited characteristics of a population over many generations, driven by natural selection.">evolution</dfn>. It is crucial to remember that individual organisms do not evolve &mdash; <strong>populations</strong> evolve as the frequency of advantageous alleles changes over time. Evolution has no direction or purpose; it simply reflects which traits happen to be best suited to the current environment.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of bacteria to survive exposure to antibiotics that would previously have killed them, resulting from natural selection favouring resistant mutations.">Antibiotic resistance</dfn> is a modern, observable example of natural selection. When a population of bacteria is exposed to an antibiotic, most individuals die. However, random mutations may have given some bacteria a resistance mechanism. These resistant individuals survive, reproduce rapidly (bacteria can divide every 20 minutes) and pass on the resistance allele. Over time, the resistant strain comes to dominate the population.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">MRSA (methicillin-resistant <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>) is a well-known example. To slow the spread of resistance, antibiotics should only be prescribed when necessary, patients must complete the full course, and overuse in agriculture should be minimised. New antibiotics are difficult to develop, so preserving the effectiveness of existing ones is critical.</p>

</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write out the four steps of natural selection without looking. Then explain how each step connects to the next.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Natural selection: variation &rarr; competition &rarr; survival and reproduction of the fittest &rarr; advantageous alleles inherited by offspring. Repeated over many generations, this drives evolution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Evidence for Evolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Evidence for evolution comes from several independent sources:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">&bull; <strong>The fossil record</strong> &mdash; fossils show gradual changes in organisms over millions of years, including transitional forms linking ancient and modern species. Fossils can be dated by the rock layers in which they are found (older strata lie deeper), by radiometric dating techniques, and by comparison with other fossils of known age. Gaps in the fossil record exist because fossilisation is rare, but new discoveries continue to fill these gaps.<br>
&bull; <strong>Comparative anatomy</strong> &mdash; different vertebrate species share similar underlying bone structures (the pentadactyl limb: five-digit structure found in human hands, bat wings and whale flippers), suggesting common ancestry despite very different functions.<br>
&bull; <strong>DNA analysis</strong> &mdash; closely related species share more DNA sequences. The more similar the DNA, the more recently the species diverged from a common ancestor. Molecular evidence now allows evolutionary trees to be constructed with great precision.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The peppered moth provides a well-documented example of natural selection within observable timescales. Before industrialisation, pale-coloured moths were better camouflaged on pale, lichen-covered tree bark; dark moths were eaten more often by birds. As industrial pollution darkened tree trunks with soot and killed lichens, dark moths gained a survival advantage. The proportion of dark moths increased substantially in industrial areas. When clean air legislation reduced pollution, the pale form recovered. This demonstrates that natural selection responds rapidly to environmental change.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The formation of a new species when populations of the same species become so genetically different, usually through geographical isolation, that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.">Speciation</dfn> occurs when populations of the same species become isolated &mdash; most commonly through a <strong>geographical barrier</strong> such as a mountain range, sea channel or river. Once isolated, the two populations face different environments and different selection pressures. Random mutations and natural selection cause each population to change in different directions over many generations. Eventually, the populations become so genetically different that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring. At this point, they are classified as separate species.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The permanent disappearance of a species when no living individuals remain, caused by failure to adapt to environmental change, new predators, disease or catastrophic events.">Extinction</dfn> occurs when a species cannot adapt fast enough to changing conditions. Causes include climate change, new predators or competitors, new diseases, habitat destruction and catastrophic events. The fossil record shows that the vast majority of species that have ever lived are now extinct. Current extinction rates are elevated compared to the geological average, linked to human activity including habitat destruction, pollution and climate change.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: describe the sequence of events that leads to speciation, starting with geographical isolation. Include at least four steps in your answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Speciation: geographical isolation &rarr; different selection pressures &rarr; populations evolve separately &rarr; reproductive isolation &rarr; two distinct species. Evidence for evolution comes from fossils, comparative anatomy and DNA analysis.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Variation arises from mutations, meiosis and sexual reproduction. It is essential for natural selection: variation &rarr; competition &rarr; survival and reproduction of the fittest &rarr; alleles inherited by offspring &rarr; population change over generations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Antibiotic resistance is natural selection in bacteria: random mutations give resistance; the antibiotic kills non-resistant bacteria; resistant bacteria survive and reproduce. Overuse of antibiotics accelerates this process.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Evidence for evolution includes fossils (gradual change over time), comparative anatomy (shared bone structures indicating common ancestry) and DNA analysis. Speciation occurs when geographically isolated populations diverge until they can no longer interbreed.</li>
</ul>
