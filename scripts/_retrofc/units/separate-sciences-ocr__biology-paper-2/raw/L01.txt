--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Homeostasis?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The regulation of the internal conditions of a cell or organism to maintain a stable internal environment, in response to both internal and external changes.">Homeostasis</dfn> is the maintenance of a stable internal environment (B3.3a). This matters because enzymes and metabolic reactions work within narrow ranges of temperature, pH, blood glucose and water balance. If any of these drift too far from the optimum, cell processes slow down or stop &mdash; potentially with fatal consequences.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">All homeostatic systems share the same three-part architecture: <dfn class="term" data-def="Cells or organs that detect changes in the internal or external environment and send information to a coordination centre.">receptors</dfn> detect deviations from the normal level, a <strong>coordination centre</strong> (such as the brain or pancreas) processes the information and sends instructions, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Muscles or glands that carry out the corrective response, restoring conditions to the set point.">effectors</dfn> (muscles or glands) carry out the response. The system operates through <dfn class="term" data-def="A control mechanism where the output of a system feeds back to reverse the original change, keeping conditions within a narrow range around a set point.">negative feedback</dfn>: once conditions return to normal, the response is switched off.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the three components of every homeostatic control system, with one example of each. This framework applies to blood glucose, temperature and water balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment using negative feedback. Every control system has three components: receptor (detects change), coordination centre (processes information), effector (produces the corrective response).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Blood Glucose Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Blood glucose concentration is monitored continuously by the <strong>pancreas</strong> (B3.3b&ndash;c). When blood glucose rises after a carbohydrate-rich meal, specialised <strong>beta cells</strong> in the pancreas detect the increase and release <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone released by beta cells in the pancreas that lowers blood glucose by stimulating cells to absorb glucose and the liver to convert glucose to glycogen for storage.">insulin</dfn> into the blood. Insulin has two effects: it causes body cells (especially muscles and liver) to absorb glucose from the blood, and it stimulates the liver to convert excess glucose into <dfn class="term" data-def="An insoluble polysaccharide stored in the liver and muscles that acts as a short-term glucose reserve.">glycogen</dfn> for storage. Both effects lower blood glucose back to normal.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n7">When blood glucose falls &mdash; for example, during exercise or between meals &mdash; <strong>alpha cells</strong> in the pancreas detect the drop and release <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone released by alpha cells in the pancreas that raises blood glucose by stimulating the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose and release it into the blood.">glucagon</dfn>. Glucagon stimulates the liver to break glycogen back down into glucose (glycogenolysis) and release it into the blood, raising glucose back to normal. The opposing actions of insulin (lowering glucose) and glucagon (raising glucose) form a classic <strong>negative feedback loop</strong> that maintains blood glucose within a safe, narrow range (B3.3b, Higher Tier).</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="An autoimmune condition in which the immune system destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas, resulting in an inability to regulate blood glucose without insulin injections.">Type 1 diabetes</dfn> is an autoimmune condition: the immune system destroys the insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. Without insulin, blood glucose rises dangerously after eating. Treatment requires regular <strong>insulin injections</strong> (or an insulin pump) combined with careful monitoring of blood glucose and carbohydrate intake. It usually develops in childhood or early adulthood and cannot be prevented (B3.3d).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition in which the body's cells become resistant to insulin, so blood glucose remains elevated despite insulin being produced. Strongly linked to obesity and a sedentary lifestyle.">Type 2 diabetes</dfn> develops when body cells become resistant to insulin &mdash; they no longer respond appropriately, so blood glucose stays elevated even though the pancreas may still produce insulin. It is strongly associated with <strong>obesity</strong>, a sedentary lifestyle and genetic factors. Treatment typically involves a <strong>controlled diet</strong> (limiting refined carbohydrates and sugars), regular <strong>exercise</strong>, and sometimes medication. Type 2 can often be prevented or managed through lifestyle changes, unlike Type 1 (B3.3d).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Type 1 = no insulin produced (autoimmune, insulin injections, usually young onset). Type 2 = insulin resistance (lifestyle factors, treated with diet and exercise). Never mix these up — write both from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Type 1 diabetes: the pancreas cannot produce insulin (autoimmune; treated with insulin injections). Type 2 diabetes: body cells become resistant to insulin (often linked to obesity; treated with diet and exercise, sometimes medication).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Temperature Regulation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Core body temperature in humans is maintained at approximately 37 &deg;C by the <strong>thermoregulatory centre</strong> in the brain (B3.3c). It receives signals from <strong>thermoreceptors</strong> in the skin (detecting external temperature) and from receptors monitoring blood temperature in the brain itself. Together these provide a complete picture of the body&rsquo;s thermal state, allowing the coordination centre to activate appropriate effectors.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Responses to Overheating</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">When the body is too hot, three main effector responses lower temperature. <strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The widening of blood vessels near the skin surface, increasing blood flow to the skin so more heat is lost to the surroundings by radiation.">Vasodilation</dfn></strong>: blood vessels near the skin surface widen, so more warm blood flows close to the skin and more heat is radiated away. <strong>Sweating</strong>: sweat glands release sweat onto the skin; water in sweat evaporates, removing heat energy from the skin (evaporative cooling). <strong>Body hairs lying flat</strong>: erector muscles relax, so hairs lie flat against the skin, reducing the insulating layer of trapped air.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Responses to Getting Too Cold</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The narrowing of blood vessels near the skin surface, reducing blood flow to the skin and conserving heat in the body's core.">Vasoconstriction</dfn>: blood vessels near the skin surface narrow, diverting blood away from the skin and reducing heat loss by radiation. <strong>Shivering</strong>: skeletal muscles contract rapidly and repeatedly; the increased metabolic activity generates heat that warms the body. <strong>Body hairs standing on end</strong>: erector muscles contract, raising hairs and trapping a thicker insulating layer of air close to the skin (more effective in furry animals than in humans).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Both sets of responses illustrate negative feedback &mdash; once body temperature returns to 37 &deg;C, the thermoreceptors detect the change and the effector responses are switched off. This cyclical on-off control is what keeps core temperature within a narrow, safe range.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">It is worth noting that the nervous and endocrine systems work together in thermoregulation. The nervous system sends rapid signals to sweat glands and erector muscles (which raise body hairs), while hormones such as adrenaline can also boost metabolic heat production in emergency cold exposure. The skin acts as the primary interface with the environment &mdash; its rich blood supply and large surface area make it the key organ for heat exchange. Evaporative cooling via sweat is particularly important during physical exercise, when the body can generate large amounts of metabolic heat through increased muscle respiration.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment using receptors, coordination centres and effectors working through negative feedback. The pancreas controls blood glucose: insulin lowers it (glucose absorbed, glycogen formed), glucagon raises it (glycogen broken down) &mdash; Higher Tier.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Type 1 diabetes: autoimmune destruction of beta cells, no insulin produced, treated with injections. Type 2 diabetes: insulin resistance, lifestyle-linked, treated with diet, exercise and medication.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Thermoregulation keeps core temperature near 37 &deg;C: too hot &rarr; vasodilation + sweating + hairs flat; too cold &rarr; vasoconstriction + shivering + hairs raised. Both are negative feedback responses.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For blood glucose questions, be precise: insulin is released when glucose is <strong>high</strong> and causes cells to absorb glucose and the liver to store it as glycogen. Glucagon (Higher Tier) is released when glucose is <strong>low</strong> and causes the liver to break glycogen back into glucose. A common error is confusing glycogen (storage form) with glucagon (the hormone). For temperature regulation, describe the mechanism, not just the outcome: say &lsquo;vasodilation allows more blood to flow close to the skin surface, increasing heat loss by radiation&rsquo;, rather than just &lsquo;more blood goes to the skin&rsquo;. For Type 1 vs Type 2 diabetes questions, always state the cause and the treatment for both.</p>
