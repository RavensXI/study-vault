--- DESCRIPTION ---
Kinetic energy, GPE, momentum, stopping distances and why crumple zones save lives.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy in Moving Objects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Whenever an object moves, it stores energy in its <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy stored in a moving object, equal to half its mass multiplied by the square of its speed.">kinetic energy</dfn> store. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">where \(E_k\) is kinetic energy in joules (J), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(v\) is speed in m/s. Notice that speed is squared — doubling speed <em>quadruples</em> kinetic energy, which is why fast driving is so much more dangerous than slow driving.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Calculate the kinetic energy of a 1200 kg car travelling at 20 m/s, then at 40 m/s. Compare the two answers to show the effect of doubling speed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Kinetic energy is proportional to the <strong>square</strong> of speed. Double the speed → four times the kinetic energy. This means braking distance also increases with the square of speed — twice as fast means four times the distance to stop with the same braking force.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Gravitational Potential Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When an object is raised above the ground, work is done against gravity and energy is stored as <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy stored in an object because of its position in a gravitational field, equal to mass × gravitational field strength × height.">gravitational potential energy</dfn> (GPE):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$E_{gpe} = mgh$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">where \(m\) is mass in kg, \(g\) is gravitational field strength (approximately 10 N/kg near Earth), and \(h\) is height in metres. When the object falls, GPE transfers to kinetic energy (assuming no friction or air resistance).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">\(\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 100 \Rightarrow \frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times v^2 = 100 \Rightarrow v^2 = 100 \Rightarrow v = 10\) m/s</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Stopping Distances and Road Safety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">When a vehicle needs to stop suddenly, the total <dfn class="term" data-def="The total distance travelled by a vehicle from when the driver first sees a hazard to when the vehicle comes to rest.">stopping distance</dfn> has two components:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Stopping distance = <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled between a driver seeing a hazard and applying the brakes — depends on reaction time and speed.">thinking distance</dfn> + <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled from when the brakes are applied until the vehicle stops — increases with the square of speed.">braking distance</dfn></p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Thinking distance increases linearly with speed (higher speed → more distance covered in the same reaction time). Factors that increase thinking distance: tiredness, alcohol or drugs, distractions. Braking distance increases with the square of speed (because kinetic energy ∝ v²). Factors that increase braking distance: worn tyres or brakes, wet or icy roads, greater vehicle mass or speed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain what happens to (a) thinking distance and (b) braking distance if speed doubles. State how factors like ice or alcohol affect each component.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Large decelerations during sudden braking generate very large forces on vehicle occupants (\(F = ma\), large negative \(a\) → large \(F\)). These forces can cause serious injury — which is why reducing deceleration time is the key principle behind vehicle safety features.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Momentum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Every moving object has <dfn class="term" data-def="The product of an object's mass and velocity; a vector quantity measured in kg m/s.">momentum</dfn>. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$p = mv$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">where \(p\) is momentum in kg m/s, \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(v\) is velocity in m/s. Momentum is a vector — its direction is the same as the velocity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">In a closed system (no external forces), the total momentum before a collision equals the total momentum after. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that in a closed system, total momentum remains constant — momentum before an event equals momentum after.">conservation of momentum</dfn>. For two objects colliding: \(m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Crumple Zones — The Physics of Saving Lives</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">When a vehicle collides, its momentum changes from its speed-before value to zero. This change in momentum is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The change in momentum of an object, equal to the force multiplied by the time over which the force acts.">impulse</dfn>:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">$$\text{impulse} = F \Delta t = \Delta p$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A specially designed section of a vehicle that deforms on impact, increasing collision time and reducing the force on occupants.">crumple zone</dfn> is the front or rear section of a car designed to deform (crumple) during a collision. By crumpling, the car takes longer to come to rest — the time over which momentum changes (\(\Delta t\)) increases. Since \(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\), a <em>longer</em> time means a <em>smaller</em> force on the passengers for the same momentum change.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">This is the precise chain of reasoning you must reproduce in an exam: <strong>crumple zone deforms → collision time increases → same momentum change occurs over a longer time → average force on occupants is reduced → injury risk is lower</strong>. Saying crumple zones &ldquo;absorb force&rdquo; or &ldquo;act as a barrier&rdquo; misses the physics and will not earn full marks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">The same principle applies to airbags (increase contact time between driver and steering wheel), seatbelts (stretch slightly to extend stopping time), cycle helmets and padded sports equipment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Conservation of Momentum: Worked Example</h3>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n35">Consider two trolleys: trolley A (2 kg, moving at 3 m/s) collides with stationary trolley B (1 kg) and they stick together. Total momentum before = 2 x 3 = 6 kg m/s. After: (2 + 1) x v = 6, so v = 2 m/s. Kinetic energy before = 9 J; after = 6 J. The 3 J difference converts to thermal energy and sound, making this an inelastic collision. In a perfectly elastic collision both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved, a distinction the examiner often tests.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Kinetic energy \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\) grows with the square of speed — doubling speed quadruples KE and braking distance. GPE = \(mgh\) transfers to KE as an object falls (conservation of energy).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Large decelerations cause large forces (\(F = ma\)), which is why sudden stops can injure passengers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Momentum \(p = mv\) is conserved in collisions. Crumple zones extend collision time, reducing the average force on occupants for the same change in momentum (\(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\)).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">The crumple zone question is one of the most frequently lost marks in this topic. You must include all steps: the crumple zone extends collision time (\(\Delta t\) increases); for the same change in momentum (\(\Delta p\)), a larger \(\Delta t\) means smaller average force (\(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\)). Simply writing &ldquo;it absorbs the impact&rdquo; or &ldquo;it reduces the force&rdquo; without the time-extension reasoning earns no marks. For kinetic energy calculations, always substitute before squaring: \(E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2\) — a common mistake is to forget the \(\frac{1}{2}\). For momentum conservation, be careful with directions — assign positive/negative signs to velocities moving in opposite directions.</p>
