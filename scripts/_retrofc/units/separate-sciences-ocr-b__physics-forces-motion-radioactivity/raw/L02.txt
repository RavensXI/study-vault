--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed and Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="How fast an object is moving — the distance it travels per unit time. Speed is a scalar quantity, measured in m/s.">Speed</dfn> tells you how fast an object is moving. It is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude but no direction. The equation for speed is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$v = \frac{s}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">where \(v\) is speed in m/s, \(s\) is distance in metres (m), and \(t\) is time in seconds (s). This rearranges to \(s = vt\) and \(t = s/v\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall typical speeds for: walking, running, cycling, car in town and on motorway, train, and sound in air. Write them from memory in m/s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Typical speeds to know: walking ≈ 1.5 m/s, running ≈ 3 m/s, cycling ≈ 6 m/s, car in town ≈ 13 m/s, car on motorway ≈ 30 m/s, train ≈ 55 m/s, sound in air ≈ 330 m/s.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Velocity and Displacement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a specified direction — a vector quantity measured in m/s.">Velocity</dfn> is the vector equivalent of speed: it has both magnitude and direction. Two cars travelling at 30 m/s in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The straight-line distance from an object's starting point to its current position, together with the direction — a vector quantity measured in metres.">Displacement</dfn> is similarly the vector equivalent of distance — it is the net distance from the starting point in a specific direction. An athlete who completes one full lap of a 400 m track has travelled 400 m (distance) but has a displacement of 0 m (they ended where they started).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s². It is a vector quantity — a negative value indicates deceleration.">Acceleration</dfn> is how quickly an object's velocity changes. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">where \(a\) is acceleration in m/s\(^2\), \(v\) is final velocity in m/s, \(u\) is initial velocity in m/s, and \(t\) is time in seconds. A negative value of \(a\) means the object is decelerating.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">A bus accelerates from 4 m/s to 16 m/s in 6 seconds. Calculate the acceleration.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">\(a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{16 - 4}{6} = \frac{12}{6} = 2\) m/s\(^2\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Now suppose the bus then brakes from 16 m/s to rest in 8 s: \(a = \frac{0 - 16}{8} = -2\) m/s\(^2\) (deceleration of 2 m/s\(^2\)).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write v² = u² + 2as from memory. State when you would use this equation rather than a = (v - u) / t.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For uniform (constant) acceleration, use:</p>
  <p>$$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$</p>
  <p>where \(s\) is distance travelled in metres. This equation is essential when time is not given or needed.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n18">Known: \(u = 20\) m/s, \(v = 0\) m/s, \(s = 50\) m.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">\(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">\(0 = 400 + 2a \times 50\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">\(100a = -400\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">\(a = -4\) m/s\(^2\) (deceleration of 4 m/s\(^2\))</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Time was not given and was not needed — this is exactly when \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) is the right tool.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Distance-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with distance on the y-axis and time on the x-axis; the gradient (slope) gives the speed.">distance-time graph</dfn> shows how far an object has travelled over time. Key features: a <strong>horizontal line</strong> means stationary; a <strong>straight diagonal line</strong> means constant speed; a <strong>steepening curve</strong> means increasing speed (acceleration).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The speed at any point equals the gradient of the line: \(\text{speed} = \frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}\). For a straight line, pick two points far apart and divide the rise by the run. For a curve, draw a tangent at the point of interest and find its gradient.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Velocity-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with velocity on the y-axis and time on the x-axis; the gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives the distance travelled.">velocity-time graph</dfn> is even more powerful. The gradient gives the acceleration. The area under the line gives the total distance travelled.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">A horizontal line means constant velocity (acceleration = 0). A line sloping upward means increasing velocity (positive acceleration). A line sloping downward means decreasing velocity (deceleration). Complex shapes can be split into triangles and rectangles to find the total area (= total distance).</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n30">A common method for measuring acceleration is to use a trolley on a ramp with a light gate connected to a data logger. The independent variable is the force applied (e.g. by varying the hanging mass); the dependent variable is the acceleration. Controlled variables include the total mass of the system. The light gate measures the time taken for a card of known width to pass — from this, both speed and acceleration can be calculated. Results plotted as acceleration against force produce a straight line through the origin if \(F = ma\) holds.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Graphs and Calculations: Exam Technique</h3>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n37">When interpreting motion graphs, look at the gradient first. A flat line on a distance-time graph means the object is stationary; a flat line on a velocity-time graph means constant velocity with zero acceleration. Steeper gradients indicate faster speed or greater acceleration. To find displacement from a velocity-time graph, calculate the area under the line — including enclosed areas between the graph line and the time axis, which represent displacement in a particular direction. A common exam error is confusing the gradient, which gives acceleration, with the area, which gives displacement, on a velocity-time graph.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Speed (scalar) and velocity (vector) both measure how fast an object moves; velocity includes direction. Displacement (vector) measures net distance and direction from start to finish. Acceleration = rate of change of velocity = \((v - u) / t\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">On a distance-time graph, gradient = speed. On a velocity-time graph, gradient = acceleration and area under the line = distance travelled.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">For uniform acceleration without time: use \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\). This is the equation to reach for when a question gives initial speed, final speed and distance — but not time.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">The most common error in motion calculations is choosing the wrong equation. Use \(a = (v - u)/t\) when time is given; use \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) when time is absent. For graph questions: on a distance-time graph, gradient = speed; on a velocity-time graph, gradient = acceleration and area under the line = distance. Always include units in your answer — marks are awarded specifically for correct units. When describing motion from a graph, describe each distinct section separately (e.g. &ldquo;from 0 to 5 s the object accelerates uniformly; from 5 to 10 s it travels at constant velocity&rdquo;).</p>
