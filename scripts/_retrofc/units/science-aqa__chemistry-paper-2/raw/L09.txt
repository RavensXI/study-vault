--- DESCRIPTION ---
How addition and condensation polymers form, plus natural polymers like DNA and proteins.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Very large molecules made by joining many small monomer molecules together in a repeating chain.">Polymers</dfn> are very large molecules made by linking together thousands of small molecules called <dfn class="term" data-def="Small molecules that join together in large numbers to form a polymer.">monomers</dfn>. The process of joining monomers together is called <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction in which many monomer molecules join together to form a long-chain polymer.">polymerisation</dfn>. For separate chemistry, you need to understand two types: addition polymerisation and condensation polymerisation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Addition Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of polymerisation where unsaturated monomers (alkenes) join together by opening their double bonds, with no other product formed.">Addition polymerisation</dfn> occurs when many unsaturated monomer molecules (alkenes) join together. The double bond (\(\text{C}=\text{C}\)) in each monomer “opens up” and bonds to the next monomer, forming a long saturated chain. No other product is formed — the only product is the polymer.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what bond must the monomer contain, what backbone does the polymer have, and what is the atom economy?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In addition polymerisation, the monomer must contain a \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) double bond. The polymer has a \(\text{C}-\text{C}\) single bond backbone. The atom economy is <strong>100%</strong> because no by-products are formed. Example: ethene (\(\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\)) polymerises to form poly(ethene).</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Common addition polymers include:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Poly(ethene)</strong> — from ethene. Used for plastic bags, bottles, and packaging.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Poly(propene)</strong> — from propene. Used for ropes, carpets, and food containers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Poly(chloroethene)</strong> (PVC) — from chloroethene. Used for window frames, pipes, and flooring.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>PTFE (Teflon)</strong> — from tetrafluoroethene. Used for non-stick coatings on pans.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Condensation Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of polymerisation where monomers with two functional groups join together, releasing a small molecule (usually water) at each link.">Condensation polymerisation</dfn> is different from addition polymerisation. It occurs when monomers with <strong>two functional groups</strong> join together, and a small molecule (usually water) is released at each link.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what small molecule is usually produced, what are the two main types, and what reactants make each type?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Condensation polymerisation produces a polymer <strong>plus a small molecule</strong> (usually \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)). This means the atom economy is less than 100%. The two main types of condensation polymer are <dfn class="term" data-def="A condensation polymer formed by the reaction between a dicarboxylic acid and a diol, linked by ester bonds.">polyesters</dfn> (from a dicarboxylic acid + a diol) and <dfn class="term" data-def="A condensation polymer formed by the reaction between a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine, linked by amide bonds.">polyamides</dfn> (from a dicarboxylic acid + a diamine).</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Polyesters</strong> are formed when a dicarboxylic acid (with two \(-\text{COOH}\) groups) reacts with a diol (with two \(-\text{OH}\) groups). An ester link (\(-\text{COO}-\)) forms between each pair, and water is released. Example: PET (polyethylene terephthalate), used in plastic bottles and clothing fibres.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Polyamides</strong> are formed when a dicarboxylic acid reacts with a diamine (with two \(-\text{NH}_2\) groups). An amide link (\(-\text{CONH}-\)) forms between each pair, and water is released. Example: nylon, used in ropes, clothing, and engineering plastics.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Natural Polymers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Many biological molecules are natural polymers formed by condensation polymerisation:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Proteins</strong> are natural polyamides. They are made from <dfn class="term" data-def="Small organic molecules that are the monomers of proteins, linked by peptide bonds during condensation reactions.">amino acid</dfn> monomers joined by peptide bonds (a type of amide link). Each amino acid has both an amine group (\(-\text{NH}_2\)) and a carboxyl group (\(-\text{COOH}\)), so they can form long chains through condensation reactions.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>DNA</strong> is a natural polymer made from nucleotide monomers. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base. The nucleotides are linked by condensation reactions between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Starch and cellulose</strong> are natural polymers of glucose (a sugar monomer). Starch is used for energy storage in plants, while cellulose provides structural support in plant cell walls.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Addition:</strong> Monomers must have \(\text{C}=\text{C}\) double bonds. Only one product (the polymer). 100% atom economy. Examples: poly(ethene), PVC.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Condensation:</strong> Monomers must have two functional groups. Two products (polymer + water). Less than 100% atom economy. Examples: polyester (PET), nylon, proteins, DNA.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what backbone bonds indicate addition polymers, and what two link types indicate condensation polymers?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You can identify the type of polymerisation from the polymer structure: if the polymer backbone contains only \(\text{C}-\text{C}\) bonds, it is an addition polymer. If the backbone contains ester links (\(-\text{COO}-\)) or amide links (\(-\text{CONH}-\)), it is a condensation polymer.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Addition polymerisation uses alkene monomers (\(\text{C}=\text{C}\)), produces only the polymer, and has 100% atom economy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Condensation polymerisation uses monomers with two functional groups, produces a polymer plus water, and has less than 100% atom economy. It forms polyesters and polyamides.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Natural polymers (proteins, DNA, starch) are also formed by condensation reactions from biological monomers.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">A very common exam question gives you a section of a polymer and asks you to identify the monomer(s) and the type of polymerisation. For addition polymers, look for a \(\text{C}-\text{C}\) backbone and add a double bond back between the two carbons in the repeating unit. For condensation polymers, look for ester or amide links — insert \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\) across the link to recover the two monomers. Always state whether water is lost.</p>
