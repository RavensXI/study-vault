--- DESCRIPTION ---
How to identify metal ions, non-metal ions, and gases using standard chemical tests.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Testing for Ions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In separate chemistry, you need to know how to identify unknown substances using a range of chemical tests. These tests are based on characteristic reactions that produce observable results — colour changes, precipitates, or gas production.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Flame Tests for Metal Ions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A test where a sample is held in a Bunsen flame; different metal ions produce characteristic flame colours.">flame test</dfn> identifies metal ions by the colour they produce when heated in a Bunsen flame. A clean nichrome wire loop is dipped in the sample, then held in the blue flame.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the exact flame test colours for lithium, sodium, potassium, calcium and copper.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Flame test colours: <strong>Lithium</strong> = crimson red. <strong>Sodium</strong> = yellow. <strong>Potassium</strong> = lilac. <strong>Calcium</strong> = orange-red. <strong>Copper</strong> = green.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Testing with Sodium Hydroxide Solution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Adding a few drops of <dfn class="term" data-def="A strong alkali (NaOH) used to test for metal ions; it produces coloured precipitates with different metals.">sodium hydroxide solution</dfn> to a solution containing metal ions produces a coloured <dfn class="term" data-def="An insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed together.">precipitate</dfn> (an insoluble solid). The colour of the precipitate identifies the metal ion:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Calcium</strong> (Ca²+): white precipitate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Magnesium</strong> (Mg²+): white precipitate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Aluminium</strong> (Al³+): white precipitate — but dissolves in excess NaOH to form a colourless solution.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Copper(II)</strong> (Cu²+): blue precipitate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Iron(II)</strong> (Fe²+): green precipitate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Iron(III)</strong> (Fe³+): brown precipitate.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Distinguishing Between White Precipitates</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Calcium, magnesium, and aluminium all produce white precipitates with NaOH. To distinguish between them:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Aluminium</strong> dissolves in <em>excess</em> NaOH (the white precipitate disappears). Calcium and magnesium do not.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Calcium</strong> can be confirmed by a flame test (orange-red flame). Magnesium does not produce a visible flame colour.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Testing for Negative Ions (Anions)</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Testing for carbonates</strong> (\(\text{CO}_3^{2-}\)): Add dilute acid to the sample. If carbonate ions are present, the mixture fizzes and produces carbon dioxide gas, which turns <dfn class="term" data-def="A saturated solution of calcium hydroxide used to test for carbon dioxide gas; it turns milky/cloudy when CO₂ is bubbled through.">limewater</dfn> milky (cloudy).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Testing for halide ions</strong> (\(\text{Cl}^-\), \(\text{Br}^-\), \(\text{I}^-\)): Add dilute nitric acid, then silver nitrate solution. The colour of the precipitate identifies the halide:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Chloride</strong>: white precipitate (silver chloride).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Bromide</strong>: cream precipitate (silver bromide).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Iodide</strong>: yellow precipitate (silver iodide).</li>
</ul>



<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Testing for sulfate ions</strong> (\(\text{SO}_4^{2-}\)): Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution. A white precipitate of barium sulfate forms if sulfate ions are present.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why acid is added first in halide and sulfate tests before adding other reagents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The acid is added first in halide and sulfate tests to remove any carbonate ions that might otherwise interfere with the results by forming precipitates of their own.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Tests for Gases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">You also need to know how to test for common gases:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Hydrogen</strong> (\(\text{H}_2\)): Hold a burning splint near the gas. It burns with a squeaky pop.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Oxygen</strong> (\(\text{O}_2\)): Insert a glowing splint into the gas. It relights.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Carbon dioxide</strong> (\(\text{CO}_2\)): Bubble through limewater. It turns milky/cloudy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Chlorine</strong> (\(\text{Cl}_2\)): Hold damp litmus paper near the gas. It bleaches the litmus paper white.</li>
</ul>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Modern laboratories use instrumental methods of analysis that are more sensitive, faster, and can handle very small samples. Examples include <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique that measures the wavelengths of light absorbed or emitted by a substance, providing a unique spectral fingerprint.">spectroscopy</dfn> and chromatography. These methods are particularly useful for detecting trace amounts of substances or for analysing complex mixtures.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list four reasons why instrumental methods are preferred over chemical tests in industry.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Instrumental methods of analysis are preferred over chemical tests in industry because they are more accurate, more sensitive (can detect smaller amounts), faster, and require smaller sample sizes.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Flame tests identify metal ions by colour: Li (crimson), Na (yellow), K (lilac), Ca (orange-red), Cu (green).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">NaOH solution produces coloured precipitates: Cu<sup>2</sup>+ (blue), Fe<sup>2</sup>+ (green), Fe<sup>3</sup>+ (brown), Ca/Mg/Al (white, with Al dissolving in excess).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Halides are tested with silver nitrate (Cl&minus; white, Br&minus; cream, I&minus; yellow). Sulfates are tested with barium chloride (white precipitate). Gases have specific tests (squeaky pop, relights splint, limewater, bleaches litmus).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">Learn the flame test colours as a list — they are frequently tested. For precipitate tests, always state the <strong>colour</strong> of the precipitate, not just that a precipitate forms. Remember: to test for halides use <strong>silver nitrate</strong> (with nitric acid first), and to test for sulfates use <strong>barium chloride</strong> (with hydrochloric acid first). A common mistake is mixing up which reagent goes with which test.</p>
