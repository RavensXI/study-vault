--- DESCRIPTION ---
What affects the speed of chemical reactions, and how collision theory explains reaction rates.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rate of Reaction</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how quickly reactants are converted into products in a chemical reaction. It can be calculated by measuring the quantity of reactant used or product formed over time.">rate of reaction</dfn> measures how quickly reactants are turned into products. It can be calculated by:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">$$\text{rate of reaction} = \frac{\text{quantity of reactant used or product formed}}{\text{time taken}}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The rate of a chemical reaction can be changed by altering the: <strong>temperature</strong>, <strong>concentration</strong> of solutions (or <strong>pressure</strong> of gases), <strong>surface area</strong> of solid reactants, or by using a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up. It lowers the activation energy needed for the reaction.">catalyst</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the 4 ways to increase reaction rate, plus name the 3 methods used to measure reaction rates.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rate of reaction can be increased by: raising temperature, increasing concentration/pressure, increasing surface area, or using a catalyst. The rate can be measured by collecting gas, measuring mass loss, or timing colour changes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Collision Theory</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that a chemical reaction can only occur when reactant particles collide with sufficient energy (the activation energy) and in the correct orientation.">Collision theory</dfn> states that for a reaction to occur, particles must: (1) <strong>collide</strong> with each other, and (2) collide with sufficient energy. The minimum energy needed for a successful collision is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum amount of energy that reactant particles need in order to react when they collide.">activation energy</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Each factor affects the rate by changing the frequency or energy of collisions:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Temperature</strong> — higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy. They move faster, collide more frequently, and a greater proportion of collisions have energy \(\geq\) activation energy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Concentration / Pressure</strong> — more particles in the same volume means more frequent collisions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Surface area</strong> — smaller pieces expose more surface, giving more area for collisions to occur.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Catalyst</strong> — provides an alternative reaction pathway with a <strong>lower activation energy</strong>, so more collisions are successful. The catalyst is not used up.</li>
</ul>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain what activation energy is, how catalysts affect it, and why catalysts don't appear in overall equations.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Collision theory: reactions only happen when particles collide with energy ≥ activation energy. Catalysts lower the activation energy — they are not used up and do not appear in the overall equation.</p>
</div>

The line levels off when the reaction is complete (one reactant has been used up). Changing temperature or concentration affects the steepness but not the final volume (if the same amount of reactant is used). Increasing the amount of reactant increases the total gas produced.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Rate of reaction = quantity of product formed or reactant used ÷ time.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Collision theory: particles must collide with energy ≥ activation energy. More frequent/energetic collisions = faster rate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Catalysts lower the activation energy without being used up; temperature, concentration, surface area all increase collision frequency.</li>
</ul>
