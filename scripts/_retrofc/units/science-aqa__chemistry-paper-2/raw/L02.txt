--- DESCRIPTION ---
How some reactions can go in both directions, and what happens at dynamic equilibrium.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reversible Reactions</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Some chemical reactions are <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that can proceed in both the forward and reverse directions. The products can react to re-form the original reactants. Shown by the symbol ⇌.">reversible</dfn> — the products can react to re-form the original reactants. A reversible reaction is shown with a double arrow (\(\rightleftharpoons\)):</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">$$A + B \rightleftharpoons C + D$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">A simple example is the thermal decomposition of ammonium chloride:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n5">$$NH_4Cl \rightleftharpoons NH_3 + HCl$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">When heated, ammonium chloride decomposes into ammonia and hydrogen chloride. When cooled, these gases recombine to form ammonium chloride again.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall what type of reaction the reverse must be if the forward reaction is exothermic, and the energy relationship.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a reversible reaction, if the forward reaction is exothermic, the reverse reaction is endothermic by the same amount of energy, and vice versa.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Dynamic Equilibrium</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n9">When a reversible reaction occurs in a <strong>closed system</strong> (where nothing can enter or leave), it eventually reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="A state in a closed system where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant but are not necessarily equal.">dynamic equilibrium</dfn>. At equilibrium:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">The rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11">The concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (but are not necessarily equal).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12">The reaction has not stopped — both forward and reverse reactions are still occurring.</li>
</ul>



<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Changing the Position of Equilibrium</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">If conditions are changed, the equilibrium shifts to oppose the change (<dfn class="term" data-def="If conditions affecting a system at equilibrium are changed, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose that change and partially counteract it.">Le Chatelier’s principle</dfn>):</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction. Decreasing temperature shifts it in the exothermic direction.</li>
      <li><strong>Concentration:</strong> Increasing the concentration of a reactant shifts equilibrium towards the products. Increasing the concentration of a product shifts it towards the reactants.</li>
      <li><strong>Pressure (gases only):</strong> Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium towards the side with fewer moles of gas.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A catalyst does <strong>not</strong> change the position of equilibrium — it speeds up both the forward and reverse reactions equally, helping equilibrium to be reached faster.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State Le Chatelier's principle and explain what effect catalysts have on equilibrium position and reaction rates.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Le Chatelier’s principle: if conditions change, equilibrium shifts to oppose the change. Catalysts speed up both reactions equally — they do not change the equilibrium position.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Haber Process</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The Haber process manufactures ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">$$N_2 + 3H_2 \rightleftharpoons 2NH_3$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The forward reaction is exothermic. Conditions used: temperature of about 450°C, pressure of about 200 atmospheres, and an iron catalyst. The temperature is a compromise — lower temperature would give a higher yield but the rate would be too slow. The iron catalyst speeds up the reaction without changing the yield.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Reversible reactions go in both directions; if forward is exothermic, reverse is endothermic (same energy).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">At dynamic equilibrium (closed system): forward rate = reverse rate; concentrations constant but not necessarily equal.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Le Chatelier’s principle: equilibrium shifts to oppose changes in temperature, concentration or pressure. Catalysts don’t change position.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When predicting how a change in conditions affects equilibrium, always state: (1) which direction the equilibrium shifts, (2) why it shifts (to oppose the change), and (3) the effect on the yield of products. For the Haber process, explain why a compromise temperature is used — not just what the conditions are.</p>
