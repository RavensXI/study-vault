--- DESCRIPTION ---
How chemists test for purity, use chromatography, and identify common gases.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pure Substances and Mixtures</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">In chemistry, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that consists of only one type of element or compound. A pure substance melts and boils at fixed, sharp temperatures.">pure substance</dfn> consists of a single element or compound. It has a sharp, fixed melting point and boiling point. A <dfn class="term" data-def="Two or more elements or compounds that are not chemically bonded together. Mixtures melt and boil over a range of temperatures.">mixture</dfn> contains two or more substances not chemically combined. Mixtures melt over a range of temperatures rather than at a sharp point.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the four gas tests: what sound does hydrogen make, what happens to oxygen with a glowing splint, what does CO₂ do to lime...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A pure substance has a sharp, specific melting point. An impure substance / mixture melts over a range of temperatures. You can test purity by checking the melting point against the known value.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Chromatography</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A technique for separating dissolved substances by their different solubilities in a solvent. Substances that are more soluble travel further up the paper.">Chromatography</dfn> is used to separate and identify substances in a mixture. In paper chromatography, a spot of the mixture is placed on a pencil line on chromatography paper. The paper is placed in a solvent. As the solvent moves up the paper, it carries the dissolved substances with it. Different substances travel different distances depending on their solubility.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the distance travelled by a substance to the distance travelled by the solvent in chromatography. Each substance has a unique Rf value in a given solvent.">R\(_f\) value</dfn> is calculated as:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by substance}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}$$</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Each substance has a unique \(R_f\) value in a given solvent. A pure substance produces a single spot; a mixture produces multiple spots.</p>

If a spot in the unknown lines up exactly with a spot from a known substance, they are the same compound.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Tests for Common Gases</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11">You need to know the tests for four gases:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why pure substances have sharp melting points while impure substances melt over a temperature range, and how you test pu...">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gas tests: Hydrogen — squeaky pop with a lighted splint. Oxygen — relights a glowing splint. Carbon dioxide — turns limewater milky/cloudy. Chlorine — bleaches damp litmus paper white.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A mixture that has been designed as a useful product, with each component present in a specific quantity. Examples include medicines, paints, cleaning products and fuels.">formulation</dfn> is a mixture designed as a useful product, where each component is present in a measured quantity to give it specific properties. Examples include medicines (active ingredient + binder + coating), paints (pigment + solvent + binder), and alloys (metals mixed in specific ratios for desired properties).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Formulations are found everywhere in everyday life. Each ingredient has a specific purpose, and changing the proportions changes the properties of the product.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">Pure substances have sharp melting/boiling points; impure substances melt over a range.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Chromatography separates mixtures; \(R_f\) values identify substances. Pure = one spot, mixture = multiple spots.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Gas tests: hydrogen (squeaky pop), oxygen (relights splint), CO\(_2\) (limewater milky), chlorine (bleaches litmus).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">When calculating \(R_f\) values, always measure from the pencil baseline (not the bottom of the paper) to the centre of the spot. Show your working clearly. Remember that \(R_f\) values are always between 0 and 1. For gas test questions, state both the test AND the positive result.</p>
