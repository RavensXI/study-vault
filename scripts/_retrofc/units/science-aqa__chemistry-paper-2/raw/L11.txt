Corrosion and alloys, the Haber process for ammonia, and NPK fertiliser production.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Corrosion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reactions with substances in its environment, such as oxygen and water.">Corrosion</dfn> is the destruction of metals by chemical reactions with substances in the environment. The most familiar example is <dfn class="term" data-def="The corrosion of iron in the presence of both oxygen and water, forming hydrated iron(III) oxide.">rusting</dfn>, which occurs when iron reacts with both oxygen and water.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write the word equation for rusting, naming the three reactants and the product hydrated iron(III) oxide.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rusting requires <strong>both</strong> oxygen and water. If either is absent, iron will not rust. The word equation is: iron + water + oxygen → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust). Rusting is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen, losing electrons in the process.">oxidation</dfn> reaction because the iron is oxidised.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Corrosion can be prevented in several ways:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Painting, greasing, or oiling</strong> — creates a physical barrier that prevents oxygen and water reaching the metal surface.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Galvanising</strong> — coating iron with a layer of zinc. Even if the zinc coating is scratched, the zinc corrodes preferentially because it is more reactive than iron (<dfn class="term" data-def="A method of rust prevention where a more reactive metal corrodes instead of the iron, protecting it even if the coating is damaged.">sacrificial protection</dfn>).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Electroplating</strong> — using electrolysis to coat a metal with a thin layer of a more attractive or less reactive metal (e.g. chromium plating on car parts).</li>
</ul>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Most metals used in everyday life are <dfn class="term" data-def="A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, designed to have improved properties such as greater strength or resistance to corrosion.">alloys</dfn> — mixtures of metals (or a metal with a non-metal). Alloys are harder and stronger than pure metals because atoms of different sizes disrupt the regular layers, preventing them from sliding over each other easily.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Examples of alloys:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Steel</strong> — iron + carbon (and sometimes other metals). Low carbon steel is easy to shape; high carbon steel is hard; stainless steel (iron + chromium + nickel) resists corrosion.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Bronze</strong> — copper + tin. Hard and resistant to corrosion.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Brass</strong> — copper + zinc. Easy to shape and has a gold-like appearance.</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Haber Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The industrial process for producing ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen, using high temperature, high pressure, and an iron catalyst.">Haber process</dfn> is one of the most important industrial chemical processes. It produces <dfn class="term" data-def="A compound of nitrogen and hydrogen (NH₃) used to make fertilisers, explosives, and many other chemicals.">ammonia</dfn> (\(\text{NH}_3\)) from nitrogen (from the air) and hydrogen (from natural gas or steam reforming).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and state the three conditions for the Haber process: the temperature in °C, pressure in atmospheres, and catalyst used.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Haber process equation: \(\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\). Conditions: temperature of <strong>450 °C</strong>, pressure of <strong>200 atmospheres</strong>, and an <strong>iron catalyst</strong>. The reaction is reversible, so these conditions are a compromise to give a reasonable yield at a reasonable rate.</p>
</div>



<div class="higher-only">
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Are These Conditions a Compromise?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The forward reaction (making ammonia) is exothermic. By <strong>Le Chatelier's principle</strong>:</p>
    <ul>
      <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Temperature:</strong> A lower temperature would give a higher yield (favours the exothermic forward reaction), but the rate would be too slow. 450 °C is a compromise between yield and rate.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Pressure:</strong> Higher pressure gives a higher yield (fewer gas molecules on the product side: 4 moles of reactants → 2 moles of products). 200 atm is used because very high pressures are expensive and dangerous.</li>
      <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Iron catalyst:</strong> Speeds up both forward and reverse reactions equally, helping the reaction reach equilibrium faster. It does not change the yield, only the rate.</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Any unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen are recycled back through the reactor to maximise overall conversion.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Fertilisers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fertilisers containing nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), the three essential elements that plants need for healthy growth.">NPK fertilisers</dfn> provide the three essential elements that plants need for growth: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Ammonia from the Haber process is a key starting material for nitrogen-containing fertilisers.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">The production of NPK fertilisers involves several steps:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Ammonia + nitric acid → ammonium nitrate</strong> (\(\text{NH}_3 + \text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3\)) — provides nitrogen.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Ammonia + sulfuric acid → ammonium sulfate</strong> — another nitrogen source.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Phosphate rock + acid → soluble phosphates</strong> — provides phosphorus.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Potassium chloride</strong> or <strong>potassium sulfate</strong> — provides potassium (mined from mineral deposits).</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name what plants need nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium for: proteins/chlorophyll, DNA/energy, and enzymes/disease.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plants need nitrogen to make proteins and chlorophyll, phosphorus for DNA and energy transfer, and potassium for enzyme function and disease resistance. Without fertilisers, crop yields would be significantly lower.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n29">NPK fertilisers can be formulated in the laboratory by reacting an acid with an alkali to produce a soluble salt. The proportions of N, P, and K can be adjusted to suit different crops and soil conditions.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Rusting requires both oxygen and water. It can be prevented by barriers (paint, grease), galvanising (sacrificial protection with zinc), or electroplating.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">The Haber process makes ammonia: \(\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3\) at 450 °C, 200 atm, iron catalyst — a compromise between yield and rate.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">NPK fertilisers provide nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium for plant growth. Ammonia is the starting material for nitrogen fertilisers like ammonium nitrate.</li>
</ul>
