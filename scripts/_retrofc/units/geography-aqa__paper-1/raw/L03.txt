--- DESCRIPTION ---
Comparing primary effects, responses and recovery in places of very different wealth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Compare Two Earthquakes?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Earthquakes happen all over the world, but their effects are not the same in every place. The same magnitude shake can kill a hundred thousand in one country and barely a dozen in another. To understand why, geographers compare two earthquakes in places of very different wealth: <strong>Nepal in 2015</strong> (a low-income country) and <strong>Christchurch, New Zealand in 2011</strong> (a high-income country). The contrast tells us how money, planning and infrastructure shape the human cost of a hazard.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Nepal, 25 April 2015</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck just before midday, with its epicentre about 80 km north-west of the capital Kathmandu. It happened where the Indian Plate is being driven into the Eurasian Plate — the same collision that built the Himalayas. The shaking lasted around fifty seconds and triggered avalanches on Mount Everest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The immediate damage caused directly by an earthquake's shaking, such as collapsed buildings, deaths and injuries.">Primary effects</dfn> were severe. Around 9,000 people were killed and 23,000 injured. Around 600,000 buildings collapsed or were badly damaged, including UNESCO heritage temples in Kathmandu's Durbar Square. Roads and water pipes broke. <dfn class="term" data-def="Damage that develops in the days, weeks and months after a disaster, such as disease, hunger and economic decline.">Secondary effects</dfn> followed. Avalanches killed 19 climbers on Everest. Nearly 8 million people were affected, with three million made homeless. Outbreaks of cholera and dysentery spread in displaced-person camps. The economy lost an estimated $7 billion — about a third of Nepal's GDP.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how many people died, how many homes destroyed, and what proportion of GDP was lost in Nepal 2015?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nepal 2015: magnitude 7.8, around 9,000 dead, 600,000 buildings destroyed, 3 million made homeless, $7 billion in damage — roughly a third of Nepal's GDP.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Responses in Nepal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Immediate responses</strong> were dominated by international aid because Nepal's own emergency services were quickly overwhelmed. Search-and-rescue teams flew in from India, China, the UK and the US. The UN, Red Cross and Oxfam supplied tents, food, clean water and medical care. Helicopters were the only way to reach mountainous villages where roads were blocked.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Long-term responses</strong> have been slower. Donors pledged $4.1 billion at a Kathmandu conference, but bureaucratic delays meant that two years later most rural homes were still not rebuilt. The government has worked with the World Bank to set new earthquake-resistant building standards, but enforcement is uneven in remote areas.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Christchurch, 22 February 2011</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck Christchurch at lunchtime. It was an aftershock of a larger 7.1 quake five months earlier. The epicentre was just 10 km from the city centre and only 5 km deep, which made the shaking unusually violent for its size. New Zealand sits where the Pacific and Australian plates meet at a complex boundary, and Christchurch sits near a previously unknown fault line.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Primary effects: 185 people died, around half in the collapse of a single six-storey building (the CTV building). Around 100,000 buildings were damaged. The Anglican cathedral lost its tower. Liquefaction — where ground water turns wet sand into a liquid — ruined large areas of suburban Christchurch.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Secondary effects included an estimated $40 billion in damage and major disruption to the city for years. Many businesses left the central business district permanently. But the death toll, given the magnitude and location, was relatively low.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: why did Christchurch (HIC) see far fewer deaths than Nepal (LIC) despite the violent shaking?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Christchurch 2011: magnitude 6.3, 185 dead, $40 billion damage. Far lower death toll than Nepal because of strict building codes, fast emergency response, public education and an experienced civil defence service.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Responses in Christchurch</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Immediate responses were rapid and well-organised. New Zealand's Civil Defence response began within minutes. Search-and-rescue teams were on site quickly, supported by international teams from Australia, the US and the UK. Hospitals coped with the casualties. Power was restored to most of the city within five days.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Long-term responses included the Canterbury Earthquake Recovery Authority (CERA), set up to coordinate the rebuild. The central business district was demolished and rebuilt to higher seismic standards. New Zealand reviewed and tightened its building code, and the experience strengthened earthquake education in schools.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Nepal's earthquake was much larger (7.8 vs 6.3 — about a hundred times more energy released). Yet its death rate was tens of times higher only partly because of the size. The bigger reason was building quality: brick and mud-mortar construction collapsed; reinforced concrete and timber-frame buildings designed to flex did not. Wealth doesn't change the geology, but it changes everything about how people meet the geology.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19a">New Zealand introduced earthquake-resistant building standards after the 1931 Hawke's Bay earthquake. Steel frames, base isolators (rubber pads under buildings that absorb shaking) and reinforced concrete were the norm in modern construction. The CTV building, which killed 115 of the 185 victims, had been built in the 1980s but later investigation found design flaws — a reminder that codes only work when they are enforced and inspected.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Side-by-Side Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The two earthquakes show that wealth changes outcomes more than magnitude does. Both were powerful, both struck near major cities, both caused billions in damage. But Nepal's death toll was around fifty times larger, despite a population that is only about five times larger than Christchurch's. The difference lies in <dfn class="term" data-def="How well a country can withstand and respond to a hazard, including building quality, warning systems, emergency services and rebuilding capacity.">resilience</dfn> — the capacity to absorb shocks and bounce back.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Nepal 2015 (LIC, M 7.8): around 9,000 deaths, 600,000 buildings destroyed, $7 billion damage — roughly a third of GDP. Heavy reliance on international aid.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Christchurch 2011 (HIC, M 6.3): 185 deaths, $40 billion damage. Strict codes, fast emergency response and education kept the death toll low.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Wealth changes outcomes more than magnitude. Building quality, warning systems and emergency services determine how many die when the ground shakes.</li>
</ul>
