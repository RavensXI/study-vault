--- DESCRIPTION ---
Tracing the structure and impacts of a named tropical storm and the responses that followed.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Hurricane Ida: A Modern Atlantic Hurricane</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In late August 2021, <dfn class="term" data-def="A 2021 Category 4 hurricane that struck Louisiana before causing catastrophic flooding in the north-eastern United States.">Hurricane Ida</dfn> swept across the Caribbean and into the United States. It is one of the most useful case studies for the AQA specification because it shows the full life of a modern hurricane: rapid intensification over warm seas, primary destruction at landfall, secondary effects hundreds of miles inland, and a clear contrast between rich-country and poor-country impact.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Path and Intensification</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Ida formed as a tropical depression near Jamaica on 26 August. It strengthened over warm Caribbean waters — sea-surface temperatures were around 30°C, well above the 26.5°C threshold. Within 36 hours it had intensified from a Category 1 to a Category 4 hurricane, with sustained winds of 240 km/h. It made landfall in Louisiana on 29 August, the same date that Hurricane Katrina had struck the same coast 16 years earlier.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where did Ida make landfall, when, and what category was it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hurricane Ida made landfall in Louisiana on 29 August 2021 as a Category 4 hurricane with sustained winds of 240 km/h, almost exactly 16 years after Katrina hit the same coast.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Primary Effects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The damage at landfall was enormous. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rise in sea level above normal tides caused by the wind and low pressure of a storm pushing water onshore.">storm surge</dfn> of around 4 metres flooded coastal communities in Louisiana. Around one million homes lost power. New Orleans went into total blackout for the first time since Katrina. Roofs were torn from buildings; trees were flattened across hundreds of square kilometres; oil platforms in the Gulf of Mexico were damaged. Around 30 people in Louisiana died as a direct result of the storm.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Secondary Effects: The Northeast Floods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">As Ida moved inland it weakened to a tropical depression but still carried huge amounts of moisture. Days later, the remnants reached New York and New Jersey. Record-breaking rainfall fell — over 80 mm in an hour in Central Park, the most ever recorded. Flash floods filled basement flats in New York where dozens of people drowned, unable to escape. Subway stations flooded. The total death toll across all states reached around 100. Damage was estimated at $75 billion, making Ida one of the costliest US hurricanes on record.</p>

    <span>Why did the rainfall break records so far inland?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture — about 7% more for every 1°C of warming. As Ida tracked north it met a stalled cold front over the north-east. The collision squeezed an enormous load of tropical moisture out of the storm in a few hours. Climate scientists pointed out that this kind of extreme rainfall is now several times more likely than it was a century ago.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Immediate Responses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Louisiana ordered mandatory evacuations from low-lying coastal parishes 24 hours before landfall. The National Guard deployed 5,000 soldiers, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) pre-positioned generators and food, and 25,000 utility workers were sent in to restore power. The President declared a major disaster, freeing federal funds. Hospitals worked through the storm; emergency shelters opened in schools and gymnasiums.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Long-Term Responses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Recovery in Louisiana took months. Power was restored to most areas within two weeks, but parts of New Orleans were dark for over a month. Federal disaster relief and private insurance paid out around $50 billion. New York City changed its rules: basement flats now require flood-proofing if they are to be legally rented out, and the subway has new flood barriers. The state also accelerated investment in stormwater infrastructure. The hurricane prompted serious debate in the US about whether building codes should be tightened in known flood zones.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three lines: how did response in Louisiana differ from a low-income country's response after a similar storm?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The US had advance warning, mandatory evacuations, federal disaster funds and insurance pay-outs of $50 billion. In a low-income country, neither the warning nor the recovery would have moved that fast.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Reducing Tropical Storm Risk</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">As with tectonic hazards, four tools cut the risk: monitoring (satellites and aircraft track storms continuously), prediction (storm tracks are now forecast accurately three to five days in advance), protection (storm shutters, sea walls, building codes) and planning (evacuation routes, shelters, public education). Ida shows that even an HIC can be devastated — especially when storms hit areas that haven't planned for that level of rain.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Saffir-Simpson scale</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Hurricanes are graded from Category 1 (winds 119–153 km/h) to Category 5 (over 252 km/h) on the <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1-to-5 scale that classifies hurricanes by their sustained wind speeds.">Saffir-Simpson scale</dfn>. The scale predicts wind damage; it does <em>not</em> predict storm-surge or rainfall flooding, which is often the biggest killer. A Category 4 storm like Ida can be devastating, but a slow-moving Category 1 can also drown a city if it dumps enough rain.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Hurricane Ida (Aug 2021): Category 4 at landfall in Louisiana, 240 km/h winds, 4-metre storm surge.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Primary effects in Louisiana (~30 dead, 1 million without power) and secondary effects in the north-east (record rain, ~100 dead overall, $75 billion damage).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Effective response: pre-emptive evacuations, FEMA disaster funds, insurance payouts. New York changed building rules afterwards.</li>
</ul>
