<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is an Ecosystem?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A community of living organisms together with the non-living elements of their environment, interacting as a system.">ecosystem</dfn> is a community of living things and the non-living environment they depend on, working together as a system. The living parts are called <dfn class="term" data-def="The living parts of an ecosystem, including plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms.">biotic components</dfn>: plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms. The non-living parts are called <dfn class="term" data-def="The non-living parts of an ecosystem, such as soil, water, climate, sunlight and rocks.">abiotic components</dfn>: soil, water, climate, sunlight and rocks. The two interact constantly. Plants need soil and sunlight; animals need plants; soil is enriched by decomposing animals. Damage one component and the others feel it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">How Energy Moves: Food Chains and Webs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Energy enters an ecosystem from the Sun and moves through it in food chains. <strong>Producers</strong> are plants that make their own food by photosynthesis. <strong>Consumers</strong> eat other organisms — primary consumers eat plants, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and so on up the chain. <strong>Decomposers</strong> (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil. A <strong>food chain</strong> is a single line; a <strong>food web</strong> shows how chains interconnect, since most animals eat several things and are eaten by several things.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write from memory: define producer, consumer and decomposer, and explain the role each plays in an ecosystem.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Producers (plants) capture sunlight; consumers (animals) eat producers or each other; decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter and recycle nutrients back to the soil.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Nutrient Cycling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Nutrients move in cycles. Plants absorb nutrients from the soil. Animals eat plants and incorporate the nutrients into their bodies. When plants and animals die, decomposers break them down, and the nutrients return to the soil. The cycle has been running for millions of years; it is one of the things that makes ecosystems sustainable.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7a">Plants take up nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium from the soil. Animals eat plants and incorporate those nutrients into their tissues; some are also passed through the food chain to predators. When organisms die, their bodies fall to the soil, and bacteria and fungi break them down chemically. The nutrients return to the soil as inorganic compounds, ready to be taken up again. This recycling means the same atoms of nitrogen or carbon may pass through dozens of organisms before settling into long-term storage. The carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle and the water cycle are all examples of nutrient cycles operating at every scale — from a pond to the whole planet.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Case Study: A UK Pond Ecosystem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A small UK pond is a textbook ecosystem. The abiotic components are the water itself, dissolved oxygen, sunlight reaching the surface, and the mud at the bottom. The biotic components include algae and pondweed (producers), water fleas and pond snails (primary consumers), small fish such as sticklebacks (secondary consumers), kingfishers and herons (top consumers), and bacteria in the mud (decomposers). The whole system fits in a few square metres but contains all the same components as a rainforest.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Suppose fertiliser runoff from a nearby field washes into the pond. Algae use the extra nutrients and grow rapidly — <strong>algal bloom</strong>. The bloom blocks sunlight reaching deeper plants, which die. Their decomposition uses up oxygen. Fish suffocate. Within weeks, an apparently small change in nutrients has cascaded through the whole ecosystem. Geographers call this the <strong>balance</strong> of an ecosystem — a balance that can be tipped easily.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Global Ecosystems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">At a much bigger scale, the world is divided into <dfn class="term" data-def="A large-scale natural ecosystem defined by climate and dominant vegetation, such as tropical rainforest, hot desert or tundra.">biomes</dfn> — large-scale ecosystems sharing similar climate and vegetation. The main biomes you need to know are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Tropical rainforests</strong> — near the equator, hot and very wet, the most biodiverse biome.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hot deserts</strong> — around 30° north and south of the equator, very dry, with extreme day-night temperature ranges.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Temperate forests</strong> — mid-latitudes, mild and wet, deciduous trees that lose their leaves in winter.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Tundra</strong> — near the Arctic, cold, treeless, with frozen ground (permafrost).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Polar regions</strong> — ice-covered, the coldest biome.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Boreal forest (taiga)</strong> — below the tundra, conifers adapted to cold, vast across Russia and Canada.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Grasslands (savannah, prairie)</strong> — between deserts and forests, with seasonal rains.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and match each biome to a typical climate: rainforest, desert, tundra, savannah.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Biomes are determined by climate. The global pattern of biomes follows latitude (rainforest at the equator, desert at 30°, tundra near the Arctic) because climate follows the global atmospheric circulation system.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why scale matters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">A pond in a school playing field, an oak woodland in Sussex, the Amazon rainforest and Antarctica are all ecosystems. Scale changes the size and complexity but not the basic principles — producers feed consumers, decomposers recycle nutrients, abiotic conditions set the limits. This is why studying a pond is a useful way to understand a continent.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">An ecosystem is a community of biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components interacting as a system.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Producers, consumers and decomposers form food chains and webs; decomposers recycle nutrients into the soil.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">A small UK pond illustrates the same principles as global biomes — balance is delicate, and changing one component cascades through the system.</li>
</ul>
