--- DESCRIPTION ---
What makes an event a hazard, why risk varies, and how plate movement explains the global pattern.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Counts as a Natural Hazard?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Geographers use a precise definition. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural event that has the potential to cause death, injury or damage to property and infrastructure.">natural hazard</dfn> is an event from the natural environment with the potential to harm people or property. The same event in an empty desert is just a phenomenon — it only becomes a hazard when it threatens human life or what humans value. Hazards split into two main families: <strong>geological</strong> hazards (earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides) caused by Earth processes, and <strong>meteorological</strong> hazards (tropical storms, droughts, floods) caused by weather and climate.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: define natural hazard in one sentence, then list the two main types with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A natural hazard only becomes a hazard when it threatens people or property. Hazards are split into geological (e.g. earthquakes) and meteorological (e.g. tropical storms).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">What Affects Hazard Risk?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The probability or chance that a natural hazard will cause harm to people or property.">Hazard risk</dfn> is the chance of being harmed by a hazard. Several factors raise or lower it. <strong>Population density</strong> matters: when more people live in the danger zone, more lives are exposed. <strong>Wealth</strong> matters in a different way — richer countries can afford early-warning systems, stronger buildings and well-funded emergency services, so the death toll is usually lower. <strong>Frequency</strong> shapes preparedness: places that experience hazards regularly tend to plan for them. The <strong>type</strong> of hazard also changes risk — a tropical storm gives days of warning, while an earthquake gives seconds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Climate change is now reshaping risk too. Rising sea levels increase coastal flood risk; warmer oceans fuel more intense tropical storms; shifting rainfall patterns expand drought zones. Risk is not fixed — it changes as the climate, population and economy change.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Earth's Inner Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">To understand why earthquakes and volcanoes happen where they do, you need to know what is under your feet. The Earth has four layers. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The thin, solid outer layer of the Earth, divided into oceanic and continental types.">crust</dfn> is the thin, solid outer skin (5–70 km thick). Below it sits the <dfn class="term" data-def="The thick, semi-molten layer of rock between the Earth's crust and core, containing convection currents that move tectonic plates.">mantle</dfn> — thick, hot, semi-molten rock that flows slowly. The <strong>outer core</strong> is liquid iron and nickel; the <strong>inner core</strong> is solid metal under enormous pressure. Heat from the core drives slow circulation in the mantle, and that circulation moves the crust above.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The inner core is hotter than the outer core, so logic suggests it should be more molten. But pressure deep inside the Earth is so extreme that atoms cannot move freely — they are forced into a solid lattice. Pressure wins out over heat. This is why seismologists can tell the inner core is solid: certain seismic waves only travel through solids, and they are detected on the far side of the planet only after passing through the inner core.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Plate Tectonics Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The crust is broken into about fifteen large pieces called <dfn class="term" data-def="A large section of the Earth's crust that moves slowly across the surface, driven by convection currents in the mantle.">tectonic plates</dfn>. They float on the mantle and move very slowly — just a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. The boundaries between plates are where almost all earthquakes and volcanic eruptions happen. There are two kinds of plate. <strong>Oceanic plates</strong> are denser, thinner and made mostly of basalt; they form the floor of the world's oceans. <strong>Continental plates</strong> are thicker, lighter and made of older granite-type rock; they make up the land masses.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: how do convection currents in the mantle make plates move?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Heat from the Earth's core creates convection currents in the mantle. Hot rock rises, cools, and sinks — dragging the plates above it. This is the engine of plate tectonics.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why Hazards Cluster on the Plate Edges</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Map the world's earthquakes and volcanoes and a striking pattern appears. The <strong>Pacific Ring of Fire</strong> traces the edge of the Pacific Plate — California, Japan, Indonesia, the Andes — and contains around 75% of all volcanoes and 90% of all earthquakes. A second band runs along the Mediterranean, the Himalayas and into south-east Asia, where Africa and India are colliding into Europe and Asia. The middle of plates — central Australia, central Africa, northern Russia — is much quieter. The pattern is the single most important piece of evidence for plate tectonics: hazards happen at boundaries, not randomly.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Inside-plate ('intraplate') hazards</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Some earthquakes do happen inside plates — for example, in the New Madrid zone in the central United States or near the Yellowstone hotspot. These intraplate hazards are rarer and usually weaker, caused by ancient faults reactivating or by a magma plume punching through the plate from below. The general rule still holds: most hazards happen at plate edges, but the rule is not absolute.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Why This Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of physical geography. It explains why earthquakes destroy Kathmandu but not London, why volcanoes line the Andes but not central Australia, and why mountain belts form where continents collide. In the next lesson, you will see how each type of plate boundary — constructive, destructive and conservative — produces a different pattern of hazards.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">A natural hazard is an event with the potential to harm people or property — geological hazards come from inside the Earth, meteorological from the atmosphere.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Hazard risk depends on population density, wealth, frequency and the type of hazard — all changing over time as the climate and economy shift.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Convection currents in the semi-molten mantle drive tectonic plates, and the global pattern of earthquakes and volcanoes — especially the Pacific Ring of Fire — traces those plate boundaries.</li>
</ul>
