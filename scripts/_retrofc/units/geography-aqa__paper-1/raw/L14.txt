--- DESCRIPTION ---
How destructive and constructive waves shape cliffs, beaches and longshore drift on UK coasts.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Kinds of Wave</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All coastal change starts with the wave. Waves are formed when wind blows across the sea surface, dragging energy from the air into the water. The distance the wind blows over — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance of open water over which a wind has blown to generate waves; longer fetch produces larger waves.">fetch</dfn> — controls how big the wave gets. Atlantic-facing UK coasts like Cornwall and Pembrokeshire have a fetch of thousands of kilometres, so they receive much bigger waves than sheltered North Sea coasts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Geographers split waves into two types. <dfn class="term" data-def="A high-energy wave with a strong backwash that erodes the coast and drags material out to sea.">Destructive waves</dfn> are tall, steep and frequent (10–14 per minute). Their <strong>backwash</strong> — the water rushing back down the beach — is more powerful than their <strong>swash</strong>, so they drag sand and shingle away from the coast. <dfn class="term" data-def="A low-energy wave with a strong swash that builds beaches by depositing sand and shingle.">Constructive waves</dfn> are lower, longer and less frequent (6–8 per minute). Their swash beats their backwash, so they push material up the beach and build it. Storm conditions favour destructive waves; calm weather favours constructive waves.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how do destructive and constructive waves differ in height, frequency and net effect on the beach?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Destructive waves are tall, steep and frequent (10–14 per minute) with strong backwash that erodes. Constructive waves are low, long and less frequent (6–8 per minute) with strong swash that builds beaches.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Weathering at the Coast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Before waves can erode a cliff, weathering often weakens the rock first. <dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of rock in place by physical, chemical or biological processes, without movement.">Weathering</dfn> happens before any material is moved. Two types matter at the UK coast. <strong>Mechanical weathering</strong> includes freeze–thaw: water seeps into cracks, freezes overnight, expands by 9% and prises the rock apart. The chalk cliffs of the South Downs lose chunks each winter this way. <strong>Chemical weathering</strong> includes carbonation: rainwater absorbs CO₂ and forms a weak carbonic acid that slowly dissolves limestone and chalk, widening cracks and joints.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Mass Movement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Once weathered, weakened cliff material falls under gravity. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="The downslope movement of rock and soil under gravity, including sliding, slumping and rock falls.">mass movement</dfn>. There are three forms to know:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Sliding</strong> — rock and soil slip down a flat surface in a single block, usually after heavy rain saturates the ground.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Slumping</strong> — saturated soft cliffs (like the boulder clay of Holderness) rotate downwards along a curved surface, leaving a stepped cliff face. Slumping is the dominant process on East Yorkshire's coast.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Rock falls</strong> — chunks of rock break off vertical or near-vertical cliffs and tumble to the beach. Common on the chalk cliffs at Beachy Head.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Wave Erosion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Destructive waves erode the coast in four ways:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Four Erosion Processes</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hydraulic power.</strong> Waves slam into a cliff and force air into cracks at high pressure. As the wave retreats the trapped air expands explosively, prising the rock apart. This is the dominant process during storms on hard-rock coasts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Abrasion</strong> (sometimes called corrasion). Waves pick up sand, pebbles and rocks and hurl them at the cliff base, sandpapering it away. You can see abrasion's smoothed, polished base on most UK sea cliffs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Attrition.</strong> The pebbles and rocks already in the water knock against each other, slowly becoming smaller, smoother and rounder. Attrition does not erode the cliff — it shapes the sediment that eventually becomes beach material.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Solution</strong> (corrosion). Slightly acidic seawater dissolves alkaline rock such as limestone and chalk. Slow but steady — it widens joints and helps caves form.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Transportation: Longshore Drift</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Eroded sediment doesn't stay still. The most important transport process at the UK coast is <dfn class="term" data-def="The zigzag movement of sediment along a beach, driven by waves arriving at an angle and then washing back down the slope.">longshore drift</dfn>. Waves usually approach the beach at an angle (driven by the prevailing wind), so their swash carries sand up the beach diagonally. Gravity then pulls the backwash straight back down the slope. The result is a zigzag movement of sediment along the coast, often hundreds of metres a year.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">On the Holderness coast, longshore drift moves sediment southwards from Flamborough Head towards Spurn Head at around 1.5 metres per day. On the south coast of England, sediment moves eastwards along the English Channel. Where this drift is interrupted by a structure such as a groyne or harbour wall, sand piles up on one side and the beach shrinks on the other.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and sketch a beach with arrows showing swash arriving at an angle and backwash returning straight down. Label the prevailing wind.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast in a zigzag pattern. The swash arrives at an angle (set by the prevailing wind), the backwash returns straight down the slope. Net movement on UK east and south coasts is southwards or eastwards.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Deposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Waves drop their sediment when they no longer have the energy to carry it. <strong>Deposition</strong> happens where the water is sheltered (inside a bay, behind a headland, or in a river estuary), where waves are constructive, or where transport is interrupted (against a groyne, for example). Sand is dropped first as it is finest; pebbles and shingle are dropped where wave energy is higher.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Wave Type Changes Through the Year</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">UK beaches change shape with the seasons. In summer, calm conditions and constructive waves rebuild the beach — sand and shingle are pushed up to form a high berm. In winter, frequent Atlantic storms send destructive waves that drag material back offshore, lowering the beach profile and exposing the cliff base to attack. The same beach can look completely different in July and January. Coastal managers use this to time soft engineering: beach nourishment is usually done in spring, before summer's natural rebuild begins.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Destructive waves (tall, frequent, strong backwash) erode coasts; constructive waves (low, less frequent, strong swash) build beaches.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Coasts are shaped by weathering (freeze–thaw, carbonation), mass movement (sliding, slumping, rock falls) and four wave-erosion processes (hydraulic power, abrasion, attrition, solution).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast in a zigzag pattern, set by the prevailing wind — around 1.5 m per day on the Holderness coast.</li>
</ul>
