--- DESCRIPTION ---
How layers, climate and species interconnect, and why biodiversity is so concentrated here.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The World's Most Biodiverse Biome</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tropical rainforests cover only 6% of the Earth's land surface but contain more than half of all species. They lie in a band on either side of the equator: the Amazon, the Congo, parts of South-East Asia and northern Australia. The climate is hot and wet all year. Average temperatures stay between 25°C and 30°C. Rainfall is over 2,000 mm a year, often falling daily in the afternoon. The lack of seasons means plants and animals can grow continuously.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Structure: The Four Layers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Tropical rainforests are layered like a multi-storey building. Each level has its own climate and species.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Emergent layer</strong> — tallest trees, up to 50 metres, breaking through the canopy. Strong winds and direct sunlight; harpy eagles nest here.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Canopy</strong> — the dense leafy roof, around 30–40 metres high. Most photosynthesis happens here, and most rainforest species live here. Howler monkeys, sloths and parrots feed in the canopy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Understorey</strong> — smaller trees, shaded by the canopy. Plants have large leaves to capture what little light reaches them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Forest floor</strong> — dim, humid, with thin soil. Decomposers work fast here, breaking down fallen leaves within weeks.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the four rainforest layers in order from tallest to ground, and one species in each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rainforests have four layers: emergent (tallest, ~50 m), canopy (most species), understorey (shade), and forest floor (decomposers). Each supports different organisms.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Interdependence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In a rainforest, climate, plants, animals and soil all depend on each other. Hot, wet conditions support fast plant growth. Plants release water vapour through their leaves (<dfn class="term" data-def="The release of water vapour from plant leaves into the atmosphere.">transpiration</dfn>), which forms clouds and falls as rain — meaning the forest <em>creates</em> much of its own rainfall. Animals pollinate plants and disperse their seeds. Decomposers recycle nutrients fast enough that the thin soil supports the world's tallest trees. Cut down enough trees and the cycle breaks — less transpiration means less rain, drier conditions, and the forest cannot regrow.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">It seems counter-intuitive that the world's most productive ecosystem sits on poor soil. But because decomposition is so fast in the heat and humidity, nutrients don't accumulate — they are taken up by plants almost immediately. The forest holds most of its nutrients in living biomass, not the soil. This is also why slash-and-burn farming destroys rainforest fertility quickly: cut the trees, and the nutrients are gone.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Adaptations of Plants</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Plants compete fiercely for light. <strong>Buttress roots</strong> spread sideways at the base of tall trees to support their height in shallow soil. <strong>Drip-tip leaves</strong> are pointed so that heavy rain runs off quickly — if water sat on leaves, mould would grow. <dfn class="term" data-def="Woody vines that climb up other trees in tropical forests rather than growing their own thick trunks.">Lianas</dfn> are woody vines that climb other trees rather than growing their own thick trunks. <strong>Epiphytes</strong> — orchids, bromeliads — live on the branches of other trees, getting nutrients from rainwater and air. Some plants flower and fruit only once every few years to avoid being predictable to predators.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Adaptations of Animals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Animals are adapted to the layered environment. <strong>Howler monkeys</strong> have prehensile tails that work as a fifth limb in the canopy. <strong>Sloths</strong> move so slowly that algae grow in their fur, providing camouflage. <strong>Poison dart frogs</strong> are brightly coloured to warn predators of toxic skin. <strong>Toucans</strong> have huge bills to reach fruit on slender branches that wouldn't support their weight. <strong>Jaguars</strong> are the only big cat that hunts mainly in water.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three sentences: how do drip-tip leaves, buttress roots and lianas help plants survive in a rainforest?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Rainforest plants compete for light. Buttress roots support tall trees in thin soil; drip-tip leaves shed heavy rain; lianas climb others; epiphytes grow on branches; flowering can be unpredictable to outwit predators.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Issues of Biodiversity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of plant and animal life in a particular habitat or in the world as a whole, often used as a measure of ecosystem health.">Biodiversity</dfn> in tropical rainforests is unmatched. The Amazon alone contains an estimated 16,000 tree species and 2.5 million insect species. This biodiversity has enormous value: 25% of modern medicines come from rainforest plants. The yew tree of the Amazon produced taxol, used to treat cancer. Many rainforest species have not yet been studied. Every hectare lost is a potential cure or food source disappearing forever. Lose biodiversity and the next pandemic, the next antibiotic, the next staple crop may be gone before we know it existed.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The 'biotic pump' — how rainforests make rain</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Up to 50% of the rainfall in the Amazon is recycled water that has passed through plants. Trees draw water from the soil and release it through their leaves; the water vapour rises, forms clouds and falls as rain hundreds of kilometres away. Recent research suggests forests act as a 'biotic pump', pulling moist air inland from the ocean. Cut the forest, and rainfall declines — not just locally but across continents.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Tropical rainforests are hot and wet all year, with four distinct layers (emergent, canopy, understorey, floor).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Climate, plants, animals and soil are deeply interdependent — cut the trees and the rainfall, soil and nutrient cycles all break.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Adaptations include buttress roots, drip-tip leaves, lianas, prehensile tails and toxic skin. Biodiversity is unmatched and yields medicines we still depend on.</li>
</ul>
