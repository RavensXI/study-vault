--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why hot deserts look the way they do and what economic activity they support.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Hot Deserts: Where and Why</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Hot deserts are not just hot — they are very dry. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A region receiving less than 250mm of rainfall a year, with extreme temperature ranges and sparse vegetation.">hot desert</dfn> receives less than 250 mm of rainfall a year. They lie in two bands at around 30° north and south of the equator, where the global atmospheric circulation system pushes dry air down. The Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari, Atacama and Australian deserts all sit on these latitudes. Most of these deserts are also far inland, beyond the reach of moist sea air, or in the rain-shadow of mountain ranges.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Climate of Hot Deserts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Daytime temperatures often reach 45°C in summer. With clear skies and no humidity, heat escapes quickly at night, and temperatures can fall to near freezing. This extreme <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between the daytime maximum and nighttime minimum temperature, often very large in deserts because there is no humidity to retain heat.">diurnal temperature range</dfn> is one reason desert weathering is so harsh. Rainfall is sparse, unreliable and often arrives as flash downpours.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where do hot deserts lie on the planet, and why does the global atmospheric circulation put them there?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hot deserts lie at around 30° north and south of the equator, where dry sinking air at the bottom of the global atmospheric circulation creates high pressure and clear skies.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Plants: Adaptations to Heat and Drought</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Desert plants survive by minimising water loss and storing what little they get. <strong>Cacti</strong> have thick, fleshy stems that store water and waxy skins that reduce evaporation. Their <strong>spines</strong> are modified leaves — less surface area than full leaves means less water lost to transpiration. <strong>Roots</strong> are either very deep (acacia trees can reach 30 metres down to the water table) or very wide and shallow (catching water from rare storms over a wide area). Some plants like the <strong>desert poppy</strong> are dormant for years and bloom only after a rare downpour.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Animals: Adaptations to Heat and Drought</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Most desert animals are <strong>nocturnal</strong>, sleeping through the day's heat in burrows and emerging at night. <strong>Camels</strong> can drink up to 100 litres of water in 10 minutes, store fat in their humps (releasing water when broken down), have wide feet that don't sink in sand, and double rows of eyelashes that block dust. <strong>Fennec foxes</strong> have huge ears that radiate heat. <strong>Kangaroo rats</strong> never need to drink — their kidneys produce extremely concentrated urine, and they get water from the seeds they eat. <strong>Reptiles</strong> like lizards bury themselves in cool sand at midday.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Case Study: The Western Desert of Australia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Australia's Western Desert covers around 270,000 km², similar in size to the UK. Average rainfall is below 250 mm. Temperatures regularly exceed 45°C. The land supports only sparse vegetation — spinifex grass, mulga trees, eucalypts adapted to drought. Indigenous Aboriginal peoples have lived there for over 40,000 years, mastering the desert long before modern technology made it accessible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Development Opportunities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hot deserts offer real economic opportunities. <dfn class="term" data-def="The mining or quarrying of valuable minerals such as iron ore, copper or gold from the ground.">Mineral extraction</dfn>: the Western Desert has iron ore, gold, copper, uranium and lithium — raw materials that drive Australia's economy. <strong>Energy</strong>: solar farms generate electricity from the abundant sunshine. <strong>Farming</strong>: with irrigation, even desert can produce crops — the Murray-Darling basin is a textbook example. <strong>Tourism</strong>: Uluru (Ayers Rock) attracts hundreds of thousands of visitors a year. <strong>Indigenous land rights</strong>: many traditional Aboriginal lands now generate income through tourism, art and royalties from mining.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three lines: how do mineral extraction, solar energy and tourism each make hot deserts economically valuable?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hot deserts support real economies: mining (iron ore, gold), solar energy, irrigated farming, and tourism (Uluru). Indigenous peoples often hold legal rights to traditional desert lands.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Challenges of Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Three challenges dominate. <strong>Extreme temperatures</strong> make working hours limited — outdoor labour stops between 10am and 4pm in summer. <strong>Water supply</strong> is the largest constraint. Mines, towns and farms all need water, and drilling deeper for groundwater can permanently lower the water table. <strong>Inaccessibility</strong>: roads, ports and airports must be built across vast distances at high cost. The Pilbara mining region in Western Australia spent decades and billions of dollars constructing the rail and port network needed to ship ore to East Asia.</p>

Commercial mining can disturb sacred sites and pollute groundwater. Australia's 1993 Native Title Act recognised Aboriginal land rights, requiring miners to negotiate with traditional owners. The 2020 destruction of the Juukan Gorge rock shelters by Rio Tinto, despite their 46,000-year history, was a major scandal — showing how economic and cultural values clash even with legal frameworks in place.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Hot deserts lie at 30° north and south of the equator, with less than 250 mm of rain a year and extreme temperature ranges.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Plants and animals are adapted: thick stems, deep roots, nocturnal behaviour, water-saving physiology.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Australia's Western Desert offers mining, solar energy, farming and tourism, but extreme heat, water scarcity and inaccessibility limit development.</li>
</ul>
