--- DESCRIPTION ---
Tracing the formation of upper, middle and lower-course river landforms with UK examples.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Each Course Has Its Own Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Different parts of a river create different landforms because the dominant process is different. The upper course is shaped by vertical erosion (cutting down), the middle course by lateral erosion (cutting sideways), and the lower course by deposition (dropping the load). Examiners expect you to know one named UK example for the major landforms — learn the chain, then learn one place name for each.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Upper Course: Interlocking Spurs and Waterfalls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In the upper course the river has not got enough energy to cut sideways through the hills, so it weaves between them. The result is <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of ridges that project alternately from each side of a V-shaped river valley, around which the river weaves in its upper course.">interlocking spurs</dfn> — ridges that project from alternate sides of the valley, with the river snaking between them. The view down the valley looks like fingers interlocking from each side.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A point where a river drops vertically over a step in the bed, usually formed where a band of hard rock lies over softer rock.">waterfall</dfn> forms where a band of hard rock lies over softer rock. The softer rock erodes faster, undercutting the hard rock above. The river falls vertically into a deep pool at the base — the <strong>plunge pool</strong> — which is itself eroded by hydraulic action and abrasion. Over thousands of years the hard rock above eventually collapses without support, and the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a steep narrow <strong>gorge</strong> behind it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Middle Course: Meanders and Ox-Bow Lakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Once the gradient flattens, the river starts swinging from side to side. The result is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A bend in a river formed where lateral erosion is greater on one side of the channel and deposition is greater on the other.">meander</dfn>. On the outer bank the water flows fastest because the channel is deeper there; this energy erodes the bank into a steep <strong>river cliff</strong>. On the inner bank the water flows slowly because the channel is shallow; the river drops material here, building a gentle <strong>slip-off slope</strong> of sand and shingle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Over time the meander becomes more and more curved as erosion deepens the outer bend and deposition builds the inner one. Eventually two outer bends erode towards each other; during a flood the river cuts across the narrow neck between them. The new straighter channel takes over and the abandoned meander loop becomes an <dfn class="term" data-def="A horseshoe-shaped lake formed when a meander is cut off from the main river during a flood, leaving the abandoned curve as a separate body of water.">ox-bow lake</dfn>. Ox-bow lakes are common on the lower River Severn near Worcester, and on the River Cuckmere in East Sussex.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Lower Course: Flood Plains, Levees and Estuaries</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Near the mouth, the river spreads out across a wide flat valley. When the river floods, water spreads beyond the channel. As it does so it slows down, and the coarsest material is dropped first — right next to the channel. Over hundreds of floods this builds <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural raised bank along a river channel, formed by repeated deposition during floods.">levees</dfn> — raised natural banks that line the river. Beyond the levees, the floodwater drops finer silt across the wider flood plain, creating fertile farmland.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">At the mouth the river meets the sea and slows further still. If the tide is strong it forms a wide <strong>estuary</strong> — the lower Severn, Thames and Humber are good UK examples. The fresh and salt water mix; mudflats and salt marsh develop along the banks. Estuaries are vital ports (Liverpool, Bristol, Hull) and important wildlife habitats.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a meander, water flows fastest on the outer bend, eroding it into a steep river cliff. On the inner bend the water is slow, so material is deposited as a gentle slip-off slope.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">A Worked UK Example: The River Tees</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The Tees rises in the Pennines and flows 137 km to the North Sea at Middlesbrough. In its upper course on Cross Fell it shows interlocking spurs and the High Force waterfall. In its middle course near Yarm it loops in textbook meanders. Near its mouth it widens into a large estuary lined with industrial complexes. One river demonstrates almost every fluvial landform in the course, which is why exam questions sometimes ask for landforms 'along' a named UK river.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Upper-course landforms (interlocking spurs, waterfalls and gorges) form by vertical erosion — High Force on the River Tees is the textbook UK example.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Middle-course meanders form because the river erodes the outer bank and deposits on the inner bank; cutting across the neck during a flood creates an ox-bow lake.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Lower-course landforms (levees, flood plains and estuaries) form by deposition as the river slows down and spreads out near the sea.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For 6-mark 'landform formation' questions, structure your answer as a numbered sequence with cause and effect at each stage. Examiners reward sequential logic over a list of features. Always include a UK named example: High Force for waterfalls, the lower Severn for ox-bow lakes, the lower Tees for an estuary. Stages without examples lose the place-detail marks; examples without stages lose the process marks.</p>
