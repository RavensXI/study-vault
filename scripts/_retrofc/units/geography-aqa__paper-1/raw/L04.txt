--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the four management strategies cut the human cost of earthquakes and eruptions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Managing Tectonic Hazards: The Four Ps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">No country can stop an earthquake or an eruption. What we can do is reduce the human cost. Geographers group risk-reduction strategies under four headings: <strong>monitoring, prediction, protection</strong> and <strong>planning</strong>. They are sometimes called the four Ps. Each one targets a different part of the disaster cycle — before, during and after the event.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Monitoring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Recording activity around a hazard such as ground movement, gas emissions or temperature, in order to give early warning.">Monitoring</dfn> means watching for warning signs. <strong>Volcanoes</strong> can be monitored using seismometers (to detect tremors caused by rising magma), GPS sensors (to spot ground bulging) and gas sniffers (sulphur dioxide rises before an eruption). The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory has detected and tracked dozens of eruptions, allowing people to leave on time. <strong>Earthquakes</strong> are harder to monitor in advance, but stress measurements along faults and changes in groundwater levels are studied. Earthquakes still strike with little warning, which is why monitoring is much more useful for volcanoes than for quakes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three things volcano scientists monitor and what each one tells them.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Volcanoes are monitored using seismometers (small tremors signal rising magma), GPS sensors (ground bulges as magma fills the chamber) and gas sniffers (sulphur dioxide rises before an eruption).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Prediction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using monitoring data and historical patterns to forecast when and where a hazard is likely to occur.">Prediction</dfn> goes beyond watching — it tries to forecast when and where. Volcanic eruptions can sometimes be predicted hours or days in advance from a combination of seismic activity, gas readings and ground deformation. The 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull in Iceland was predicted accurately enough that nearby farms were evacuated. <strong>Earthquake prediction is much harder.</strong> No reliable method exists for predicting the day, hour or street where an earthquake will strike. The best science can do is give probabilities — for example, 'there is a 60% chance of a magnitude 6+ earthquake in the San Francisco Bay Area in the next 30 years.' That kind of forecast is useful for planners but does not warn individuals.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Japan's system doesn't predict earthquakes hours in advance — it gives <em>seconds</em>. Sensors detect the fast-moving but harmless P-waves of a quake, calculate the size, and send alerts to phones, trains and factories before the slower, destructive S-waves arrive. Even ten seconds is enough to stop a bullet train, shut off gas mains and warn people to take cover. It saved hundreds of lives in the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Protection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Designing buildings, infrastructure and defences to withstand the impact of a natural hazard.">Protection</dfn> is the strongest tool in the kit. It means designing buildings, infrastructure and defences that can withstand the hazard.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Earthquake-resistant buildings.</strong> Reinforced concrete frames, steel cross-bracing and base isolators (rubber-and-steel pads that let buildings sway) absorb shaking. Tokyo's skyscrapers can flex by several metres in an earthquake without falling.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Lava barriers and trenches.</strong> Around Mount Etna in Sicily, concrete barriers have been built to divert lava away from villages. They cannot stop the eruption but they can save homes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Tsunami sea walls.</strong> Japan's coastline is lined with concrete walls up to 12 metres high. They saved lives in 2011, although the Fukushima wave overtopped them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Strong public buildings.</strong> Schools, hospitals and police stations are built to higher seismic standards than ordinary homes, so they can act as refuges and continue functioning after a disaster.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Planning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Preparing communities for a hazard through education, drills, hazard maps, evacuation routes and emergency supplies.">Planning</dfn> is what saves lives in the moments and hours after the hazard hits. <strong>Hazard maps</strong> identify which areas are likely to be flooded by tsunami, buried by lava or shaken hardest. Local authorities use them to decide what to build where. <strong>Evacuation routes</strong> are signposted in volcanic and tsunami zones. Japan runs nationwide earthquake drills every September; in California, schools practise 'drop, cover, hold on' regularly. Emergency-services rehearsals, well-stocked supplies and clear chains of command turn what could be a chaotic scramble into a co-ordinated response.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence each: what does each of the four Ps actually do to reduce risk?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four Ps work together. Monitoring gathers data, prediction interprets it, protection makes places stronger, and planning prepares people. Remove any one and the others become weaker.</p>
</div>

Insurance companies use them to set premiums; planners use them to decide where to allow new schools and hospitals; residents use them to plan escape routes. Iceland's volcano hazard maps cover every active system.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why HICs Manage Better Than LICs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">High-income countries can afford the technology, the building codes and the trained services. Japan invests billions in tsunami walls; New Zealand drills its schoolchildren; Iceland tracks every volcano in real time. Low-income countries face a harder choice. Money spent on hazard management is money not spent on health, education or roads. International aid programmes (such as those run by the UN and World Bank) help, but the gap remains. The lesson is that risk reduction is not just a science problem — it is a development problem.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Monitoring uses seismometers, GPS and gas sensors to spot warning signs — very effective for volcanoes, less so for earthquakes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Prediction works hours or days ahead for some volcanoes; reliable earthquake prediction does not yet exist, but seconds-warning systems (Japan) save lives.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Protection (earthquake-resistant buildings, sea walls, lava barriers) and planning (hazard maps, drills, evacuation routes) save the most lives, especially in HICs.</li>
</ul>
