--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why tropical storms form on certain latitudes and how the global pressure system drives weather.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Global Atmospheric Engine</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The world's weather is not random. It is driven by the way the atmosphere circulates between the equator and the poles. The Sun heats the equator more strongly than the poles, and this temperature difference sets the air in motion. Geographers describe this with the <dfn class="term" data-def="A simplified model of how air rises, sinks and moves between the equator and the poles to redistribute heat around the planet.">global atmospheric circulation model</dfn>. It explains why the equator is hot and wet, why the Sahara and Australian deserts sit at the same latitudes, and why the UK is windy and changeable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Pressure Belts and Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The model splits the atmosphere into three giant <strong>circulation cells</strong> in each hemisphere. At the equator, hot air rises. As it rises it cools and releases moisture, producing the heavy rainfall of the rainforest belt. This is a zone of <dfn class="term" data-def="An area where air rises, creating cloud and rain at the surface.">low pressure</dfn>. The risen air spreads polewards and sinks again at around 30° north and south. Sinking air warms and dries, creating the world's <dfn class="term" data-def="An area where air sinks, creating dry conditions at the surface.">high pressure</dfn> deserts — the Sahara, Arabian and Australian deserts all sit on this belt. Air then either flows back to the equator (the trade winds) or polewards to around 60°, where it meets cold polar air, rises again, and produces another belt of low pressure and stormy weather. The UK sits on this belt — which is why our weather is so unpredictable.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write from memory: where on the planet is air rising, and where is it sinking? Match each to a typical climate.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Air rises at the equator (low pressure, rainforest) and at 60° (low pressure, UK weather). Air sinks at 30° (high pressure, deserts) and at the poles (high pressure, polar deserts).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Tropical Storms: Where and When</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A large rotating storm with very strong winds and heavy rain that forms over warm tropical oceans.">Tropical storms</dfn> have several names depending on where they form: <strong>hurricanes</strong> in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, <strong>typhoons</strong> in the western Pacific, and <strong>cyclones</strong> in the Indian Ocean. They all form between 5° and 30° north and south of the equator — never on the equator itself, because the spin needed to rotate the storm only exists away from it. The peak season is late summer in each hemisphere, when ocean temperatures are highest. Most form between June and November in the northern hemisphere.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">How a Tropical Storm Forms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Several conditions must come together for a storm to develop. The ocean must be at least 26.5°C, and warm to a depth of about 50 metres — this provides the energy. The air must be unstable so that it rises easily, and there must be enough <dfn class="term" data-def="The deflection of moving air or water caused by the Earth's rotation, to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern.">Coriolis effect</dfn> to set the storm spinning — this is why storms form away from the equator. Wind speeds must also be similar from sea level upwards, otherwise the storm gets sheared apart before it can organise.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When all these conditions align, warm moist air rises rapidly off the ocean surface. As it rises it cools, and the water vapour condenses into clouds — releasing huge amounts of latent heat. This heat warms the surrounding air, causing it to rise faster, drawing in more warm air from the sea below. The Coriolis effect bends the inflowing air into a spiral. The storm is born.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">The eye of the storm is a calm, clear column at the centre, around 30–65 km wide, where air is descending. Around the eye is the <strong>eye wall</strong> — a ring of towering thunderstorms with the strongest winds and heaviest rain. Outside the eye wall, spiral rain bands stretch hundreds of kilometres. The whole system can be over 1,000 km across, but the most violent activity is concentrated in the eye wall.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Climate Change and Tropical Storms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Climate change is heating the oceans, and warmer water means more energy available to fuel storms. Most scientists agree on three changes for a warmer world: <strong>more intense</strong> tropical storms (higher wind speeds, more rain), <strong>storms reaching higher latitudes</strong> (places like New York and Lisbon may face threats they have not faced before), and <strong>longer storm seasons</strong>. Whether the total <em>number</em> of storms is increasing is less certain — some research suggests fewer but stronger storms.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in your own words: how does each of the three required conditions (warm sea, low wind shear, Coriolis) help a tropical storm grow?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tropical storms need ocean water above 26.5°C, low wind shear, and the Coriolis effect away from the equator. Climate change makes storms more intense, longer-lived and capable of reaching higher latitudes.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The Coriolis effect is what makes storms spin. It only works when an object is moving across latitudes — on the equator itself, the deflection is zero. Without Coriolis, low-pressure systems can grow but they cannot organise into a rotating storm. That is why hurricanes appear at 10–30° north or south but never directly on the line.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Global atmospheric circulation creates pressure belts: low at the equator and 60°, high at 30° and the poles. These explain global climate patterns.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Tropical storms need warm oceans (26.5°C+), low wind shear and the Coriolis effect — all reasons they form between 5° and 30° from the equator.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Climate change is making storms more intense, expanding their range polewards and lengthening the storm season.</li>
</ul>
