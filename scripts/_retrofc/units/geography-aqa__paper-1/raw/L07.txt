--- DESCRIPTION ---
How extreme UK weather events form and the social, economic and environmental damage they cause.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">UK Weather Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK does not face hurricanes or volcanic eruptions, but it does experience a range of weather hazards. Storms, heavy rain, snow and ice, drought, heatwaves and strong winds all cause disruption every year. The UK's position on the boundary between cold polar air and warm tropical air — the polar front — gives it the changeable weather it is famous for. As the climate warms, several of these hazards are becoming more extreme.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Types of UK Weather Hazard</h2>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Wind storms.</strong> Atlantic depressions can bring 100 km/h winds to UK coasts. Storms are now formally named (e.g. Storm Eunice, Feb 2022, with gusts of 196 km/h on the Isle of Wight).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Heavy rain and flooding.</strong> Persistent low pressure can bring weeks of rain. The Cumbria floods of December 2015 and the winter floods of 2019–20 are recent examples.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Snow and ice.</strong> Cold continental air can bury parts of the UK. The 'Beast from the East' of 2018 dumped half a metre of snow and shut schools and motorways.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Heatwaves.</strong> The UK passed 40°C for the first time in July 2022. Wildfires broke out around London and railway tracks buckled.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Drought.</strong> The summer of 2022 was the driest since 1976; reservoirs fell to 50% and hosepipe bans covered most of England.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three different UK weather hazards and a recent example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK weather hazards include storms, floods, heatwaves, droughts and snow events. Recent extreme examples include Storm Eunice (Feb 2022, 196 km/h gusts) and the July 2022 heatwave (UK passed 40°C for the first time).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Case Study: Storm Desmond, December 2015</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A December 2015 Atlantic storm that brought record-breaking rainfall to north-west England, causing severe flooding in Cumbria.">Storm Desmond</dfn> was an Atlantic depression that struck the UK on 4–5 December 2015. Powered by a corridor of warm, moist air from the Atlantic — sometimes called an <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrow band of concentrated water vapour in the atmosphere that can transport huge volumes of moisture from the tropics towards higher latitudes.">atmospheric river</dfn> — it dumped extraordinary amounts of rain onto north-west England. Honister Pass in the Lake District recorded 341.4 mm of rain in 24 hours, then a UK record. Rivers in Cumbria, including the Eden and the Kent, burst their banks within hours.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Causes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Several factors made Desmond so extreme. <strong>Saturated ground</strong>: November had already been wet, so the soil could absorb little more. <strong>Steep relief</strong>: the Lake District's mountains forced the moist air upwards, intensifying rainfall (orographic rainfall). <strong>Atmospheric river</strong>: a narrow corridor of tropical moisture funnelled directly at the UK. <strong>Climate change</strong>: warmer Atlantic waters held more water vapour, raising rainfall totals by an estimated 40% compared to a pre-industrial Desmond.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13a">The combination is what made the storm record-breaking. Storms with one or two of these factors happen most years. A storm with all four is something the UK has rarely seen before. As the climate continues to warm, atmospheric rivers carrying more moisture will become more frequent. Saturated ground in autumn will carry forward into winter rain events more often. Defences sized for 20th-century rainfall totals will be tested more regularly. The pattern is not abstract — it is being measured year by year by the Met Office. Storm Desmond is now studied as a textbook case of <dfn class="term" data-def="A weather or climate event whose probability has been changed by human-driven climate change.">attribution science</dfn>, where researchers measure how much human-driven climate change has shifted the odds of a specific extreme event.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Impacts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Around 5,200 homes were flooded across Cumbria and Lancashire. Bridges in Carlisle, Cockermouth and Pooley Bridge collapsed. The town of Carlisle was hit hardest — 2,200 properties flooded, including a hospital that had to be evacuated. Two people died directly. Total economic cost was estimated at over £500 million. The clean-up took months; some families were not back in their homes for over a year.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Responses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Environment Agency issued severe flood warnings 24 hours before the worst rain. The Army was deployed to evacuate residents. Mountain rescue teams worked through the night. After the immediate emergency, the government provided £50 million in financial aid. The flood response prompted a major review of flood defences nationally, and Cumbria's defences were rebuilt to higher standards.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three sentences: how does Storm Desmond show that UK weather is becoming more extreme?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Storm Desmond's 341 mm of rain in 24 hours broke the UK record. Climate change is estimated to have boosted the rainfall by around 40%. UK weather extremes are becoming more frequent and more intense.</p>
</div>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">UK weather hazards include storms, floods, heatwaves, droughts and snow events — all becoming more extreme with climate change.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Storm Desmond (Dec 2015) brought 341 mm of rain in 24 hours to Cumbria, flooding 5,200 homes including a Carlisle hospital.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Met Office data confirms the UK climate is becoming more extreme: 9 of the 10 wettest years on record since 2000, plus the first 40°C reading in 2022.</li>
</ul>
