--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a river changes from source to mouth and the erosion, transport and deposition behind each shape.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Source to Mouth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every river begins at a <strong>source</strong> — usually high ground where rainfall, snowmelt or a spring releases water. From there it flows downhill through a <strong>channel</strong>, joined by smaller streams (tributaries) at <strong>confluences</strong>, until it reaches its <strong>mouth</strong> at the sea or a lake. The total area of land drained by the river and its tributaries is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The total area of land drained by a river and its tributaries; sometimes called a catchment.">drainage basin</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Geographers split the river into three sections: the <strong>upper course</strong> (close to the source, steep and narrow), the <strong>middle course</strong> (gentler gradient, wider valley) and the <strong>lower course</strong> (very gentle gradient, wide flat valley). The same river looks completely different in each section, because the dominant processes change as you move downstream.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Long Profile</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing how a river's gradient changes from its source to its mouth, typically a smooth concave curve.">long profile</dfn> is a side-on diagram of the river from source to mouth. The line is steepest at the source (high gradient) and flattens out towards the mouth (low gradient), forming a smooth concave curve. The shape reflects how the river uses its energy: in the upper course energy fights friction with the rough bed and banks, so most goes into vertical erosion; further downstream the smoother channel needs less energy for friction, so the river can carry more water and a larger load with a much gentler slope.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Cross Profile</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing the shape of a river valley and channel when sliced across; the shape changes from V-shaped at the source to wide and flat near the mouth.">cross profile</dfn> looks at the river from the side, slicing across the valley. The shape changes dramatically downstream:</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Cross-Profile Shapes by Course</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Upper course.</strong> Narrow, steep-sided V-shaped valley. The channel itself is narrow and shallow, with a rough bed of large angular boulders. Vertical erosion dominates because the river is cutting down towards sea level. Interlocking spurs jut from alternate sides of the valley.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Middle course.</strong> Wider valley with gentler slopes. The channel is broader and deeper. Lateral (sideways) erosion becomes more important as the river starts to swing across its valley. Meanders begin to form.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Lower course.</strong> Very wide, almost flat valley. The channel is wide and deep but the gradient is tiny. Deposition dominates — the river drops the load it has carried downstream. Wide flood plains and levees develop.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which type of erosion dominates each course of a river?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Upper course = vertical erosion (V-shaped valley, interlocking spurs). Middle course = lateral erosion (meanders). Lower course = deposition (flood plain, levees, estuary).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Erosion Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Rivers erode in four ways — the same processes that operate at the coast, plus one more.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hydraulic action.</strong> Fast-flowing water hits the bed and banks, forcing air into cracks and prising them apart. Most powerful in upland streams during floods.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Abrasion</strong> (corrasion). Sand and pebbles carried by the water scrape against the bed and banks, sandpapering them away. The dominant erosion process in most UK rivers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Attrition.</strong> The pebbles in the water knock against each other, becoming smaller, smoother and rounder. Attrition shapes the load, not the bed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Solution</strong> (corrosion). Slightly acidic river water dissolves alkaline rock such as limestone and chalk — slow but steady.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Erosion can also be split by <em>direction</em>. <strong>Vertical erosion</strong> deepens the channel and dominates in the upper course (helping form V-shaped valleys). <strong>Lateral erosion</strong> widens the channel by cutting sideways — dominant in the middle and lower courses, helping form meanders.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Transportation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A river carries its load in four ways:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Traction.</strong> Large boulders and pebbles roll along the bed. Only happens during floods when the river has the most energy.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Saltation.</strong> Smaller pebbles bounce along the bed in a series of hops, picked up briefly by the current and dropped again.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Suspension.</strong> Fine sand, silt and clay are held up in the water column. This is what makes rivers look brown after heavy rain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Solution.</strong> Dissolved minerals, especially from limestone catchments, are carried invisibly in the water.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Deposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Rivers deposit their load when they no longer have the energy to carry it. This happens when the river slows down — for example, when the gradient flattens, when the channel widens, when the river enters the sea, or after a flood when discharge drops. Larger material is dropped first (it needs more energy to carry); the finest particles travel furthest. This is why the upper course has angular boulders, the middle course rounded pebbles, and the lower course mud and silt.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four transport processes from largest to smallest particle size.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Transport processes by particle size: traction (largest, rolled along the bed) → saltation (bounced) → suspension (held up in the water) → solution (dissolved). Bigger particles need more energy to move.</p>
</div>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Discharge</strong> is the volume of water passing a point each second, measured in cubic metres per second (cumecs). It increases downstream as more tributaries join. The River Severn has a discharge of around 2 cumecs at its source on Plynlimon in mid-Wales but over 100 cumecs by the time it reaches Worcester — and over 600 cumecs during major floods. Higher discharge means more energy to erode and transport, which is why floods reshape rivers so dramatically.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">A river's long profile is a smooth concave curve from a steep source to a gentle mouth; its cross profile changes from a narrow V-shape (upper) to a wide flat valley (lower).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Rivers erode by hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition and solution — with vertical erosion dominating upstream and lateral erosion dominating downstream.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Rivers transport their load by traction, saltation, suspension and solution, and deposit it where they slow down — bigger material first, finest material last.</li>
</ul>
