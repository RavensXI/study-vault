--- DESCRIPTION ---
Early chapters (Genesis, Exodus): Jeanette's childhood, the mother's domineering control, the church community, the 'missionary kid' identity, and early belonging.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Genesis: Jeanette's Origins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel's opening chapter is titled <strong>"Genesis"</strong> — the first book of the Bible, concerned with creation and origins. Winterson immediately establishes the parallel: just as Genesis tells the story of God creating the world, this chapter tells the story of Jeanette's world being created for her by her mother. The mother has adopted Jeanette as a project: <strong>"She had given me to the Lord... like Samuel."</strong> The biblical allusion to Samuel — a child dedicated to God's service from birth — establishes that Jeanette has been claimed before she has any say in the matter. Her identity has been decided for her.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The opening line is one of the most famous in modern British fiction: <strong>"Like most people I lived for a long time with my mother and father. My father liked to watch the wrestling, my mother liked to wrestle."</strong> The <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of a word in two different senses for comic or ironic effect — here, 'wrestle' means both the sport (father) and the mother's aggressive engagement with the world.">pun</dfn> on <strong>"wrestle"</strong> immediately establishes the mother as the dominant, combative figure and the father as passive. It also establishes the novel's distinctive <dfn class="term" data-def="The way humour or understatement is used to reveal uncomfortable truths — Winterson uses deadpan comedy to describe what is actually a controlling, extreme upbringing.">ironic tone</dfn>: Winterson describes extraordinary, even disturbing events with dry wit, forcing the reader to laugh at what is actually a suffocating, controlling environment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="The opening line sets up the entire novel: the mother is the dominant force, the father is invisible, and the tone is ironic. Winterson uses humour to reveal disturbing truths — always analyse the GAP between the comic surface and the dark reality beneath.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The opening line establishes the mother's dominance, the father's passivity, and the novel's ironic tone. Winterson uses humour — the pun on 'wrestle' — to describe what is actually a controlling upbringing. The gap between comic delivery and disturbing content is a key technique.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Mother's World: Friends and Enemies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jeanette's mother has divided the entire world into <strong>"friends"</strong> and <strong>"enemies."</strong> Friends include the church congregation, the Bible, missionary work, and anyone who shares her worldview. Enemies include <strong>"the next door neighbours, sex, slugs, and the French"</strong> — a list whose absurd juxtaposition is characteristic of Winterson's humour. By placing sex alongside slugs and the French, Winterson shows how the mother's moral categories are both arbitrary and absolute. There is no middle ground: you are either righteous or damned.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The mother controls every aspect of Jeanette's life. She does not send Jeanette to school until forced to by the authorities, preferring to educate her at home through the Bible. She monitors Jeanette's reading, friendships, and activities. When Jeanette goes deaf (a real medical condition), her mother ignores it for months because she believes God will provide. Winterson presents the mother's behaviour without explicit judgment, instead using the child narrator's accepting tone to let the reader see the <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where what the reader understands is different from what the narrator or character understands — Jeanette's innocent acceptance of her mother's behaviour makes its extremity more visible to the reader.">dramatic irony</dfn>: what Jeanette accepts as normal, the reader recognises as neglect and control.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Church as Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">For the young Jeanette, the church is not just a place of worship — it is her entire social world. The congregation functions as an extended family, with figures like <strong>Elsie Norris</strong> providing warmth and affection that the mother cannot. Jeanette is valued within the church: she preaches sermons, participates in evangelism, and is seen as destined for missionary work. Winterson shows how the church gives Jeanette <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of being accepted and valued by a community — the church provides Jeanette with purpose and identity before her sexuality is discovered.">belonging</dfn> and purpose — which makes her eventual expulsion even more devastating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The church's services are vivid and physical: <strong>"speaking in tongues"</strong>, exorcisms, laying on of hands, and passionate preaching. Winterson describes these scenes with a mixture of awe and irony — the child Jeanette finds them exciting and meaningful, while the adult narrator's tone subtly suggests their excess. The church is presented as a place of genuine spiritual experience but also of emotional manipulation: the congregation's fervour creates a pressure to conform that leaves no room for individual difference.</p>

Winterson uses this clash between Jeanette's religious world and the wider world for both humour and pathos: Jeanette is genuinely bewildered by a world that does not share her mother's categories. The school scenes establish a recurring pattern — Jeanette is an outsider wherever she goes, whether in the church (later, because of her sexuality) or in the secular world (because of her upbringing).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Exodus: Departure and Discovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The second chapter, <strong>"Exodus"</strong> — titled after the book of the Bible about the Israelites' departure from slavery in Egypt — introduces Jeanette's first experiences beyond the mother's control. She goes to school, encounters secular children, and begins to see that other ways of living exist. The title is significant: just as Exodus tells of liberation from bondage, this chapter begins Jeanette's slow movement away from her mother's total control. But the exodus is incomplete — Jeanette remains deeply embedded in the church throughout her childhood, and her loyalty to the community is genuine.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Winterson uses the early chapters to establish the emotional stakes of the novel. If Jeanette's relationship with the church were simply oppressive, her later expulsion would be a straightforward liberation. But because the church also provides love, belonging, and purpose, leaving it means losing <em>everything</em>. Winterson builds the foundation of belonging first, so that its destruction has maximum emotional impact.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="The church isn't simply evil — it provides genuine belonging and purpose. Winterson builds this foundation so that Jeanette's expulsion later is devastating rather than liberating. Always acknowledge the church's positive role alongside its destructiveness.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The early chapters establish the church as Jeanette's entire world — providing identity, belonging, and purpose. Winterson builds this positive foundation deliberately so that its later destruction has maximum emotional impact. The church is not simply oppressive; it is also the only community Jeanette knows.</p>
</div>
