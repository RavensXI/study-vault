--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme: Identity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Identity is the novel's central concern. Jeanette's identity is initially constructed for her: the mother names her <strong>"a weapon in the war against the world,"</strong> the church assigns her a role as future missionary, and the community treats her as its protege. Jeanette accepts this identity throughout her childhood — she genuinely believes she is destined for missionary work and that the church's worldview is correct. The crisis comes when her sexuality reveals a part of her identity that the church cannot accommodate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Winterson presents identity as something that must be <em>discovered</em> rather than <em>assigned</em>. The church tries to assign Jeanette a fixed identity; her sexuality reveals that identity is more complex and fluid than any institution can prescribe. The orange demon tells Jeanette that <strong>"everyone has a demon"</strong> — meaning everyone has an authentic self that may conflict with what their community expects. Jeanette's journey is from assigned identity to discovered identity, from conformity to <dfn class="term" data-def="Being true to oneself — living according to one's own identity rather than the expectations imposed by others.">authenticity</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Identity is the CENTRAL theme. Frame: assigned identity (mother/church give Jeanette a role) vs discovered identity (sexuality reveals her authentic self). The novel argues identity must be discovered, not prescribed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The novel traces Jeanette's movement from assigned identity (given by the mother and church) to discovered identity (revealed through her sexuality). Winterson argues that identity must be discovered, not prescribed — and that institutions which deny this cause lasting damage.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Theme: Religion vs Sexuality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The church presents Jeanette's sexuality as incompatible with her faith — as demonic possession requiring exorcism. But Winterson refuses this binary. Jeanette does not abandon God when she is expelled: <strong>"I miss God. I miss the company of someone utterly loyal."</strong> She separates God from the church, maintaining a personal faith while rejecting the institution that weaponised it. Winterson's argument is that religion and sexuality are not inherently in conflict — the conflict is created by <dfn class="term" data-def="A powerful organisation that sets and enforces rules — the church as institution creates the conflict between faith and sexuality, not God.">institutional</dfn> interpretation, not by God.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">This was a radical position in 1985. The dominant narrative — in evangelical Christianity, in politics (Section 28), and in much of mainstream culture — was that homosexuality was sinful. Winterson's novel challenged this by presenting a protagonist who is both devout and gay, and who sees no contradiction between the two. The church, not Jeanette, is the source of the conflict.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Theme: Mother-Daughter Relationship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The mother-daughter relationship is the novel's emotional core. The mother loves Jeanette but cannot love her as she is — only as the mother wants her to be. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Love that depends on the recipient meeting certain conditions — the mother's love requires Jeanette to be the person the mother has decided she should be.">conditional love</dfn> is both the source of Jeanette's early security and the cause of her eventual trauma. The mother's control is motivated by genuine devotion: she believes she is saving Jeanette's soul. This makes her both sympathetic and destructive.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The relationship is not destroyed by the novel's end. Jeanette returns at Christmas; the mother makes tea. The bond persists, damaged but unbroken. Winterson refuses the neat narrative of either total reconciliation or permanent estrangement. Instead, she presents the messy, unresolved reality of a relationship between two strong women who love each other but cannot agree on what love requires.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Theme: Storytelling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Storytelling is both a theme and a technique. The novel contains multiple layers of narrative — realist memoir, fairy tales, biblical parallels — all telling the same story differently. Winterson writes: <strong>"Everyone who tells a story tells it differently... the important thing is to tell and retell."</strong> This argues that truth is not fixed but multiple: there is never only one way to understand experience, just as there is never only one way to live. The church insists on a single, authoritative truth (the Bible); Winterson insists on plural, personal truths.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">The novel asks whether you can have both belonging and freedom. The church provides belonging (community, identity, purpose) but demands the surrender of freedom (you must think, feel, and behave as prescribed). When Jeanette is expelled, she gains freedom but loses belonging. The novel suggests this is a false choice imposed by rigid institutions: in a more compassionate community, both would be possible. Elsie represents this possibility — she offers love without conditions.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Exam Technique</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Extract analysis:</strong> When you are given a printed extract, zoom into 2–3 quotations with word-level analysis. Discuss language techniques (irony, bathos, imagery, sentence structure) and their effects on the reader. Do not retell the plot. Let AO1 (personal response) and AO2 (language and structure) lead your analysis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Wider-text response:</strong> When asked about the novel beyond the extract, cover 3+ moments from across the text, tracing the same theme or technique. Embed context (AO4) naturally within analysis rather than in a separate paragraph: <em>"Writing in 1985, when Section 28 would soon prohibit the 'promotion' of homosexuality, Winterson's presentation of Jeanette's love as natural was a deliberately radical act."</em> Check your exam board's specimen paper for the precise question format and mark allocation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="For the exam, prepare 3-4 key quotations for each major theme (identity, religion/sexuality, mother-daughter, storytelling). Each quotation should be short enough to embed and rich enough for word-level analysis.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Prepare quotations for each theme: identity ('everyone has a demon'), religion/sexuality ('I miss God'), mother-daughter ('friends and enemies'), storytelling ('everyone tells a story differently'). Embed context naturally within your analysis.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Identity:</strong> Assigned (by the church) vs discovered (through sexuality) — Winterson argues identity must be found, not prescribed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Religion vs sexuality:</strong> Not inherently in conflict — the church creates the conflict through institutional interpretation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Mother-daughter:</strong> Conditional love — the relationship is damaged but not destroyed, messy and unresolved.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Storytelling:</strong> 'Everyone tells a story differently' — truth is multiple, not singular.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">Structure your Part (b) around themes, not plot. Each paragraph should: state Winterson's purpose, embed a quotation, analyse language/form/structure, and connect to context (AO4). E.g.: <em>"Winterson presents identity as discovered, not assigned. The orange demon tells Jeanette 'everyone has a demon' — meaning everyone has an authentic self. 'Demon' is deliberately provocative: what the church calls evil, Winterson redefines as identity. This subversion was radical in 1985."</em></p>
