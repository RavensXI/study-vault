--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jeanette and Katy: The Second Relationship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the exorcism, Jeanette outwardly conforms — but her compliance is a survival strategy, not genuine repentance. When she meets Katy, a fellow member of the congregation, Jeanette falls in love again. This second relationship is significant because it proves the exorcism was futile: Jeanette's sexuality is not a demonic possession that can be expelled. It is <em>who she is</em>. Winterson presents Jeanette's love for Katy with the same tenderness as her love for Melanie, reinforcing the novel's central argument that same-sex love is natural and persistent.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The chapter title <strong>"Joshua"</strong> is resonant: in the Bible, Joshua leads the Israelites into the Promised Land after years of wandering. For Jeanette, the 'Promised Land' is not a physical place but <dfn class="term" data-def="Being true to oneself — living according to one's own identity rather than the expectations of others.">authenticity</dfn> — the possibility of living as herself. However, just as Joshua's conquest involved warfare, Jeanette's journey towards authenticity involves violent conflict with her community.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Jeanette's second relationship (Katy) is the turning point: it proves the exorcism failed and that her sexuality is identity, not possession. The church's response escalates from exorcism to total expulsion.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The second relationship with Katy proves the exorcism failed. Jeanette's sexuality is identity, not demonic possession — it cannot be prayed away. This forces the final confrontation between Jeanette and the church.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Church's Final Rejection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When the church discovers Jeanette's relationship with Katy, there is no second exorcism. Instead, Jeanette is expelled completely. The pastor denounces her publicly; the congregation turns against her; even her mother, initially, refuses to support her. Winterson presents this second rejection as more devastating than the first because there is no offer of redemption — the church has decided that Jeanette is beyond saving. She is not just sinful but <strong>"unteachable."</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The escalation from exorcism (first time) to expulsion (second time) reveals the church's true priorities. The first time, they attempted to change Jeanette because they still valued her gifts — her preaching, her intelligence, her commitment. The second time, they simply remove her. Winterson suggests that the church's 'love' was always conditional on conformity. Once it became clear that Jeanette would not conform, even her considerable talents could not save her.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Leaving Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Jeanette leaves home — expelled not just from the church but from her mother's house. She gets a job at a funeral parlour (an ironic choice: surrounded by the dead while trying to start a new life) and begins to build an independent existence. Winterson describes the departure without sentimentality: <strong>"I left and walked across the town to work driving an ice cream van (and later helping at a funeral parlour)."</strong> The mundane detail — walking across town, going to work — contrasts sharply with the emotional enormity of what has happened. This is classic Winterson: using <dfn class="term" data-def="A deliberate anticlimax where something serious is followed by something mundane — Winterson describes the loss of everything (home, church, mother) in the language of everyday routine.">bathos</dfn> to convey pain through understatement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jeanette's departure is both a loss and a liberation. She loses her community, her identity within the church, and her relationship with her mother. But she gains the freedom to be herself. Winterson presents both sides without insisting that liberation is easy or painless. The novel does not have a triumphant ending — Jeanette returns to visit her mother at Christmas and finds the relationship altered but not destroyed. The mother is still controlling, still obsessive, but she makes Jeanette a cup of tea. This small gesture contains the novel's most complex emotional truth: love persists even when relationships are broken.</p>

In Winterson's novel, eating the fruit — accepting your true identity — is necessary for growth. The title <em>Oranges Are Not the Only Fruit</em> now becomes clear: the church offers only one way of being (oranges), but there are other fruits — other ways of living — that are equally valid.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Ruth: Return and Ambiguity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The final chapter is titled <strong>"Ruth"</strong> — the biblical story of a woman who chooses loyalty to her mother-in-law over returning to her own people. Winterson's choice is characteristically ambiguous: Ruth is about chosen loyalty, but Jeanette's return to her mother at Christmas is complicated. She returns but does not stay. She is loyal to the relationship but has rejected the community. The biblical parallel is inverted: Ruth chose to belong; Jeanette chooses to leave, then returns on her own terms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The novel ends without full resolution. Jeanette has established an independent life but has not escaped the influence of her upbringing. Her mother is still there, still difficult, still the most powerful personality in the room. Winterson refuses a neat ending because real life does not offer one. The novel's final message is that identity is not a destination but a process — Jeanette is still becoming herself.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="The title's meaning becomes clear at the end: oranges = the church's single acceptable way of being. But 'oranges are not the only fruit' — there are other valid ways to live. The title is a manifesto for accepting difference.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The title's meaning: 'oranges' represent the church's single acceptable identity, but <em>oranges are not the only fruit</em> — there are other valid ways of being. Jeanette's expulsion is not just punishment but the beginning of her freedom to choose her own fruit.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Jeanette's second relationship (Katy) proves the exorcism failed — her sexuality is identity, not possession.</li>
