--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jeanette: Narrator and Protagonist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jeanette is both the novel's narrator and protagonist — a dual role that creates the ironic tone Winterson is known for. As a child, Jeanette accepts her world uncritically: the church is good, the mother is all-powerful. As the adult narrator looking back, Winterson allows the reader to see what the child cannot — the extremity of the control, the absurdity of the binary worldview, the cruelty of the exorcism. This gap between child's acceptance and reader's understanding creates continuous <dfn class="term" data-def="When the reader understands something the narrator does not — Jeanette's innocent acceptance makes her mother's control more visible.">dramatic irony</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Jeanette's most striking quality is resilience. She survives the exorcism, the loss of Melanie, the expulsion from the church, and the rejection by her mother — without becoming bitter. She misses God: <strong>"I miss God. I miss the company of someone utterly loyal."</strong> She returns to visit her mother at Christmas. Winterson presents Jeanette not as a victim but as someone who builds a new identity from the ruins of the old one. Her wit — inherited from Winterson's own voice — is her primary survival tool.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Jeanette is resilient, not bitter. She misses God but rejects the church's interpretation. She uses humour and storytelling as survival tools. Never reduce her to a simple victim.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jeanette is resilient rather than bitter. She separates God from the church, maintains her faith while rejecting institutional cruelty, and uses wit and storytelling as survival tools.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Mother: Domination and Contradiction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The mother is the novel's most complex character — simultaneously loving and suffocating, devout and hypocritical, powerful and fragile. She is never named, which universalises her as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A universal character type that represents a broader category — the unnamed mother becomes an archetype of parental control.">archetype</dfn> of parental control. She adopted Jeanette as a <strong>"weapon in the war against the world"</strong> — a missionary project. She controls Jeanette's education, friendships, diet, and beliefs. Her world is divided into <strong>"friends and enemies"</strong> with no middle ground.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Yet Winterson does not reduce the mother to a villain. She is funny, charismatic, and formidable. Her energy fills every room. She is the most alive character in the novel — her passion, even when misdirected, is genuine. She is a woman whose own life has been limited by class and gender, and who has found in the church the only arena where her talents are valued. The mother's <dfn class="term" data-def="A deep inconsistency within a character — the mother is simultaneously loving and controlling, devout and hypocritical.">contradictions</dfn> make her fascinating rather than simply hateful.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Elsie Norris</strong> is an elderly member of the congregation who provides Jeanette with warmth and affection the mother cannot. Elsie tells stories, listens, and treats Jeanette as a person rather than a project. She represents an alternative model of Christian love — gentle, patient, unconditional — contrasting with the mother's controlling fervour and the church's judgmental authority. When Elsie dies, Jeanette loses her only reliable source of kindness. Winterson uses Elsie to show that the problem is not Christianity itself but how certain people practise it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Pastor Finch and the Church Leadership</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Pastor Finch represents institutional religious authority — the male hierarchy controlling the congregation. He leads the exorcism and pronounces the expulsion. Winterson presents him not as individually cruel but as a figure embodying institutional power's inability to accommodate difference. His authority depends on the congregation's obedience, so any visible deviation must be crushed publicly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Miss Jewsbury: The Secret Hypocrite</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Miss Jewsbury</strong> is a congregation member who, it is hinted, is herself a closeted lesbian. She approaches Jeanette after the exorcism offering shelter and ultimately a sexual relationship, and there are suggestions that she has experienced same-sex desire but hidden it. Winterson uses Miss Jewsbury to show that Jeanette is not unique — others share her identity but have chosen concealment. Miss Jewsbury's secrecy reveals the <dfn class="term" data-def="The pretence of having higher moral standards than one actually possesses — the church condemns Jeanette while harbouring members with the same desires.">hypocrisy</dfn> at the congregation's heart: it condemns openly what it tolerates secretly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Melanie and Katy: Love as Catalyst</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Melanie</strong> is Jeanette's first love — tender, innocent, and joyful until the church intervenes. Melanie's response to condemnation contrasts with Jeanette's: she submits completely, repents, and marries a man. She functions as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character who contrasts with the protagonist, highlighting their qualities — Melanie's submission highlights Jeanette's resistance.">foil</dfn> — her conformity highlights Jeanette's refusal to deny herself. <strong>Katy</strong> is Jeanette's second love, whose discovery triggers the final break. Neither Melanie nor Katy is developed as fully as Jeanette or the mother — they are catalysts for the novel's central conflicts rather than complex characters in their own right.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="For character questions, discuss Winterson's METHODS: contrast (Jeanette vs Melanie), archetypes (the unnamed mother), structural irony (Miss Jewsbury's hidden identity), and the dual role of narrator/protagonist.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Every character serves a structural purpose: the Mother (control/love contradiction), Elsie (alternative love), Pastor Finch (institutional authority), Melanie (submission as foil), Katy (catalyst for final break), Miss Jewsbury (hypocrisy exposed).</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Jeanette is resilient, not bitter — she separates God from the church and uses wit as survival.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The mother is complex: simultaneously loving and controlling. She is never named, becoming an archetype.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Elsie represents gentle, unconditional Christian love — the opposite of the church's controlling authority.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Miss Jewsbury reveals the church's hypocrisy: it condemns Jeanette publicly while tolerating hidden same-sex desire.</li></ul>
