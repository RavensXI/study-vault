<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Stores and Conservation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another. The total energy of a closed system remains constant.">law of conservation of energy</dfn> is one of the most fundamental principles in physics: energy cannot be created or destroyed — only transferred between stores. In any closed system, the total energy before a process equals the total energy after. Energy that appears to 'disappear' has been <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy transferred to less useful stores, usually thermal energy in the surroundings. Cannot easily be recovered for useful work.">dissipated</dfn> — spread out into thermal energy in the surroundings where it cannot easily be recovered.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">There are eight main energy stores: <strong>kinetic</strong> (moving objects), <strong>gravitational potential</strong> (objects at height), <strong>elastic potential</strong> (stretched or compressed materials), <strong>chemical</strong> (food, fuels, batteries), <strong>thermal</strong> (hot objects), <strong>magnetic</strong>, <strong>electrostatic</strong> and <strong>nuclear</strong>. Energy transfers between stores via four pathways: heating, radiation, electrical working, and mechanical working.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="List the eight energy stores from memory: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, chemical, thermal, magnetic, electrostatic, nuclear. These come up regularly in extended questions.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It transfers between stores (kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic, chemical, thermal, and others). Any energy that is 'wasted' is dissipated as thermal energy in the surroundings.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Work Done</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When a force moves an object through a distance, <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance in the direction of the force. Measured in joules (J).">work is done</dfn>. Work done equals the energy transferred. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$W = F \times d$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Where \(W\) is work done in joules (J), \(F\) is force in newtons (N), and \(d\) is distance moved in the direction of the force in metres (m). One joule of work is done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m. Work done and energy transferred are equivalent — both are measured in joules.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Example: a person pushes a shopping trolley with 40 N of force across 5 m. Work done = \(40 \times 5 = 200\) J. This energy is transferred from the person's chemical energy store into the kinetic energy of the trolley (and some to thermal energy through friction with the floor).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Kinetic and Gravitational Potential Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A moving object stores <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy stored in a moving object. Calculated as ½mv², where m is mass in kg and v is speed in m/s.">kinetic energy (KE)</dfn>:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$KE = \frac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(m\) is mass in kg and \(v\) is speed in m/s. Notice that KE depends on \(v^2\) — doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy. This is why road speed limits have such a large effect on collision severity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">An object at height stores <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy stored in an object due to its height above a reference point. Calculated as mgh, where g is gravitational field strength.">gravitational potential energy (GPE)</dfn>:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">$$GPE = m \times g \times h$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Where \(m\) is mass (kg), \(g\) is gravitational field strength (10 N/kg on Earth's surface), and \(h\) is height (m). A 3 kg book placed on a shelf 1.5 m high gains GPE = \(3 \times 10 \times 1.5 = 45\) J relative to the floor.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Energy Transfer: Falling Object</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">When an object falls freely (ignoring air resistance), GPE converts into KE. At the start all energy is stored as GPE; at the bottom all has converted to KE. At any point in between, total energy = GPE + KE = constant. This allows you to calculate the speed of a falling object using \(GPE_{lost} = KE_{gained}\), i.e. \(mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\). The masses cancel, giving \(v = \sqrt{2gh}\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Example:</strong> A ball dropped from 5 m above the ground (ignoring air resistance): \(v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 5} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \text{ m/s}\) just before hitting the ground. In reality, air resistance dissipates some energy as thermal energy in the air, so the actual speed would be slightly less.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">No device transfers energy with 100% efficiency — some is always dissipated as wasted thermal energy. <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of input energy that is usefully transferred. Calculated as useful output energy divided by total input energy.">Efficiency</dfn> is calculated as:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful output energy}}{\text{total input energy}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Efficiency can be expressed as a decimal (0 to 1) or multiplied by 100 to give a percentage. A motor that receives 400 J of electrical energy and performs 280 J of useful work has efficiency = \(\frac{280}{400} = 0.70\) = 70%. The remaining 120 J is wasted as heat and sound. Engineers improve efficiency through lubrication (reducing friction), streamlining (reducing air resistance) and thermal insulation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input. Always less than 1 (or 100%). If given a percentage, convert to decimal before using in further calculations. The higher the efficiency, the less energy is wasted.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy. No device is 100% efficient. KE = ½mv² and GPE = mgh are the two key energy store equations — both appear regularly in exam calculations.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n30">Energy is conserved — it transfers between stores but is never created or destroyed. Dissipated energy is thermal energy in the surroundings.</li><li data-narration-id="n31">Work done = force × distance (\(W = Fd\)), measured in joules. Work done equals energy transferred.</li><li data-narration-id="n32">Kinetic energy: \(KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\). Doubling speed quadruples KE. Gravitational potential energy: \(GPE = mgh\).</li><li data-narration-id="n33">Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input. Engineers reduce waste through lubrication, streamlining and insulation.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">For calculation questions involving KE = ½mv², remember the v is squared — a very common error is to forget to square the velocity. Always show your working clearly: write the equation, substitute values with units, then evaluate. For 4-mark compare/suggest questions on energy transfers, name the energy stores at the start and end and describe the pathway (e.g. 'mechanical working'). For 6-mark extended responses on efficiency and energy, evaluate whether the energy loss is acceptable given the context — this is the levels-mark difference between Secure and Mastering.</p>
