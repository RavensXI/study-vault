--- DESCRIPTION ---
What forces do, how they pair up, and why mass and weight are fundamentally different things.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Force?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A force is a push or a pull that acts on an object. Forces can change the shape of an object, change its speed, or change the direction it is moving. They are measured in <strong>newtons (N)</strong> and are <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has both magnitude (size) and direction. Forces, velocity, acceleration and displacement are all vectors.">vector quantities</dfn> &mdash; meaning both their size and direction must be stated.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Forces come in two categories. <dfn class="term" data-def="A force that requires physical contact between two objects, such as friction, air resistance, normal contact force, or tension.">Contact forces</dfn> require the objects to be touching &mdash; examples include friction, air resistance, tension in a rope, and the normal contact force (the push a surface exerts on an object resting on it). <dfn class="term" data-def="A force that acts between objects that are not in contact, such as gravitational force, magnetic force, or electrostatic force.">Non-contact forces</dfn> act at a distance without the objects touching &mdash; examples include gravity, magnetic forces and electrostatic forces.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three contact forces and three non-contact forces from memory. Then describe one way each type of non-contact force is useful in everyday life.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Contact forces (friction, tension, normal contact, air resistance) require physical contact. Non-contact forces (gravity, magnetism, electrostatics) act at a distance without touching. Forces are vectors &mdash; they have both size and direction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Mass and Weight</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Mass and weight are different quantities that students frequently confuse. <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). Mass is a scalar quantity and does not depend on location.">Mass</dfn> is the amount of matter in an object. It is a <strong>scalar</strong> quantity (magnitude only, no direction), measured in <strong>kilograms (kg)</strong>, and it does not change wherever you go in the universe.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The force of gravity acting on an object&rsquo;s mass, measured in newtons (N). Weight depends on the gravitational field strength at that location.">Weight</dfn> is the gravitational force acting on an object&rsquo;s mass. It is a <strong>vector</strong> quantity, measured in <strong>newtons (N)</strong>, and it depends on where you are. The same astronaut has the same mass on Earth and on the Moon, but a much smaller weight on the Moon because the Moon&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="The force exerted per kilogram of mass by a gravitational field. On Earth&rsquo;s surface, g = 10 N/kg (or 9.8 N/kg to 2 significant figures).">gravitational field strength</dfn> is much weaker.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The relationship between mass, weight and gravitational field strength is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$W = m \times g$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Where \(W\) is weight (N), \(m\) is mass (kg), and \(g\) is gravitational field strength (N/kg). On Earth&rsquo;s surface, \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\) (or 9.8 N/kg to two significant figures). Weight is measured using a <strong>newton meter</strong> (spring balance) and always acts downwards towards the centre of the attracting body.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Free-Body Diagrams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing all the forces acting on a single object, represented as arrows drawn from the object in the direction each force acts.">free-body diagram</dfn> shows all the forces acting on a single object as arrows. The length of each arrow represents the magnitude of the force; the direction of the arrow shows where the force acts. By convention, forces are drawn acting from the centre of the object outwards in the direction they act.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A book resting on a table has two forces acting on it: its <strong>weight</strong> (downwards, caused by gravity) and the <strong>normal contact force</strong> from the table (upwards, caused by the table pushing back on the book). When these two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction, the book is in <strong>equilibrium</strong> &mdash; it does not accelerate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">When forces acting on an object are <strong>balanced</strong> (the resultant force is zero), the object either remains at rest or continues at constant velocity. When forces are <strong>unbalanced</strong>, the resultant force causes acceleration in the direction of the larger force. This is the foundation of Newton&rsquo;s First Law.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Draw a free-body diagram from memory for a skydiver falling at terminal velocity. Label all forces with their correct names and directions. Explain why the skydiver is not accelerating.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a free-body diagram, balanced forces (equal size, opposite direction) mean zero resultant force and no acceleration. Unbalanced forces produce acceleration. Arrows show direction; length shows magnitude.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Resultant Forces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The single force that has the same effect as all the individual forces acting on an object combined. Also called the net force.">resultant force</dfn> is the overall effect of all forces combined. For forces acting along the same line, you simply add or subtract them. For example, if a car engine produces a forward force of 2000 N and friction produces a backward force of 800 N, the resultant force is 1200 N forwards &mdash; the car accelerates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">When forces act at an angle to each other, you use a <strong>scale drawing</strong> (or Pythagoras&rsquo;s theorem for right angles) to find the resultant. Resolving forces into components is a Higher-tier skill, but understanding that the resultant is the &ldquo;net&rdquo; force is essential at all levels.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Newton&rsquo;s Third Law states: when object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on object A. These are called a <strong>Newton&rsquo;s Third Law pair</strong>. They are always equal in size, opposite in direction, the same type of force, and act on <em>different</em> objects.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">For example, when a person stands on the ground, the Earth pulls the person downwards (weight) and the person pulls the Earth upwards with an equal gravitational force. Separately, the ground pushes the person upwards (normal contact force) and the person pushes the ground downwards with an equal contact force. These are two distinct force pairs. A common error is treating weight and normal contact force as a Newton&rsquo;s Third Law pair &mdash; they are not, because they act on the same object (the person).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Forces are vectors (size + direction). Contact forces require touching (friction, tension, normal); non-contact forces act at a distance (gravity, magnetism, electrostatics).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Mass (kg) is the amount of matter and does not change location. Weight (N) = m &times; g; it depends on gravitational field strength and acts downwards.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Free-body diagrams show all forces on one object. Balanced forces mean zero resultant force and no acceleration. The resultant force is the net combined effect of all forces.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">The mass vs weight distinction is a reliable mark in every paper. Always state that mass is in <strong>kg</strong> (a scalar) and weight is in <strong>N</strong> (a vector force caused by gravity). In free-body diagram questions, check whether forces are balanced or unbalanced before deciding if there is acceleration. For Newton&rsquo;s Third Law questions, confirm that paired forces act on <em>different</em> objects &mdash; if they act on the same object they cannot be a Third Law pair.</p>
