--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Atomic Structure and Isotopes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every atom has a nucleus made of <dfn class="term" data-def="Positively charged particles found in the nucleus of an atom, equal in number to the element's atomic number.">protons</dfn> (positive charge) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Neutral particles found in the nucleus of an atom, with roughly the same mass as protons.">neutrons</dfn> (no charge). Electrons (negative charge) occupy energy levels around the nucleus. The number of protons defines the element — carbon always has 6 protons, oxygen always has 8. In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, giving different mass numbers.">Isotopes</dfn> are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number (protons) but different mass numbers (protons + neutrons). We write isotopes in nuclear notation: \(^A_Z X\) where \(A\) is the mass number and \(Z\) is the atomic number. For example, carbon-12 (\(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\)) has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 (\(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\)) has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Some isotopes have unstable nuclei that emit radiation spontaneously — these are <strong>radioactive isotopes</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Isotopes = same element, same proton number, different neutron number, different mass number. Radioactive decay is RANDOM and SPONTANEOUS — it is not triggered by external conditions such as temperature or pressure.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Isotopes have the same atomic number (protons) but different mass numbers (protons + neutrons). Radioactive decay is a random, spontaneous process — individual decay events cannot be predicted.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Types of Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Unstable nuclei emit three main types of radiation, each with different properties:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">• <dfn class="term" data-def="A particle consisting of 2 protons and 2 neutrons emitted from an unstable nucleus. Identical to a helium-4 nucleus. Stopped by paper or a few cm of air.">Alpha (α) particles</dfn> — 2 protons + 2 neutrons (equivalent to a helium-4 nucleus, \(^4_2\text{He}\)). Strongly ionising because of their large charge and mass. Weakly penetrating: stopped by a sheet of paper, a few centimetres of air, or skin.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="A high-speed electron emitted from the nucleus when a neutron converts into a proton. Stopped by a few mm of aluminium.">Beta (β) particles</dfn> — a high-speed electron emitted when a neutron converts into a proton inside the nucleus. Moderately ionising and moderately penetrating: stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="High-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted from the nucleus. Very penetrating — only significantly reduced by thick lead or several metres of concrete.">Gamma (γ) rays</dfn> — electromagnetic radiation emitted when the nucleus releases excess energy. Weakly ionising but highly penetrating: only significantly reduced by thick lead or several metres of concrete. Often emitted alongside alpha or beta decay.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">In nuclear equations, both mass numbers and atomic numbers must balance on each side of the arrow.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Alpha decay:</strong> the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2. Example — uranium-238 decays to thorium-234:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">$$^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + ^{4}_{2}\text{He}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Beta decay:</strong> a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron from the nucleus. Mass number stays the same, atomic number increases by 1. Example — carbon-14 decays to nitrogen-14:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">$$^{14}_{6}\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_{7}\text{N} + ^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Gamma emission:</strong> the nucleus releases excess energy as a gamma ray. No change to mass number or atomic number — it is not shown as a separate particle in the equation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Alpha: mass number −4, atomic number −2. Beta: mass number unchanged, atomic number +1. Gamma: no change to either. Always check both numbers balance in a nuclear equation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Alpha: −4 mass, −2 atomic. Beta: same mass, +1 atomic. Gamma: no change. Both mass numbers and atomic numbers must balance in every nuclear equation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Ionisation and Detecting Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing electrons from atoms to create charged ions. Ionising radiation can damage DNA and living cells.">Ionisation</dfn> occurs when radiation knocks electrons from atoms, creating charged ions. Alpha particles are the most ionising — they transfer their energy quickly because of their large charge and mass. Gamma rays are the least ionising but travel the furthest. Ionisation in living cells can damage DNA, potentially causing mutations or cancer. The greater the ionising power, the lower the penetrating power — alpha radiation is stopped quickly because it transfers its energy so readily.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The activity of a radioactive source is measured in <strong>becquerels (Bq)</strong>, where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. In the laboratory, a <strong>Geiger–Müller (GM) tube</strong> detects ionising radiation and records a count rate in counts per second or per minute. When measuring a source, always account for <strong>background radiation</strong> — the low-level radiation always present in the environment from natural sources (radon gas, cosmic rays, food and building materials) and, to a much smaller extent, artificial sources.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Half-Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay, or for the activity/count rate to halve.">Half-life</dfn> is the time taken for half of the unstable nuclei in a sample to decay — equivalently, the time for the activity to halve. Radioactive decay is <strong>random</strong>: we cannot predict when any individual nucleus will decay, but statistical behaviour of large numbers of nuclei is reliable and predictable.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n20">After one half-life, half the original unstable nuclei remain. After two half-lives, a quarter remain. After three, an eighth. For example, if a sample has an activity of 800 Bq and its half-life is 3 hours, after 9 hours (3 half-lives): \(800 \rightarrow 400 \rightarrow 200 \rightarrow 100\) Bq.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Reading a Half-Life Graph</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">On a graph of activity against time, the curve falls steeply at first and then more gradually — this is an exponential decay curve. To read the half-life from the graph: identify the starting activity, find the time at which it has halved, and read across. Then check: does the activity halve again in the same time interval? It should — half-life is constant for a given isotope regardless of how much of the material is left.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">In the laboratory, plotting count rate against time (after subtracting background count rate) gives the same exponential shape. The corrected count rate should be used — not the raw reading — to ensure you are measuring only the decay of the source.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Practice half-life calculation by repeated halving: start with the initial activity and keep halving until you reach the required time. Count how many halvings you did — each one equals one half-life.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Half-life is the time for activity (or number of unstable nuclei) to halve. It is constant for a given isotope. Radioactive decay cannot be sped up or slowed down by changing physical conditions such as temperature or pressure.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Radioactive isotopes have unstable nuclei that decay spontaneously and randomly.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Alpha (α): 2 protons + 2 neutrons, most ionising, least penetrating. Beta (β): fast electron, moderate. Gamma (γ): electromagnetic, least ionising, most penetrating.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">In decay equations, both mass number and atomic number must balance. Alpha: −4 mass, −2 atomic. Beta: +1 atomic, same mass.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Half-life is the time for activity to halve. It is constant for a given isotope and cannot be changed by external conditions.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">For nuclear equation questions, write the equation and then check: do mass numbers balance? Do atomic numbers balance? Missing particles can be found by subtraction. For half-life questions, use the step-by-step repeated-halving method rather than trying to use a formula — it is less error-prone and shows your working clearly. For 6-mark extended responses on radioactivity, always connect properties of the radiation (ionising power, penetrating power) to the application or risk being discussed — this is what earns levels-mark credit.</p>
