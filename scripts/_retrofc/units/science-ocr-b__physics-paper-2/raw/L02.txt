--- DESCRIPTION ---
Speed, velocity, acceleration, and how to read distance-time and velocity-time graphs.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed and Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled per unit time. Speed is a scalar quantity, measured in metres per second (m/s).">Speed</dfn> tells you how fast an object moves, but not which direction. It is a <strong>scalar</strong> quantity &mdash; it has magnitude but no direction. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$s = v \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Where \(s\) is distance (m), \(v\) is speed (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). Rearranged: \(v = \frac{s}{t}\) or \(t = \frac{s}{v}\). Typical speeds to be familiar with: walking ~1.5 m/s, running ~3 m/s, cycling ~6 m/s, a car in a town ~13 m/s, and the speed of sound in air ~340 m/s.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a stated direction. Velocity is a vector quantity &mdash; it has both magnitude and direction, measured in m/s.">Velocity</dfn> is speed in a stated direction. It is a <strong>vector</strong> quantity. An object travelling around a circular track at constant speed has a <em>changing velocity</em>, because direction is continuously changing &mdash; so it is always accelerating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Distance</strong> (scalar) measures the total path length travelled. <dfn class="term" data-def="The straight-line distance from start to finish, measured in a specific direction. Displacement is a vector quantity.">Displacement</dfn> (vector) measures how far &mdash; and in what direction &mdash; an object is from its starting point. A runner who completes one full lap of a 400 m track has covered a distance of 400 m but has a displacement of zero.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and sort these into scalars and vectors: speed, velocity, distance, displacement, force, mass, acceleration. Then write one sentence explaining why a circular-track runner always has a changing velocity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Scalars have magnitude only: speed, distance, mass, energy. Vectors have magnitude AND direction: velocity, displacement, force, acceleration. An object moving in a circle has a constantly changing velocity (direction changes) even if speed is constant.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in metres per second squared (m/s<sup>2</sup>). Acceleration is a vector quantity.">Acceleration</dfn> is how quickly velocity changes. It can mean speeding up, slowing down (deceleration), or changing direction. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Where \(a\) is acceleration (m/s<sup>2</sup>), \(v\) is final velocity (m/s), \(u\) is initial velocity (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). A negative acceleration (deceleration) means the object is slowing down. For example, a car braking from 20 m/s to rest in 4 s has \(a = \frac{0 - 20}{4} = -5 \text{ m/s}^2\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Distance-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. The gradient of the line gives the speed of the object.">distance-time graph</dfn>, the gradient (slope) of the line equals the speed. Interpreting the shapes:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">&bull; <strong>Horizontal line</strong> &mdash; the object is stationary (speed = 0).<br>&bull; <strong>Straight diagonal line</strong> &mdash; constant speed; steeper gradient means faster.<br>&bull; <strong>Curve getting steeper</strong> &mdash; the object is accelerating.<br>&bull; <strong>Curve getting shallower</strong> &mdash; the object is decelerating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">To find speed at a specific moment on a curved distance-time graph, draw a <strong>tangent</strong> to the curve at that point and calculate the gradient of the tangent. The tangent is a straight line that just touches the curve at one point and has the same slope as the curve there.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Draw a distance-time graph from memory showing: stationary, constant speed, then accelerating. Label the gradient for each section and state what it represents.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a distance-time graph: gradient = speed. Horizontal = stopped. Straight line = constant speed. Curve getting steeper = accelerating. To find instantaneous speed on a curve, draw a tangent and calculate its gradient.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Velocity-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and velocity on the y-axis. The gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives distance travelled.">velocity-time graph</dfn>, two pieces of information can be extracted:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">&bull; The <strong>gradient</strong> equals the acceleration. Steep upward slope = large acceleration. Horizontal line = constant velocity (zero acceleration). Downward slope = deceleration.<br>&bull; A line <strong>below the x-axis</strong> means the object is moving in the opposite direction to the positive direction defined in the question.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n22">The <strong>area under the line</strong> on a velocity-time graph equals the distance travelled. For a triangular shape: area = \(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\). For a rectangular shape: area = base &times; height. Complex shapes are split into triangles and rectangles and summed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Uniform and Non-Uniform Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Uniform (constant) acceleration produces a straight line on a velocity-time graph. Non-uniform acceleration (changing acceleration) produces a curve. For uniform acceleration, you can also use:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">$$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Where \(s\) is distance (m). This equation is useful when time is not given &mdash; for example, calculating braking distance given initial speed and deceleration. Real-world journeys almost always involve non-uniform acceleration &mdash; average speed is found by dividing total distance by total time.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Motion and Road Safety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The physics of motion has direct consequences for road safety. <strong>Stopping distance</strong> is the total distance a vehicle travels from when a driver notices a hazard to when the vehicle comes to rest. It has two parts:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">&bull; <strong>Thinking distance</strong> &mdash; the distance covered at constant speed during the driver&rsquo;s reaction time (typically 0.2&ndash;0.9 s depending on alertness). Increased by tiredness, alcohol, or drug use.<br>&bull; <strong>Braking distance</strong> &mdash; the distance covered while decelerating to rest. Increased by higher speed (because kinetic energy \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\) grows with the square of speed), wet or icy roads (reduced friction), or worn tyres and brakes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Because braking distance scales with \(v^2\), doubling speed more than doubles stopping distance. A car travelling at 30 mph on a dry road requires roughly 23 m to stop; at 60 mph, the stopping distance is approximately 73 m &mdash; more than three times greater for twice the speed. This is why speed limits in residential areas are low &mdash; the consequences of a collision at lower speed are far less severe.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Speed (m/s) is scalar. Velocity (m/s + direction) is vector. Acceleration (m/s<sup>2</sup>) = change in velocity &divide; time. All three link via the equations s = vt and a = (v - u)/t.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Distance-time graph: gradient = speed. Horizontal = stopped, straight line = constant speed, curve = changing speed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Velocity-time graph: gradient = acceleration. Horizontal = constant velocity. Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance; both increase with speed.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">The most common mistake on graph questions is confusing a <em>steep</em> section with a large value. On a distance-time graph, steep means <em>fast</em>; on a velocity-time graph, steep means large <em>acceleration</em>, not large speed. Always state what the gradient or area represents before calculating it. For &lsquo;Suggest&rsquo; questions about motion, identify the relevant equation (distance-speed-time or acceleration equation) and apply it to the novel scenario given.</p>
