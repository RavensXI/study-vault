--- DESCRIPTION ---
Newton's laws, F = ma, momentum, and why braking distance grows quickly with speed.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Newton's First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues at the same speed and direction, unless acted on by an unbalanced resultant force.">Newton's First Law</dfn> states that an object remains at rest, or continues to move at a constant velocity, unless a <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall force on an object when all individual forces are combined, taking into account their direction.">resultant force</dfn> acts on it. A resultant force is the single force that represents the combined effect of all forces acting on an object.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">If the resultant force is <strong>zero</strong>, a stationary object stays still and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction. If there is a resultant force, the object <strong>accelerates</strong> — meaning it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. A spacecraft drifting in deep space, where there is no friction or air resistance, will continue at the same velocity indefinitely unless a thruster fires. This is not because a force is pushing it — it is because no force is acting to slow it down.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Objects do NOT need a force to keep moving. A resultant force is only needed to CHANGE an object's motion. If all forces are balanced, the object's velocity stays the same.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Objects do not need a force to keep moving at constant velocity. A resultant force is only needed to change the speed or direction of an object — this is a very common exam misconception.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Newton's Second Law: F = ma</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass: F = ma.">Newton's Second Law</dfn> tells us that the resultant force on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$F = m \times a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Where \(F\) is force in newtons (N), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(a\) is acceleration in m/s². A bigger force produces a bigger acceleration (if mass stays the same), and a heavier object needs a bigger force for the same acceleration. The equation rearranges to \(a = \frac{F}{m}\) or \(m = \frac{F}{a}\). Always use the <strong>resultant force</strong>, not any single force in isolation. If a car engine provides 3 000 N but friction and air resistance total 1 000 N, the resultant force is 2 000 N forward.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Newton's Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force, acting on a different object.">Newton's Third Law</dfn> states: for every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force. These forces always act on <strong>different objects</strong> and are always the same type of force. When a swimmer pushes water backwards, the water pushes the swimmer forwards with an equal force. When a rocket expels gases downwards, the gases push the rocket upwards with an equal force.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">A common mistake is to confuse a Third Law pair with balanced forces. Balanced forces act on the <em>same</em> object. A Third Law pair always involves forces on <em>different</em> objects. Weight (Earth pulling you down) and normal contact force (ground pushing you up) are NOT a Third Law pair — both act on you. The Third Law pair of your weight is the gravitational pull you exert on the Earth.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Third Law pairs: equal in size, opposite in direction, same type of force, act on DIFFERENT objects. Balanced forces act on the same object — these are not a Third Law pair.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Newton's Third Law pairs are always equal in size, opposite in direction, the same type of force, and act on different objects from each other.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Momentum</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="The product of an object's mass and velocity. A vector quantity measured in kg m/s.">Momentum</dfn> is the product of an object's mass and velocity:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">$$p = m \times v$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Where \(p\) is momentum in kg m/s, \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(v\) is velocity in m/s. In a closed system (no external forces), the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after — this is the <dfn class="term" data-def="In a closed system, total momentum before an event equals total momentum after. Momentum is neither created nor destroyed.">conservation of momentum</dfn>. If a 2 kg trolley moving at 4 m/s collides with and sticks to a stationary 2 kg trolley, momentum before = \(2 \times 4 = 8\) kg m/s. After, the combined 4 kg mass moves at \(\frac{8}{4} = 2\) m/s. Momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is not.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Thinking Distance, Braking Distance and Stopping Distance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total distance a vehicle travels from the moment a hazard is seen to when it stops. Equals thinking distance plus braking distance.">Stopping distance</dfn> is the sum of two parts:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">• <strong>Thinking distance</strong> — the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time (between seeing the hazard and pressing the brake). Factors that increase thinking distance: tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions, higher speed.<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance a vehicle travels after the brakes are applied until it stops. Depends on speed, road conditions, tyre and brake condition.">Braking distance</dfn> — the distance travelled after the brakes are applied. Factors that increase braking distance: higher speed, wet or icy roads, worn tyres, worn or overheated brakes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Speed has a particularly large effect on braking distance because kinetic energy depends on \(v^2\). Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy, so the brakes must do four times more work to stop the vehicle — meaning roughly four times the braking distance. This is why motorway speed limits are strictly enforced.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Large braking forces are needed to stop quickly. A very large braking force causes the tyres to skid, reducing control. Modern vehicles use <strong>anti-lock braking systems (ABS)</strong> to prevent skidding by modulating brake pressure automatically.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Doubling speed roughly quadruples braking distance because KE is proportional to v². This is why speed matters so much for road safety.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance. Braking distance is proportional to \(v^2\) — doubling speed roughly quadruples braking distance. Speed is the most significant factor in serious collisions.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Newton's First Law: objects keep their velocity unless a resultant force acts. Objects do not need a force to keep moving.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Newton's Second Law: \(F = ma\). Always use the resultant force. Rearrange to find acceleration or mass as needed.</li><li data-narration-id="n30">Newton's Third Law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction on a different object.</li><li data-narration-id="n31">Momentum \(p = mv\) is conserved in closed systems. Stopping distance = thinking distance + braking distance, and braking distance grows with speed squared.</li></ul>
