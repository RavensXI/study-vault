--- DESCRIPTION ---
How waterfalls, gorges, interlocking spurs, meanders, oxbow lakes, levées, flood plains and estuaries are formed by erosion and deposition.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Upper Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the upper course, the river channel is narrow and the gradient is steep. The dominant process is <dfn class="term" data-def="Erosion that cuts downwards into the river bed, deepening the channel.">vertical erosion</dfn>, which cuts downwards into the river bed. This creates steep-sided V-shaped valleys and distinctive erosional landforms including interlocking spurs, waterfalls and gorges. On the River Tees, this upper section begins at Cross Fell (893 m above sea level) near the source.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Interlocking Spurs</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Ridges of hard rock that project out on alternating sides of a valley, around which the river winds.">Interlocking spurs</dfn> are areas of hard rock left behind as the river erodes softer rock more quickly. The river winds between these ridges, which project from alternate sides of the valley. They are characterised by a steep gradient, narrow valley floor and a winding, narrow river channel.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n5">Waterfalls</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A sudden descent of a river over a vertical or very steep slope, formed where hard rock overlies softer rock.">waterfall</dfn> forms where a band of hard rock lies over softer rock. The formation is a step-by-step process:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>Stage 1</strong> — falling water and rock particles loosen and wear away the softer rock through <dfn class="term" data-def="Erosion caused by rocks and sediment scraping against the river bed and banks.">abrasion</dfn>.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 2</strong> — the hard rock above is undercut as erosion of the soft rock continues through <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of water hitting the river banks and bed, compressing air in cracks and breaking the rock apart.">hydraulic action</dfn>, creating an overhang.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 3</strong> — the unsupported hard rock overhang collapses into the <dfn class="term" data-def="A deep pool at the base of a waterfall, carved by the force of falling water and rock.">plunge pool</dfn>, where it is broken up by attrition and washed away.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 4</strong> — erosion continues and the waterfall slowly retreats (migrates) upstream. The process then repeats.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the hard rock at High Force waterfall that sits above softer rock.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>High Force</strong> on the River Tees is a well-known example of a waterfall in the upper course. The hard Whin Sill (a band of dolerite) overlies softer limestone and shale, creating the conditions for waterfall formation.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Gorges</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrow, steep-sided valley formed as a waterfall retreats upstream over time.">gorge</dfn> is the steep-sided valley left behind as a waterfall retreats upstream. Every time the overhanging hard rock collapses, the waterfall moves back and the gorge grows longer. The river takes up the whole width of the valley floor, and the sides are steep — almost vertical.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Middle Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In the middle course, the valley widens and the gradient decreases. The river has more energy for <dfn class="term" data-def="Erosion that cuts sideways into the river banks, widening the channel.">lateral erosion</dfn> (sideways erosion), and both erosion and <dfn class="term" data-def="The dropping of material that was being transported by the river, usually when the river loses energy.">deposition</dfn> occur. This creates meanders and oxbow lakes.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Meanders</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A bend or curve in a river channel, formed by erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside of the bend.">meander</dfn> is a bend in the river channel. Water travels fastest on the <strong>outside</strong> of the bend, where it erodes the bank through hydraulic action and abrasion, creating a steep <dfn class="term" data-def="A steep, eroded bank on the outside of a meander bend, formed by fast-flowing water.">river cliff</dfn>. On the <strong>inside</strong> of the bend, the water flows more slowly and loses energy, depositing sediment to form a gently sloping <dfn class="term" data-def="A gentle slope of deposited material on the inside of a meander bend, formed by slow-moving water.">slip-off slope</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, describe how meanders on the River Tees between Darlington and Yarm change over time.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>On the River Tees, meanders are found in the middle course between Darlington and Yarm. The outside bends gradually erode laterally into the edges of the valley, while the meander position shifts slowly downstream over time.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Oxbow Lakes</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n17">An <dfn class="term" data-def="An arc-shaped lake formed when a meander loop is cut off from the main river channel.">oxbow lake</dfn> forms when a meander loop is cut off from the main channel. The process occurs over four stages:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n18">
<li><strong>Stage 1</strong> — the meander moves laterally across the valley, and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The narrow strip of land between two meander bends that is gradually being eroded away.">neck</dfn> between two bends gets narrower over time.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 2</strong> — during a flood, the river takes the shortest route and cuts across the narrow neck, creating a new, straighter channel.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 3</strong> — when floodwater drops, the river returns to its original channel, but this is repeated each time flooding occurs.</li>
<li><strong>Stage 4</strong> — the old meander loop is permanently cut off from the main channel, forming an oxbow lake. Over time, it is colonised by plants and gradually dries up.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Lower Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">In the lower course, the river flows through a wide, flat valley. The channel becomes wider and deeper, with a smoother, almost semi-circular cross-section due to deposits of silt and mud. The gradient is very gentle — on the River Tees, the lower course drops from just 60 metres to sea level across roughly half the river’s total length. Deposition is the dominant process here.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Flood Plains</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A wide, flat area of land in the lower course of a river, formed by repeated flooding and deposition of sediment.">flood plain</dfn> is a low-lying area of land either side of the river in the lower course. Its width is created by <dfn class="term" data-def="The gradual sideways movement of meanders across the valley floor over time.">meander migration</dfn>, where the outside bends erode laterally into the edges of the valley, gradually cutting a wider valley. When floods recede, a layer of sediment is deposited across the flood plain, making it slightly higher and more fertile. This builds up over hundreds of years.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Levées</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n24">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural embankment along the banks of a river, built up by repeated flooding and deposition of sediment.">levée</dfn> is an embankment along the river banks, often several metres higher than the surrounding flood plain. When a river bursts its banks, the water slows down as it hits the flat land and begins depositing material. The heaviest sediment is deposited first, right next to the channel, gradually building up the banks. Each flood adds more material, raising the levée higher over time. The river bed also rises with deposited material, meaning the river level can sit <strong>above</strong> the level of the surrounding flood plain.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why a breached levée leads to particularly bad flooding.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>If a levée is breached (broken through), flooding can be <strong>worse</strong> than normal because the land behind the levée sits lower than the river. Water pours in rapidly and struggles to drain back because the levée blocks the return route.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Estuaries</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n27">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The tidal mouth of a large river where it meets the sea, typically wide and filled with deposited sediment.">estuary</dfn> is the tidal mouth of a river, where it meets the sea. Estuaries are characterised by large amounts of deposited sediment (<dfn class="term" data-def="Fine-grained fertile soil deposited by water, typically made of clay, silt and sand.">alluvium</dfn>) because the flat land means the river moves very slowly, with little gravitational energy to carry material. The River Tees estuary near Middlesbrough has extensive mudflats and is surrounded by flat, low-lying land. Estuaries flood easily because deposited sediment reduces the depth of the channel, and the surrounding flood plain offers no gradient to carry water away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Estuaries are often important industrial areas because the flat land is easy to build on and the proximity to the sea supports shipping and trade. The River Tees estuary historically supported major chemical works and industry.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Rivers create distinctive landforms at each stage of their course. The upper course is dominated by vertical erosion (waterfalls, gorges, interlocking spurs), the middle course by a combination of erosion and deposition (meanders, oxbow lakes), and the lower course by deposition (flood plains, levées, estuaries).</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Waterfalls</strong> form where hard rock overlies soft rock and retreat upstream over time, leaving a gorge behind.</li>
<li><strong>Meanders</strong> have a river cliff (fast, erosion) on the outside and a slip-off slope (slow, deposition) on the inside, and can eventually form oxbow lakes.</li>
<li><strong>Levées</strong> build up naturally through repeated flooding, and estuaries are wide, sediment-rich zones where the river meets the sea.</li>
</ul>
