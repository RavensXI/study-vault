--- DESCRIPTION ---
The devastating effects of Typhoon Haiyan on the Philippines, the immediate and long-term responses, and strategies for reducing tropical storm damage.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Case Study: Typhoon Haiyan (2013)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Typhoon Haiyan struck the Philippines in <strong>November 2013</strong>. It was one of the strongest tropical storms ever recorded — a <strong>Category 5</strong> typhoon on the Saffir-Simpson scale, with sustained wind speeds of up to 315 km/h. The city of <dfn class="term" data-def="A coastal city in the Philippines that was devastated by Typhoon Haiyan in 2013.">Tacloban</dfn> on the eastern coast was one of the worst-affected areas.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the ocean the Philippines sits in that spawns typhoons.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Philippines is a group of islands in <strong>South-East Asia</strong>, located in the western Pacific Ocean. It sits directly in the path of typhoons that form in the Pacific, making it one of the most storm-affected countries in the world.</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Typhoon Haiyan effects and responses comparison" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_typhoon_haiyan.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Primary Effects</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Effects that happen immediately as a direct result of a natural hazard.">Primary effects</dfn> are the immediate impacts caused directly by the storm:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>6,300 people were killed</strong>, most of whom drowned in a <strong>5-metre storm surge</strong> that swept through coastal communities.</li>
<li><strong>1 million homes were destroyed</strong>, leaving 4.1 million people homeless.</li>
<li>Flooding was widespread, destroying <strong>1.1 million tonnes of crops</strong>.</li>
<li>Power lines and trees were brought down, <strong>blocking roads</strong> and cutting off access for emergency services.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Secondary Effects</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Effects that occur as a result of the primary effects, often in the days and weeks after the event.">Secondary effects</dfn> are knock-on consequences that follow the primary effects:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li><strong>600,000 people</strong> were displaced from their homes and forced to live in <strong>1,100 emergency camps</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>10,000 schools</strong> were destroyed, denying children access to education for months.</li>
<li>Broken sewer pipes contaminated water supplies with <strong>sewage</strong>, allowing waterborne diseases like cholera to spread.</li>
<li>Soil was contaminated with <strong>sea salt</strong> from the storm surge, preventing crops from growing. The price of rice rose by <strong>12%</strong>, making food even harder for survivors to afford.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n10">Immediate Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Actions taken in the hours, days and weeks immediately after a disaster to save lives and meet basic needs.">Immediate responses</dfn> focused on saving lives and meeting basic needs:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n12">
<li><strong>800,000 people</strong> were evacuated before the storm hit, saving many lives.</li>
<li>The indoor sports stadium in Tacloban was used as an <strong>evacuation centre</strong> — however, many people who sheltered there drowned when the storm surge flooded the building.</li>
<li>The Philippine government sent out <strong>emergency medical supplies</strong>, though some were washed away by floodwaters before they could be distributed.</li>
<li><strong>$89 million</strong> was raised by 33 countries (including the UK) to provide food, clean water and medical care.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Long-Term Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Actions taken over months and years after a disaster to rebuild and improve resilience for the future.">Long-term responses</dfn> aimed to rebuild and reduce the impact of future storms:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
<li>The Philippine government introduced the <strong>“Build Back Better”</strong> programme, rebuilding homes and infrastructure to higher standards so they could better withstand future storms.</li>
<li>A <strong>storm surge warning system</strong> was installed along the coast to give earlier warnings.</li>
<li>A <strong>no-build zone</strong> was established along the coastline, preventing construction in the most vulnerable areas.</li>
<li><dfn class="term" data-def="Coastal trees and shrubs that grow in saltwater and help absorb wave energy, protecting the coast from storm damage.">Mangroves</dfn> were replanted along the coast to act as a natural barrier against future storm surges.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Managing Tropical Storms: The Three Ps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Countries can reduce the damage and death toll from tropical storms using the <strong>three Ps</strong>: prediction, protection and planning.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Prediction</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">Unlike earthquakes, tropical storms can be <strong>tracked and predicted</strong> because they form over the ocean and take days to reach land. Aircraft fly over (or even through) storms to measure wind speed and pressure. <dfn class="term" data-def="Spacecraft that orbit the Earth and use technology to track the movement, pressure and rainfall of weather systems.">Satellites</dfn> track cloud movement from space. Weather sensors and drones can be sent into the eye of the storm. Since 1992, <dfn class="term" data-def="The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, a US agency that uses supercomputers to predict tropical storms.">NOAA</dfn> has used supercomputers to analyse data from storms and predict their path — by 2013, they could give a <strong>5-day warning</strong> of a storm hitting within 400 km. However, predictions are not always acted upon — the people of Tacloban were warned about Haiyan but many ignored the warning because they expected a minor Category 1 storm, not a Category 5.</p>
</div>
</div></div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Protection</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">Protection involves reducing the physical damage a storm can cause. Measures include: installing <strong>window shutters</strong> that lock to prevent glass shattering; fitting <strong>roof straps</strong> between walls and roofs to stop roofs being blown off; reinforcing <strong>garage doors</strong> and entrance doors to prevent them buckling in high winds; installing <strong>emergency electricity generators</strong> so hospitals and schools have power if cables are damaged; and securing loose objects like garden furniture that could become dangerous projectiles in high winds.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Planning</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">Planning means being prepared before a storm arrives. This includes: knowing the location of <strong>evacuation centres</strong> and routes; preparing a <strong>disaster kit</strong> with food, water, a torch and a first-aid kit; having <strong>fuel in the car</strong> ready for evacuation; informing family and friends of what to do; and training emergency services to respond quickly. Since tropical storms lose energy over land (because they are powered by warm ocean water above 26.5°C), the best advice is often to <strong>move inland</strong> away from the coast.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Without looking, recall which of the 3 Ps wealthier countries spend more on.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Wealthier countries (HICs) can use more of the three Ps than poorer countries (LICs/NEEs). They have the money for satellite monitoring, reinforced buildings and well-trained emergency services. In the Philippines, an NEE, many people could not afford to evacuate or protect their homes, which increased the death toll.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Typhoon Haiyan demonstrated the devastating power of a Category 5 tropical storm, with the storm surge causing the majority of deaths. The response involved both immediate aid and long-term rebuilding, while the three Ps — prediction, protection and planning — offer ways to reduce the impact of future storms.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Haiyan</strong> killed 6,300 people (mostly from the 5 m storm surge), destroyed 1 million homes and left 4.1 million homeless.</li>
<li><strong>Responses</strong> included evacuation of 800,000, $89 million in international aid, Build Back Better, no-build zones and mangrove replanting.</li>
<li><strong>The three Ps</strong> — prediction (satellites, NOAA), protection (shutters, roof straps) and planning (evacuation routes, disaster kits) — can significantly reduce the impact of tropical storms.</li>
</ul>
