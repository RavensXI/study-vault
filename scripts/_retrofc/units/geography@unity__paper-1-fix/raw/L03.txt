--- DESCRIPTION ---
Comparing the effects and responses of earthquakes in an LIC (Haiti 2010) and an HIC (Christchurch 2011), and how wealth affects vulnerability.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">How Wealth Affects Tectonic Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Tectonic hazards strike both wealthy and poor countries — the plates do not recognise national borders. However, the <strong>effects</strong> of a tectonic event and the <strong>response</strong> to it differ enormously depending on a country’s wealth. To understand this, we compare two real earthquake case studies: the <strong>Haiti earthquake (2010)</strong> in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A low-income country with limited wealth, infrastructure and public services.">low-income country (LIC)</dfn>, and the <strong>Christchurch earthquake (2011)</strong> in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A high-income country with significant wealth, advanced infrastructure and well-funded public services.">high-income country (HIC)</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name a primary AND secondary effect of earthquakes, then define immediate and long-term responses.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Effects are split into <strong>primary effects</strong> (direct results of the earthquake, such as deaths and building collapse) and <strong>secondary effects</strong> (indirect consequences that follow, such as disease and job losses). Responses are split into <strong>immediate responses</strong> (emergency actions in the first hours and days) and <strong>long-term responses</strong> (rebuilding and recovery over months and years).</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="The three Ps of hazard management: predict, protect, and plan" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_three_ps.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Case Study: Haiti Earthquake, 2010 (LIC)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">On <strong>12 January 2010</strong>, a magnitude 7.0 earthquake struck near the capital city of <strong>Port-au-Prince</strong>, Haiti. Haiti is located on the boundary between the Caribbean Plate and the North American Plate. At the time, Haiti was the poorest country in the Western Hemisphere, with a <dfn class="term" data-def="Gross National Income per person — a measure of a country’s wealth divided by its population.">GNI per capita</dfn> of just $810 and a <dfn class="term" data-def="Gross Domestic Product — the total value of all goods and services produced by a country in one year.">GDP</dfn> of $11.5 billion. It ranked 126th in the world by wealth.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Primary Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>230,000 people killed</strong>, 300,000 injured and 1.3 million left homeless.</li>
<li>180,000 buildings destroyed and a further 200,000 damaged.</li>
<li>Most of the city’s poorly built <dfn class="term" data-def="The basic physical systems of a country, including roads, bridges, water supply and power networks.">infrastructure</dfn> was damaged or destroyed.</li>
<li>Main transport hubs (airports and ports) were badly damaged, making it difficult to bring in aid.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n8">Secondary Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li>2 million people left without food and water. <dfn class="term" data-def="A bacterial disease spread through contaminated water, causing severe diarrhoea and dehydration.">Cholera</dfn> spread rapidly, and power cuts were widespread.</li>
<li>70% of the population was left without work, devastating the economy.</li>
<li>The country became entirely reliant on international aid. Crime increased as desperation grew.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n10">Immediate Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The immediate response was <strong>slow and poorly organised</strong>. Haiti’s government was unable to coordinate relief because its own buildings and infrastructure had been destroyed. Aid from other countries was slow to arrive, largely because the airports and ports were damaged. Even when aid did arrive, it could not be distributed easily because roads were blocked by rubble and agencies did not know where to send supplies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Eventually, the <strong>USA stepped in</strong> and took over the relief effort, sending 10,000 military troops with helicopters and specialist equipment. Satellite images were used to direct aid to people in need. Bottled water and purification tablets were distributed. 235,000 people were moved out of Port-au-Prince. Dead bodies were buried in mass graves to prevent the spread of disease.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Long-term Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The long-term recovery was also <strong>slow and poorly organised</strong>. The UK donated over £20 million, the EU gave $330 million, and the World Bank cancelled Haiti’s debt repayments for five years. However, because of a lack of leadership within Haiti, only <strong>2% of the $1.1 billion</strong> raised by 23 international aid agencies was actually released to the country.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, how many people in Haiti were still homeless one year after the quake, and what % of roads were blocked?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>One year after the earthquake, <strong>1 million people were still homeless</strong> and without running water or sanitation. 98% of roads were still blocked by rubble. 70% of people had not returned to work. Haiti has still not fully recovered.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Case Study: Christchurch Earthquake, 2011 (HIC)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">On <strong>22 February 2011</strong>, a magnitude 6.3 earthquake struck the city of <strong>Christchurch</strong>, New Zealand. New Zealand sits on the boundary between the Pacific Plate and the Australian Plate. It is a wealthy HIC with a GNI per capita of $40,020 and a GDP of $171.71 billion, ranked 52nd in the world.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Primary Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n19">
<li><strong>185 people killed</strong> and 7,100 injured — far fewer than Haiti despite significant damage.</li>
<li>100,000 buildings damaged across the city.</li>
<li>80% of water and sewage pipes damaged.</li>
<li>Widespread damage to the city’s power supply and road network, costing an estimated $40 billion.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Secondary Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li>Some homes were flooded, while others were destroyed by fires caused by broken gas pipes.</li>
<li>Some people had limited access to food and water in the immediate aftermath.</li>
<li>Businesses were closed for a long period, leading to job losses and reduced incomes.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Immediate Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The immediate response was <strong>rapid and well organised</strong>. $7 million of international aid arrived within the first three weeks, along with aid workers from charities including the Red Cross. Rescue workers from across New Zealand were supported by urban search and rescue teams from the UK, Taiwan and Australia. 300 Australian police officers flew in to support local police, helping to maintain order and reassure residents. 30,000 people were provided with portable toilets to reduce the risk of disease.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Long-term Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The long-term recovery was <strong>effective and well organised</strong>. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A government body created after the Christchurch earthquake with special powers to change planning laws and coordinate rebuilding.">Canterbury Earthquake Recovery Authority</dfn> was established with special powers to change planning laws and coordinate rebuilding across the city. Insurance companies paid out $898 million to help repair damaged buildings. 10,000 new affordable homes were built, and 80% of roads, water and sewage pipes were repaired within just six months.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Comparing the Two Earthquakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The contrast between Haiti and Christchurch is striking and demonstrates clearly how wealth affects the impact of a tectonic hazard.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Effects</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The primary effects were far worse in Haiti. Despite the Christchurch earthquake being smaller in magnitude (6.3 vs 7.0), the death toll in Haiti was <strong>over 1,200 times greater</strong> (230,000 vs 185). This is because New Zealand had invested heavily in the <dfn class="term" data-def="Prediction, Protection and Planning — the three strategies used to reduce the impact of tectonic hazards.">Three Ps</dfn> (prediction, protection and planning). Buildings were constructed to withstand earthquakes, emergency services were well-trained, and the population knew what to do. In Haiti, buildings were poorly constructed with little or no earthquake-resistant design, so they collapsed on a massive scale.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">The secondary effects were also far more severe in Haiti. In Christchurch, only “some” people were affected, whereas in Haiti <strong>2 million</strong> people were left without food and water and <strong>70%</strong> of the population lost their jobs. Haiti’s lack of development meant its infrastructure was poor before the earthquake even struck, leaving it much more vulnerable.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n33">New Zealand’s wealth allowed it to mobilise help quickly, both domestically and from overseas. Emergency services reached people rapidly, infrastructure was repaired within months, and insurance covered much of the rebuilding cost. Haiti, by contrast, could not help itself — its government buildings were destroyed, its emergency services were overwhelmed, and it had no money for rebuilding. The USA eventually had to take over the relief effort. Even years later, Haiti had not recovered.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the Three Ps that wealthy countries invest in to mitigate earthquake impacts.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Being a wealthy country does not <em>prevent</em> earthquakes from happening, but it does enable a country to invest in the Three Ps — better prediction, stronger protection and more effective planning — which significantly reduces the number of deaths, speeds up the response and shortens recovery time.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The Haiti and Christchurch earthquakes show that wealth is a crucial factor in determining how badly a country is affected by a tectonic hazard. Both countries experienced destructive earthquakes, but Haiti’s poverty meant far more deaths, slower responses and a recovery that is still incomplete.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Haiti (LIC)</strong> suffered 230,000 deaths, slow and poorly organised responses, and has still not fully recovered — largely due to poverty, weak infrastructure and poor governance.</li>
<li><strong>Christchurch (HIC)</strong> suffered 185 deaths, rapid and well-organised responses, and rebuilt within months — thanks to wealth, strong infrastructure and effective government.</li>
<li><strong>Wealth determines</strong> how much a country can invest in the Three Ps, which directly affects the number of deaths, the speed of recovery and the long-term impact on people’s lives.</li>
</ul>
