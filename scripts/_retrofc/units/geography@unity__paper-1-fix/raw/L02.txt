--- DESCRIPTION ---
Constructive, destructive and conservative plate boundaries, and why millions of people still choose to live near tectonic hazards.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Four Types of Plate Boundary</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Where tectonic plates meet, the boundary between them is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="The point where two tectonic plates meet, also called a plate margin.">plate boundary</dfn> (or plate margin). The type of boundary depends on the direction the plates are moving. There are four types, and each produces different tectonic hazards.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name all 4 plate boundaries, describing each plate's movement.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four plate boundaries are: <strong>constructive</strong> (plates move apart), <strong>destructive</strong> (plates move together, one sinks), <strong>conservative</strong> (plates slide past each other), and <strong>collision</strong> (two continental plates push together).</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Three types of plate boundary: constructive, destructive, and conservative" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_plate_boundaries.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Constructive Plate Boundary</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">At a <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates move apart, allowing magma to rise and form new crust.">constructive plate boundary</dfn>, tectonic plates move away from each other. As they separate, magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap, cooling to form new crust. This process creates <dfn class="term" data-def="A wide, gently sloping volcano formed by runny basaltic lava at a constructive plate boundary.">shield volcanoes</dfn> — wide, gently sloping volcanoes made from runny basaltic lava. Occasional small earthquakes also occur as the plates pull apart.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A key example is the <strong>Mid-Atlantic Ridge</strong>, which runs down the centre of the Atlantic Ocean. Iceland sits directly on this ridge, which is why it has frequent volcanic activity.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Destructive Plate Boundary</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8">At a <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates move towards each other and the denser oceanic plate is forced beneath the continental plate.">destructive plate boundary</dfn>, plates move towards each other. Because oceanic crust is denser than continental crust, the oceanic plate is forced beneath the continental plate in a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="The process where a denser tectonic plate is forced beneath a less dense plate at a destructive boundary.">subduction</dfn>. The area where this happens is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="The zone at a destructive plate boundary where the oceanic plate is being pushed down into the mantle.">subduction zone</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">As the oceanic plate sinks into the mantle, it melts and the pressure forces magma upwards, creating <dfn class="term" data-def="A steep-sided volcano built from alternating layers of ash and lava, typically found at destructive plate boundaries.">composite volcanoes</dfn> — steep, explosive volcanoes made of alternating layers of ash and lava. Strong earthquakes and <dfn class="term" data-def="A large ocean wave caused by an underwater earthquake or volcanic eruption displacing water.">tsunamis</dfn> are also common at destructive boundaries.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">The western coast of South America is a classic destructive boundary. The Nazca Plate (oceanic) is being subducted beneath the South American Plate (continental), creating the Andes mountain range and volcanoes such as Cotopaxi in Ecuador. Japan is another example, sitting where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate, leading to frequent earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.</p>
</div>
</div></div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n11">Conservative Plate Boundary</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n12">At a <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates slide past each other in opposite directions or at different speeds.">conservative plate boundary</dfn>, two plates slide past each other. They can move in opposite directions, or in the same direction at different speeds. No magma reaches the surface, so <strong>no volcanoes</strong> form. However, the friction between the plates builds up pressure, and when that pressure is released it causes frequent, often powerful earthquakes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The most famous example is the <strong>San Andreas Fault</strong> in California, where the Pacific Plate slides past the North American Plate. Both plates move northwards, but the Pacific Plate moves faster (30–50 mm per year). Major cities like Los Angeles and San Francisco sit directly on this fault line.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Collision Plate Boundary</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n15">At a <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates of continental crust push together, forcing land upwards to form fold mountains.">collision plate boundary</dfn>, two plates made of continental crust move towards each other. Because both plates have the same density, neither can be subducted. Instead, the land between them is forced upwards, creating <dfn class="term" data-def="Mountains formed when two continental plates collide and the rock between them is pushed upwards.">fold mountains</dfn>. Strong earthquakes are common, but there are no volcanoes because no plate is being melted in the mantle.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the two plates colliding to form the Himalayas and how fast they grow yearly.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The <strong>Himalayas</strong> were formed by the collision of the Indian Plate and the Eurasian Plate. This collision is still happening today, which is why the Himalayas continue to grow by about 1 cm per year and why the region experiences frequent earthquakes.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why Do People Live Near Tectonic Hazards?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">It is estimated that around 750 million people live within 60 miles of an active or dormant volcano. Millions more live along major fault lines. There are several reasons why people choose to stay in areas at risk from tectonic hazards.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Historical Settlement</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Many settlements near plate boundaries have existed for hundreds or even thousands of years. Cities like Los Angeles and San Francisco on the San Andreas Fault were established long before people understood plate tectonics. Over time these settlements have grown into major cities with millions of residents. People are attached to their homes, families and communities, and do not want to leave.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Fertile Soil</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Volcanic eruptions deposit ash over the surrounding land. Over time this ash breaks down into extremely <dfn class="term" data-def="Soil that is rich in nutrients and minerals, allowing crops to grow well.">fertile soil</dfn>, which is ideal for growing crops. In poorer countries especially, people are attracted to volcanic areas because the rich soil allows them to grow food to feed their families and to sell commercially. For example, rice paddies surround Mount Agung in Bali, Indonesia, despite the volcano’s history of major eruptions.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Mining and Minerals</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Volcanic eruptions bring valuable minerals to the surface, including sulphur, zinc and precious metals. Mining these minerals provides jobs and income for local communities. At Mount Ijen in East Java, Indonesia, workers mine sulphur from inside the active volcano’s crater — a dangerous but economically important activity.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Tourism and Employment</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Volcanoes and tectonic landscapes attract thousands of tourists each year. Adventure tourism, sightseeing and geothermal spas create jobs in hospitality, transport and guiding. In Iceland, geothermal energy from volcanic activity heats homes and powers industry, making it a practical advantage to live near tectonic activity.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Trust in Prediction and Protection</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Many people trust that scientists can predict eruptions and earthquakes in time for evacuations. They also have confidence in modern engineering — earthquake-resistant buildings, early warning systems and well-rehearsed emergency plans. This sense of security encourages people to stay, especially in wealthier countries where these measures are well-funded.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall 3 reasons people settled hazard-prone coastlines, before knowing about plate tectonics.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Tectonic hazards frequently occur along coastlines, which is also where many large human settlements developed — originally for access to food, trade and transport. People settled in these areas before the science of plate tectonics was understood, and the settlements are now too large and established to relocate.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The four types of plate boundary — constructive, destructive, conservative and collision — each produce different hazards depending on how the plates interact. Despite the risks, hundreds of millions of people live near plate boundaries for economic, historical and practical reasons.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Destructive boundaries</strong> produce the most hazards (composite volcanoes, strong earthquakes, tsunamis) because the subduction of oceanic crust generates intense pressure and melting.</li>
<li><strong>Conservative boundaries</strong> produce no volcanoes but cause powerful earthquakes from the friction of plates sliding past each other.</li>
<li><strong>People stay</strong> near hazards because of fertile soil, mining, tourism, historical attachment, and trust in modern prediction and protection.</li>
</ul>
