<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Climate Change and the Quaternary Period</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns, occurring both naturally and as a result of human activity.">Climate change</dfn> is a long-term shift in global temperatures and weather patterns. The Earth’s climate has changed naturally for millions of years, cycling between cold <dfn class="term" data-def="A cold period when global temperatures drop and ice sheets expand across large areas of the Earth.">glacial</dfn> periods (ice ages) and warmer <dfn class="term" data-def="A warmer period between glacials when temperatures rise and ice retreats, like the present day.">interglacial</dfn> periods. We are currently in an interglacial.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this box and recall: How long did cold vs. warm phases last in the Quaternary period?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="The most recent geological period, spanning the last 2.5 million years, characterised by repeated cycles of glaciation.">Quaternary period</dfn> covers the last 2.5 million years. During this time, the Earth has experienced repeated cycles of glacial and interglacial periods, with cold phases lasting around 100,000 years and warmer phases lasting around 10,000 years.</p>
</div>

<img alt="Climate change mitigation versus adaptation strategies" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_climate_responses.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Evidence for Climate Change</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Scientists use several types of evidence to reconstruct past climates:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Ice cores</strong> — drilled from ice sheets in Antarctica and Greenland, containing air bubbles trapped up to 800,000 years ago. The levels of oxygen, carbon dioxide and methane in these bubbles reveal past temperatures.</li>
<li><strong>Ocean sediments</strong> — layers of material on the sea floor contain remains of tiny organisms that show what temperatures were like when they were alive.</li>
<li><strong>Tree rings</strong> — wider rings indicate warm, wet growing seasons; narrow rings suggest cold, dry conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Pollen analysis</strong> — pollen preserved in peat bogs and lake sediments reveals which plants grew in the past, indicating the climate at the time.</li>
<li><strong>Modern evidence</strong> — sea level rise, retreating glaciers, increasing sea temperatures, more frequent extreme weather events and increased wildfire activity all indicate current warming.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Natural Causes of Climate Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Four natural factors have driven climate change throughout Earth’s history:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Volcanic Activity</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">Major volcanic eruptions eject dust, ash and sulphuric acid into the upper atmosphere. This material is spread around the world by high-altitude winds and acts like a blanket, <strong>blocking and reflecting sunlight</strong> back into space. This causes a <strong>global cooling effect</strong> that can last for several years. However, volcanoes also release <strong>carbon dioxide</strong> (a greenhouse gas), which over much longer time periods contributes to warming. The cooling effect of a single large eruption is more immediate and dramatic than the warming from its CO₂ emissions.</p>
</div>
</div></div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Orbital Changes (Milankovitch Cycles)</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Earth’s orbit around the sun is not a perfect circle — it is <dfn class="term" data-def="An oval-shaped orbit, meaning the Earth is sometimes closer to and sometimes further from the sun.">elliptical</dfn>, and this shape changes over a 100,000-year cycle. When the orbit is more elongated, the Earth is further from the sun for part of its orbit, receiving less radiation and cooling down. The Earth’s <strong>axial tilt</strong> also changes between 21.5° and 24.5° over a 41,000-year cycle, altering how much sunlight the poles receive. Finally, the Earth <strong>wobbles</strong> on its axis in a 20,000-year cycle (like a spinning top), which changes the timing of seasons. When all three factors combine to reduce solar radiation, a glacial period can begin.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Sunspot Activity</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Dark patches on the sun’s surface that indicate increased solar activity and higher energy output.">Sunspots</dfn> are dark patches on the sun’s surface. A “spotty” sun produces <strong>more radiation</strong>, warming the Earth. Fewer sunspots mean less solar output and a slight cooling effect. The sun has had <strong>fewer sunspots</strong> over the last 50 years, which should produce mild cooling — yet the Earth has continued to warm, suggesting something else (human activity) is having a much larger effect.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Ice Reflection (Albedo Effect)</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">When ice sheets form, their white surface reflects sunlight back into space rather than absorbing it as heat. This keeps the Earth cool even if other conditions favour warming, and is one reason why <strong>glacial periods last so long</strong>. Conversely, when ice melts, the darker land or ocean surface absorbs more heat, accelerating warming further. This is known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The proportion of sunlight reflected by a surface; ice has a high albedo (reflects more), while darker surfaces have a low albedo (absorb more heat).">albedo effect</dfn>.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Human Causes of Climate Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">While natural climate change has occurred for millions of years, human activity since the Industrial Revolution (around 1800) has dramatically accelerated warming. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The increase in greenhouse gas concentrations caused by human activities, trapping more heat in the atmosphere than the natural greenhouse effect.">enhanced greenhouse effect</dfn>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">The Greenhouse Effect</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The natural process by which greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap heat from the sun, keeping the Earth warm enough to support life.">greenhouse effect</dfn> is a natural process. The sun’s energy (ultraviolet radiation) passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth’s surface. The surface gives off heat (infrared radiation), but greenhouse gases — including <strong>carbon dioxide (CO₂)</strong>, <strong>methane (CH₄)</strong> and <strong>nitrous oxide (N₂O)</strong> — trap some of this heat, preventing it from escaping into space. Without this natural effect, the Earth would be about 33°C colder. The problem is that human activities are adding extra greenhouse gases, trapping <strong>more</strong> heat than the natural system would.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Three Main Human Causes</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n18">
<li><strong>Burning fossil fuels</strong> — coal, oil and gas are burned for electricity generation, transport (petrol and diesel) and home heating/cooking. This releases carbon dioxide that was stored underground for millions of years back into the atmosphere.</li>
<li><strong>Agriculture</strong> — large-scale cattle farming produces excessive <strong>methane</strong> from cattle digestion. Rice paddies also release methane through the decay of organic matter in waterlogged fields.</li>
<li><strong>Deforestation</strong> — cutting down trees (especially tropical rainforests) for farming and urban development removes the trees that absorb CO₂. When trees are burned through <dfn class="term" data-def="A farming method where vegetation is cut down and burned to clear land for agriculture.">slash and burn</dfn>, the carbon they stored is released directly into the atmosphere.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name 3 ways population growth and wealth worsen climate change.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Population growth and rising wealth make the problem worse. More people means more fossil fuel use, more agriculture and more deforestation. As countries become wealthier, people buy more cars, devices and meat — all of which increase greenhouse gas emissions.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Consequences of Climate Change</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Effects on the UK</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n22">
<li><strong>Sea level rise</strong> and <strong>coastal flooding</strong> threaten low-lying communities, especially on the east and south-east coasts.</li>
<li>More frequent <strong>extreme weather events</strong> — heatwaves, droughts and flooding.</li>
<li>More <strong>heat-related deaths</strong>, especially among the elderly.</li>
<li><strong>Loss of habitats</strong> as species cannot adapt fast enough to changing temperatures.</li>
<li>On the positive side: ability to <strong>grow new crops</strong> (such as grapes and olives), <strong>less heating needed</strong> in homes, fewer cold-related deaths, and a <strong>longer summer tourism season</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Worldwide Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li><strong>Sub-Saharan Africa</strong> will face more severe drought, making food and water even harder to find.</li>
<li><strong>Polar bears and seals</strong> face extinction as Arctic sea ice shrinks.</li>
<li><strong>Coral reef bleaching</strong> is increasing as ocean temperatures and acidity rise, especially in the Great Barrier Reef.</li>
<li><strong>Ski resorts</strong> in the European Alps may close due to lack of snow.</li>
<li>North and Central American <strong>maize production</strong> could fall by 12%, raising cereal prices.</li>
<li>Global <strong>fishing levels</strong> could fall by 40%, affecting the diets of 40 million people.</li>
<li><strong>70% of Asia</strong> will face increased flood risk.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Responding to Climate Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">There are two approaches to dealing with climate change: <dfn class="term" data-def="Actions taken to reduce or prevent the causes of climate change, such as cutting greenhouse gas emissions.">mitigation</dfn> (reducing the causes) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Actions taken to adjust to the effects of climate change and live with its consequences.">adaptation</dfn> (living with the effects).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Mitigation Strategies</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n28">
<li><strong>Alternative energy</strong> — replacing fossil fuels with renewable sources such as wind, solar and hydroelectric power to reduce CO₂ emissions.</li>
<li><strong>Carbon capture and storage (CCS)</strong> — capturing CO₂ from power stations and pumping it underground into old coal mines and oil wells, where it forms compounds with the surrounding rock. CCS could store 10–55% of global CO₂ emissions by 2100, saving the UK £30 billion in climate target costs. However, it allows continued fossil fuel burning and the stored CO₂ could eventually leak.</li>
<li><strong>Afforestation</strong> — planting new forests to absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere through photosynthesis.</li>
<li><strong>International agreements</strong> — countries have come together to agree on reducing emissions:
            <ul>
<li><strong>2005 Kyoto Protocol</strong> — the first legally binding agreement, where industrialised countries agreed to cut CO₂ by 5.2% by 2012 (though the USA never ratified it).</li>
<li><strong>2009 Copenhagen Accord</strong> — extended Kyoto targets and raised money for LICs to tackle their emissions.</li>
<li><strong>2015 Paris Agreement</strong> — 196 countries signed a universal agreement to reduce CO₂, keep temperature rise well below 2°C, review progress every 5 years, and raise US$100 billion a year to help poorer countries.</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Adaptation Strategies</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n30">
<li><strong>Changing agriculture</strong> — moving crop production to areas with suitable new climates (e.g. growing crops further north), growing <strong>drought-resistant crops</strong> that need less water, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The artificial watering of land to help crops grow, often using water from rivers or groundwater.">irrigating</dfn> farmland to replace declining rainfall.</li>
<li><strong>Managing water supply</strong> — reducing personal water use (shorter showers, fixing leaks, water-efficient appliances) and investing in <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing salt from seawater to produce fresh drinking water.">desalination</dfn> to produce fresh water from the sea (London already provides 400,000 homes with desalinated Thames water, though the process uses a lot of energy).</li>
<li><strong>Defending against sea level rise</strong> — building flood defences such as <strong>sea walls</strong> (Blackpool) and <strong>flood barriers</strong> (Thames Barrier in London, which currently gives a 1-in-1,000-year flood rate, though this may reduce to 1-in-100 as sea levels continue to rise). Some communities may ultimately need to <strong>relocate inland</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain: What's the key difference between mitigation and adaptation?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The key difference: <strong>mitigation</strong> tries to stop climate change from getting worse (reducing the cause), while <strong>adaptation</strong> accepts that some change will happen and focuses on living with the effects. Both are needed because even if all emissions stopped today, the climate would continue changing for decades.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Climate change has both natural and human causes. While natural cycles have driven glacial and interglacial periods over millions of years, human activities since the Industrial Revolution have dramatically accelerated warming. The consequences are global, affecting weather patterns, sea levels, ecosystems and food production. Both mitigation and adaptation are needed to address the challenge.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Natural causes</strong> include volcanic activity, orbital changes, sunspot cycles and the albedo effect — these have driven climate change for millions of years.</li>
<li><strong>Human causes</strong> (fossil fuels, agriculture, deforestation) have enhanced the greenhouse effect, causing rapid warming since around 1800.</li>
<li><strong>Mitigation</strong> (renewables, CCS, international agreements) reduces the causes, while <strong>adaptation</strong> (changing agriculture, water management, flood defences) helps us live with the effects.</li>
</ul>
