--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rock Type and Coastal Shape</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The shape of a coastline depends on the type of rock it is made from. Some rocks are hard and resistant to erosion (such as granite, limestone and chalk), while others are soft and erode quickly (such as clay and sandstone). Where hard and soft rocks are found side by side, the coast is eroded at different rates, creating distinctive landforms.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Concordant and Discordant Coastlines</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A coastline where rock layers run parallel to the shore, so waves hit the same rock type.">concordant coastline</dfn> has rock layers running parallel to the shore. The waves hit the same type of rock along its length. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A coastline where different rock types run at right angles to the shore, so waves erode each type at a different rate.">discordant coastline</dfn> has alternating bands of hard and soft rock running at right angles to the shore. The Jurassic Coast in Dorset is a well-known example of a discordant coastline.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Erosional Landforms</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Headlands and Bays</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n7">On a discordant coastline, the softer rock is eroded more quickly by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming an indent called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wide, curved indentation in the coastline formed by the erosion of softer rock between two areas of harder rock.">bay</dfn>. The harder, more resistant rock is left jutting out into the sea as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrow area of hard rock that sticks out into the sea, formed because it resists erosion more than the surrounding softer rock.">headland</dfn>. At Swanage Bay, the hard chalk and limestone form headlands (Ballard Point and Peveril Point), while the softer clay between them has been eroded to form the bay.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name a rock type found in bays and a rock type found in headlands.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Headlands and bays form on <strong>discordant coastlines</strong> where alternating hard and soft rock runs at right angles to the shore. The soft rock erodes faster, creating a bay, while the hard rock remains as a headland.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Caves, Arches, Stacks and Stumps</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Once a headland is exposed, it is attacked by waves from all sides. The formation sequence is:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n11">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Stage 1</div>
<h4>Crack</h4>
<p>Hydraulic action forces air into a weakness or joint in the headland, gradually widening it into a large crack.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Stage 2</div>
<h4>Cave</h4>
<p>Continued hydraulic action and abrasion widen the crack into a cave. The cave grows larger as waves compress air inside it.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Stage 3</div>
<h4>Arch</h4>
<p>If the cave is eroded right through the headland, it forms a natural arch with open air above and sea below.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Stage 4</div>
<h4>Stack</h4>
<p>The roof of the arch is weakened by weathering and wave erosion until it collapses, leaving a tall pillar of rock called a stack standing separate from the headland.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">Stage 5</div>
<h4>Stump</h4>
<p>The stack is further eroded at its base until it collapses into a low, flat <dfn class="term" data-def="A low remnant of rock left after a stack has been eroded and collapsed.">stump</dfn>, visible only at low tide. Old Harry Rocks at Studland in Dorset show stacks and stumps at various stages.</p>
</div>
</div><h3 data-narration-id="n12">Cliffs and Wave-Cut Platforms</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Waves attack the base of a cliff between the high and low tide marks through hydraulic action, abrasion and solution. This erosion creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A small indentation cut into the base of a cliff by wave erosion between the high and low tide marks.">wave-cut notch</dfn>. Over time, the notch deepens and the rock above is left unsupported as an overhang. Eventually the overhang becomes too heavy and collapses. This process repeats, causing the cliff to retreat inland.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The flat area of rock left behind at the base of the retreating cliff is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A flat, gently sloping platform of rock left at the base of a cliff as it retreats, exposed at low tide.">wave-cut platform</dfn>. It slopes gently towards the sea (about 3–4°) and is only exposed at low tide. Rock pools, seaweed and barnacles are commonly found on wave-cut platforms. Washing Ledge on the Jurassic Coast is a good example.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">The Jurassic Coast stretches along the south coast of England through Devon and Dorset. Its rocks are up to 250 million years old and contain many dinosaur fossils from the Jurassic period. The coastline displays a wide range of landforms because it has both concordant and discordant sections. Lulworth Cove is a famous example of a cove on the concordant section — waves broke through the hard limestone at the front and then rapidly eroded the softer clay behind, creating a near-circular bay.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Depositional Landforms</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Beaches</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The zone of deposited material between the low water line and the limit of storm waves.">beach</dfn> is the zone of deposited material that extends from the low water mark to the limit of storm waves. Beaches form in sheltered environments like bays, where constructive waves deposit sediment. The beach profile shows larger material (pebbles and shingle) higher up the beach — thrown there by high-energy storm waves — and finer sand near the low water mark, deposited by lower-energy waves. Chesil Beach in Dorset is one of the UK’s most famous shingle beaches.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Sand Dunes</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Sand dunes form above the high water mark where onshore winds blow dry sand inland. Sand collects around obstacles such as rocks or driftwood, forming small <dfn class="term" data-def="Small, newly formed sand dunes closest to the sea, only a few metres tall.">embryo dunes</dfn>. As vegetation like marram grass colonises the sand, its roots bind the grains together and the dunes become more stable. Over time, dunes grow taller and develop further inland. Mature dunes can reach 15 metres. Occasionally, high winds create a “blowout” — a hollow where loose sand has been blown away.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Spits</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended stretch of beach material attached to the mainland at one end, extending out to sea or across a bay.">spit</dfn> is a long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle that is attached to the mainland at one end and extends out to sea at the other. Spits form where longshore drift carries sediment past a change in the direction of the coastline (such as a river estuary). The sediment is deposited in the sheltered water beyond the headland. Changes in wind direction can curve the end of the spit inland, forming a hooked tip. Salt marshes often develop in the sheltered water behind the spit.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what stops a spit from fully forming across an estuary?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A spit cannot grow all the way across an estuary because the river current is strong enough to carry sediment out to sea, preventing the spit from joining the opposite bank.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Bars and Tombolos</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25">If a spit grows across a bay where there is no river to break it up, it can join two headlands to form a <dfn class="term" data-def="A ridge of sand or shingle that stretches across a bay, joining two headlands and trapping a lagoon behind it.">bar</dfn>. A lagoon of trapped water often forms behind the bar. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A bar of sand or shingle that connects an island to the mainland.">tombolo</dfn> is a special type of bar that connects an island to the mainland.</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26">Think of deposition as the sea “building” features. Beaches are the simplest — sediment dropped in sheltered areas. Spits are beaches that “grow” past a change in coastline direction. Bars are spits that have grown all the way across a bay. Sand dunes are wind-blown sand above the beach. Each one is formed because the sea (or wind) loses energy and drops material.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The UK coastline features a wide range of landforms shaped by erosion and deposition. Erosion creates headlands and bays, caves, arches, stacks, stumps, cliffs and wave-cut platforms. Deposition creates beaches, sand dunes, spits and bars. The type and hardness of rock determines which landforms develop in each location.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Discordant coastlines</strong> produce headlands and bays because soft rock erodes faster than hard rock.</li>
<li><strong>Caves → arches → stacks → stumps</strong> form in sequence as headlands are eroded by hydraulic action and abrasion.</li>
<li><strong>Spits</strong> form where longshore drift deposits sediment past a change in coastline direction; <strong>bars</strong> form when a spit grows across a bay with no river.</li>
</ul>
