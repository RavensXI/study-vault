--- DESCRIPTION ---
Physical and human causes of river flooding, the hydrological cycle within a drainage basin, and how to read and interpret flood hydrographs.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Rivers Flood?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Flooding occurs when a river receives more water than its channel can hold. The excess water spills over the banks and onto the surrounding land. Most flooding is caused by a combination of physical and human factors working together. To understand these causes, you first need to know how water moves through a <dfn class="term" data-def="The area of land drained by a river and all its tributaries.">drainage basin</dfn>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">The Drainage Basin System</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">When <dfn class="term" data-def="Any form of moisture that falls from the atmosphere, including rain, snow, sleet and hail.">precipitation</dfn> falls, it enters the drainage basin as an input. The water can then be:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n5">
<li><strong>Stored</strong> — on trees (<dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which precipitation is caught and held by vegetation before reaching the ground.">interception</dfn>), on the surface (lakes, reservoirs), in the soil (<dfn class="term" data-def="Water held in the spaces between soil particles.">soil moisture</dfn>), or underground (<dfn class="term" data-def="Water stored in permeable rock below the water table.">groundwater</dfn>).</li>
<li><strong>Transferred</strong> — across the ground as <dfn class="term" data-def="Water flowing over the land surface into a river channel, often when the ground is saturated or impermeable.">surface runoff</dfn>, through the soil as <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of water through the soil towards a river channel.">throughflow</dfn>, or through rock as <dfn class="term" data-def="The slow movement of water through permeable rock towards a river channel.">groundwater flow</dfn>.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Anything that speeds up the transfer of water into the river channel — or reduces the amount of water stored — increases the risk of flooding.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Physical Causes of Flooding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">There are three key physical factors that affect flood risk. They often work together, and a combination of two or three makes flooding much more likely.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Rainfall</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Persistent rainfall</strong> (long periods of steady rain, common in winter <dfn class="term" data-def="An area of low atmospheric pressure that brings cloud, rain and wind to the UK, often for extended periods.">depressions</dfn>) saturates the soil. Once the soil is full of water, it cannot absorb any more, so additional rain flows across the surface as runoff directly into the river. <strong>Sudden heavy bursts</strong> of rain (common in summer) can also cause flooding because dry, baked soil is hard for water to <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of water soaking into the soil from the surface.">infiltrate</dfn>. <strong>Snowmelt</strong> is a third cause — when accumulated snow melts rapidly, large volumes of water are released as surface runoff, especially when the ground beneath is still frozen.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Geology</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">The rock type surrounding a river affects how quickly water reaches the channel. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock that does not allow water to pass through it.">Impermeable rock</dfn> (such as granite) does not allow water to soak through, so rainfall runs off the surface quickly into the river. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock that allows water to pass through it, storing it underground.">Permeable rock</dfn> (such as chalk and sandstone) allows water to <dfn class="term" data-def="The downward movement of water through rock.">percolate</dfn> through and be stored as groundwater, slowing its journey to the river. Areas with impermeable rock therefore have a higher flood risk.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Relief (Shape of the Land)</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11">Steep slopes cause water to flow quickly downhill as surface runoff, giving it little time to infiltrate the soil. This means water reaches the river channel rapidly. Low-lying areas have gentle slopes where water moves slowly and can build up as surface water, increasing flood risk. Valleys at the bottom of steep mountains are particularly vulnerable because they collect water from a large surrounding area.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what is the rainfall at Cow Green, its altitude, and the rock type?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>On the River Tees, the upper course at Cow Green Reservoir receives over <strong>2,000 mm</strong> of rainfall per year on slopes up to 893 m high. The rock here is impermeable, so water reaches the river quickly. Snow also accumulates in winter and melts rapidly. This combination of high rainfall, steep relief and impermeable rock makes the upper Tees particularly prone to flooding.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Human Causes of Flooding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Human activities can significantly increase flood risk by changing how water moves through the drainage basin.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
<li><strong>Urbanisation</strong> — building houses, roads and car parks replaces natural soil and vegetation with <dfn class="term" data-def="Surfaces such as concrete, tarmac and roofing that do not allow water to soak through.">impermeable surfaces</dfn> like concrete and tarmac. Rainfall cannot infiltrate and instead runs off directly into drains and rivers. As gardens and fields disappear under new development, this problem intensifies.</li>
<li><strong>Deforestation</strong> — removing trees reduces interception (water caught on leaves and trunks) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The release of water vapour from plants through their leaves.">transpiration</dfn> (water absorbed by roots and released through leaves). More water therefore reaches the river as surface runoff. Ploughed farmland without hedgerows also offers little resistance to water flow.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Storm Hydrographs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph showing how the discharge of a river changes over time in response to a rainfall event.">storm hydrograph</dfn> shows how a river responds to a period of rainfall. It plots two things: rainfall (shown as bars) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The volume of water flowing through a river channel at a given point, measured in cubic metres per second (cumecs).">discharge</dfn> (shown as a line). Discharge is the volume of water flowing through the river at a given point, measured in cumecs (cubic metres per second).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Features of a Hydrograph</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n19">
<li><strong>Peak rainfall</strong> — the time of the heaviest rainfall.</li>
<li><strong>Peak discharge</strong> — the point at which the river reaches its highest flow. This occurs <em>after</em> peak rainfall because water takes time to travel from the land to the river channel.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The time delay between peak rainfall and peak discharge on a storm hydrograph.">Lag time</dfn></strong> — the gap in time between peak rainfall and peak discharge. A <strong>short</strong> lag time means water reaches the river quickly, increasing flood risk. A <strong>long</strong> lag time means water takes longer to arrive, reducing flood risk.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The section of a hydrograph where discharge is increasing towards peak discharge.">Rising limb</dfn></strong> — the section of the graph where discharge is increasing as water enters the river.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The section of a hydrograph where discharge is decreasing after peak discharge.">Falling limb</dfn></strong> — the section where discharge decreases as water drains through the system.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the 4 factors that cause a flashy hydrograph.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A <strong>flashy hydrograph</strong> (steep rising limb, short lag time, high peak discharge) indicates a river that floods quickly. This is common in urban areas (impermeable surfaces), areas with steep slopes, impermeable rock, or deforested catchments. A <strong>flat hydrograph</strong> (gentle rising limb, long lag time, lower peak discharge) is typical of rural, vegetated areas with permeable rock.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">What Affects Lag Time and Peak Discharge?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The same amount of rainfall can produce very different hydrographs depending on the characteristics of the drainage basin:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n23">
<li><strong>Impermeable rock or concrete</strong> → less infiltration → more surface runoff → shorter lag time, higher peak discharge.</li>
<li><strong>Steep slopes</strong> → water moves faster downhill → shorter lag time.</li>
<li><strong>Deforestation/lack of vegetation</strong> → less interception → more water reaches the river → higher peak discharge.</li>
<li><strong>Dense vegetation/forest</strong> → more interception and infiltration → longer lag time, lower peak discharge.</li>
<li><strong>Saturated soil</strong> → cannot absorb more water → increased surface runoff → shorter lag time.</li>
</ul>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Flooding is caused by a combination of physical factors (rainfall, geology, relief) and human factors (urbanisation, deforestation). Storm hydrographs help us understand and predict how a river will respond to rainfall, with lag time and peak discharge being the most important features to identify.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical causes</strong> include persistent/heavy rainfall, impermeable rock and steep slopes — all of which speed up water reaching the river.</li>
<li><strong>Human causes</strong> such as urbanisation (impermeable surfaces) and deforestation (less interception) increase surface runoff and reduce lag time.</li>
<li><strong>Storm hydrographs</strong> show how a river responds to rainfall — a short lag time and high peak discharge indicate a river that floods quickly.</li>
</ul>


--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>When reading a storm hydrograph in the exam, always identify the <strong>lag time</strong> (gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge) and the <strong>peak discharge</strong> value. If asked to compare two hydrographs, focus on which has a shorter lag time and higher peak — that river is more likely to flood. Always explain <em>why</em> using drainage basin characteristics (rock type, land use, slope, vegetation).</p>
