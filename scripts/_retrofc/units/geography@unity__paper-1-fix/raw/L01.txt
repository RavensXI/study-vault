--- DESCRIPTION ---
What makes a natural event a hazard, the theory of plate tectonics, and where earthquakes and volcanoes occur around the world.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Natural Hazard?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A natural event that poses a threat to people and property.">natural hazard</dfn> is a natural event that threatens people and property. Not every natural event is a hazard — a volcanic eruption on a remote, uninhabited island is simply a natural event. It only becomes a hazard when it has the potential to cause death, injury, damage or economic loss.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and name one tectonic and one weather hazard.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Natural hazards are grouped into two main types: <strong>tectonic hazards</strong> (earthquakes and volcanoes, caused by movement of the Earth’s crust) and <strong>weather hazards</strong> (tropical storms, floods, droughts, heatwaves, caused by atmospheric conditions).</p>
</div>
<figure class="diagram">
<img alt="Four types of natural hazard: tectonic, climatic, geomorphic, and biological" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_natural_hazard_types.jpg"/>
</figure>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Factors Affecting Hazard Risk</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Three main factors determine how dangerous a natural hazard is:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Scale of the hazard</strong> — a magnitude 9 earthquake poses far greater risk than a magnitude 1 earthquake. A Category 5 tropical storm is more destructive than a Category 1.</li>
<li><strong>Wealth of the country</strong> — wealthier countries can afford stronger buildings, better warning systems, trained emergency services and medical support. Poorer countries have fewer resources to prepare or respond.</li>
<li><strong>Population size and proximity</strong> — the more people living near a hazard, the greater the risk. As the world’s population grows and more land is inhabited, more people are exposed to natural hazards.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Structure of the Earth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">To understand tectonic hazards, you need to know how the Earth is built. The Earth has four main layers:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li><strong>Inner core</strong> — solid metal at the very centre, extremely hot (around 6,000°C) and under immense pressure.</li>
<li><strong>Outer core</strong> — liquid metal surrounding the inner core. Its movement generates the Earth’s magnetic field.</li>
<li><strong>Mantle</strong> — a thick layer of semi-liquid rock (magma) that can flow very slowly. <dfn class="term" data-def="Circular movements of hot magma rising and cool magma sinking within the mantle.">Convection currents</dfn> in the mantle drive plate movement.</li>
<li><strong>Crust</strong> — the thin, solid outer layer we live on. It makes up just 1% of the Earth’s volume.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n10">Two Types of Crust</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The crust is not one solid piece. It is broken into large slabs called <dfn class="term" data-def="Large sections of the Earth’s crust that float on the mantle and move slowly over time.">tectonic plates</dfn>, and these plates are made of two different types of crust:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Continental vs Oceanic Crust</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Continental crust</strong> is 25–70 km thick, less dense, very old (up to 3.8 billion years) and made mainly of granite. It is not destroyed at plate boundaries. <strong>Oceanic crust</strong> is much thinner (5–10 km), denser, younger (usually less than 200 million years old) and made mainly of basalt. Because it is denser, oceanic crust sinks beneath continental crust when they collide, and it is recycled back into the mantle at <dfn class="term" data-def="A plate boundary where two plates move towards each other and one is forced beneath the other.">destructive plate margins</dfn>.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Plate Tectonics Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific theory that the Earth’s crust is divided into large plates that move slowly due to convection currents in the mantle.">Plate tectonics theory</dfn> explains that the Earth’s crust is divided into large plates that move slowly over time. Scientists have strong evidence for this:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
<li><strong>Volcanoes</strong> — if the crust were one solid piece, magma could not escape to the surface. The existence of volcanoes proves the crust is broken.</li>
<li><strong>Pattern of hazards</strong> — earthquakes and volcanoes occur in clear lines along plate boundaries, not randomly. This linear pattern shows where the edges of the plates are.</li>
<li><strong>GPS measurements</strong> — satellites track the movement of the plates in real time, measuring shifts of a few centimetres per year.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">What Makes the Plates Move?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The most widely accepted explanation is <dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that heat from the Earth’s core causes circular currents in the mantle, which drag the tectonic plates.">convection theory</dfn>. The Earth’s core reaches temperatures of around 6,000°C. This intense heat causes magma in the mantle to rise towards the crust. As it rises, it cools and sinks back towards the core. This creates circular convection currents that flow through the mantle, and the pressure and movement of these currents drags the tectonic plates along with them.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="From memory, how many cm per year do plates move?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Tectonic plates move very slowly — typically just a few centimetres per year. This movement is far too slow to see or feel, but over millions of years it reshapes continents and ocean basins.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Distribution of Tectonic Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Earthquakes and volcanoes are not spread randomly across the globe. They follow a clear pattern:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li>Most occur in <strong>lines along plate boundaries</strong>, especially where plates meet along coastlines of continental landmasses.</li>
<li>The <dfn class="term" data-def="A horseshoe-shaped zone around the edge of the Pacific Ocean where about 75% of the world’s volcanoes and 90% of earthquakes occur.">Pacific Ring of Fire</dfn> is the most active zone — a horseshoe-shaped belt around the Pacific Ocean where around 75% of the world’s volcanoes and 90% of earthquakes are found.</li>
<li>Some hazards occur <strong>away from plate boundaries</strong>. The Yellowstone supervolcano (USA) and Kilauea (Hawaii) sit in the middle of a tectonic plate, above <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed area of intense volcanic activity in the mantle, not linked to plate boundaries.">hotspots</dfn> where magma rises from deep in the mantle.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Without looking, why does the UK have few earthquakes?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The UK has very few earthquakes and no active volcanoes because it sits in the middle of the Eurasian Plate, far from any plate boundary. Countries on plate boundaries — like Japan, Indonesia and Chile — experience frequent tectonic hazards.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Natural hazards are natural events that threaten people and property. The Earth’s crust is broken into tectonic plates that move on convection currents in the mantle. Where these plates meet, earthquakes and volcanoes are concentrated in clear linear patterns.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hazard risk</strong> depends on the scale of the event, the wealth of the country, and how many people live nearby.</li>
<li><strong>Convection currents</strong> in the mantle drive the movement of tectonic plates, causing hazards where plates interact.</li>
<li><strong>Distribution</strong> is linear along plate boundaries (especially the Pacific Ring of Fire), with a few exceptions at hotspots.</li>
</ul>


--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>When asked to “describe the distribution” of tectonic hazards, always mention: (1) the linear pattern along plate boundaries, (2) a named example like the Pacific Ring of Fire, and (3) exceptions such as hotspot volcanoes away from boundaries. Use compass directions and specific locations rather than vague language.</p>
