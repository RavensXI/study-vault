=== L5 Moor House & St John Rivers ===

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s rescue by the Rivers family, her inheritance, and St John’s chilling marriage offer.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rescue at Moor House</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Collapsed on the moor after three days without food, Jane is taken in at Moor House by St John Rivers and his sisters Diana and Mary. Brönte uses this rescue to make a quiet argument: kindness, not romance, is the true Christian virtue. Diana and Mary are presented in warm, vivid detail — “both were fair complexioned and slender… both possessed faces full of distinction and intelligence.” For the first time, Jane is offered a <dfn class="term" data-def="A home or group of people who provide care, belonging and support.">family</dfn> that is intellectually her equal.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and write, in one sentence, what Diana and Mary offer Jane that no previous household has.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Rivers sisters give Jane the first experience of intellectual friendship among equals. Brönte uses them to argue that friendship and mental life matter as much as romantic love — Jane must prove she can live without Rochester before she can return to him.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">St John Rivers: Cold Virtue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">St John is the novel’s third model of religion. If Brocklehurst is hypocritical and Helen is patient, St John is rigorous. Brönte describes him as “a cold, cumbrous column” — strong but lifeless. He is “handsome” but “austere”, and his sermons are “stern, almost condemning.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="Duty demanded by faith or principle rather than chosen out of desire.">religious duty</dfn> that drives him is real, but Brönte makes clear it has no warmth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">St John finds Jane a post as a village schoolmistress in Morton. She accepts with quiet relief. The work is honest and the pay modest, and Jane rents a tiny cottage. Brönte uses Morton to show that Jane is now fully self-supporting: her independence began with her refusal at Thornfield and is now lived materially.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Inheritance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">St John reveals a surprising truth: Jane’s uncle John Eyre has died and left her £20,000. In an age when a governess might earn £30 a year, this is an enormous sum. It makes Jane one of the richest women in the novel. Crucially, Brönte also reveals that the Rivers are Jane’s cousins: she has found blood family at last.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Jane insists on splitting the money equally four ways. Brönte makes the moral choice explicit: Jane wants family more than fortune. The inheritance is central to the novel’s argument. It transforms Jane from dependant to <dfn class="term" data-def="Financial, legal and social freedom allowing a woman to make her own decisions.">independent</dfn> woman. Only now can she return to Rochester without the taint of needing him.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Missionary Work and the Proposal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">St John plans to travel to India as a missionary and asks Jane to come with him — not out of love, but because he believes she has the right temperament for missionary work. He proposes marriage as the proper form for their partnership, telling her: “God and nature intended you for a missionary’s wife.” Brönte makes his language icy and impersonal, a deliberate contrast to Rochester’s theatrical warmth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jane considers the offer seriously. She says she could work beside him as a sister, but to marry him would be to die inside: “I should still have my unblighted self to turn to; my natural unenslaved feelings with which to communicate in moments of loneliness. There would be recesses in my mind which would be only mine.” The vocabulary of slavery is striking: Brönte draws on abolitionist discourse to frame a loveless marriage as bondage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain, in one sentence, why St John’s offer is a second, different kind of temptation for Jane.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>St John is Rochester’s opposite. Rochester offers love without marriage; St John offers marriage without love. Brönte puts both propositions to Jane so the novel can argue that true equality requires both legal union and felt affection.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Voice Across the Moor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">At the moment Jane is almost persuaded, Brönte introduces a moment of the supernatural. Jane hears Rochester’s voice calling her name: “‘Jane! Jane! Jane!’” She turns to the window and answers aloud. The scene is deliberately ambiguous — is it a divine sign, a hallucination, or the kind of telepathic bond that Gothic and Romantic fiction often allowed? Brönte does not explain it. The effect is to treat Jane’s inner voice as authoritative: she acts on what she feels.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Victorian Missionary Fiction</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In the 1840s, missionary work was a celebrated Christian calling, and marrying a missionary wife was considered a high moral choice. Brönte is not condemning the work itself — the novel ends with St John’s letters from India as a kind of benediction. But she refuses to let such work be presented as <dfn class="term" data-def="A choice approved by society rather than freely made by the person.">socially obligatory</dfn> for a woman. St John’s ‘Christian’ case for Jane’s marriage is, Brönte shows, simply another Victorian pressure on women to surrender their will.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Turning Point</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Jane refuses St John and leaves Moor House to find Rochester. She is now free: financially independent, morally tested, blood-related to the Rivers, and emotionally ready. Brönte has arranged the plot so that the return to Thornfield carries no compromise. Every test Rochester posed before — money, mistress, manipulation — has been answered.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Moor House gives Jane her first family of equals and, through St John’s revelation, her inheritance and relations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">St John offers marriage without love: a second, colder temptation that Jane must refuse to fully establish her independence.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The “voice on the moor” grants Jane’s inner feelings authority — Brönte treats her conscience as the compass the novel has been training.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Moor House section is often overlooked by students who race to the ending. Strong answers treat it as the novel’s second great temptation. Compare St John and Rochester explicitly: one is cold duty, the other hot feeling. Brönte uses Jane’s refusal of both to argue that real marriage needs the full person. Use the inheritance as evidence that Victorian freedom had to be financial as well as moral.</p>
