Mapping themes, handling extract-based essays, and planning 40-mark responses on Jane Eyre.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Core Themes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Jane Eyre</em> weaves a tight bundle of themes: independence, class, gender, religion, passion versus duty, and the Gothic or supernatural. Thinking of these as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A network of related ideas that runs through a text and connects different scenes together.">thematic web</dfn> rather than a checklist helps you to move confidently between them in an essay — Jane’s independence is bound up with her class position, which is shaped by Victorian gender expectations.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the six key themes of Jane Eyre and one short quotation that supports each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six key themes are: independence, class, gender, religion, passion versus duty, and the Gothic. Build a quotation bank with one supporting line per theme — colour-coding helps in revision.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Independence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Independence is the novel’s spine. Brönte shows it as moral, financial and legal. Jane resists Mrs Reed (“I am not deceitful”), Rochester (“I am no bird”) and St John (“I care for myself”). Her inheritance turns the moral stance into a material one. Linking the theme to the Victorian <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal doctrine that merged a married woman's identity and property with her husband's.">coverture</dfn> law shows why Jane’s financial autonomy is so hard-won — and so radical.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Class and Gender</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Class and gender are intertwined. Jane is a poor, plain, unmarried woman working as a governess — a role Victorian society treated as awkward: educated but not a lady, paid but not respectable. Blanche Ingram represents the alternative: titled, beautiful, rich, but morally empty. Brönte pointedly allows Jane to win. When Jane asserts “I have as much soul as you, and full as much heart”, she refuses to let class and gender dictate her moral worth.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Brönte critiques Victorian religious fashions through three figures: Brocklehurst (hypocrisy), Helen (patient submission) and St John (rigorous duty). Jane rejects all three and instead creates a faith that allows love, equality and autonomy. Her marriage of <dfn class="term" data-def="Living faith sincerely in day-to-day love, work and honesty.">practical Christianity</dfn> is Brönte’s preferred model — religion lived through affection, not spectacle or repression.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Passion versus Duty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brönte repeatedly tests Jane between passion (Rochester) and duty (St John). The novel argues that neither alone is sufficient. Jane’s refusal to become Rochester’s mistress is duty; her refusal to marry St John is passion; the final marriage is the balance of both. Use this dialectic to avoid reducing the novel to a love story: it is a moral argument about what love should be.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Gothic and the Supernatural</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Gothic runs through every setting: the red room, the locked third storey, Bertha’s laughter, the split chestnut, the voice on the moor. Brönte uses these conventions not for decoration but to make a psychological point. Victorian women had few ways to express anger or desire directly, so the Gothic becomes the novel’s language of <dfn class="term" data-def="Feelings that cannot be voiced directly and emerge through symbols, settings or doubles.">repressed emotion</dfn>. Bertha is the most charged example.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recite from memory the five-step plan for a 40-mark response on Jane Eyre.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plan for 5 minutes, write for 40, check for 5. Your plan should list: (1) extract thesis, (2) two methods from the extract, (3) two supporting moments from other volumes, (4) one context strand, (5) conclusion linking back to the question.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Reading the Extract Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">OCR’s 40-mark question gives you an extract plus a prompt to start with it and then explore the whole novel. A strong plan anchors you: a sharp opening that states Brönte’s argument; an extract paragraph; two supporting paragraphs from earlier and later in the novel; one contextual paragraph; a conclusion. Never leave the extract behind for too long — examiners want to see you moving between it and the wider text.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Using Context Properly</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The best context is the shortest. A four-word phrase — ‘the 1840s coverture law’, ‘Evangelical moral rigour’, ‘colonial anxiety about Creole women’ — can unlock a quotation. What examiners hate is a standalone context paragraph that reads like a Wikipedia summary. Always tie context to a specific line.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Using the Authorial Voice</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Begin sentences with Brönte as the subject: ‘Brönte presents…’, ‘Brönte uses…’, ‘Brönte constructs…’. Saying ‘Jane decides’ describes the plot; saying ‘Brönte has Jane decide’ analyses the choice. This single grammatical shift is the quickest way to move from a middle to a high band.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Structural words are also useful signals of analysis: ‘juxtaposes’, ‘parallels’, ‘foreshadows’, ‘mirrors’. They announce to the examiner that you are thinking about how the novel is shaped, not just what happens.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Final Checks Before You Submit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Run three final checks. Have you quoted from at least three different parts of the novel? Have you used Brönte’s name at least once per paragraph? Does every quotation lead to close analysis rather than paraphrase? If yes to all three, you are in the top bands. Manage time strictly — finishing a weaker answer beats abandoning a stronger one mid-paragraph.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Jane Eyre’s six themes — independence, class, gender, religion, passion/duty, Gothic — are interlocking. Build quotation banks across the web.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Plan for 5 minutes, write for 40, check for 5. Move continually between extract and whole novel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Use the authorial voice (‘Brönte presents…’) and tie context to a specific quotation rather than presenting it as background information.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">A useful rule: never leave a quotation without analysis, and never leave analysis without a link. End every paragraph with a sentence that ties back to Brönte’s argument — ‘This shows that Brönte is arguing…’. Without that tie-back even excellent close reading can plateau at a middle band.</p>
