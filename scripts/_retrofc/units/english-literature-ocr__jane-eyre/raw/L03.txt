--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane as governess, her charged encounters with Rochester, and the growing mystery of Bertha Mason.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Jane Arrives at Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane takes up the post of governess to Adèle Varens, the ward of Mr Rochester. Brönte uses first-person narration to describe her first impressions of Thornfield: “a gentleman’s manor-house… battlemented”, grand but strangely empty. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of 18th- and 19th-century fiction using old houses, secrets and the supernatural to explore fear and desire.">Gothic</dfn> atmosphere is present from the start: the third storey is locked, and a strange laugh — later revealed to be Bertha’s — floats down the corridor.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and name three Gothic features of Thornfield that signal something is hidden.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thornfield is built as a Gothic shell: battlements, locked third storey, disembodied laughter, nighttime fires. Brönte uses these conventions to plant the secret of Bertha long before Jane knows who laughs above her head.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The First Meeting on the Lane</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Jane meets Rochester before she knows who he is. Out walking on the road, she witnesses his horse slip on ice; the man swears, falls, and accepts her help. Brönte reverses the conventional romantic meet-cute. Jane does not recognise “the master” and he does not treat her as an inferior: she becomes a helper to a wounded man. The scene plants the idea that their relationship will be about <dfn class="term" data-def="Treating two people as moral and intellectual equals despite social differences.">equality</dfn>, not rescue, long before Rochester acknowledges it.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">One night Jane wakes to a demonic laugh and finds Rochester’s bed ablaze. She saves him by throwing water. Afterwards, Rochester’s voice is warm and confessional: “You have saved my life: I have a pleasure in owing you so immense a debt.” The fire is <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring image, idea or object in a text.">motif</dfn> — destructive, passionate, purifying. Bertha sets three fires in the novel, and each time she burns toward something Rochester is hiding.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Rochester blames Grace Poole, the servant hired to watch Bertha. Brönte lets Jane believe him, but the reader is planted with unease. This is dramatic irony — we know, retrospectively, that Rochester is lying.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Rochester’s Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Rochester is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A Romantic literary hero: brooding, morally complicated, with a troubled past.">Byronic hero</dfn>: brooding, sardonic, with “dark eyes”, a “heavy brow” and an unhappy history. He is physically unattractive but magnetic to Jane because he <em>listens</em>. He asks her opinions and takes them seriously, telling her, “You have rather the look of another world.” The conversations between them are dense with wit and equality — Jane responds sharply when he calls her “plain”: “‘I don’t think, sir, you have a right to command me.’”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brönte uses Rochester to explore the Victorian double-standard of gender: he has a history with a French opera-dancer (Adèle’s mother Celine) but is still a desirable marriage prospect, while Jane must be untouched. Jane’s refusal to be his mistress later in Volume II is therefore an act of moral resistance, not prudish obedience.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Blanche Ingram and Jealousy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Rochester invites a house party. Blanche Ingram — tall, beautiful, titled — flirts with him openly. Jane watches from the sidelines, sketching herself as “plain, obscure, little” in charcoal and Blanche in full colour, a form of deliberate self-punishment. Brönte uses this scene to dramatise Victorian ideals of female beauty and Jane’s refusal to compete. Rochester is testing Jane; Blanche is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character used to highlight qualities in another by contrast.">foil</dfn> — everything Jane is not and Rochester does not want.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain, in one sentence, why Blanche Ingram is important to the novel’s argument about what Rochester values.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Blanche is Rochester’s test of his own feelings. When he discovers she is only interested in his fortune, he proves to himself that Jane’s intelligence and integrity matter more than beauty or rank. Her presence is not a romantic obstacle but a moral sieve.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Proposal in the Garden</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Rochester proposes to Jane on midsummer’s eve in the garden. His language is theatrical: “I ask you to pass through life at my side — to be my second self, and best earthly companion.” Jane’s reply is the novel’s most famous assertion of female equality: “I am no bird; and no net ensnares me: I am a free human being with an independent will.” She accepts but insists on equality, not rescue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The scene ends with a gathering storm and a bolt of lightning splitting the great chestnut tree. Brönte uses pathetic fallacy to hint that the proposal rests on shaky ground. The split tree is later revealed as a symbol of the split couple: Bertha is alive and Rochester is already married.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Through this section of the novel, Bertha remains hidden. She laughs at night, sets fire to Rochester’s bed, attacks her brother Mason with a knife, and tears Jane’s wedding veil before the ceremony. Brönte uses her <dfn class="term" data-def="A ghost-like or doubled figure who represents the hidden self of another character.">doppelgänger</dfn> status deliberately: Bertha acts out the anger Jane cannot voice. Critics Gilbert and Gubar later called her “the madwoman in the attic” — a figure that became shorthand for the suppression of Victorian female rage.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Thornfield is a Gothic shell: locked storey, strange laughter, fire and a split tree all plant the secret of Bertha Mason.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Jane and Rochester’s relationship is built on intellectual equality — his Byronic darkness matters less than the fact that he listens.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The proposal scene ends in storm and split tree — Brönte uses pathetic fallacy to foreshadow the disaster of the interrupted wedding.</li>
</ul>
