--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Character Analysis Means in Jane Eyre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Strong OCR answers on <em>Jane Eyre</em> treat characters as Brönte’s tools rather than real people. Each character is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character used by the author to embody an idea, belief or social argument.">vehicle</dfn> for an idea about gender, class or faith. Your job is to analyse what she does with them: what quality they carry, how that quality changes, and what Brönte argues by showing us that change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and write out the three-part structure every character paragraph must follow in a top-band answer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A character paragraph needs three moves: (1) the writer’s choice (‘Brönte presents…’), (2) a short embedded quotation, (3) a link to Victorian context or theme. Miss any one and the paragraph drops a band.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Jane: an Arc in Five Settings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Jane’s development is mapped onto the five main settings. At <strong>Gateshead</strong> she is the angry child (“I am not deceitful”); at <strong>Lowood</strong> she is the disciplined pupil learning from Helen and Miss Temple; at <strong>Thornfield</strong> she finds voice (“I am no bird”); at <strong>Moor House</strong> she is the independent heiress (“I care for myself”); at <strong>Ferndean</strong> she is the chosen equal (“Reader, I married him”). Treating the novel as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A novel that traces the moral and psychological growth of a single character.">Bildungsroman</dfn> lets you link geography to growth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A useful revision task is to match one short quotation to each stage. Keep the quotations short — examiners reward embedded quotation over block quotes. Four words, accurately placed, beats a forty-word block quote.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Rochester begins as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A Romantic hero: brooding, charismatic, morally compromised, with a troubled past.">Byronic hero</dfn> — brooding, sardonic, secretive. Brönte uses him to interrogate Victorian ideas of male dominance. Track him in three phases: the mysterious master (Thornfield), the would-be bigamist (the interrupted wedding), and the humbled husband (Ferndean). He is never simply redeemed; rather he is <em>rebalanced</em>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Useful quotations: “Bigamy is an ugly word”; “You have saved my life”; “my old hope of life is returning”. Each corresponds to a different phase of his arc. Always attribute Rochester’s change to Brönte’s authorial purpose, not to his own decision.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Bertha: the Madwoman in the Attic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Bertha Mason is often treated as plot-furniture. She is actually Brönte’s most charged character. She is Jane’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A doubled figure whose behaviour mirrors or externalises another character's hidden self.">doppelgänger</dfn>: every time Jane represses rage, Bertha acts it out — the torn veil, the fires. She is also a figure of colonial violence: Brönte inherits and perpetuates Victorian language about Creole women that modern criticism (notably Jean Rhys and Gayatri Spivak) has interrogated.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A top-band candidate handles Bertha by holding two readings together: what Brönte intends (Bertha as Rochester’s past coming home) and what she accidentally reveals (the silencing of a Creole woman to secure a white heroine’s happiness). Both are legitimate; neither cancels the other.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">St John Rivers: Cold Duty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">St John is not villainous but <em>wrong for Jane</em>. Brönte uses him to show a second kind of temptation: a marriage of duty without desire. His “cold, cumbrous column” description is a physical metaphor for moral rigidity. Track his three roles: rescuer, cousin, suitor. Each role tests Jane’s autonomy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the three-tier religious scheme Brönte uses in the novel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brönte builds a three-tier religious scheme: Brocklehurst (hypocritical), Helen (patient), St John (rigorous). None of them is a model for Jane, who creates a fourth model — faith with love — through her marriage.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Minor Characters as Moral Instruments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Helen Burns, Miss Temple, Mrs Reed, Grace Poole and Adèle all play small but weighted roles. The most useful technique is to ask: what does this character reveal when placed next to Jane? Helen’s patience exposes Jane’s fire; Mrs Reed’s cruelty sharpens Jane’s speech; Adèle’s vanity offers a child-version of Blanche’s adult shallowness. Brönte writes no minor character carelessly — each serves the novel’s argument.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Use a <dfn class="term" data-def="Point-Evidence-Analysis-Link: a four-move paragraph that finishes by tying analysis back to the question.">PEAL</dfn> template. Open with Brönte’s intention; embed a quotation; zoom in on a single word; lift to Victorian context; link back to the question. The key difference from basic PEE or PEA is that L — the link — reminds the examiner you are still answering the original prompt.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Avoid biographical paragraphs (‘Brönte’s father was a clergyman’) unless the biography directly illuminates the line you quoted. Otherwise it becomes a detour that costs you time and earns no marks.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Building a Quotation Bank by Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">For every main character, aim to memorise four short quotations — one for each major stage. For Jane: ‘I am not deceitful’ (Gateshead), ‘soulless and heartless’ (Thornfield), ‘I am no bird’ (proposal), ‘Reader, I married him’ (ending). For Rochester: ‘Bigamy is an ugly word’ (wedding), ‘my old hope… is returning’ (Ferndean). For St John: ‘a cold, cumbrous column’ and ‘formed for labour, not for love’. Building these tiny banks per character means that in the exam you can pull an accurate phrase from memory without panicking about long recitations.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Treat every character as Brönte’s vehicle for an idea, not as a real person — always use the authorial voice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Jane’s development maps onto the novel’s five settings; pick one short quotation per setting for your revision bank.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Hold two readings of Bertha together: Brönte’s intended plot function and the postcolonial critique of her silencing.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">When writing about characters, never describe them without analysing Brönte’s choices. The phrase ‘Brönte presents Jane as…’ should appear in your topic sentence every time. Follow with a short embedded quotation and a context link. This three-move pattern, repeated consistently, is what separates middle and top band responses at OCR.</p>
