--- DESCRIPTION ---
The Thornfield fire, a blinded Rochester, and Jane’s return to marry him as his equal.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Return to Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Jane travels back from Moor House to find Thornfield a “blackened ruin.” The Gothic house that once hid Bertha has been destroyed by fire. Brönte handles the reveal slowly: Jane asks an innkeeper what has happened, and he tells her that Bertha set the fire, stood on the battlements “waving her arms… and shouting out”, and jumped to her death. Rochester tried to save her and lost his sight and a hand in the flames. The physical destruction of Thornfield is a structural <dfn class="term" data-def="A cleansing or destructive process that allows moral renewal.">purgation</dfn>: nothing of the old secret remains.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain, in one sentence, why Brönte destroys Thornfield before Jane returns to Rochester.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Thornfield has to burn so that Rochester has no legal wife, no grand house, and no means of domination. Brönte engineers a level playing field — Jane’s independence now meets Rochester’s vulnerability.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ferndean: a Humbler Setting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Rochester is now at Ferndean, a small, damp house deep in the woods. Jane arrives at dusk and watches him from behind a tree. He is described in pitiful terms: “His form was of the same strong and stalwart contour as ever: his port was still erect, his hair was still raven black… but in his countenance I saw a change.” Brönte uses the Gothic setting differently now — not to hide secrets but to mark humility. The wilderness matches Rochester’s reduced state.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Jane announces herself and Rochester at first doubts his hearing. Brönte lets her play quietly with him, asking questions, refilling his glass. The comic tenderness is deliberate. For the first time in the novel, Jane leads the conversation: she is the arriving figure, he is the dependant.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Critics have argued that Rochester is punished through his blindness and injury. Brönte does not frame it as punishment but as a <dfn class="term" data-def="The lowering of a proud or powerful person's status to bring moral equality.">humbling</dfn>. Rochester admits: “I have known you, Jane, only a few minutes, and already I feel my old hope of life is returning.” His new dependence on Jane rebalances their relationship. Where once he tested her, now she guides him.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">He also admits a spiritual change. He describes a moment of despairing prayer in which he called out “Jane! Jane! Jane!” — the very cry Jane heard across the moor. Brönte leaves the supernatural frame unexplained but emotionally insisted: their souls have called to each other.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Marriage of Equals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The novel’s famous final chapter opens: “Reader, I married him.” The direct address, active verb and short sentence give Jane complete control of her own story. Brönte has spent a whole novel earning the simplicity of that line. Jane is rich, related to the Rivers, and legally free of any claim on her; Rochester is blind, dependent, and legally widowed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Jane describes the marriage in language of <dfn class="term" data-def="Treating two partners as moral and spiritual equals within a marriage.">mutuality</dfn>: “I am my husband’s life as fully as he is mine… We are precisely suited in character — perfect concord is the result.” Brönte uses the syntactic balance of the clauses to mirror the balance she claims between them. Note that Jane narrates the marriage — it is her voice that carries the reader into the happy ending.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write out the famous opening line of the final chapter. Why do examiners love candidates who quote it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>‘Reader, I married him’ is the novel’s most quoted line because it compresses the whole argument: Jane is the agent, the reader is her equal, and marriage is her active choice rather than her fate.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Closing Paragraphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">After two years, Rochester’s sight partially returns — enough to see their first son. Brönte ties up the story carefully: Adèle is placed in a kinder school; Diana and Mary marry well; and the novel ends with St John’s letters from India. His final words — “Amen; even so come, Lord Jesus!” — give the book a religious closing note, honouring his sincerity while the plot rewards Jane’s.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Brönte ends by including rather than erasing St John. The novel has three models of faith — Brocklehurst’s hypocrisy, Helen’s patience, St John’s rigour — and only Helen and St John survive into the closing pages. Jane’s marriage is the fourth model: faith lived through <dfn class="term" data-def="Giving love and honesty their proper place in a Christian life.">affectionate honesty</dfn>. It is no accident that all three other Christian figures are memorialised but none is presented as her equal.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Modern readers often question Bertha’s death. Her suicide conveniently frees Rochester for Jane. Critics including Jean Rhys (whose <em>Wide Sargasso Sea</em>, 1966, retells Bertha’s story) have argued that Brönte’s happy ending is built on a colonial silencing. A good essay acknowledges the discomfort without dismissing Brönte’s own moral scheme: she is writing against Rochester’s old life, but also within the 1847 conventions of Victorian reader expectation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why the Ending Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Jane Eyre ends as a novel about <em>return</em> rather than rescue. Brönte has insisted that Jane must become financially independent, morally tested, and emotionally ready before she can marry. The happiness of the final chapter is only possible because every earlier setting — Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, Moor House — has been walked through to its moral end. The Bildungsroman is complete.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Thornfield’s destruction by fire clears the legal and physical obstacles between Jane and Rochester — a structural purgation before reunion.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">At Ferndean, Rochester is humbled and Jane leads. The ‘voice on the moor’ is reframed as their mutual call.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">‘Reader, I married him’ completes Brönte’s Bildungsroman: Jane’s return is a marriage of equals, earned through independence, testing and choice.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Great answers on the ending avoid two traps: treating the fire as happy-accident plot convenience, and treating Rochester’s injuries as authorial punishment. Brönte does both for a clear moral purpose — to equalise Jane and Rochester. Use the phrase ‘Brönte humbles Rochester so Jane can meet him as an equal’ as your structural argument when writing about the closing chapters.</p>
