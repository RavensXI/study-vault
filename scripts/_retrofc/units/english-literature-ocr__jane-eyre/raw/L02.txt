--- DESCRIPTION ---
Jane’s abuse at Gateshead, the horrors of Lowood, and the friendships that forge her character.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Opening: Jane at Gateshead</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Brönte begins <em>Jane Eyre</em> with the line, “There was no possibility of taking a walk that day.” The weather is cold and stormy — a deliberate piece of <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of weather or landscape to reflect the emotion of a character or scene.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>. It sets the atmosphere for Jane’s life at Gateshead: confined, watched and unwelcome. Jane introduces herself by retreating behind a curtain with a book — her first act in the novel is to seek refuge in reading, establishing her inner life as her only freedom.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and write in one sentence why Brönte chose to open with Jane hiding behind a curtain with a book.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The opening image — Jane hidden behind a curtain, reading — announces her central conflict: a mind seeking freedom inside a body surrounded by hostility. The whole novel is the story of that mind finding its voice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">John Reed and the Red Room</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">John Reed, Jane’s cousin, strikes her for daring to read “his” books. Brönte presents him as the tyranny of privilege in miniature: “You have no business to take our books… you are a dependant.” Jane fights back — “my habitual mood of humiliation… was gone” — and is punished by being locked in the red room. The red room is more than a punishment; it is where she later suffers what seems a supernatural experience and faints.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The red room is a powerful symbol. It is associated with blood (violence, menstruation, female suffering) and with Uncle Reed’s death. Critics call rooms like this <dfn class="term" data-def="A space in a Gothic novel that traps a female character and reflects her oppression.">Gothic confinement</dfn>. Jane’s resistance to unfair punishment begins here — her willingness to speak out against injustice is the thread that runs through the whole novel.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Before Jane leaves for Lowood, she tells her aunt: “I am not deceitful: if I were, I should say I loved you; but I declare I do not love you.” The declarative, adult syntax in the mouth of a ten-year-old is deliberately jarring. Brönte lets Jane speak in a voice too old for her, staking a claim to <dfn class="term" data-def="The right to speak and act for oneself as a person of equal value.">moral agency</dfn>. Mrs Reed is shocked because Victorian children were expected to be silent, not to pass judgment on adults.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">This scene also plants an important idea: truth-telling is dangerous for women. Brönte will return to it in Jane’s later refusal to lie to Rochester and her later resistance to St John Rivers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Arrival at Lowood</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Lowood School is modelled on Cowan Bridge, the charity school that killed two of Brönte’s own sisters. It is run by Mr Brocklehurst, a hypocritical Evangelical clergyman who preaches austerity to the girls while his own family wear “false French curls”. He tells Miss Temple that the girls should not be fed “the fragile body” and that privation will save their souls. Brönte uses Brocklehurst to attack Victorian <dfn class="term" data-def="Claiming virtues you do not possess, usually in a religious setting.">religious hypocrisy</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The school’s cruelty is physical. The girls walk two miles through “benumbing” cold to church, then stand in the wind; they are served burnt porridge and thin slices of bread; they are punished for small “infractions of the rules.” Brocklehurst publicly humiliates Jane by making her stand on a stool and calling her “a liar” in front of the whole school.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Helen Burns: Patience and Resistance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Jane’s first real friend, Helen Burns, is her moral opposite. While Jane is fiery and combative, Helen accepts unjust punishment with Christian patience: “It is not violence that best overcomes hate… but reading the New Testament.” Brönte does not make her choice simple. Jane thinks Helen is too passive, but Helen’s calmness gives Jane a model of integrity to balance against her own temper.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain, in one sentence, what Helen Burns adds to Jane’s character that Jane cannot learn alone.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Helen Burns models Christian forbearance: holding to truth without violent resistance. Jane’s later ability to leave Rochester without fury — and to return on her own terms — owes much to Helen’s example.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Helen dies of consumption after a typhus epidemic sweeps the school, and Jane sleeps beside her in her final hours. Helen’s last words — “I am going to God” — introduce the novel’s strand of sincere, interior faith, contrasted with Brocklehurst’s performance. After Helen’s death, reforms come to Lowood; Jane stays for six more years and rises to become a teacher.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Miss Temple and the shaping of Jane</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Miss Temple, the superintendent, is the first adult to treat Jane with respect. She listens to Jane’s account of Gateshead, investigates the lie Brocklehurst told, and publicly clears her name. Jane says she “had imbibed from her something of her nature and much of her habits.” When Miss Temple leaves to marry, Jane feels the “calm” of Lowood leave with her — prompting her to advertise for the governess position at Thornfield. Brönte uses this to structure the novel as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A novel that follows the moral and psychological growth of a character from youth to adulthood.">Bildungsroman</dfn>, showing each setting shaping Jane’s adulthood.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Gateshead establishes Jane’s central conflict: a passionate interior life inside a world that treats her as worthless.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Lowood is Brönte’s attack on Victorian religious hypocrisy; Helen Burns offers a Christian patience that tempers Jane’s fire.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Miss Temple and Helen shape the adult Jane — the novel is a Bildungsroman, with each setting contributing to her moral growth.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When writing on the early sections, resist the urge to retell Jane’s suffering. Examiners reward candidates who treat Brönte as an author making choices: why the red room, why Helen’s patience, why Brocklehurst’s hypocrisy? Use Victorian context (charity schools, treatment of dependants, religious hypocrisy) to sharpen, not replace, your close reading.</p>
