--- DESCRIPTION ---
The interrupted wedding, Bertha revealed, Jane’s refusal to stay as Rochester’s mistress.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Morning of the Wedding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The wedding day arrives with Brönte tightening every thread she has laid. Jane dresses in plain white and writes of “a sense of something different” — a foreboding that goes beyond bridal nerves. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The suggestion of later events through earlier hints, objects or images.">foreshadowing</dfn> of the split chestnut, the torn veil and Bertha’s laughter has prepared both Jane and the reader for disaster. The language of the wedding itself is understated: Rochester hurries her to the church “without pausing”, and Jane is led rather than walks.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the three Gothic warnings Brönte plants before the wedding scene.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Brönte plants three Gothic warnings before the wedding: the split chestnut after the proposal, Bertha tearing the wedding veil, and Rochester’s hurried manner. The scene that follows is not a shock but an inevitable consequence of secrets long hidden.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Interruption at the Altar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">At the critical moment, the solicitor Mr Briggs and Richard Mason step forward. Briggs announces: “I affirm and can prove that Edward Fairfax Rochester was, on the 20th of October… married to my sister, Bertha Antoinetta Mason… She is now living.” The formal, legal register is deliberately flat. Brönte chooses law over emotion because what has happened is the exposure of a legal crime: <dfn class="term" data-def="The crime of marrying one person while already being legally married to another.">bigamy</dfn>. Rochester’s exclamation — “Bigamy is an ugly word!” — half-admits the charge.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Rochester takes the wedding party up to the third storey. Bertha is described as “a figure ran backwards and forwards… it grovelled, seemingly, on all fours; it snatched and growled like some strange wild animal.” The animal imagery is disturbing, and modern readers are right to note its <dfn class="term" data-def="The study of how texts reflect and reproduce ideas about race and empire.">colonial</dfn> overtones: Bertha is Creole, from the Caribbean, and Brönte’s language is shaped by nineteenth-century racial anxieties that later critics have sharply interrogated.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Rochester justifies himself in a long speech: his father arranged the marriage for Bertha’s dowry; she became “mad” and violent; he has kept her locked up for years. Jane listens in silence. Brönte does not ask us to condemn Rochester outright — but she also refuses to absolve him. His wealth comes from Bertha’s fortune; his freedom has been bought by her imprisonment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Jane’s Moral Crisis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">After the revelation, Rochester begs Jane to stay as his mistress: they could live together in the south of France where no one would know. He argues that his marriage is a legal fiction and that their love is real. Jane’s internal monologue becomes the novel’s moral centre: “Laws and principles are not for the times when there is no temptation: they are for such moments as this…” She chooses principle over feeling, even though she loves him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Brönte intensifies the pressure with a famous image: “I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself.” Jane’s refusal is not cold. Her prose breaks into exclamations and second-person address — “Reader, it was irksome” — showing how close she is to giving way. The victory is hard-won.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain, in one sentence, why Brönte has Jane leave with nothing rather than take any of Rochester’s money.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jane leaves with no money, no plan and no help from Rochester. Brönte makes the choice absolute: Jane’s independence is worthless if it is funded by the man she refuses. Moral agency must be material as well as spiritual.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Flight from Thornfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Jane leaves Thornfield at dawn, before Rochester wakes, and takes a coach as far as her small purse allows. She is dropped on Whitcross — an exposed crossroads on the moor — and walks for days without food or shelter. Brönte uses the moor as a landscape of moral testing: “Whitcross is no town, nor even a hamlet; it is but a stone pillar set up where four roads meet.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="Using landscape or setting to echo or mirror a character's inner state.">pathetic fallacy</dfn> puts Jane’s inner emptiness into visible form.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Jane is reduced to begging and is finally taken in by the Rivers family. Her choice has cost her everything except her self-respect. Brönte uses this physical deprivation to make a moral argument: virtue under these conditions is more meaningful than easy virtue under comfort.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Religion, law and Victorian marriage</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In the 1840s, Victorian women had few legal rights within marriage. The doctrine of <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal doctrine under which a married woman's legal rights and property merged with her husband's.">coverture</dfn> meant that a wife’s property, body and legal identity belonged to her husband. A married man could not divorce his wife on grounds of insanity. Readers in 1847 would understand that Rochester has no legal escape — and that Jane’s refusal to be his mistress is also a refusal of the only role Victorian society would allow her if she stayed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Why the Scene Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The interrupted wedding is the novel’s structural hinge. Up to this point the narrative has moved towards union; after it, the narrative splits Jane and Rochester apart for the whole of Volume III. The decision to leave is not a plot detour but the moral engine of the book. Jane only returns once she is his equal in law, money and conscience.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The interrupted wedding is prepared through Gothic foreshadowing and delivered in legal, rather than emotional, language.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Jane’s refusal to be Rochester’s mistress shows that Brönte values moral principle above romantic feeling.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Jane leaves with nothing — Brönte insists that independence must be material and spiritual, not bought with a man’s money.</li>
</ul>
