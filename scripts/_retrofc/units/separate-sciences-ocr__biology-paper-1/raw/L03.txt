--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Cellular Respiration?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A universal chemical process occurring continuously in all living cells that breaks down glucose to transfer energy, supplying ATP for life processes.">Cellular respiration</dfn> is a chemical process that occurs <strong>continuously</strong> in every living cell — animals, plants, fungi and bacteria all respire. It is not the same as breathing (ventilation) — respiration is a chemical reaction inside cells that transfers energy from glucose to a molecule called <dfn class="term" data-def="Adenosine triphosphate — the molecule that stores and transfers energy within cells for biological processes.">ATP</dfn>. This energy drives all life processes, from muscle contraction and nerve impulse transmission to protein synthesis and active transport.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Respiration is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, usually as heat, causing the temperature of the surroundings to increase.">exothermic</dfn> reaction — it transfers energy to the surroundings, which is why your body generates warmth. This transferred energy is vital: without it, cells could not carry out any of their functions.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Do NOT confuse respiration with breathing. Respiration is a chemical reaction inside cells that transfers energy from glucose to ATP. Always say 'transfers energy' rather than 'produces' or 'creates' energy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cellular respiration is a universal, exothermic chemical process that occurs continuously in all living cells. It transfers energy from glucose to ATP. It is NOT the same as breathing.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Aerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Aerobic respiration</strong> uses oxygen and occurs in the <strong>mitochondria</strong>. It produces a large yield of ATP, making it the most efficient form of respiration. This is why organisms with high energy demands (like mammals) rely primarily on aerobic respiration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The word equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} + \text{energy (ATP)}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The balanced symbol equation: \(C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Glucose is completely broken down in aerobic respiration. All the chemical energy stored in the glucose molecule is transferred — some to ATP for cellular work, and some as heat to the surroundings. This complete breakdown is why aerobic respiration releases much more ATP than anaerobic respiration.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Anaerobic Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Anaerobic respiration</strong> occurs when oxygen is <strong>not available</strong> or in short supply — for example, during intense exercise when blood cannot deliver oxygen fast enough to muscles. It takes place in the <strong>cytoplasm</strong> and produces much less ATP than aerobic respiration because glucose is only partially broken down.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">In animal cells: \(\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{lactic acid}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The incomplete breakdown of glucose produces <dfn class="term" data-def="A waste product of anaerobic respiration in animal cells that builds up in muscles during intense exercise, causing fatigue and cramps.">lactic acid</dfn>, which builds up in muscles during intense exercise, causing cramps and fatigue. After exercise, the body needs extra oxygen to break down the accumulated lactic acid — this is called the <strong>oxygen debt</strong>. You continue to breathe heavily after exercise to repay this debt: the extra oxygen is used to oxidise lactic acid in the liver, converting it back to glucose or carbon dioxide and water.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">In plants and fungi (including yeast): \(\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide}\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">This process is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Anaerobic respiration in yeast and some plant cells that produces ethanol and carbon dioxide from glucose.">fermentation</dfn>. It is used commercially in <strong>brewing</strong> (yeast produces ethanol for alcoholic drinks) and <strong>bread-making</strong> (yeast produces carbon dioxide that makes dough rise; the ethanol evaporates during baking).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">The rate of respiration can be investigated using <strong>yeast</strong> in glucose solution or <strong>germinating peas</strong>. You can test for the products of respiration experimentally: breathing out through <strong>limewater</strong> turns it milky, confirming the presence of carbon dioxide. Using a <strong>temperature probe</strong> in a flask of germinating seeds or active yeast shows a temperature increase over time, confirming that respiration is exothermic.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">For yeast fermentation, seal the flask with a bung connected to limewater via a delivery tube. As yeast respires anaerobically, carbon dioxide bubbles through the limewater, turning it milky. The rate at which this occurs can be compared across different sugar concentrations or temperatures.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration in a table: conditions (oxygen/no oxygen), location (mitochondria/cytoplasm), products, and ATP yield (large/small). This is a classic 4-mark compare question.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aerobic respiration (with oxygen, in mitochondria) produces a large yield of ATP. Anaerobic respiration (without oxygen, in cytoplasm) produces much less ATP. In animals it makes lactic acid; in fungi and plants it makes ethanol and carbon dioxide.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Synthesis of Biological Molecules</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Respiration links to the synthesis and breakdown of key biological molecules. You need to understand the role of sugars, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol as building blocks.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Building Carbohydrates, Proteins and Lipids</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Carbohydrates:</strong> Simple sugars (<dfn class="term" data-def="A small, single molecule that can bond with other monomers to form a polymer. Examples include simple sugars, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol.">monomers</dfn>) such as glucose join together to form complex carbohydrates (polymers such as starch and glycogen). Glucose is the key monomer used in respiration. Starch is the storage form of glucose in plants; glycogen is the storage form in animals (stored in liver and muscles).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Proteins:</strong> Amino acid monomers join together in specific sequences (determined by DNA) to form protein polymers. Proteins are essential for enzymes, structural components (collagen, keratin), hormones (insulin) and antibodies. The sequence and type of amino acids determines the protein's shape and function.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Lipids:</strong> Formed from <dfn class="term" data-def="Organic acid molecules with a long hydrocarbon chain; combined with glycerol to form lipids (fats and oils).">fatty acids</dfn> and glycerol. Lipids are used for long-term energy storage (they contain more energy per gram than carbohydrates), thermal insulation, and making cell membranes. Lipids are not polymers in the strict sense because they are not made of repeating identical monomers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The energy transferred by respiration is used both to build these molecules and to break them down. For example, energy from respiration drives the synthesis of proteins from amino acids, and the conversion of glucose to glycogen for storage. When the body needs energy, glycogen is broken back down into glucose for respiration.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Glucose is the key monomer: joins to form starch (plant storage) or glycogen (animal storage). Amino acids join to form proteins. Fatty acids + glycerol form lipids. All these reactions require energy from respiration to proceed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Glucose monomers form starch (plant) or glycogen (animal) by polymerisation. Amino acids form proteins. Fatty acids and glycerol form lipids. Energy from respiration drives the synthesis of all these biological molecules.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Exercise and Respiration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">During exercise, muscles need more energy, so the rate of respiration increases. The body responds by increasing <strong>heart rate</strong> (to deliver more oxygenated blood to muscles), increasing <strong>breathing rate and depth</strong> (to take in more oxygen and remove more carbon dioxide), and breaking down <strong>glycogen</strong> stored in muscles and the liver into glucose. If exercise is intense enough that blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, muscles switch to anaerobic respiration, producing lactic acid. After exercise stops, continued heavy breathing supplies extra oxygen needed to break down lactic acid in the liver — this is the <strong>oxygen debt</strong>.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n31">Cellular respiration is an exothermic process in all living cells that transfers energy from glucose to ATP. Aerobic respiration (mitochondria, with oxygen) produces a large ATP yield. Anaerobic respiration (cytoplasm, no oxygen) produces a small ATP yield — lactic acid in animals; ethanol + CO₂ in plants and fungi.</li><li data-narration-id="n32">Oxygen debt is the extra oxygen needed after intense exercise to oxidise accumulated lactic acid in the liver.</li><li data-narration-id="n33">Glucose monomers polymerise to form starch (plant storage) or glycogen (animal storage). Amino acids form proteins. Fatty acids and glycerol form lipids. Respiration provides the energy to build and break down these biological molecules.</li></ul>
