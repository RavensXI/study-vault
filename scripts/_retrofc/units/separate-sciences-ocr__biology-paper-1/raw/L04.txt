--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Photosynthesis?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which plants and algae use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. It occurs in chloroplasts.">Photosynthesis</dfn> is the process by which green plants and algae produce food. They are the main <strong>producers</strong> of biomass for life on Earth — almost all food chains begin with a photosynthetic organism. Photosynthesis takes place in the <strong>chloroplasts</strong>, which contain the green pigment <dfn class="term" data-def="The green pigment found in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.">chlorophyll</dfn> that absorbs light energy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Photosynthesis is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, usually as light, causing the temperature of the surroundings to decrease.">endothermic</dfn> reaction — it takes in energy from the surroundings in the form of light. This is the opposite of respiration, which is exothermic. The word equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{glucose} + \text{oxygen}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The balanced symbol equation: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Photosynthesis is a <strong>two-stage process</strong>. In the first stage, light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll and used to split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a by-product. In the second stage, the hydrogen from water is combined with carbon dioxide to produce glucose. Both stages occur in the chloroplasts.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Learn both word and symbol equations for photosynthesis. Remember it is endothermic (takes in light energy), the opposite of respiration which is exothermic.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction in chloroplasts: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\). Light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll. It is a two-stage process: light splits water, then hydrogen combines with CO\(_2\) to make glucose.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What Happens to the Glucose?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Glucose produced by photosynthesis has several important uses in the plant:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Respiration</strong> — glucose is broken down to transfer energy for the plant's life processes. <strong>Starch storage</strong> — excess glucose is converted to insoluble starch for storage (starch does not affect osmosis because it is insoluble). <strong>Cellulose</strong> — glucose is converted to cellulose for building cell walls. <strong>Amino acids and proteins</strong> — glucose is combined with nitrate ions absorbed from the soil to make amino acids, which are assembled into proteins. <strong>Lipids</strong> — glucose can be converted to lipids (fats and oils) for energy storage in seeds.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Limiting Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Three main factors affect the rate of photosynthesis: <strong>light intensity</strong>, <strong>carbon dioxide concentration</strong> and <strong>temperature</strong>. Each can become a <dfn class="term" data-def="A factor that prevents the rate of photosynthesis from increasing further, even if other conditions are favourable.">limiting factor</dfn> — the factor in shortest supply that prevents the rate from increasing.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Each Factor Affects the Rate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Light intensity</strong> — as light intensity increases, the rate increases because more energy is available for the light-dependent stage. At high intensity, the rate levels off because CO\(_2\) concentration or temperature becomes the limiting factor. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A mathematical relationship where one variable is proportional to the inverse of the square of another. For photosynthesis: light intensity is proportional to 1/distance squared.">inverse square law</dfn> applies: \(\text{light intensity} \propto \dfrac{1}{\text{distance}^2}\). Doubling the distance from a light source quarters the light intensity reaching the plant.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Carbon dioxide concentration</strong> — increasing CO\(_2\) provides more raw material for the carbon fixation stage, so the rate increases. Eventually, the rate plateaus when another factor (light or temperature) becomes limiting. In commercial greenhouses, farmers pump in extra CO\(_2\) to maximise crop yields.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Temperature</strong> — enzymes controlling photosynthesis work faster as temperature increases up to an optimum. Above this, enzymes denature and the rate drops sharply. Below the optimum, molecules have less kinetic energy and collide less frequently with enzyme active sites.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n16">The three factors interact (Higher Tier). On a graph of rate vs. light intensity, you might see the rate plateau even though light is still increasing — this means CO\(_2\) or temperature is now the limiting factor. To increase the rate further, you would need to increase whichever factor is limiting. All three must be sufficient for the maximum rate to be achieved.</p>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="When interpreting rate graphs, identify the plateau. If increasing light no longer raises the rate, then CO₂ or temperature is the limiting factor. State which factor is limiting and explain why.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A limiting factor is the one in shortest supply that prevents the rate from increasing further. On a graph, it shows as a plateau. The three limiting factors (light, CO\(_2\), temperature) interact — all must be sufficient for maximum rate. The inverse square law: doubling distance quarters light intensity.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Investigating Photosynthesis (PAG B5)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A common practical uses <strong>Elodea</strong> (pondweed). Place the pondweed underwater in a beaker with a lamp nearby and count oxygen bubbles produced per minute at different distances from the lamp. As the lamp moves further away, light intensity decreases (inverse square law) and fewer bubbles are produced, indicating a lower rate of photosynthesis.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">For more accurate results, collect the gas in a <strong>gas syringe</strong> or measuring cylinder over water and measure the volume per unit time, rather than counting individual bubbles. Repeat each distance at least three times and calculate a mean to improve reliability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">You can also test leaves for starch using <strong>iodine solution</strong>: boil the leaf in water to kill the cells, then in ethanol to remove chlorophyll (the green colour), rinse in water, and add iodine. Blue-black = starch present (photosynthesis occurred). Brown-orange = no starch (no photosynthesis). This test can show which parts of a variegated leaf photosynthesise — only the green parts.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Inverse Square Law — Worked Example</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">The inverse square law states: \(\text{light intensity} \propto \dfrac{1}{d^2}\), where d is the distance from the light source.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Example:</strong> A lamp is 10 cm from pondweed and the rate of photosynthesis is 40 bubbles/min. If the lamp is moved to 20 cm (double the distance), the light intensity drops to \(\frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4}\) of its original value. Expected new rate ≈ 10 bubbles/min.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">This relationship is important when interpreting experimental results — a graph of rate vs. distance should show a curved decline, not a straight line. A graph of rate vs. \(\frac{1}{d^2}\) should give a straight line through the origin if light is the only limiting factor.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction in chloroplasts: CO₂ + H₂O → glucose + O₂ (using light). Light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll. Glucose is used for respiration, starch storage, cellulose, amino acids and lipids.</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Three limiting factors control the rate: light intensity, CO₂ concentration and temperature. A plateau on a rate graph means another factor has become limiting. The inverse square law: light intensity ∝ 1/d².</li><li data-narration-id="n28">Practical: Elodea bubble-counting (PAG B5) investigates the effect of light intensity. The iodine starch test shows where photosynthesis has occurred in a leaf.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">For limiting factor graph questions, always identify the plateau first, then explain which factor is limiting at that point and what would need to change to increase the rate further. For the 4-mark calculate question on the inverse square law, show the formula, substitute, and state the correct units. For the starch test practical, the sequence is: boiling water → ethanol → water rinse → iodine — getting the order wrong loses marks.</p>
