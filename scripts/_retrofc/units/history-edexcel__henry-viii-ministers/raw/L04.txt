--- DESCRIPTION ---
Wolsey’s diplomacy: Treaty of London, Field of the Cloth of Gold, and the 1520s collapse.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Foreign policy was the area in which Wolsey most directly served Henry VIII&rsquo;s personal ambitions &mdash; and the area in which his successes were greatest, his failures most painful, and the historiographical debate most lively. Was Wolsey a Renaissance peacemaker who used diplomacy to make a second-rate kingdom matter in Europe? Or a warmonger who chased his master&rsquo;s glory until reality bit back? The truth is more interesting than either label.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Wolsey&rsquo;s Aims Abroad</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Wolsey served two sets of aims at once. Henry&rsquo;s aims were straightforward and old-fashioned: military glory, the recovery of English claims in France, and a male heir whose rule could not be threatened. Wolsey&rsquo;s own aims were more sophisticated &mdash; to position England as the <dfn class="term" data-def="In diplomacy, a state that holds the deciding weight between two larger powers, allowing it to influence outcomes by choosing sides.">balance</dfn> between the two great European powers, France under Francis I and the Habsburg empire under Charles V, so that England could broker peace and shape European affairs without bearing the full cost of war.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">There is also a third strand sometimes mentioned by historians: Wolsey&rsquo;s personal hope, encouraged by Pope Leo X&rsquo;s grant of legateship for life, that he might one day become Pope himself. Twice in the 1520s &mdash; in 1521 and 1523 &mdash; he was a candidate at papal conclaves, and twice the Habsburg interest blocked him. Whether or not this drove his diplomacy, the prospect coloured his relationships with Charles V and the papacy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: three aims of Wolsey&rsquo;s foreign policy and one detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wolsey&rsquo;s foreign policy aimed to satisfy Henry&rsquo;s desire for glory, to make England the broker between France and the Habsburgs, and (probably) to advance Wolsey&rsquo;s own ambition for the papal throne.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Treaty of London, 1518</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Wolsey&rsquo;s diplomatic masterpiece was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A peace treaty signed at London in October 1518 in which more than twenty European powers, including France, the Holy Roman Empire, the papacy and the German princes, pledged not to attack one another &mdash; brokered by Cardinal Wolsey.">Treaty of London</dfn> of October 1518. Pope Leo X had proposed a five-year truce among Christian princes to prepare a crusade against the Ottoman Turks, who had captured Belgrade and threatened central Europe. Wolsey turned the papal idea into something larger and more permanent. He summoned ambassadors of more than twenty European powers &mdash; France, the Holy Roman Empire, Spain, the papacy, the major Italian states, the German princes, the Swiss cantons &mdash; to London. They signed a non-aggression pact: any signatory who attacked another would face combined retaliation by the rest.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The treaty made Henry the public guarantor of European peace, and Wolsey personally the broker. The pope&rsquo;s plan had been overshadowed; Wolsey kept the credit. For a kingdom of two and a half million people sitting on the edge of Europe, it was a remarkable diplomatic coup. Judged by the realistic aim &mdash; making Henry a major European broker despite England&rsquo;s modest power &mdash; the treaty was a triumph.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Field of the Cloth of Gold, 1520</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Two years later, Wolsey staged perhaps the most spectacular diplomatic theatre of the sixteenth century. Between 7 and 24 June 1520, in a valley near Calais, Henry VIII and Francis I met for two and a half weeks of jousting, wrestling, music, banquets and Mass under a tent of cloth-of-gold thread. Around 6,000 English and 6,000 French courtiers attended; a temporary palace was constructed for Henry; great fountains ran with wine. The cost to England alone was approximately &pound;15,000 &mdash; about a fifth of annual royal revenue.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The lavish 1520 summit between Henry VIII and Francis I near Calais, named after the cloth-of-gold tents erected for the meeting; it staged Anglo-French amity but did not prevent later conflict.">Field of the Cloth of Gold</dfn> was a magnificent display, but it was theatre, not policy. Almost as soon as Henry returned to England he met Charles V at Gravelines, and within two years he was at war with France. The summit cemented Wolsey&rsquo;s reputation for spectacle but did nothing to alter the underlying Habsburg-Valois rivalry.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">This is a trap students fall into. Judged by what England could realistically achieve, the Treaty of London was a triumph &mdash; it made Henry a European arbiter for the first time since the Hundred Years War. The peace lasted four years before being undermined by the death of Maximilian (1519), Charles V&rsquo;s election as Emperor, and the renewal of Habsburg-Valois conflict in Italy. None of these factors was within Wolsey&rsquo;s control. The collapse was not a Wolsey failure; it was the structural condition of European politics.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Increasing Difficulties in the 1520s</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The 1520s steadily eroded the 1518 settlement. The election of Charles V as Holy Roman Emperor in 1519 created a single ruler over Spain, the Netherlands, Austria and parts of Italy &mdash; a Habsburg colossus that France could not tolerate. War broke out between Charles and Francis in 1521, and England was dragged in on the imperial side. Wolsey&rsquo;s 1522&ndash;23 invasion of France under the Duke of Suffolk reached as far as Montdidier &mdash; only fifty miles from Paris &mdash; before supply collapsed and the army turned home.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1525 the alliance with Charles V had also collapsed. After the Battle of Pavia, Charles refused to share the spoils with Henry, leading directly to the failed Amicable Grant. Wolsey then reversed England&rsquo;s alliance, signing the Treaty of the More with France (1525) and the League of Cognac (1526) to restrain Habsburg power. The diplomatic somersault was forced rather than chosen, and made England look unreliable. The 1527 Sack of Rome by Charles V&rsquo;s troops left Pope Clement VII in imperial custody &mdash; a disaster for Wolsey, since the pope&rsquo;s independence was essential for the planned annulment of Henry&rsquo;s marriage to Catherine of Aragon. By the late 1520s Wolsey&rsquo;s European balancing act had become a series of expensive course corrections, none of them yielding the male heir Henry actually wanted.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: three reasons why Wolsey&rsquo;s foreign policy ran into trouble in the 1520s.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wolsey&rsquo;s 1520s foreign policy ran into trouble because of Charles V&rsquo;s 1519 imperial election, the renewal of Habsburg-Valois war from 1521, and the 1527 Sack of Rome &mdash; all events outside his control but for which Henry held him responsible.</p>
</div>

Peter Gwyn&rsquo;s 1990 study argues that Wolsey was a serious diplomat working with very limited material; John Guy treats foreign policy as the area in which Wolsey was most clearly his own master, even when forced to deliver Henry&rsquo;s wars.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Wolsey&rsquo;s foreign policy aimed to win Henry military glory, balance England between France and the Habsburgs, and (probably) advance Wolsey&rsquo;s own bid for the papacy &mdash; ambitious goals for a kingdom of only 2.5 million people.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The 1518 Treaty of London was a major diplomatic success, signed by more than twenty European powers and making Henry the public guarantor of European peace; the 1520 Field of the Cloth of Gold was magnificent theatre but not policy.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">From 1519 onwards Charles V&rsquo;s imperial election, Habsburg-Valois war, the 1525 Pavia crisis and the 1527 Sack of Rome wrecked Wolsey&rsquo;s balancing act &mdash; setbacks largely caused by European structural change rather than Wolsey&rsquo;s own incompetence.</li></ul>
