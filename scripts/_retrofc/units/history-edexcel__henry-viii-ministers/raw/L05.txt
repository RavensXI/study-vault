--- DESCRIPTION ---
Henry’s hunt for an annulment, Clement VII’s refusal, and how Wolsey lost everything.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">By 1527 Henry VIII had decided that his marriage to Catherine of Aragon must end. Within two years that decision &mdash; known to contemporaries as the King&rsquo;s &lsquo;Great Matter&rsquo; &mdash; had wrecked Wolsey&rsquo;s career, brought England into open conflict with the papacy, and set the country on the road to the Reformation. The story is not just about Henry&rsquo;s desire for a male heir; it is about how the failure of one annulment broke the most powerful minister in early Tudor England.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Catherine of Aragon and the Succession Problem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Henry VIII&rsquo;s first wife, daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain, mother of the future Mary I, married to Henry from 1509 to 1533.">Catherine of Aragon</dfn> had been Henry&rsquo;s wife since 1509. She had borne him six children, but only one &mdash; Princess Mary, born 1516 &mdash; survived infancy. By the late 1520s Catherine was in her early forties and had not been pregnant since 1518. To Henry, the message was clear: there would be no male heir from this marriage. England had only ever been ruled by one queen regnant, the disastrous Empress Matilda in the 1140s, whose reign produced civil war. Henry believed a female successor would invite another <dfn class="term" data-def="The fifteenth-century civil wars between the Houses of Lancaster and York that ended with Henry Tudor&rsquo;s victory at Bosworth in 1485.">Wars of the Roses</dfn>.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">There was also a religious anxiety. Catherine had first been married to Henry&rsquo;s elder brother Arthur, who died in 1502. Pope Julius II had granted a special dispensation to allow her marriage to Henry. By 1527 Henry had convinced himself that this dispensation was invalid, citing the Old Testament book of Leviticus: &lsquo;If a man shall take his brother&rsquo;s wife, it is an unclean thing &hellip; they shall be childless.&rsquo; The lack of a son was, he argued, divine judgement on a marriage God had never sanctioned.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who was Catherine of Aragon, why did Henry believe the marriage was invalid, and which Bible passage did he cite?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Henry sought an annulment, not a divorce. He argued that his marriage to Catherine of Aragon &mdash; his late brother&rsquo;s widow &mdash; had been forbidden by Leviticus, and that Pope Julius II had no power to dispense with God&rsquo;s law. The lack of a male heir was his evidence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Why an &lsquo;Annulment&rsquo; and Not a &lsquo;Divorce&rsquo;?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Period vocabulary matters here. Henry did not ask for a divorce in the modern sense; he asked for an <dfn class="term" data-def="A papal declaration that a marriage was never legally valid in the first place, leaving both parties free to remarry.">annulment</dfn> &mdash; a declaration that the marriage had never been valid in the first place. Only the Pope could grant such a ruling. From 1527 Henry pressed Wolsey to obtain one. Wolsey, as Cardinal and papal <dfn class="term" data-def="A senior representative of the Pope sent to a country with delegated papal authority, often to hear major legal cases.">legate</dfn>, was the obvious instrument: he had handled English business with Rome for fifteen years and had the connections to make a discreet ruling possible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Pope Clement VII and the Sack of Rome</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Wolsey&rsquo;s timing could hardly have been worse. In May 1527, just weeks before Henry began his campaign, the unpaid army of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V mutinied and sacked Rome itself. Pope Clement VII fled to the Castel Sant&rsquo;Angelo and emerged a virtual prisoner of the emperor. Crucially, Charles V was Catherine of Aragon&rsquo;s nephew. Granting Henry his annulment would mean publicly humiliating Charles&rsquo;s aunt, just as Charles&rsquo;s troops controlled the papacy. Clement VII had every reason to delay and no political cover to decide.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Wolsey nevertheless persuaded Clement to send a second legate, Cardinal Campeggio, to England in 1528 to hear the case alongside him. Campeggio&rsquo;s instructions from Rome, however, were to delay rather than decide. He travelled slowly, arrived in October, and spent months trying to persuade Catherine to take the veil and retire to a convent. Catherine refused absolutely. She insisted, in public and in private, that her marriage to Arthur had never been consummated and that her marriage to Henry was therefore valid in every way.</p>

The historian Diarmaid MacCulloch emphasises that Cromwell held genuine reformist convictions; G.W. Bernard sees Henry himself driving the religious change. Either way, &lsquo;the divorce caused the Reformation&rsquo; is too simple.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Blackfriars Court, 1529</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In May 1529 the legatine court finally opened at Blackfriars in London. The proceedings were extraordinary: a Queen of England summoned to defend her marriage. Catherine appeared on 21 June, knelt at Henry&rsquo;s feet, and made a speech that became famous. She declared that she had been a true and obedient wife, that she had been a virgin when Henry first took her to bed, and that she could expect no impartial judgement in his realm. She then walked out of the court and refused to return. The case continued without her.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">Through July, Wolsey and Campeggio worked through the legal arguments. On 23 July, just as Wolsey expected to deliver judgement, Campeggio adjourned the court for the Roman summer recess. Days later, news arrived that Clement VII had revoked the case to Rome. The Blackfriars court would never reconvene. England&rsquo;s most powerful minister had been outmanoeuvred in front of his king.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what happened at Blackfriars, why Catherine walked out, and how Campeggio defeated Wolsey.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Blackfriars legatine court of May&ndash;July 1529 collapsed when Cardinal Campeggio adjourned proceedings for the Roman summer and Clement VII recalled the case to Rome. Wolsey had failed to deliver the only thing Henry now wanted.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Boleyn Faction and Wolsey&rsquo;s Enemies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By the time Blackfriars collapsed, Wolsey&rsquo;s position at court had already been undermined. <dfn class="term" data-def="Henry&rsquo;s second wife, mother of Elizabeth I, executed for treason in 1536; her family rose to dominate court politics in the late 1520s.">Anne Boleyn</dfn> had been Henry&rsquo;s preferred future queen since 1526 or 1527. Her father, Thomas Boleyn, and uncle, the Duke of Norfolk, led a faction at court that resented Wolsey&rsquo;s monopoly of patronage. They also blamed him personally for failing to deliver the annulment quickly. Anne herself believed &mdash; with some reason &mdash; that Wolsey had only paid lip-service to her cause and was secretly working for a French marriage instead.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Fall, October 1529</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Within weeks of the Blackfriars failure, Wolsey was finished. In October 1529 he was charged under the medieval statute of <em>praemunire</em> &mdash; technically, exercising foreign (papal) jurisdiction without royal consent. It was a transparent political device, but a legally watertight one: as papal legate, Wolsey had spent fifteen years doing exactly what the statute forbade. He surrendered the Great Seal, lost the chancellorship to Sir Thomas More, and forfeited Hampton Court and York Place to the king. He retreated to his archbishopric of York. In November 1530, summoned south on charges of treason, he died at Leicester Abbey on the journey, sparing Henry a public execution.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20">John Guy treats the failure of the annulment as the immediate, sufficient cause: Henry needed a son and Wolsey could not deliver. Eric Ives stresses Anne Boleyn&rsquo;s direct role in poisoning Henry&rsquo;s mind against the cardinal. C.S.L. Davies sees Wolsey&rsquo;s fall as the predictable end of a system in which all power flowed through one minister: when that minister failed once on a major issue, the whole edifice was vulnerable. The Amicable Grant of 1525 was the first warning shot; the failed annulment of 1529 was the killing blow.</p>
