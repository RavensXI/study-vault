--- DESCRIPTION ---
From ‘Defender of the Faith’ to Supreme Head: the statutes that broke England with Rome.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In 1521 Pope Leo X awarded Henry VIII the title <em>Fidei Defensor</em> &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="&lsquo;Defender of the Faith&rsquo; &mdash; a title granted to Henry VIII by Pope Leo X in 1521 for his book defending the seven sacraments against Martin Luther.">Defender of the Faith</dfn> &mdash; for a book Henry himself had written attacking Martin Luther. By 1534 the same monarch had broken with Rome, declared himself <dfn class="term" data-def="The title given to Henry VIII by the Act of Supremacy of November 1534, making him head of the Church in England in place of the Pope.">Supreme Head</dfn> of the Church in England, and was executing those who refused to swear it. The break with Rome is the most decisive constitutional change of the entire sixteenth century &mdash; and it is impossible to understand without seeing it as a chain of causes, not a single decision.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Defender of the Faith, 1521</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Henry&rsquo;s early reign was loudly Catholic. His 1521 treatise <em>Assertio Septem Sacramentorum</em> defended the seven sacraments and papal authority against Luther&rsquo;s Wittenberg theses of 1517. Pope Leo X rewarded the work with the new papal title. Henry kept it on his own coinage long after he had broken with Rome &mdash; an irony that points to a basic truth. Henry never thought of himself as a heretic. He believed England&rsquo;s religion was Catholic; he simply came to believe, by 1532, that an English king need not answer to a foreign Pope to be one.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Henry Moved Against the Pope, 1529&ndash;33</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The trigger was the failure of the annulment. After Blackfriars collapsed in 1529 and Pope Clement VII revoked the case to Rome, Henry concluded that no English answer could be obtained through Roman channels. From 1530 his ministers began to gather material for a different argument: that the king of England was an <em>imperium</em>, a self-sufficient sovereignty answerable to no foreign prince. The collection of patristic and historical texts that supported this argument was known as the <em>Collectanea satis copiosa</em> and was presented to Henry in 1530.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n6">A second motive was money. The English Church owned roughly a fifth of the country&rsquo;s landed wealth and paid annual fees to Rome. Cutting those fees and seizing the larger wealth that lay behind them would, in time, transform royal finance. A third motive was theological. A small but influential group at court &mdash; including Cromwell, Cranmer and Anne Boleyn herself &mdash; held genuine evangelical sympathies and saw the break with Rome as an opportunity for religious reform.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list four reasons Henry broke with Rome &mdash; one constitutional, one financial, one theological, one personal.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The break with Rome rested on four overlapping pressures: the failure of the annulment, the king&rsquo;s claim to imperial sovereignty, the wealth of the English Church, and the evangelical sympathies of Cromwell, Cranmer and Anne Boleyn. No single one explains it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Reformation Statutes, 1532&ndash;33</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">From 1532 Cromwell drafted a sequence of Acts that dismantled papal authority piece by piece. The Submission of the Clergy (May 1532) extracted from English bishops a promise to make no new canon law without royal assent. The Act in Conditional Restraint of Annates (1532) cut off the financial fees the bishops paid Rome on appointment. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (April 1533), drafted personally by Cromwell, declared that &lsquo;this realm of England is an empire&rsquo; and made it illegal to appeal any English legal case to Rome &mdash; including, crucially, Catherine of Aragon&rsquo;s defence of her marriage. Five weeks later Cranmer pronounced the marriage void.</p>

Cranmer wanted English-language scripture. Anne Boleyn was reading evangelical books from France and lobbying Henry on their merits. The fiscal opportunity of monastic land was already being mapped. G.W. Bernard&rsquo;s 2005 study <em>The King&rsquo;s Reformation</em> argues Henry himself drove change; Diarmaid MacCulloch credits Cromwell with the architectural design. Both views agree the divorce alone is insufficient.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Acts of Succession and Supremacy, 1534</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Two parliamentary statutes of 1534 finished the legal break. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1534 statute that recognised Princess Elizabeth as heir to the throne, declared Mary illegitimate, and required all subjects to swear an oath upholding the new succession.">Act of Succession</dfn> (March 1534) recognised Anne Boleyn&rsquo;s daughter Elizabeth as heir and declared Princess Mary illegitimate. Crucially, it required every adult subject to swear an oath upholding the new succession. To refuse the oath was treason. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1534 statute that declared the king of England the &lsquo;only Supreme Head on earth of the Church of England&rsquo;, ending papal authority in England.">Act of Supremacy</dfn> (November 1534) went further: it declared Henry &lsquo;the only Supreme Head on earth of the Church of England&rsquo;, ending papal authority and giving the crown power over doctrine, appointments and church revenues.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13">In December 1534, Parliament passed the Treason Act, which made it treason to deny in writing or speech the royal supremacy. From this point, refusing to acknowledge Henry&rsquo;s headship was a capital offence. Pope Paul III, who succeeded Clement VII in 1534, drafted a bull of excommunication against Henry &mdash; though he did not formally publish it until 1538.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what each of the three 1534 statutes (Succession, Supremacy, Treason) did, and how they fitted together.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1534 statutes worked as a system. The Act of Succession made an oath compulsory. The Act of Supremacy made the king head of the Church. The Treason Act made denial of these laws a capital crime. Together they gave Cromwell the legal weapons to enforce the break with Rome.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Cromwell&rsquo;s Enforcement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Cromwell did not simply draft these laws &mdash; he ran the machinery that made them work. As Vicegerent in Spirituals from 1535 he had authority over every English bishop. He ordered that the oath of supremacy be administered to clergy across the country and required bishops to preach against papal authority. He set up an information network that fed reports of dissident speech back to Westminster, and he issued royal injunctions in 1536 ordering parish priests to teach the Pater Noster, Creed and Ten Commandments in English.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">The teeth of the system were the treason laws. The most senior victim was Sir Thomas More, Lord Chancellor 1529&ndash;32, who refused the oath and was executed in July 1535. John Fisher, Bishop of Rochester, was executed two weeks earlier. Three Carthusian priors were hanged, drawn and quartered at Tyburn in May 1535 for the same offence. The deaths sent a clear message: the new church would not tolerate principled refusal, however senior the refuser.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Henry was made &lsquo;Defender of the Faith&rsquo; by Pope Leo X in 1521 for attacking Luther. He never abandoned the title or thought of himself as a heretic, even after breaking with Rome.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Between 1532 and 1533 Cromwell drafted statutes that ended papal jurisdiction in England piece by piece &mdash; the Submission of the Clergy, Act in Conditional Restraint of Annates, and Act in Restraint of Appeals.
