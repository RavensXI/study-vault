--- DESCRIPTION ---
Wolsey’s reforms of justice and finance: Star Chamber, subsidy and the Eltham Ordinances.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">As Lord Chancellor from 1515, Thomas Wolsey ran the daily business of England: he chaired the king&rsquo;s council, presided over the senior law courts, raised taxation, regulated wages and prices, and tried to reform the royal household. His domestic record is the most contested part of his career. To his admirers he was an energetic reformer who opened justice to the poor and modernised royal finance; to his critics he was a self-serving bully who stretched the law and stored up enemies. Both pictures contain truth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Justice: Chancery and Star Chamber</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Wolsey poured most of his judicial energy into two prerogative courts. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The senior royal court of equity, presided over by the Lord Chancellor, that judged cases on principles of fairness rather than the strict rules of common law.">Court of Chancery</dfn>, which he chaired as Lord Chancellor, judged cases on principles of fairness, or <dfn class="term" data-def="A branch of English law that allows judges to decide cases according to fairness rather than strict rules, used to provide remedies the common law could not.">equity</dfn>. It was attractive to litigants who could not afford the slow, expensive common-law courts: a widow cheated of her dower, a tenant evicted by an over-mighty landlord, a small landowner crushed by a powerful neighbour. Under Wolsey the volume of Chancery cases roughly doubled.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A royal court that met in a chamber decorated with stars at Westminster, used by the king&rsquo;s council to try cases that local courts could not handle, especially those involving powerful nobles.">Star Chamber</dfn>, which met at Westminster in a room decorated with gold stars, was Wolsey&rsquo;s instrument for disciplining the powerful. It dealt with cases the local common-law courts dared not touch &mdash; nobles enclosing common land, gentry intimidating juries, riots and corrupt officials. Wolsey personally heard around 120 cases a year, compared with about 12 a year under Henry VII. Famously, he made an example of the Earl of Northumberland in 1515 for retaining illegal armed followers, and humiliated Sir Amyas Paulet by forcing him to wait for hours at Wolsey&rsquo;s chamber door.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which two courts did Wolsey expand, and what did each one do?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wolsey expanded the Court of Chancery, which judged cases on equity, and the Star Chamber, which disciplined powerful nobles. Both bypassed the slow common-law courts and made justice more available to ordinary people.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">In the short term, yes. Cheap, fast equity drew thousands of cases and the Star Chamber genuinely curbed the worst noble abuses. In the longer term the system overheated &mdash; courts were swamped, common-law judges resented the encroachment, and Wolsey&rsquo;s personal style invited accusations of bias. Critics argued that he favoured petitioners willing to pay clerks generously. After his fall, the volume of Star Chamber business shrank.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Enclosure: The 1517 Commission</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The fencing or hedging of common fields and pastures into private holdings, often to convert arable land to sheep pasture, which displaced peasant farmers.">Enclosure</dfn> &mdash; the conversion of arable common land to sheep pasture &mdash; was a flashpoint of the period. Sir Thomas More attacked it bitterly in <em>Utopia</em> (1516): &ldquo;Your sheep, that were wont to be so meek and tame and so small eaters, now become so great devourers and so wild that they eat up and swallow down the very men themselves.&rdquo; In 1517 Wolsey launched a national commission to investigate enclosures since 1488. The commissioners gathered evidence in 28 counties and prosecuted 264 landowners in Chancery, ordering them to restore their land to tillage.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">The result was symbolic rather than transformative. Many cases dragged on for years; some landowners simply paid fines and continued enclosing. Yet Wolsey signalled to the Crown&rsquo;s subjects that the powerful would not be left unchecked, and to landowners that even great men were within the king&rsquo;s reach. By 1523 he had to compromise &mdash; abandoning enforcement to win Parliament&rsquo;s support for war taxation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Finance: The Subsidy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Henry&rsquo;s wars were ruinously expensive. To pay for them Wolsey overhauled royal taxation. The medieval &lsquo;fifteenth and tenth&rsquo; raised a fixed sum regardless of changing wealth and bore most heavily on the poor. Wolsey replaced it with the <dfn class="term" data-def="A new direct tax introduced under Wolsey from 1513 onwards that was based on the assessed value of an individual&rsquo;s wealth rather than fixed local quotas.">subsidy</dfn>, a tax assessed on each taxpayer&rsquo;s actual income from land, goods or wages. Tax commissioners visited each parish to record wealth, and the rate could be varied to match need. In 1523 Wolsey demanded an unprecedented &pound;800,000; Parliament resisted, and only around &pound;200,000 was eventually raised over four years &mdash; still a huge sum for the period.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12">The subsidy was a genuine fiscal innovation that survived into the seventeenth century. But it had limits. Each new round needed Parliament&rsquo;s consent, taxpayers learned to undervalue their property, and Wolsey&rsquo;s 1523 demand exhausted political goodwill. The 1525 Amicable Grant &mdash; covered in the next lesson &mdash; was a forced response when Parliament refused another subsidy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what was the subsidy, why was it different, and what was the most it raised?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Wolsey&rsquo;s subsidy taxed people on their actual wealth, replacing the flat-rate fifteenth and tenth. Between 1513 and 1529 it raised around &pound;325,000 &mdash; far more than any earlier system &mdash; but Parliament always controlled how much was granted.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Eltham Ordinances, 1526</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In January 1526 Wolsey issued the <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of household reforms drawn up by Wolsey in 1526 at Eltham Palace, restructuring the Privy Chamber and cutting unnecessary expenditure.">Eltham Ordinances</dfn>, a set of household regulations drafted at Eltham Palace. They reorganised the king&rsquo;s domestic establishment, cut waste, set wages and food allowances, and reduced the number of gentlemen of the Privy Chamber from twelve to six. Officially the aim was efficiency and economy in royal expenditure. Unofficially, contemporaries noticed something more pointed: the men ejected from the Privy Chamber happened to include several who were challenging Wolsey&rsquo;s influence over Henry, including William Compton, Henry Norris and the rising Boleyn faction.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Once the immediate political moment passed, many of the dismissed gentlemen drifted back to court. Historians like John Guy treat the Ordinances as a mixture of genuine administrative reform and factional self-defence &mdash; a textbook example of why Wolsey accumulated enemies even as he served the king well.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception &mdash; &lsquo;Wolsey&rsquo;s domestic reforms were a complete success&rsquo;</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Each reform was real but limited. The Star Chamber expansion overloaded itself. The 1517 enclosure commission produced prosecutions but few lasting changes on the ground. The subsidy raised more money but ran into political resistance. The Eltham Ordinances were quietly diluted within months. Wolsey was a serious reformer who repeatedly failed to make his reforms permanent &mdash; partly because Henry&rsquo;s priorities (war, prestige) kept overriding administrative consistency.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Wolsey expanded equity justice through the Court of Chancery and used the Star Chamber to discipline powerful nobles, hearing around ten times more cases than under Henry VII.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">His 1517 enclosure commission and the new subsidy tax (from 1513) modernised royal policy &mdash; the subsidy taxed real wealth rather than fixed quotas &mdash; but each ran into political resistance from landowners and Parliament.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">The 1526 Eltham Ordinances reorganised the royal household but also conveniently removed Wolsey&rsquo;s rivals from the Privy Chamber, showing how administrative reform and factional politics blurred under his ministry.</li></ul>
