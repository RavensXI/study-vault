--- DESCRIPTION ---
Cromwell’s rise from Putney to chief minister: how he replaced Wolsey by 1534.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In 1529, when Wolsey fell, no one expected Thomas Cromwell to rule England. He had no royal blood, no Cambridge degree, no church preferment, and a master who had just been disgraced. Five years later he was Henry VIII&rsquo;s most trusted minister, the engineer of the break with Rome, and arguably the most powerful commoner in English history. The story of how he got there is a story about loyalty, parliamentary skill, and a willingness to give the king what every other adviser said could not be done.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Putney Origins and Early Career</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Cromwell was born around 1485 in Putney, then a village outside London. His father Walter was variously a blacksmith, a brewer, a fuller and a frequent defendant in the local manor courts &mdash; not poor, but not respectable either. By his early twenties Thomas had left England for the continent. He spent time as a soldier in Italy, a clerk in a Florentine bank, a cloth merchant in the Low Countries, and learned Latin, Italian, French and the workings of European commerce in the process. These were not the qualifications of a Tudor minister; they were the qualifications of a man who would think differently from one.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Back in London by around 1514, Cromwell built a legal practice and entered Wolsey&rsquo;s service in the early 1520s. He worked on the cardinal&rsquo;s pet project: dissolving small monasteries to fund Wolsey&rsquo;s new colleges at Oxford and Ipswich. The work taught him three things he would later use on a national scale &mdash; how to value monastic property, how to draft Acts of Parliament, and how to manage royal commissioners on the ground. By 1529 he was Wolsey&rsquo;s most trusted secular agent.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: where was Cromwell born, what did he do on the continent, and which patron brought him into government?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cromwell rose from a Putney commoner background through trade, soldiering and law on the continent, then through service to Wolsey. He had no university degree and no church living &mdash; unusual for a Tudor minister.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Surviving Wolsey&rsquo;s Fall</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When Wolsey fell in October 1529, Cromwell looked finished. Most of the cardinal&rsquo;s servants scattered or hid. Cromwell did the opposite. He stayed at his master&rsquo;s side, organised the legal defence, and worked openly to limit the damage. He also &mdash; according to the contemporary chronicler George Cavendish &mdash; wept publicly for Wolsey, then took horse for Westminster to fight for a place in the new order.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The gamble paid off. Henry was impressed by the loyalty &mdash; loyalty to a fallen patron was, paradoxically, the best evidence that Cromwell would later be loyal to him. Within weeks Cromwell had secured election to the new <dfn class="term" data-def="The Parliament summoned by Henry VIII in November 1529, which sat in seven sessions until 1536 and passed the legislation of the break with Rome.">Reformation Parliament</dfn>, sitting first as MP for Taunton in November 1529. The seat was a back-bench borough; the platform it provided was not.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">From the Commons to the Council</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Through 1530 and 1531 Cromwell built his reputation in the Commons. He drafted bills, marshalled votes, and demonstrated a mastery of <dfn class="term" data-def="An Act of Parliament &mdash; a law passed by both Houses and given royal assent. Cromwell would make statute the central engine of Tudor government.">parliamentary statute</dfn> that no one in royal service could match. By the end of 1530 he was a sworn member of the Royal Council, attending its meetings and trusted with sensitive royal business. By April 1532 he was Master of the King&rsquo;s Jewels, controlling part of the royal finances. By 1533 he was Chancellor of the Exchequer.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">The promotions tracked his usefulness. Cromwell offered Henry something Wolsey, More and the bishops could not: a route to the annulment that did not run through Rome. The argument was statutory. If Parliament could declare that England was a self-sufficient empire whose king answered to no foreign prince, then no English court &mdash; not even Cardinal Campeggio&rsquo;s &mdash; needed papal permission to rule on the king&rsquo;s marriage. By 1532 this was an idea Henry was prepared to act on. Cromwell drafted the legislation that turned it into law.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">Henry VIII was not led by his ministers, but he was not their puppet-master either. Diarmaid MacCulloch&rsquo;s 2018 biography argues Cromwell shaped the legal architecture of the break with Rome and quietly promoted his own evangelical religious convictions, including the English Bible. Geoffrey Elton went further in 1953, calling Cromwell the architect of a &lsquo;Tudor revolution in government&rsquo;. Revisionists like John Guy and David Starkey have softened that claim &mdash; the changes were piecemeal, not a planned revolution &mdash; but few historians now treat Cromwell as merely the king&rsquo;s clerk.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Handling the Annulment, 1532&ndash;33</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Cromwell&rsquo;s breakthrough year was 1532. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1532 declaration extracted from the English clergy that they would make no new church laws without royal assent &mdash; the first major statutory step against papal authority.">Submission of the Clergy</dfn>, drafted under his direction, forced the English Church to accept that no canon law could be made without royal assent. In August 1532 Archbishop Warham died; Henry, on Cromwell&rsquo;s advice, appointed <dfn class="term" data-def="Archbishop of Canterbury from 1533, leading evangelical, drafter of the Book of Common Prayer; he annulled Henry&rsquo;s first marriage in May 1533.">Thomas Cranmer</dfn> &mdash; an evangelical sympathetic to the king&rsquo;s case &mdash; in his place. Anne Boleyn was already pregnant by January 1533. Cranmer pronounced Henry&rsquo;s marriage to Catherine void on 23 May 1533, his marriage to Anne valid five days later, and Anne was crowned on 1 June. The Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533), drafted by Cromwell, made it illegal to appeal any English legal case &mdash; including the annulment &mdash; to Rome.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Chief Minister, 1534</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">In April 1534 Cromwell was appointed Principal Secretary, an old office he transformed into the central coordinating role of Tudor government. By 1535 he was also Vicegerent in Spirituals &mdash; the king&rsquo;s deputy in church affairs, with authority over every bishop in England. From these two posts he ran domestic government, foreign correspondence, royal finances and church reform simultaneously. No previous minister had concentrated so many functions in one office. By the time of the Act of Supremacy (November 1534), Cromwell was the king&rsquo;s chief minister in everything but title.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: three offices Cromwell held by 1535 and what each one let him do.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1535 Cromwell was Principal Secretary, Master of the Rolls and Vicegerent in Spirituals. He coordinated government, finance, foreign correspondence and church reform from a single desk &mdash; a concentration of power without precedent.</p>
</div>

Diarmaid MacCulloch stresses Cromwell&rsquo;s evangelical conviction &mdash; not merely a careerist but a believer who used office to advance a religious cause. John Guy and David Starkey argue the rise was contingent and circumstantial: Cromwell happened to offer Henry the right solution at the right moment, and the constitutional changes were driven by short-term need rather than long-term plan. The truth probably combines all three: a gifted lawyer with a religious agenda, in the right place at the right time.</p>
