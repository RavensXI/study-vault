--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the 1525 Amicable Grant collapsed in revolt and revealed the limits of Wolsey’s power.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">In the spring of 1525 Wolsey demanded a non-parliamentary tax so unpopular that it triggered open revolt across East Anglia, forced Henry VIII to back down within weeks, and revealed for the first time that the cardinal&rsquo;s authority had limits. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A non-parliamentary forced loan demanded by Wolsey in 1525 to pay for an invasion of France, abandoned after popular resistance.">Amicable Grant</dfn> is a small episode with a large lesson: even at his height, Wolsey could not override the political nation when he asked too much.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Why Did Wolsey Need More Money?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In February 1525 the Holy Roman Emperor <dfn class="term" data-def="Holy Roman Emperor (1519&ndash;1556) and King of Spain, nephew of Catherine of Aragon, the dominant ruler of Habsburg Europe.">Charles V</dfn> won a sensational victory over Francis I of France at the battle of Pavia. The French king was captured, his army shattered, and France lay vulnerable. Henry VIII saw the moment for a long-cherished dream: to invade France, claim the French crown, and join Charles V in dismembering the kingdom. He demanded an army within months.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">The problem was money. Parliament had only just granted the 1523 subsidy, which was still being collected and had raised far less than hoped. Calling Parliament again so soon was politically impossible &mdash; MPs had openly resisted Wolsey&rsquo;s demands two years before. Wolsey therefore proposed a non-parliamentary tax, which he carefully labelled the &lsquo;Amicable Grant&rsquo; to suggest a friendly, voluntary contribution to the king&rsquo;s war.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in what year was the Amicable Grant demanded, why was it needed, and what was happening in France?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Amicable Grant was demanded in March 1525 to fund Henry&rsquo;s planned invasion of France after Charles V&rsquo;s victory at Pavia. Parliament had refused another subsidy, so Wolsey tried to bypass it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">What Was Demanded?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The terms were severe. Clergy were asked to pay one-third of their incomes; laymen one-sixth of theirs. There was no consultation, no parliamentary consent, and no upper or lower limit on individual assessments &mdash; commissioners simply fixed sums and demanded payment. Worse, this came on top of the still-unfinished 1523 subsidy. To many of Wolsey&rsquo;s subjects it looked like the same tax, demanded twice, by a minister who had decided he no longer needed Parliament&rsquo;s permission.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">Resistance began almost immediately. In Kent, when the commissioners arrived, taxpayers cited <dfn class="term" data-def="A historic English principle that taxation requires the consent of Parliament, dating back to Magna Carta in 1215.">Magna Carta</dfn> and refused to pay without parliamentary consent. In London the city merchants protested. The greatest trouble came in East Anglia, the centre of England&rsquo;s cloth industry, where economic recession and high unemployment meant taxpayers had nothing to give.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The East Anglian Revolt</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">By early May 1525, around 4,000 men had assembled at Lavenham in Suffolk in open revolt. They were led by John Greene and John Coxford, but the wider mood was one of communal resistance. The Dukes of Norfolk and Suffolk were sent north to confront the rebels; recognising that this was not a feudal rebellion against the king but a tax strike, the dukes opened negotiations rather than engaging in battle. The rebels insisted they were loyal to Henry &mdash; they would pay any tax legally granted by Parliament, but not this. The dukes had little choice but to agree.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">Within days of the rising, Henry VIII abruptly cancelled the Amicable Grant. He claimed in public that he had never authorised it &mdash; a transparent rewriting of the record &mdash; and that Wolsey alone bore responsibility. The contrast was stark: Henry kept the credit for clemency; Wolsey absorbed the blame.</p>

    <span>Misconception &mdash; &lsquo;The Amicable Grant was a peasants&rsquo; revolt&rsquo;</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">It was not. The leadership came from yeomen, clothiers and small employers, supported by clergy who themselves resented the one-third demand. The rebels invoked Magna Carta, refused to march on London, dispersed when the Grant was cancelled, and were not seriously punished. This was a tax revolt of the political nation &mdash; the people whose consent Tudor government depended on &mdash; not a populist uprising. That is precisely why it was so dangerous to Wolsey.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Consequences for Wolsey&rsquo;s Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The collapse of the Amicable Grant had three serious consequences. First, the planned invasion of France was abandoned. Henry never crossed the Channel in 1525, and within months Charles V signed the Treaty of Madrid with Francis I, leaving England with no return on Wolsey&rsquo;s preparations. The diplomatic humiliation was profound.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Second, Wolsey&rsquo;s reputation was permanently dented. The episode showed the political nation that the cardinal could be defied; it showed Henry that Wolsey could trigger crises; and it gave his enemies, especially the Duke of Norfolk and the rising Boleyn faction, a stick with which to beat him. The historian C.S.L. Davies argued that 1525 marks the beginning of Wolsey&rsquo;s political vulnerability, even though his fall lay four years away.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Third, the constitutional principle that taxation needed parliamentary consent was vividly reasserted. Subsequent Tudor governments &mdash; including Henry VIII himself in the Reformation Parliament of 1529&ndash;36 &mdash; would work through Parliament far more carefully. The Amicable Grant was, paradoxically, a moment when the political nation taught the Crown a lesson about its own limits.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: three consequences of the Amicable Grant&rsquo;s collapse, with one detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The collapse of the Amicable Grant cancelled the 1525 invasion of France, damaged Wolsey&rsquo;s political standing, and reasserted the principle that taxation required parliamentary consent. Henry blamed Wolsey alone &mdash; a warning sign of how the king would one day let him fall.</p>
</div>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">G.W. Bernard&rsquo;s 1986 study <em>War, Taxation and Rebellion in Early Tudor England</em> argued that the Amicable Grant was a watershed: it showed Tudor government could not collect direct taxation without parliamentary consent, and forced Wolsey to retreat from the most ambitious tax demand of the early sixteenth century. C.S.L. Davies and John Guy treat the episode as the moment Henry first publicly distanced himself from his minister &mdash; rehearsing, on a small scale, the move he would make against Wolsey in 1529 and against Cromwell in 1540.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
