--- DESCRIPTION ---
Government reform, three more royal marriages and the Cleves disaster that brought Cromwell down.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Between 1534 and 1540 Thomas Cromwell rebuilt the machinery of English government, managed the king through three new marriages, and orchestrated the fall of the most famous queen in Tudor history. Then, in the space of three months in the summer of 1540, the same machinery turned on him. He went to the block on Tower Hill on 28 July with a German bride still officially Henry&rsquo;s wife and a conservative faction in command of the Privy Chamber. The story of Cromwell&rsquo;s last six years is the story of Tudor government modernising under one minister &mdash; and then proving, decisively, that the king was still in charge.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Fall of Anne Boleyn, May 1536</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Anne Boleyn had been Cromwell&rsquo;s political ally during the break with Rome. By spring 1536, that alliance had broken. The two clashed over how monastic wealth should be spent &mdash; Anne wanted it directed to education and charity, Cromwell wanted it to fund the crown and to bind gentry buyers to the Reformation. They also disagreed over foreign policy: Anne favoured France, Cromwell wanted reconciliation with Charles V&rsquo;s empire after Catherine of Aragon&rsquo;s death in January 1536. Anne had also failed to produce a male heir; her January 1536 miscarriage of a son weighed heavily on Henry.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">In late April 1536 Cromwell took action. Five men were arrested on charges of adultery with the queen, including her brother George (incest) and the court musician Mark Smeaton, the only one who confessed &mdash; almost certainly under torture. <dfn class="term" data-def="A senior gentleman of the king&rsquo;s Privy Chamber, lifelong friend of Henry VIII; one of the men accused of adultery with Anne Boleyn and executed on 17 May 1536.">Henry Norris</dfn> denied everything to the end. Anne was arrested on 2 May, tried at the Tower on 15 May, and convicted by a jury that included her own uncle, the Duke of Norfolk, and her former suitor Henry Percy. Cranmer annulled the marriage on 17 May; Anne was beheaded with a French sword on 19 May 1536. Within ten days Henry had married Jane Seymour.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: two reasons Cromwell turned against Anne, what charges were used against her, and the date of her execution.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Anne Boleyn fell because she clashed with Cromwell over the use of monastic wealth and over the imperial alliance, and because she had failed to produce a male heir. She was arrested on 2 May 1536, convicted on charges of adultery and incest, and beheaded on 19 May. Henry married Jane Seymour ten days later.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Jane Seymour and the Birth of Edward, 1537</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Henry VIII&rsquo;s third wife (c.1508&ndash;37), a quiet conservative gentlewoman of the privy chamber; mother of Prince Edward (later Edward VI); died of childbed fever twelve days after giving birth.">Jane Seymour</dfn> was a quiet conservative member of the Privy Chamber who had served both of Henry&rsquo;s previous wives. Her appeal lay in her gentleness and her conservative religious sympathies, which steadied an exhausted king. She used her influence carefully: most famously interceding to ask for the restoration of the dissolved abbeys at the height of the Pilgrimage of Grace &mdash; Henry told her sharply not to meddle in his affairs. On 12 October 1537, after a long labour, she gave birth to a son: Prince Edward, Henry&rsquo;s long-awaited male heir. Twelve days later, on 24 October, Jane died of puerperal fever. Henry mourned her for years.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Reform of Government and Royal Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In the same years Cromwell continued to remake government. Geoffrey Elton called this a &lsquo;Tudor revolution in government&rsquo;; later historians (John Guy, David Starkey) think the changes were piecemeal. Either way the changes were real. Cromwell turned the office of <dfn class="term" data-def="The senior royal secretary, an old office Cromwell transformed from 1534 into the central coordinating role of Tudor government, handling correspondence with foreign courts, bishops and royal officers.">Principal Secretary</dfn> into the central coordinating role of Tudor government. He created new revenue departments &mdash; the Court of Augmentations to manage monastic income, the Court of First Fruits and Tenths to manage church revenues, the Court of Wards for feudal incomes, and the Court of General Surveyors. Each had a permanent staff and account books separate from the medieval Exchequer.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10">Cromwell also began the transition from the medieval royal household to a smaller, more formal Privy Council. By 1540 around 19 named councillors met regularly with formal agendas and clerks taking minutes &mdash; the predecessor of the modern Cabinet. Whether all of this counts as a &lsquo;revolution&rsquo; is debated, but contemporaries certainly noticed: government in 1540 was conducted in writing, through department heads, with parliamentary statute as the engine of policy &mdash; very different from the personal monarchy of 1509.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Geoffrey Elton&rsquo;s 1953 book <em>The Tudor Revolution in Government</em> argued Cromwell deliberately built a modern, bureaucratic state &mdash; Privy Council, secretary of state, parliamentary statute as the engine of policy. Revisionists led by John Guy and David Starkey have argued the changes were responses to immediate problems rather than a planned revolution. Diarmaid MacCulloch&rsquo;s 2018 biography agrees Cromwell was a brilliant administrator but locates his deepest motive in evangelical religious conviction. The revisionist position is now mainstream, but every reading agrees Cromwell concentrated more government in fewer hands than any English minister had previously managed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Management of Parliament</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Cromwell&rsquo;s most lasting innovation was his use of <dfn class="term" data-def="An Act of Parliament &mdash; a law passed by both Houses with royal assent. Cromwell turned statute into the central engine of Tudor policy, replacing royal proclamation in major matters of religion and government.">parliamentary statute</dfn> as the engine of state. Between 1532 and 1540 the Reformation Parliament passed legislation of a scope no medieval monarch could have imagined: the Acts in Restraint of Appeals (1533), of Supremacy (1534), of Succession (1534), the two Acts of Dissolution, the Statute of Uses (1536), and the Act of Six Articles (1539). Cromwell drafted bills, lobbied MPs through patronage and persuasion, and managed proceedings on the floor of the Commons. He treated Parliament as a working partner in government, not merely a tax-granting body. The medieval doctrine that the king-in-Parliament was the highest authority in the realm was now operating practice.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Cleves Marriage Disaster, 1539&ndash;40</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">By 1539, Catholic Europe was reuniting under Pope Paul III&rsquo;s diplomatic offensive. Charles V and Francis I had signed the Truce of Nice (1538), and Cromwell feared a joint Catholic invasion. His response was a Protestant alliance: a marriage between Henry and <dfn class="term" data-def="Henry VIII&rsquo;s fourth wife (1515&ndash;57), sister of the Protestant Duke of Cleves; her brief marriage to Henry was annulled in July 1540 after he found her unattractive in person. She lived comfortably in England as the king&rsquo;s &lsquo;sister&rsquo; until 1557.">Anne of Cleves</dfn>, sister of the Protestant Duke of Cleves on the Lower Rhine. Hans Holbein the Younger was sent to paint her in 1539. Cromwell&rsquo;s ambassadors enthused over her appearance. Henry, looking at the portrait, agreed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16">When Anne arrived at Rochester on New Year&rsquo;s Eve 1539, Henry made his trademark surprise visit in disguise to greet his future wife. He found her plain-looking, awkward in the language and unsuited to courtly flirtation. The marriage went ahead on 6 January 1540 because cancelling would humiliate the German alliance, but Henry was furious. He told Cromwell that he &lsquo;liked her not&rsquo;. Within weeks the king was seeking grounds for an annulment. Anne, sensibly, agreed to it; the marriage was declared void on 9 July 1540, and she was rewarded with manors and the title of &lsquo;the king&rsquo;s sister&rsquo;.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: why Cromwell arranged the Cleves marriage, what went wrong personally, and how Henry got out of it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cromwell arranged the marriage to Anne of Cleves in late 1539 to build a Protestant alliance against a feared Catholic coalition between Charles V and Francis I. Henry found Anne unattractive in person on New Year&rsquo;s Eve 1539 but went through with the wedding on 6 January 1540. The marriage was annulled on 9 July 1540, three weeks before Cromwell&rsquo;s execution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Fall, June&ndash;July 1540</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Cromwell&rsquo;s enemies moved fast. The Duke of Norfolk, his niece Catherine Howard, and Bishop Stephen Gardiner formed the conservative faction. Through spring 1540 they presented Catherine, a teenage cousin of Anne Boleyn, to the king at Norfolk&rsquo;s house in Lambeth. Henry was infatuated. By June, with the Cleves marriage looking like a personal humiliation, Henry was prepared to listen to the conservatives&rsquo; long-running case against Cromwell &mdash; that he was a heretic who had pushed evangelical reform too far, and a low-born minister now elevated above his station as Earl of Essex (April 1540).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">On 10 June 1540, at a Privy Council meeting, Norfolk personally tore the Order of the Garter from Cromwell&rsquo;s neck and accused him of treason and heresy. Cromwell was arrested without trial, denied a hearing, and condemned by Act of Attainder &mdash; the same legal weapon he had used against the Nun of Kent and Anne Boleyn. Henry kept him alive long enough to provide a witness statement on the Cleves annulment, then executed him on Tower Hill on 28 July 1540 &mdash; the same day Henry married Catherine Howard. The man who had concentrated more power than any commoner in English history died on charges he was given no chance to answer.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Diarmaid MacCulloch&rsquo;s 2018 biography argues the Cleves marriage was the trigger but not the cause: Cromwell had accumulated too many enemies and pushed evangelical reform faster than Henry comfortably wanted. G.W. Bernard&rsquo;s <em>The King&rsquo;s Reformation</em> stresses Henry&rsquo;s personal anger at being humiliated and his susceptibility to conservative arguments once Catherine Howard had caught his eye. Geoffrey Elton saw the fall as a contingent disaster &mdash; a gifted minister destroyed by court faction politics. The shared judgement is that Cromwell&rsquo;s methods (attainder, oaths, surveillance) had created precisely the legal arsenal his enemies could now turn on him.</p>
  </div></div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Anne Boleyn was destroyed in May 1536 after she clashed with Cromwell over monastic wealth and the imperial alliance. Jane Seymour gave Henry his male heir Edward in October 1537 but died twelve days later.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">Cromwell rebuilt government around the Principal Secretary, four new revenue courts (Augmentations, First Fruits, Wards, General Surveyors), a smaller Privy Council, and parliamentary statute as the engine of policy.</li><li data-narration-id="n26">The Cleves marriage of January 1540 was Cromwell&rsquo;s response to a feared Catholic coalition. He was executed by Act of Attainder on 28 July, the same day Henry married Catherine Howard.</li></ul>
