<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Atomic Structure and the Particle Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Everything around you is made of <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element, consisting of a nucleus surrounded by electrons.">atoms</dfn>. An atom has a tiny, positively charged <dfn class="term" data-def="The central part of an atom containing protons and neutrons, with almost all of the atom’s mass concentrated here.">nucleus</dfn> at its centre, made up of protons and neutrons. Negatively charged electrons orbit the nucleus at different distances. The nuclear radius is much smaller than the overall radius of the atom, and almost all of the atom’s mass is concentrated in the nucleus.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">A typical atom has a radius of about \(1 \times 10^{-10}\) m. The nucleus is roughly 10,000 times smaller than the atom itself, meaning atoms are mostly empty space. To put this in perspective, if an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be no bigger than a marble sitting at the centre — yet that marble would contain virtually all of the atom’s mass.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Inside the nucleus, <strong>protons</strong> carry a positive charge and <strong>neutrons</strong> carry no charge. The number of protons defines which element the atom belongs to (its atomic number). In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the overall charge is zero. If an atom gains or loses electrons it becomes a charged particle called an <dfn class="term" data-def="An atom that has gained or lost electrons, giving it a net positive or negative charge.">ion</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Draw a labelled diagram of atomic structure from memory, including the relative sizes of the atom and nucleus. Examiners often test whether you know almost all mass is in the nucleus.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The atom has a positively charged nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by negatively charged electrons. The nuclear radius is much smaller than the atomic radius, and almost all the mass is in the nucleus.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">How the Atomic Model Changed Over Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Our understanding of the atom developed through key scientific discoveries. Each new model replaced the last when experimental evidence showed the old model could not explain what scientists observed. This is a powerful example of how the scientific method works — models are updated or replaced as new evidence emerges.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Development of Atomic Theory</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Thomson’s Model (1897):</strong> J.J. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the “plum pudding” model — a sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded throughout, like plums in a pudding. This was the first model to include subatomic particles.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden (1909):</strong> The alpha particle scattering experiment fired positively charged alpha particles at a thin sheet of gold foil. Most alpha particles passed straight through, showing the atom is mostly empty space. However, a small number were deflected at large angles, and a few bounced straight back. This could only be explained by a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre of the atom. The plum pudding model was abandoned.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Bohr’s Model (1913):</strong> Niels Bohr refined the nuclear model by suggesting electrons orbit the nucleus at specific, fixed energy levels (shells), rather than randomly. Electrons can only exist in these allowed orbits, and they jump between energy levels by absorbing or emitting specific amounts of energy. This explained the emission spectra of elements — the distinct colours of light produced when elements are heated.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Modern model:</strong> Later work showed that electrons do not follow neat circular orbits. Instead, they exist in “clouds” of probability around the nucleus. For GCSE, the Bohr model with its electron shells is the version you need to use and understand.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Particle Arrangements in the Three States of Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A model that explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases in terms of the arrangement and movement of particles.">particle model</dfn> explains the properties of solids, liquids and gases based on how their particles are arranged and how they move. Although the particles are far too small to see directly, the model successfully predicts observable properties like shape, volume and compressibility.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In a <strong>solid</strong>, particles are held in fixed positions by strong forces of attraction. They vibrate about these positions but cannot move from place to place. This is why solids have a fixed shape and a fixed volume. The regular arrangement of particles in many solids forms a <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles, found in many solids such as metals and salts.">lattice</dfn> structure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In a <strong>liquid</strong>, particles are close together but can slide past each other because the forces between them are weaker than in a solid. Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container. In a <strong>gas</strong>, particles are far apart with negligible forces between them. They move rapidly in random directions, colliding with each other and the walls of their container. Gases fill any container they are placed in and can be compressed because of the large spaces between particles.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">What Is Density?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how much mass is contained in a given volume of a substance, calculated as mass divided by volume.">Density</dfn> tells you how much mass is packed into a given volume. A dense material has a lot of mass in a small volume. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Where \(\rho\) is density (\(\text{kg/m}^3\)), \(m\) is mass (kg), and \(V\) is volume (\(\text{m}^3\)). You may also see density expressed in \(\text{g/cm}^3\). To convert, remember that \(1 \text{ g/cm}^3 = 1000 \text{ kg/m}^3\). You must be able to rearrange this equation to find mass (\(m = \rho \times V\)) or volume (\(V = \frac{m}{\rho}\)).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Practise rearranging the density equation for mass and for volume. In the exam, always show the equation, substitution and units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Density = mass ÷ volume. Solids generally have higher densities than liquids because their particles are more closely packed. Gases have much lower densities because particles are spread far apart.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Calculating Density</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Question:</strong> A metal block has a mass of 540 g and a volume of \(200 \text{ cm}^3\). Calculate its density in \(\text{g/cm}^3\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Answer:</strong> Write the equation: \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\). Substitute: \(\rho = \frac{540}{200} = 2.7 \text{ g/cm}^3\). This matches the density of aluminium. To express in SI units: \(2.7 \text{ g/cm}^3 = 2700 \text{ kg/m}^3\).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Investigating Density (PAG P1)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">To measure the density of a <strong>regular solid</strong> (such as a cuboid), measure its mass on a balance and calculate its volume using a ruler: \(V = \text{length} \times \text{width} \times \text{height}\). For a cylinder, use \(V = \pi r^2 h\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">For an <strong>irregular solid</strong> (like a pebble), use a eureka (displacement) can. Fill the can until water is level with the spout. Gently lower the object into the water and collect the displaced water in a measuring cylinder. The volume of displaced water equals the volume of the object. Alternatively, use a measuring cylinder directly: record the initial water level, submerge the object, and read the new level. The difference is the object’s volume.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">For a <strong>liquid</strong>, measure the mass of an empty measuring cylinder on a balance. Pour in the liquid and measure the total mass. The liquid’s mass equals the difference between the two readings. Read the volume from the measuring cylinder scale at the bottom of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The curved surface of a liquid in a container, caused by surface tension. Read volume measurements at the bottom of the meniscus.">meniscus</dfn>, then apply \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Density Differs Between States</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27">In solids, particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement, giving high density. In liquids, particles are still close but less ordered, so density is slightly lower. In gases, particles are far apart with large gaps, so density is much lower — typically about 1000 times less than solids.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Crucially, <strong>mass is conserved</strong> during a change of state. When ice melts to water, the same number of particles is present but they occupy a slightly different volume. When water boils to steam, the mass stays the same but the volume increases enormously, so the density drops dramatically. An unusual exception is water itself: ice is slightly <em>less</em> dense than liquid water, which is why ice floats.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Improving Accuracy in Density Experiments</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29">Common sources of error include: reading the measuring cylinder at an angle (parallax error), not drying the object before measuring its mass, and air bubbles clinging to the surface of an irregular solid during displacement. To improve accuracy, take repeat measurements and calculate a mean, use a balance with higher resolution, and ensure the object is fully submerged.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Atoms have a small, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electrons. The atomic model developed from Thomson’s plum pudding, through Rutherford’s nuclear model, to Bohr’s electron shells.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">The particle model explains solids (fixed, vibrating in a lattice), liquids (close, sliding past each other) and gases (far apart, rapid random motion).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Density \(\rho = \frac{m}{V}\). You must be able to calculate density, rearrange the equation, and describe experiments to measure it for regular solids, irregular solids and liquids.</li>
</ul>
