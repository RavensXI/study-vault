--- DESCRIPTION ---
Hooke's law, elastic and plastic deformation, weight, gravity and gravitational PE.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Stretching and Deformation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">To stretch, bend or compress an object, more than one force must be applied. A single force would simply cause the object to accelerate (Newton's Second Law). Two opposing forces are needed for deformation — for example, a weight pulling a spring down while the support holds it up.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">When forces are applied to a spring, it can undergo two types of deformation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">• <dfn class="term" data-def="Deformation that is reversible — the object returns to its original shape when the force is removed.">Elastic deformation</dfn> — the object returns to its original shape and size when the force is removed. The spring "bounces back."<br>• <dfn class="term" data-def="Deformation that is permanent — the object does not return to its original shape when the force is removed.">Plastic deformation</dfn> — the object is permanently deformed and does not return to its original shape. The spring has been stretched beyond its limit.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Hooke's Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded.">Hooke's Law</dfn> states that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, as long as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum force that can be applied to a spring before it no longer returns to its original length. Beyond this point, the relationship between force and extension is no longer linear.">elastic limit</dfn> is not exceeded. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$F = k \times e$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Where \(F\) is force (N), \(k\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how stiff a spring is, equal to the force per unit extension, measured in N/m.">spring constant</dfn> (N/m), and \(e\) is extension (m). A larger spring constant means a stiffer spring that is harder to stretch. Extension means the <em>change</em> in length — so \(e = \text{stretched length} - \text{original length}\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">On a <strong>force-extension graph</strong>, the linear (proportional) region appears as a straight line through the origin. The gradient of this straight line equals the spring constant \(k\). Beyond the elastic limit, the graph curves upwards — this is the non-linear region where Hooke's Law no longer applies and the spring is undergoing plastic deformation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="On a force-extension graph, the gradient of the straight-line section equals the spring constant k. Beyond the elastic limit, the relationship is no longer proportional and the spring won't return to its original length.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hooke's Law: \(F = ke\). The force-extension graph is a straight line through the origin up to the elastic limit. Beyond this, deformation becomes plastic (permanent).</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">$$E_e = \frac{1}{2} \times k \times e^2$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Where \(E_e\) is elastic potential energy (J), \(k\) is the spring constant (N/m), and \(e\) is the extension (m). This equation only works within the elastic limit (the linear region). On a force-extension graph, the elastic potential energy equals the <strong>area under the straight-line section</strong> (which forms a triangle: \(\frac{1}{2} \times F \times e\)). When the spring is released, this stored energy is transferred to kinetic energy or other forms.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Gravity and Weight</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">All matter has a <dfn class="term" data-def="A region around a mass where other masses experience a force of attraction.">gravitational field</dfn> that attracts other masses. Every object with mass creates a gravitational field around it, but the field is only noticeable for very massive objects like planets, moons and stars. The gravitational field strength at the Earth's surface is \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\), meaning every kilogram of mass experiences a gravitational force of 10 N.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Weight is the force of gravity on an object: \(W = m \times g\). It is measured using a newton meter (spring balance) and always acts downwards towards the centre of the Earth. The acceleration due to gravity in free fall equals \(g = 10 \text{ m/s}^2\) — numerically the same as gravitational field strength but with different units.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Gravitational Potential Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">When an object is raised above the ground, work is done against gravity and energy is stored as gravitational potential energy (GPE):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">$$E_p = m \times g \times h$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Where \(E_p\) is GPE (J), \(m\) is mass (kg), \(g\) is gravitational field strength (N/kg), and \(h\) is height (m). If the object falls, this GPE is converted to kinetic energy (assuming no air resistance). At the top of a roller coaster, GPE is at its maximum; at the bottom, it has all been transferred to kinetic energy.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Work Done and Energy Transfer</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance. Work done = force × distance.">Work done</dfn> equals the energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance. The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">$$W = F \times d$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Where \(W\) is work done (J), \(F\) is force (N), and \(d\) is distance along the line of action of the force (m). One joule of work is done when a force of one newton moves an object one metre. Work done and energy transferred are always equal — 1 J = 1 N m. The distance must be measured in the <strong>same direction</strong> as the force. If you carry a box horizontally, you do no work against gravity because the force (weight) acts downwards, perpendicular to your motion.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Remember: 1 joule = 1 newton-metre. When a question asks for energy transferred by a force, use W = Fd. When asked about raising an object, use E_p = mgh. Both measure energy in joules.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Work done \(W = Fd\) = energy transferred. Gravitational PE \(E_p = mgh\). Elastic PE \(E_e = \frac{1}{2}ke^2\). All measured in joules (J).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred or work is done, measured in watts (W). 1 watt = 1 joule per second.">Power</dfn> is the rate of doing work or transferring energy:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">$$P = \frac{W}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Where \(P\) is power (W), \(W\) is work done or energy transferred (J), and \(t\) is time (s). One watt equals one joule per second. A 60 W light bulb transfers 60 J of energy every second. A more powerful engine can do the same amount of work in less time — or more work in the same time — compared to a less powerful one. For example, two students who both climb the same flight of stairs do the same work against gravity (\(W = mgh\)), but the student who climbs faster has a greater power output.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Hooke’s Law: \(F = ke\) up to the elastic limit. Beyond this, deformation is plastic (permanent). Elastic PE = \(\frac{1}{2}ke^2\) (area under the F-e graph).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Weight \(W = mg\) (\(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\) on Earth). GPE \(E_p = mgh\). Work done \(W = Fd\). Power \(P = \frac{W}{t}\). All energy measured in joules.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Gravitational fields cause attraction between all masses. Stronger fields exist around more massive objects.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">Hooke’s Law practical (PAG P2) is commonly examined. You should be able to describe the method, plot force against extension, identify the elastic limit on the graph, and calculate the spring constant from the gradient. Always use the equation \(F = ke\) for the linear section only. Remember that extension means the <em>change</em> in length, not the total length.</p>
