<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed and Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The distance travelled per unit time, measured in metres per second (m/s). Speed is a scalar quantity.">Speed</dfn> tells you how fast an object is moving. It is a scalar quantity — it has magnitude but no direction. The equation linking distance, speed and time is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$s = v \times t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Where \(s\) is distance (m), \(v\) is speed (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). You can rearrange this to find speed (\(v = \frac{s}{t}\)) or time (\(t = \frac{s}{v}\)). Typical speeds to know include: walking (~1.5 m/s), running (~3 m/s), cycling (~6 m/s), a car in town (~13 m/s or 30 mph), and the speed of sound in air (~340 m/s).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a given direction. Velocity is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.">Velocity</dfn> is speed in a stated direction. It is a vector quantity. An object travelling in a circle at constant speed has a <strong>changing velocity</strong> because its direction constantly changes — meaning it is always accelerating, even though its speed stays the same.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Similarly, <strong>distance</strong> (scalar) measures total ground covered, while <dfn class="term" data-def="The shortest straight-line distance from start to finish, measured in a specific direction. Displacement is a vector.">displacement</dfn> (vector) measures how far and in what direction from the start. A runner who completes a 400 m lap of a track covers a distance of 400 m but has a displacement of zero — they end up back where they started.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="The vector/scalar distinction is commonly examined. Remember: speed, distance, mass and energy are scalars. Velocity, displacement, force and acceleration are vectors.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Speed and distance are scalars (magnitude only). Velocity and displacement are vectors (magnitude + direction). An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a changing velocity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s². Acceleration is a vector quantity.">Acceleration</dfn> is the rate at which velocity changes. An object accelerates when it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Where \(a\) is acceleration (\(\text{m/s}^2\)), \(v\) is final velocity (m/s), \(u\) is initial velocity (m/s), and \(t\) is time (s). A negative value of acceleration means the object is decelerating (slowing down). For example, a car braking from 20 m/s to 0 m/s in 5 s has an acceleration of \(a = \frac{0 - 20}{5} = -4 \text{ m/s}^2\).</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n12">For uniform (constant) acceleration, you also need the equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">$$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Where \(s\) is distance (m). This is useful when time is not given in the question. It is particularly important for braking distance calculations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Distance-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and distance on the y-axis. The gradient of the line represents the speed of the object.">distance-time graph</dfn>, the gradient (slope) of the line equals the speed. You can interpret the shape of the line as follows:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">• A <strong>horizontal line</strong> means the object is stationary (speed = 0).<br>• A <strong>straight diagonal line</strong> means constant speed — the steeper the gradient, the faster the speed.<br>• A <strong>curve getting steeper</strong> means the object is accelerating.<br>• A <strong>curve getting shallower</strong> means the object is decelerating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">To find the speed at a specific instant on a curved d-t graph, draw a <dfn class="term" data-def="A straight line that touches a curve at exactly one point and has the same gradient as the curve at that point.">tangent</dfn> to the curve at that point and calculate the gradient of the tangent.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Velocity-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">On a <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph with time on the x-axis and velocity on the y-axis. The gradient represents acceleration and the area under the line represents distance travelled.">velocity-time graph</dfn>, two key pieces of information can be extracted:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">• The <strong>gradient</strong> equals the acceleration. A steeper slope means greater acceleration. A horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration). A downward slope means deceleration.<br>• A line <strong>below the x-axis</strong> means the object is moving in the opposite direction.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n23h">The <strong>area under the line</strong> on a velocity-time graph equals the distance travelled. For a triangle, the area is \(\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}\). For a rectangle, it is \(\text{base} \times \text{height}\). For irregular shapes, split the area into triangles and rectangles and add them together.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="In the exam, always state what the gradient represents. For d-t graphs: gradient = speed. For v-t graphs: gradient = acceleration. Practise reading values from graphs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a distance-time graph, gradient = speed. On a velocity-time graph, gradient = acceleration.</p>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24h" data-revision-tip="In the exam, always state what the area under a velocity-time graph represents. Practise calculating areas of triangles and rectangles on v-t graphs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On a velocity-time graph, the area under the line = distance travelled. Split complex shapes into triangles and rectangles to calculate the total area.</p>
</div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Any moving object has kinetic energy. The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">$$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times m \times v^2$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Where \(E_k\) is kinetic energy (J), \(m\) is mass (kg) and \(v\) is speed (m/s). Because speed is <strong>squared</strong>, doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy. This has critical implications for braking distances and road safety: a car travelling at 40 mph has four times the kinetic energy of one at 20 mph, meaning it needs four times the braking distance to stop.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Average Speed and Non-Uniform Motion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Most real journeys involve varying speeds. When an object's speed varies, you calculate average speed by dividing the total distance by the total time:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">$$\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">On a distance-time graph with a curved line, you can estimate speed at a particular moment by drawing a tangent and finding its gradient. The average speed between two points is found by drawing a straight line (chord) between them and calculating its gradient.</p>

To investigate acceleration, use two light gates at different positions and compare the speeds.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Speed is scalar; velocity is a vector (speed + direction). Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">On d-t graphs: gradient = speed (draw tangent for curves). On v-t graphs: gradient = acceleration, area under = distance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Key equations: \(s = vt\), \(a = \frac{v-u}{t}\), \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), \(E_k = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\). Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy and braking distance.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">Graph interpretation is heavily tested in OCR Gateway Physics. For distance-time graphs, calculate speed from the gradient. For velocity-time graphs, calculate acceleration from the gradient and distance from the area under the line. Always show your working: identify the values you’re reading from the graph, write the formula, substitute, and include units in your answer. Remember to draw tangents on curved graphs to find instantaneous values.</p>
