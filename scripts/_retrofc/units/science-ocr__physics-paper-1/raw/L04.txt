--- DESCRIPTION ---
Newton's three laws of motion, resultant forces, momentum and inertia.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Newton’s First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues at the same speed and direction, unless acted on by an unbalanced (resultant) force.">Newton’s First Law</dfn> states that an object remains at rest or continues to move at a constant velocity unless acted upon by a <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall force on an object when all individual forces are combined, taking into account their direction.">resultant force</dfn>. This means:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">• If the resultant force is <strong>zero</strong>, a stationary object stays still and a moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction.<br>• If there is a <strong>resultant force</strong>, the object accelerates — which means it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">This law applies to all objects, from cars on motorways to planets orbiting the Sun. A book on a table remains stationary because its weight (downward) is exactly balanced by the normal contact force from the table (upward). A spacecraft drifting in deep space, where there is no friction or air resistance, will continue at the same velocity forever unless a thruster fires.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="A common misconception: objects do NOT need a force to keep moving. A resultant force is only needed to CHANGE an object’s motion. If forces are balanced, motion doesn’t change.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Objects do not need a force to keep moving at constant velocity. A resultant force is only needed to change the speed or direction of an object.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Newton’s Second Law: F = ma</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass: F = ma.">Newton’s Second Law</dfn> tells us that the resultant force on an object is equal to its mass multiplied by its acceleration:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$F = m \times a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where \(F\) is force in newtons (N), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(a\) is acceleration in \(\text{m/s}^2\). This equation tells you two important things: a <strong>bigger force</strong> produces a <strong>bigger acceleration</strong> (if mass stays the same), and a <strong>heavier object</strong> needs a <strong>bigger force</strong> for the same acceleration. It can be rearranged to find acceleration (\(a = \frac{F}{m}\)) or mass (\(m = \frac{F}{a}\)).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Remember that \(F\) in this equation is always the <strong>resultant force</strong>, not just any single force. If a car’s engine provides 3000 N of driving force but friction and air resistance total 1000 N, the resultant force is \(3000 - 1000 = 2000 \text{ N}\) forward.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Weight, Mass and Gravitational Field Strength</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms (kg). Mass does not change with location.">Mass</dfn> is the amount of matter in an object and is measured in kg. It does not change with location. <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of gravity acting on an object, measured in newtons (N). Weight depends on mass and gravitational field strength.">Weight</dfn> is the force of gravity acting on that mass, measured in newtons. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$W = m \times g$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(W\) is weight (N), \(m\) is mass (kg) and \(g\) is gravitational field strength (N/kg). On Earth, \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\). On the Moon, \(g \approx 1.6 \text{ N/kg}\), so a 70 kg person would weigh 700 N on Earth but only 112 N on the Moon. Their mass remains 70 kg in both locations. Weight is measured using a newton meter (spring balance), while mass is measured on a balance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The numerical value of \(g\) at the Earth’s surface also equals the <strong>acceleration due to gravity</strong> in free fall: \(g = 10 \text{ m/s}^2\). This means that a falling object (ignoring air resistance) accelerates at \(10 \text{ m/s}^2\) regardless of its mass.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Newton’s Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force, acting on a different object.">Newton’s Third Law</dfn> states: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. These action-reaction pairs act on <strong>different objects</strong> and are always the <strong>same type of force</strong>. For example:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">• When you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you with an equal force (both are normal contact forces).<br>• When the Earth pulls you down by gravity (your weight), you pull the Earth up with the same gravitational force.<br>• A swimmer pushes water backwards; the water pushes the swimmer forwards.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A common mistake is to identify weight and normal contact force as a Third Law pair. They are not — both act on the <em>same</em> object (you). A true Third Law pair involves forces on <em>different</em> objects.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Third Law pairs always: (1) are equal in size, (2) act in opposite directions, (3) are the same type of force, and (4) act on DIFFERENT objects. If both forces act on the same object, they are NOT a Third Law pair.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Newton’s Third Law pairs are equal in size, opposite in direction, the same type of force, and act on different objects.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Terminal Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">When a skydiver jumps from a plane, they initially accelerate because their weight is much greater than air resistance. As speed increases, air resistance increases (it depends on speed, surface area and shape). Eventually, air resistance equals weight. At this point the resultant force is zero and the skydiver reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum velocity an object reaches when the driving force equals the resistive force, so there is no resultant force and no acceleration.">terminal velocity</dfn> — constant speed, zero acceleration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">When the parachute opens, the surface area dramatically increases, causing air resistance to suddenly become much greater than weight. The skydiver decelerates. As they slow down, air resistance decreases until a new, lower terminal velocity is reached — slow enough for a safe landing.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The product of an object’s mass and velocity. Momentum is a vector quantity measured in kg m/s.">Momentum</dfn> is a measure of how hard it is to stop a moving object. The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">$$p = m \times v$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29">Where \(p\) is momentum (kg m/s), \(m\) is mass (kg), and \(v\) is velocity (m/s). In a <strong>closed system</strong> (where no external forces act), the total momentum before a collision equals the total momentum after — this is the <strong>conservation of momentum</strong>. For example, if two trolleys of equal mass collide and stick together, their combined speed will be half of the original speed of the moving trolley.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of an object. Inertial mass equals force divided by acceleration.">Inertia</dfn> is a measure of how difficult it is to change an object’s velocity. An object with more mass has more inertia. Inertial mass is defined as the ratio of force to acceleration: \(m = \frac{F}{a}\). This is essentially Newton’s Second Law rearranged.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">First Law: no resultant force = no change in motion. Second Law: \(F = ma\) (\(F\) is always the resultant force). Third Law: equal and opposite forces of the same type on different objects.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Weight \(W = mg\). On Earth, \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\). Mass is constant; weight varies with gravitational field strength. \(g\) also equals the acceleration in free fall.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Momentum \(p = mv\) is conserved in closed systems. Terminal velocity occurs when driving force = resistive force (resultant = 0).</li>
</ul>

Show the equation, substitute values with units, and state the direction of acceleration if asked. For Newton’s Third Law, always name both forces, state they are equal and opposite, and emphasise they act on different objects.</p>
