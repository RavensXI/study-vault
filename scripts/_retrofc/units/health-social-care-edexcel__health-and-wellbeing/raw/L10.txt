The six life stages and how PIES development changes from infancy to later adulthood.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Lifespan Development Matters for Component 3</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Component 3 is a synoptic exam, which means it draws on knowledge from Components 1 and 2 as well as the Component 3 content itself. One of the most important areas you need to revise is <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of growth and change that occurs throughout a person's life, from birth through to later adulthood.">human lifespan development</dfn> from Component 1. In the exam, you may need to explain how an individual’s life stage affects their health needs, their ability to follow recommendations, or the type of support they require.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Six Life Stages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Pearson specification divides human life into six stages. Each stage has distinct characteristics across the four <dfn class="term" data-def="A classification system used to categorise development into four areas: Physical, Intellectual, Emotional and Social.">PIES</dfn> categories: Physical, Intellectual, Emotional and Social development.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six life stages in order with the age range for each, from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six life stages are: <strong>infancy</strong> (birth to 2 years), <strong>early childhood</strong> (3–8 years), <strong>adolescence</strong> (9–18 years), <strong>early adulthood</strong> (19–45 years), <strong>middle adulthood</strong> (46–65 years) and <strong>later adulthood</strong> (65+ years).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Infancy (Birth to 2 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Physical:</strong> Rapid growth in weight and height. Development of gross motor skills (sitting, crawling, walking) and fine motor skills (grasping, holding). Infants follow the same pattern of development but at different rates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Rapid development of language skills (babbling, first words) and thinking skills such as memory and recall.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Attachments are formed with primary carers. Emotional wellbeing is based on bonding, attachment, security and contentment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Social:</strong> Strong dependence on adults and carers. Socialisation happens through the family. Infants engage in solitary play.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Early Childhood (3–8 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Physical:</strong> Continued growth. Mastery of gross and fine motor skills (running, writing, using scissors).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Increased curiosity about the world. Language fluency develops. Strong grasp of memory and recall.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Increased independence. Wider range of relationships formed. Emotional wellbeing still based on attachment, security and contentment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Social:</strong> Social circle widens. Close friendships are formed. Socialisation continues through family and also friends and carers. Social play develops (playing with others rather than alone).</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Adolescence (9–18 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Physical:</strong> Onset of <dfn class="term" data-def="The period during which the body matures physically from childhood to adulthood, involving the development of primary and secondary sexual characteristics.">puberty</dfn> with primary and secondary sexual characteristics developing. Physical differences between males and females become more pronounced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Complex and abstract thinking develops, allowing adolescents to consider hypothetical situations and reason logically.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Greater independence and more freedom to make decisions. Concerns over self-image and self-esteem may increase. Emotional wellbeing remains based on attachment, security and contentment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Social:</strong> Wide range of formal and informal relationships develop and have influence. Peer pressure becomes important. Intimate relationships may form.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Early Adulthood (19–45 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Physical:</strong> Peak physical fitness is reached. Full height attained. Sexual maturity reached; women are at their most fertile.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Mastery of abstract and creative thinking. Careers become important. Some individuals may return to education.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Independent living and control over own life. Emotional wellbeing based on attachment, security and contentment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Social:</strong> Intimate and long-lasting relationships are formed, including marriage, civil partnerships and parenthood.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Can use accumulated knowledge and experience for complex decision making. Some may retire towards the end of this stage.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Emotional:</strong> May experience changes in self-image and self-esteem linked to retirement or the ageing process. Emotional wellbeing based on attachment, security and contentment.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Social:</strong> May have more time to socialise as children grow up and leave home. Social networks may shift.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Physical:</strong> The ageing process continues. Decline in strength and fitness. Loss of mobility. Loss of muscle tone and skin elasticity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> May experience decline in cognitive ability, such as loss of memory and recall. Risk of conditions like dementia.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Emotional:</strong> May become more dependent on others, which can affect self-esteem. Emotional wellbeing remains based on attachment, security and contentment.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Social:</strong> May experience bereavement and a reduction in social circle. Risk of social isolation and loneliness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Key Patterns Across the Life Stages</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35">Notice that emotional wellbeing at every stage is based on attachment, security and contentment. This is a recurring theme throughout the spec. Physical development follows a pattern of growth and peak in early adulthood, followed by gradual decline from middle adulthood onwards. Intellectual development progresses from simple to complex thinking, and social development moves from dependence on family towards wider relationships and then, in some cases, back towards a smaller social circle in later life. Understanding these patterns helps you predict how an individual’s life stage will affect their health needs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n36">Factors Affecting Growth & Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n37">Growth and development across the life stages can be affected by a range of factors. These mirror the factors you studied in Component 3’s Area A, but here the focus is on long-term developmental impact rather than current health status.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Physical factors</strong> — inherited conditions (sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, muscular dystrophy), illness, mental ill health, physical ill health, disabilities, sensory impairments.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39"><strong>Lifestyle factors</strong> — nutrition, physical activity, smoking, substance misuse.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40"><strong>Emotional factors</strong> — fear, anxiety, grief, happiness, security, attachment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Social factors</strong> — supportive and unsupportive relationships, social inclusion/exclusion, bullying, discrimination.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n42"><strong>Cultural factors</strong> — religion, gender roles, gender identity, sexual orientation, community participation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n43"><strong>Environmental factors</strong> — housing conditions, home environment, exposure to pollution.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n44"><strong>Economic factors</strong> — employment situation, financial resources.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n45" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain how an adolescent and an older adult would respond differently to the same health recommendation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Factors can affect development positively or negatively, and different factors impact different PIES areas. For example, bullying (a social factor) can negatively affect emotional development (low self-esteem) and social development (withdrawal from friendships).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n50">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n46">There are six life stages, each with distinct PIES characteristics: infancy, early childhood, adolescence, early adulthood, middle adulthood and later adulthood.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n47">Seven categories of factors (physical, lifestyle, emotional, social, cultural, environmental, economic) can affect growth and development positively or negatively.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n48">In the synoptic exam, link the individual’s life stage and PIES development to the recommendations and support they need.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n49">In the Component 3 exam, case studies will often specify the individual’s age. Use this to identify their life stage and then explain how the typical PIES characteristics of that stage affect their health needs and their ability to follow recommendations. For example, a person in later adulthood may have declining physical fitness, making vigorous exercise unrealistic — so chair-based exercises would be more appropriate.</p>
