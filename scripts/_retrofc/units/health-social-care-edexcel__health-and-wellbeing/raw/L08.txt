--- DESCRIPTION ---
Established health recommendations and the formal and informal support available to follow them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Recommendations Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Once a health professional has assessed an individual’s physiological and lifestyle indicators, the next step is to make <dfn class="term" data-def="Advice given by a health or social care professional about actions an individual should take to improve their health and wellbeing.">recommendations</dfn> — specific actions the person can take to improve their health and wellbeing. These recommendations must be person-centred, realistic and linked to the indicators that were identified as abnormal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In the Component 3 exam, you will often be given a case study with physiological data and asked to suggest appropriate recommendations. This lesson covers the established recommendations and the support available to help people follow them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Established Recommendations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Pearson spec identifies eight key areas where recommendations can be made. Each one targets a different aspect of physical health.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and match each health recommendation to the indicator it addresses, from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The eight established recommendations are: improving resting heart rate and recovery rate after exercise, improving blood pressure, maintaining a healthy weight, eating a balanced diet, getting enough physical activity, quitting smoking, sensible alcohol consumption, and stopping substance misuse.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Recommendations in Detail</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Improving resting heart rate and recovery rate</strong> — a resting heart rate above 100 bpm is considered high. Regular cardiovascular exercise (such as brisk walking, swimming or cycling) strengthens the heart muscle, lowering the resting rate over time. A faster recovery rate after exercise shows improved <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of the body to carry out everyday activities without undue fatigue, including cardiovascular endurance, muscular strength and flexibility.">fitness</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Improving blood pressure</strong> — high blood pressure (140/90 mmHg or above) increases the risk of stroke and heart disease. Recommendations include reducing salt and saturated fat intake, taking regular exercise, managing stress and, if necessary, taking prescribed medication.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Maintaining a healthy weight</strong> — a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m² is considered healthy. Overweight or obese individuals are advised to increase physical activity and reduce calorie intake. Underweight individuals may need to eat more nutrient-rich foods.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Eating a balanced diet</strong> — the Eatwell Guide recommends eating plenty of fruit, vegetables and starchy carbohydrates, moderate amounts of protein and dairy, and small amounts of unsaturated fats and oils. Sugary and high-fat foods should be limited.</p>



<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Getting enough physical activity</strong> — the UK Chief Medical Officers recommend that adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity. Children and young people should aim for 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity every day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Quitting smoking</strong> — smoking damages the lungs and cardiovascular system. Recommendations include using nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum or inhalers), prescription medications, and support from NHS Stop Smoking services.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Sensible alcohol consumption</strong> — the UK guidelines recommend no more than 14 units of alcohol per week, spread over three or more days, with several alcohol-free days each week. Binge drinking is particularly harmful.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Stopping substance misuse</strong> — misuse of substances such as cannabis, opiates (e.g. heroin) or other drugs has severe physical and mental health consequences. Recommendations focus on accessing professional treatment and support services.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Formal Support</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Organised, structured support provided by trained professionals, volunteers, charities or support groups to help individuals improve their health.">Formal support</dfn> comes from trained professionals and organised services. It includes:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Professionals</strong> — GPs, nurses, dietitians, physiotherapists, counsellors and other qualified practitioners who provide expert advice, treatment and monitoring.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Trained volunteers</strong> — people who give their time and have received training to support others, for example at NHS Stop Smoking services or mental health helplines.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Support groups</strong> — organised groups where people with similar conditions meet to share experiences and encouragement, such as Alcoholics Anonymous, Weight Watchers or local diabetes groups.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Charities</strong> — organisations such as the British Heart Foundation, Mind or Macmillan Cancer Support that provide information, helplines, practical resources and sometimes financial assistance.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define formal and informal support and give two examples of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Formal support is provided by professionals, trained volunteers, support groups and charities. Informal support comes from friends, family, neighbours, community members and work colleagues.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Informal Support</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Informal support comes from the people around the individual in their everyday life. It is not organised or professionally delivered, but it can be just as important.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Friends</strong> — can provide emotional encouragement, exercise with the individual, or help them stick to a healthier routine.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Family</strong> — can help with practical tasks such as cooking healthier meals, attending appointments together, or providing transport.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Neighbours</strong> — can check on the individual, offer lifts to appointments, or reduce feelings of isolation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Community and work colleagues</strong> — workplace wellbeing programmes, community sports clubs or faith groups can all provide motivation and social support.</li>
</ul>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">There are eight established recommendations for improving health, ranging from improving heart rate to stopping substance misuse.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Formal support (professionals, volunteers, support groups, charities) and informal support (friends, family, neighbours, community) both play vital roles.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">In the exam, always link recommendations to specific indicators and use both types of support in your answers.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">When recommending improvements in the exam, always make your suggestions specific to the case study. Do not just write “eat a balanced diet” — explain what that means for the individual, for example “replace fast food lunches with home-prepared meals following the Eatwell Guide.” Then suggest both formal support (e.g. dietitian referral) and informal support (e.g. family helping with meal preparation) to show depth.</p>
