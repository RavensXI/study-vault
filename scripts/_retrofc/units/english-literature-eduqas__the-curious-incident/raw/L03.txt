<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Act 2: The Truth Comes Out</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 2 opens in the aftermath of Christopher's discovery of the hidden letters. He now knows his mother is alive. He wants to find out more — but before he can act, Ed discovers that Christopher has found the letters. The confrontation between father and son is one of the most emotionally charged scenes in the play. Ed does not deny what he has done. Instead, he confesses: to hiding the letters, to lying about Judy's death, and — most shockingly — to killing Wellington.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Ed killed Wellington because, after Judy left him for Roger Shears, his relationship with Mrs Shears also deteriorated and ended. In a moment of rage and grief, he killed her dog. He lied about this to Christopher because the truth would have exposed the web of lies he had constructed about Judy's death. Ed's betrayals are interconnected: each lie required another lie to sustain it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what two things does Ed confess to in Act 2, and why are the two confessions connected?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ed confesses both to killing Wellington — in a rage after his relationship with Mrs Shears collapsed — and to lying about Judy's death. The two lies are connected: admitting he killed Wellington would have forced him to explain why, which would have revealed Judy's affair and her departure to London.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Ed Lied: The Logic of Deception</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ed tells Christopher that he did not want him to know about his mother leaving with Mr Shears. He believed — wrongly — that telling Christopher his mother was dead was kinder than telling him she had chosen to leave. This is the fundamental irony at the heart of the play: Ed, who loves Christopher deeply, lies to him repeatedly, believing he is protecting him. But Christopher's most deeply held value is honesty — 'I do not tell lies.' Ed's lies destroy the foundation of the one relationship Christopher most depends on.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Stephens uses this contradiction to explore how love and deception are not opposites. Ed is not a villain. He is a frightened, grieving man who made catastrophically wrong choices. His love for Christopher is genuine and consistent throughout the play. The moral complexity of the father-son relationship — love that simultaneously protects and harms — is one of the play's central preoccupations.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Christopher's response to Ed's confession is logical rather than emotional — which makes it devastating. He does not shout or cry. He calculates: if Father has lied about Mother dying, and Father has lied about not killing Wellington, then Father might kill him. This is a cold, precise chain of reasoning that follows from Christopher's absolute commitment to logical deduction. It is not paranoia — it is the perfectly rational conclusion of a mind that takes all information equally literally. Stephens uses this moment to show how Christopher's truthfulness makes Ed's lies uniquely destructive: Christopher cannot reframe them as well-intentioned; they are simply lies, and lies can be lethal.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Decision to Run: Christopher Flees to London</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Convinced his father might harm him, Christopher decides to go to London to find his mother. This decision represents the central act of courage and independence in the play. For Christopher, everything about the journey is terrifying: crowds, strangers, unpredictable sensory environments, trains, the Underground. He has never travelled independently. He takes his pet rat Toby with him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The journey to London is the play's most ambitious theatrical sequence. Stephens and director Marianne Elliott, working with Frantic Assembly's choreographers, stage the overwhelming experience of a busy train station through the ensemble. The cast physically embodies the crowd that overwhelms Christopher: people rush past him, press against him, fill his field of vision. The graph-paper set, designed by Bunny Christie, shifts and accelerates. Mathematical projections may flash across the walls. The staging makes Christopher's sensory experience visceral for the audience.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this: how does the play stage the train journey to London, and why is this theatrically distinctive?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The train journey to London is staged through the ensemble: the cast physically embodies the crowd and sensory chaos Christopher experiences. Frantic Assembly's choreography makes his sensory overload a live theatrical event rather than a described experience.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Toby on the Underground</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">During the journey, Christopher's pet rat Toby escapes onto the Underground tracks. Christopher goes onto the tracks to retrieve him — a moment of pure, focused determination. In the stage production, this sequence is one of the most theatrically striking: the ensemble and lighting create the underground environment around Christopher, who moves with terrified precision through the chaos.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Toby's role in the play is significant. He is Christopher's consistent companion — a creature whose needs Christopher understands and can meet. In a world of confusing human relationships, Toby is simple, dependable, and loyal. His presence during the London journey is a reminder of what Christopher carries with him: the capacity for care and responsibility even in extremity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Arriving at Judy's: The Reunion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Christopher eventually finds Judy's address and arrives in London. The reunion with his mother is not what he imagined. Judy is overwhelmed with guilt and shock. She is living with Roger Shears, who quickly makes clear that Christopher's arrival is an imposition. The domestic reality of Judy's life in London is shabby and uncomfortable, not the idealized vision Christopher might have constructed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Judy's response to seeing Christopher is immediate and physical — she weeps, she holds him, she is unable to speak coherently at first. This contrasts sharply with Christopher's precise, factual account of how he got there. Stephens uses this contrast to show two different kinds of love: Judy's emotional, demonstrative love, and Christopher's love expressed through competence, determination, and the sheer act of arriving.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Roger Shears (Mr Shears) is Mrs Shears's ex-husband who has been living with Judy in London. His relationship with Christopher is immediately hostile. Roger finds Christopher difficult, resents the disruption his presence causes, and eventually becomes violent — which forces Christopher to leave and return to Swindon. Roger's hostility is partly the play's way of showing that the London alternative to Ed is not straightforwardly better. Ed lies but loves; Roger is honest about his irritation but has no investment in Christopher's wellbeing. The play avoids simplifying the choice between the two adults.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Returning to Swindon: Shared Custody</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">After Roger becomes aggressive, Christopher leaves and returns to Swindon. His parents arrange a form of shared custody. Christopher does not simply forgive Ed — the damage done by the lies is real. But the play allows the possibility of reconciliation: Ed acknowledges what he has done, shows consistent remorse, and — in a gesture of reparation — gives Christopher a golden retriever puppy, Sandy. This gift is important: it cannot undo the lies, but it is an act of love on Christopher's own terms — a creature, a responsibility, something Christopher can care for.</p>
