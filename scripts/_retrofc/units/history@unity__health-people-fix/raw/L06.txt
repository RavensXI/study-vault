--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Introduction: A Time of Transition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The 18th century was a time of transition for medicine in Britain. Vesalius, Paré, and Harvey had made important breakthroughs, but many old ideas about disease and treatment <strong>stubbornly persisted</strong>. This lesson looks at what stayed the same (<dfn class="term" data-def="Things that stay the same over time.">continuity</dfn>) and what was genuinely new (<dfn class="term" data-def="New developments, ideas, or practices that represent a break from the past.">change</dfn>) in how people understood and treated disease.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What Stayed the Same (Continuity)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Even though science was becoming more important, many old ideas about disease were still popular among ordinary people.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Close this and, from memory, name 2 old beliefs about the cause of illness that persisted.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Persistent Old Beliefs</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n20">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that disease was caused by 'bad air' or foul-smelling vapours rising from rotting matter, swamps, or sewage.">Miasma</dfn></strong> — the belief that “bad air” or foul smells caused illness — was still widely accepted.</li>
<li>The ancient Greek idea of the <dfn class="term" data-def="An ancient Greek medical theory, developed by Hippocrates and Galen, which claimed the body contained four liquids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) that needed to be in balance for good health.">Four Humours</dfn> had not disappeared entirely.</li>
<li>Many people still believed that illness was a <strong>punishment from God</strong>.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h3>The Medical “Marketplace”</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For most people, healthcare in the 1600s–1700s was like a <strong>marketplace</strong> — they could choose between different practitioners, none of whom were reliably effective:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-revision-tip="Without looking, who gave haircuts, pulled teeth, and were often the only medical help for the poor?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Who Could You Visit for Medical Help?</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Practitioners who combined haircutting with minor surgery such as tooth-pulling, bloodletting, and treating wounds.">Barber-surgeons</dfn>:</strong> Poorly trained practitioners who gave haircuts <em>and</em> performed minor operations like tooth-pulling and bloodletting. Surgery was seen as a low-status craft.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A person who prepared and sold medicines and herbal remedies.">Apothecaries</dfn>:</strong> Sold medicines and potions, often with little formal training. They were the forerunners of modern pharmacists and often the only medical help for poorer people.</li>
<li><strong>Wise women:</strong> Used traditional knowledge of herbs and plants, sometimes mixed with superstition. Often the first point of contact for rural communities.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of unqualified or fraudulent people selling fake medicines and promising miracle cures.">Quack doctors</dfn>:</strong> Travelling salesmen who sold fake “cure-all” medicines. There was no regulation to stop them, and desperate people were easy targets.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The lack of regulation meant medical treatment was very inconsistent. Wealthy patients could afford physicians, but even physicians often relied on outdated ideas like bloodletting and purging.</p>
<h3>New Treatments from Overseas</h3>
<p>As European exploration expanded in the 16th and 17th centuries, new treatments arrived from abroad:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n22">
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A substance derived from the bark of the cinchona tree in South America.">Quinine</dfn></strong> from South American tree bark was used to treat <dfn class="term" data-def="A dangerous disease spread by mosquito bites, causing recurring fever, chills, and sometimes death.">malaria</dfn> and was one of the first genuinely effective imported medicines.</li>
<li><strong>Opium</strong> was used as a powerful painkiller, though it caused serious addiction.</li>
<li><strong>Tobacco</strong> was believed to be a cure-all for almost every illness — a claim since disproved.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Supernatural Beliefs Persisted</h3>
<p>Even as new scientific ideas spread, supernatural beliefs remained widespread. Many people believed in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that the touch of a reigning monarch could cure scrofula (a form of tuberculosis affecting the lymph nodes in the neck).">King’s Touch</dfn> — the idea that the monarch’s touch could cure <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of tuberculosis that affects the lymph nodes in the neck, causing swelling.">scrofula</dfn> (a form of tuberculosis known as the “King’s Evil”). Thousands attended royal ceremonies hoping to be healed. This shows how slowly scientific thinking replaced superstition.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What Was New (Change)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The 18th century was part of the <dfn class="term" data-def="An intellectual movement of the 17th and 18th centuries that emphasised reason, science, and individual rights over tradition and superstition.">Enlightenment</dfn>, when science and reason were becoming very important. This spirit of enquiry began to influence medicine.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what changed about hospitals in the 18th century, from care to what?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Key Changes in the 18th Century</div>
<ul data-narration-id="n23">
<li><strong>The growth of hospitals:</strong> Before, hospitals were mostly places of “care” run by the Church. New hospitals were now built with the aim of <strong>curing</strong> patients.</li>
<li><strong>Doctors trained in hospitals:</strong> Physicians began observing patients in wards, learning through direct experience rather than only from books.</li>
<li><strong>Free dispensaries:</strong> Set up for the poor, these provided medicine and advice without requiring payment for a doctor’s visit.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Case Study: John Hunter (1728–1793)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>John Hunter</strong> shows the new scientific approach to medicine. Often called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A title given to John Hunter in recognition of his pioneering approach to surgery based on scientific observation, experimentation, and anatomical knowledge rather than tradition.">“Father of Scientific Surgery”</dfn>, he pushed a new way of thinking about medical practice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Instead of accepting old ideas from books, Hunter taught that surgeons must learn through <strong>observation and experiment</strong>. He was a skilled <dfn class="term" data-def="A scientist who studies the structure of the human body, including its organs, bones, muscles, and other internal parts.">anatomist</dfn> who carried out many dissections to understand how the body worked.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Hunter’s methods and legacy in detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hunter collected <strong>thousands of anatomical specimens</strong> (preserved body parts, organs, and bones) in a museum at his home. He used these to teach students with hands-on experience rather than just textbook lectures.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">He experimented on himself and on animals, studying how diseases progressed and how the body healed. He improved techniques for treating wounds and aneurysms, helping turn surgery from a simple craft into a <strong>respected science</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">His collection later became the basis of the Hunterian Museum at the Royal College of Surgeons in London.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h3>Hunter’s Surgical Innovations</h3>
<p>One of Hunter’s most celebrated achievements was treating a <dfn class="term" data-def="A dangerous swelling of a blood vessel caused by a weakness in the vessel wall.">popliteal aneurysm</dfn> — a dangerously swollen artery behind the knee. The standard treatment was <strong>amputation</strong>, which was brutal and often fatal. Hunter tied off the artery higher up the leg, allowing other blood vessels to expand and keep the leg alive. This showed his approach in action: <strong>observe the problem, hypothesise a solution, test it</strong>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Body-snatching and the problem of dissection</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p>Getting bodies for dissection was a major problem. Henry VIII had granted surgeons just <strong>4 hanged criminals per year</strong> for anatomical study. As demand grew, this was hopelessly inadequate.</p>
<p>The result was a grim trade in stolen corpses. <dfn class="term" data-def="People who illegally dug up freshly buried corpses from graveyards and sold them to surgeons and medical schools for dissection.">“Resurrection men”</dfn> stole fresh bodies from graves and sold them to surgeons and medical schools. Hunter himself obtained bodies this way — many surgeons saw it as a necessary evil.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Hunter’s self-experimentation and extreme dedication</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p>Hunter was so dedicated that he deliberately <strong>infected himself with a sexually transmitted disease</strong> to study its effects. This was an extreme and dangerous example of his belief that direct observation was the only way to truly understand disease.</p>
<p>He also ran a large medical practice, trained <strong>hundreds of students</strong>, and wrote influential books on dentistry, gunshot wounds, and venereal diseases. His teaching shaped a generation of surgeons who spread his evidence-based methods.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h3>Hunter and the Professionalisation of Surgery</h3>
<p>Surgeons had already broken away from the barbers in <strong>1745</strong> to form the Company of Surgeons. Hunter pushed that change further, arguing that surgery should become a respected profession built on science, not a trade. His campaigning helped establish the <dfn class="term" data-def="The professional body that oversees the practice of surgery in England.">Royal College of Surgeons</dfn> in 1800, which still oversees surgery in England today.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="From memory, name one surgical technique Hunter pioneered, beyond just observation and experiment.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — John Hunter</div>
<p>John Hunter promoted <strong>observation and experiment</strong> over reliance on old textbooks. He collected thousands of anatomical specimens to teach students, pioneered new surgical techniques (including tying off aneurysms instead of amputating), and helped establish surgery as a respected profession through the Royal College of Surgeons. His methods helped transform surgery into a respected, evidence-based science.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The 18th century was a time of transition. New scientific thinking was taking hold — especially through figures like John Hunter — but most people still relied on old beliefs and traditional remedies. Real, widespread change would not come until the 19th century.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li><strong>Old beliefs persisted:</strong> Miasma, the Four Humours, and supernatural explanations remained common, alongside quackery and herbal remedies.</li>
<li><strong>New institutions emerged:</strong> Hospitals shifted from care to cure, doctors trained through observation, and free dispensaries helped the poor.</li>
<li><strong>John Hunter bridged old and new:</strong> He championed observation and experimentation, helping transform surgery into a respected, evidence-based profession.</li>
</ul>
