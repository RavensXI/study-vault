--- DESCRIPTION ---
Three Renaissance rebels who proved Galen wrong — through dissection, battlefield surgery and experiments on circulation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Introduction: The Renaissance Rebels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For over a thousand years, the ancient Greek physician <dfn class="term" data-def="An ancient Greek physician (c. 129–216 AD) whose ideas about anatomy and the body dominated European medicine for over 1,300 years.">Galen</dfn> dominated European medicine. Doctors read Galen’s books rather than investigating the body for themselves. During the <dfn class="term" data-def="A period of cultural, artistic, and intellectual rebirth in Europe (roughly 14th–17th centuries), characterised by renewed interest in classical learning and a spirit of enquiry that encouraged observation and experimentation.">Renaissance</dfn>, a new spirit of enquiry encouraged people to challenge ancient authority and seek knowledge through direct observation. Three figures — Vesalius, Paré, and Harvey — led this revolution.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain the common approach of Vesalius, Paré, and Harvey and why it was different to Galen.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>All three of these figures shared a common approach: they relied on <strong>practical observation and experimentation</strong> rather than blindly accepting the teachings of Galen. This was a fundamental shift in how medicine was practised and taught.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Andreas Vesalius: The Father of Anatomy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Andreas Vesalius was a Flemish <dfn class="term" data-def="A scientist who studies the structure of the human body, including its organs, bones, muscles, and other internal parts.">anatomist</dfn> who transformed our understanding of the human body. He believed successful surgery required proper knowledge of anatomy. Most professors sat reading Galen’s texts while a barber-surgeon did the cutting. Vesalius carried out <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of cutting open and examining a dead body in order to study its internal structure.">dissections</dfn> himself on the bodies of executed criminals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">By studying human anatomy in detail, Vesalius proved that Galen — who had only dissected animals like monkeys and pigs — had made over <strong>300 mistakes</strong>. For example, Vesalius showed that the human jawbone is <strong>one bone, not two</strong> as Galen had claimed.</p>

<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Vesalius’s key publication and its impact</span>

</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1543, Vesalius published his groundbreaking book, <em>‘De Humani Corporis Fabrica’</em> (<em>On the Fabric of the Human Body</em>). It contained detailed, accurate drawings of the human body based on his own dissections.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A mechanical device invented by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 that allowed books to be produced quickly and cheaply, enabling ideas to spread far more widely than hand-copied manuscripts ever could.">printing press</dfn> spread his work across Europe, correcting centuries of error. For the first time, doctors across the continent could learn accurate anatomy.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Without looking, how many errors did Vesalius find in Galen's work and what was the name of his 1543 book?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Vesalius</div>
<p>Vesalius proved Galen wrong in over 300 places. His 1543 book, <em>‘On the Fabric of the Human Body’</em>, used detailed illustrations from real human dissections. The printing press spread his corrected anatomy across Europe.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">William Harvey: The Circulation Expert</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">William Harvey was an English doctor who challenged <dfn class="term" data-def="Galen's incorrect theory that blood was produced in the liver from digested food, flowed outward through veins, and was 'burned up' by the body's organs as fuel.">Galen’s theory</dfn> that blood was made in the liver and “burned up” by the body. Through careful experiments and dissection, Harvey proved that <strong>blood circulates around the body</strong> in a continuous loop.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">He demonstrated that the <strong>heart acts as a pump</strong>, forcing blood through the arteries and veins. He calculated the volume of blood pumped per heartbeat and showed it was far too much for the body to constantly produce and consume — it had to be the same blood going round and round.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Harvey’s publication and its limitations</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n13">Harvey published his work in 1628 in <em>‘An Anatomical Account of the Motion of the Heart and Blood in Animals’</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">However, his discovery had <strong>little practical use at the time</strong> because <dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of blood from one person into the veins of another.">blood transfusions</dfn> were not yet possible. Without knowledge of blood groups, transfusions were very dangerous. It would be centuries before his discovery could save lives directly.</p>
<p>There was also a gap in Harvey’s theory: he could not explain how blood passed from arteries to veins. Microscopes of his time were too weak to reveal <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny blood vessels that connect arteries to veins, allowing the exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and waste products between blood and body tissues.">capillaries</dfn> — the tiny vessels that complete the circuit. This gave opponents ammunition to attack his work, and he faced such ridicule that he went into semi-retirement. Capillaries were not found until after his death, when <strong>Marcello Malpighi</strong> identified them in <strong>1661</strong> using an improved microscope.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and state the year Harvey published his circulation findings and why this had limited immediate impact.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Harvey</div>
<p>Harvey proved that blood <strong>circulates</strong> around the body and that the heart acts as a <strong>pump</strong>. He published his findings in 1628. His work disproved Galen but had limited immediate practical impact because blood transfusions were not yet possible.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Ambroise Paré: The Father of Modern Surgery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Ambroise Paré was a French army surgeon who made big advances in surgical techniques. He regularly treated soldiers with gunshot wounds on the battlefield. The standard method was to burn wounds with boiling oil — a process called <dfn class="term" data-def="The medical practice of burning a wound with a hot iron or boiling oil to seal it and prevent infection.">cauterisation</dfn> — which was agonising for the patient.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">One day, Paré <strong>ran out of hot oil</strong> and had to improvise. He made a soothing cream from <strong>egg yolk, rose oil, and turpentine</strong>. To his surprise, this was far less painful and helped wounds heal much better than cauterisation.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Paré’s use of ligatures</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">Paré also promoted silk threads called <dfn class="term" data-def="Threads (often silk) used to tie off blood vessels during surgery to stop bleeding.">ligatures</dfn> to tie off arteries after amputation, instead of burning the wound shut. This was much less painful.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">However, ligatures carried a <strong>higher risk of infection</strong> because the threads could introduce germs into the wound. Without any understanding of germs or antiseptics, this was a serious drawback.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<p>Paré also designed <strong>artificial limbs</strong> (prosthetics) for soldiers who had lost arms or legs in battle.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Paré and the Bezoar Stone — a famous experiment</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p>The King of France believed in the healing power of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A stone-like mass formed in the stomach of a goat or other animal.">Bezoar Stone</dfn> — a stone from a goat’s stomach — which was widely believed to cure any poison.</p>
<p>Paré proposed a test. A condemned prisoner was offered a choice: die by hanging, or take a deadly poison and be treated with the Bezoar Stone. If he survived, he would go free. The prisoner chose the poison and the stone. He died in agony hours later, proving the stone had <strong>no healing power</strong>.</p>
<p>This grim experiment shows the Renaissance emphasis on <strong>testing ideas through observation</strong> rather than accepting traditional beliefs.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="From memory, name Paré's alternative to cauterisation and one other thing he is famous for, like disproving the Bezoar Stone.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Paré</div>
<p>Paré discovered — <strong>by chance</strong> — that a mixture of egg yolk, rose oil, and turpentine was better than cauterisation for treating gunshot wounds. He also promoted <strong>ligatures</strong> instead of burning to stop bleeding after amputation, though this increased infection risk. He designed artificial limbs and used scientific testing to disprove superstitious remedies like the Bezoar Stone.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Why Were These Figures Significant?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">You need to know not just <em>what</em> Vesalius, Paré, and Harvey discovered, but <em>why</em> their work mattered:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n24">
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1543</div>
<h4>Vesalius publishes <em>On the Fabric of the Human Body</em></h4>
<p>Proved Galen wrong in over 300 places. Established the principle that anatomy must be learned through human dissection, not ancient texts. The printing press spread his corrections across Europe.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1545 onwards</div>
<h4>Paré develops new surgical techniques</h4>
<p>Replaced cauterisation with a healing balm and promoted ligatures. Showed that chance observation and practical experience could improve medicine, even without formal theory.</p>
</div>
<div class="timeline-event">
<div class="timeline-date">1628</div>
<h4>Harvey publishes his work on blood circulation</h4>
<p>Proved the heart is a pump and blood circulates. Demonstrated that careful experimentation could overturn ideas accepted for over 1,000 years.</p>
</div>
</div>
<h2>How Quickly Did Medicine Change?</h2>
<p>Despite these breakthroughs, <strong>change was very slow</strong>. Their discoveries did not immediately transform everyday medical practice. In the hundred years after Paré’s death, thousands of people across Europe were still executed for <dfn class="term" data-def="The practice of accusing people (usually women) of using supernatural powers to cause harm.">witchcraft</dfn> — a stark reminder that superstition remained deeply rooted, even as science was advancing.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Vesalius, Paré, and Harvey moved medicine forward during the Renaissance. They challenged Galen’s teachings and showed that practical observation and experimentation mattered more than trusting ancient texts.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n27">
<li><strong>Vesalius corrected anatomy:</strong> He proved Galen wrong in over 300 places. His 1543 book, spread by the printing press, gave doctors accurate anatomy for the first time.</li>
<li><strong>Paré improved surgery through chance:</strong> Running out of hot oil led him to a better wound treatment. He introduced ligatures, though infection remained a problem.</li>
<li><strong>Harvey proved circulation:</strong> He showed blood circulates and the heart is a pump, disproving Galen. But practical benefits were limited until blood transfusions became possible centuries later.</li>
</ul>
