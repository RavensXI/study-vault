<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five Pillars of Sunni Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Islam is built on five core practices known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The five essential religious duties of Sunni Islam: Shahadah (declaration of faith), Salah (prayer), Zakah (almsgiving), Sawm (fasting) and Hajj (pilgrimage).">Five Pillars</dfn>. These are not optional extras &mdash; they are <dfn class="term" data-def="Fard — compulsory; an obligatory religious duty in Islam.">fard</dfn> (compulsory) for every adult Muslim of sound mind. Together they shape a Muslim&rsquo;s daily life, social relationships and relationship with Allah. The Prophet Muhammad described Islam as being &ldquo;built upon five things&rdquo; (Hadith, Sahih al-Bukhari). Surah 3:17&ndash;21 praises those who are patient, truthful, devout, and charitable &mdash; qualities embodied by the Five Pillars.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list all five pillars with their Arabic names and English meanings. Then state the Shi'a equivalent framework.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Five Pillars: <strong>Shahadah</strong> (declaration of faith), <strong>Salah</strong> (five daily prayers), <strong>Zakah</strong> (2.5% of savings given to those in need), <strong>Sawm</strong> (fasting during Ramadan), <strong>Hajj</strong> (pilgrimage to Makkah at least once). This is the foundational framework of Sunni practice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Shahadah &mdash; Declaration of Faith</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Islamic declaration of faith: 'There is no God but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah.' Reciting the Shahadah sincerely is the act of becoming Muslim.">Shahadah</dfn> is the declaration: &ldquo;There is no God but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah.&rdquo; It is the most fundamental act of Islamic faith. Reciting it sincerely and with understanding is the act that makes a person Muslim. It is whispered in a newborn&rsquo;s ear, said at regular intervals in prayer and at the hour of death. Surah 3:17&ndash;21 describes those who consistently bear witness (&ldquo;shahada&rdquo;) to Allah&rsquo;s oneness as among the most praiseworthy of believers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Salah &mdash; Prayer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Ritual prayer performed five times daily by Muslims, involving specific positions, recitations and conditions of purity.">Salah</dfn> is compulsory prayer five times a day: Fajr (before sunrise), Zuhr (after midday), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (after sunset) and Isha (night). Before praying, Muslims perform <dfn class="term" data-def="Ritual washing (ablution) carried out before prayer, involving washing the hands, face, arms, head and feet in a set order.">wudu</dfn> (ritual washing). On Fridays, Sunni Muslim men are expected to pray <dfn class="term" data-def="The Friday congregational prayer, compulsory for adult Muslim men, held in the mosque at midday on Fridays.">Jummah</dfn> (the Friday congregational prayer) at the mosque. Surah 29:45 states: &ldquo;Recite what has been revealed to you of the Scripture; keep up the prayer &mdash; prayer restrains outrageous and unacceptable behaviour.&rdquo; Surah 15:98&ndash;99 connects glorification of Allah in prayer with certainty of faith. Shi&rsquo;a Muslims perform the same five prayers but may combine them into three prayer times during the day.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Sawm &mdash; Fasting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fasting during the month of Ramadan — abstaining from food, drink, smoking and sexual relations from dawn to sunset.">Sawm</dfn> involves complete fasting during the holy month of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ninth month of the Islamic calendar, during which Muslims fast from dawn to sunset in obedience to Allah's command.">Ramadan</dfn>. Muslims abstain from all food, drink, smoking and sexual relations from dawn to sunset. Surah 2:183&ndash;185 commands: &ldquo;You who believe, fasting is prescribed for you, as it was prescribed for those before you, so that you may be mindful of God.&rdquo; Within Ramadan, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Night of Power — one of the last ten nights of Ramadan, believed to be the night on which the first verses of the Qur'an were revealed to Muhammad.">Laylat al-Qadr</dfn> (Night of Power) commemorates the first revelation of the Qur&rsquo;an to Muhammad and is described in the Qur&rsquo;an as better than a thousand months of worship. Those who cannot fast (due to illness, pregnancy or travel) may break their fast and compensate later. Ramadan ends with the festival of Id-ul-Fitr.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Zakah &mdash; Almsgiving</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Obligatory almsgiving in Islam — giving 2.5% of annual savings above the nisab threshold to those in need.">Zakah</dfn> means &ldquo;purification&rdquo; or &ldquo;growth.&rdquo; It requires every Muslim whose savings exceed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum level of wealth above which Zakah becomes compulsory, roughly equivalent to the value of 85 grams of gold.">nisab</dfn> (minimum threshold) to give 2.5% of their annual savings to those in need. Surah 9:58&ndash;60 specifies eight categories of those entitled to receive Zakah, including the poor, the indebted and travellers in need. Zakah purifies the giver&rsquo;s remaining wealth from greed, supports the poor and reduces inequality within the <dfn class="term" data-def="The worldwide community of Muslims, bound together by shared faith and practice.">ummah</dfn> (global Muslim community).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Hajj &mdash; Pilgrimage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The annual pilgrimage to Makkah, compulsory at least once in a lifetime for Muslims who are physically and financially able.">Hajj</dfn> takes place in the twelfth month of the Islamic calendar and must be performed at least once in a lifetime by every Muslim who is physically and financially able. Surah 2:124&ndash;130 describes Ibrahim and Isma&rsquo;il building the Ka&rsquo;aba and establishing the rites of pilgrimage. Surah 22:25&ndash;30 describes the sacred sites and the command to proclaim Hajj to all people. Hajj rites include walking around the Ka&rsquo;aba seven times (<dfn class="term" data-def="Circumambulation of the Ka'aba — walking around it seven times anticlockwise as part of the Hajj or Umrah rituals.">tawaf</dfn>), walking between the hills of Safa and Marwa, standing on the plain of Arafat (<dfn class="term" data-def="The climax of Hajj — standing on the plain of Arafat in prayer and meditation, representing the Day of Judgement.">wuquf</dfn>), and symbolically stoning the devil. Hajj creates a profound sense of equality and unity &mdash; all pilgrims wear identical white garments (<dfn class="term" data-def="The two unsewn white garments worn by all male pilgrims during Hajj, symbolising equality before Allah and the state of ritual purity.">ihram</dfn>), erasing distinctions of wealth and rank.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi&rsquo;a Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Shi&rsquo;a Muslims have their own framework of obligatory acts. While the Five Pillars are broadly shared, Shi&rsquo;a Islam lists <dfn class="term" data-def="The ten compulsory acts of worship in Shi'a Islam, which include the Five Pillars plus additional duties such as Khums, Jihad, Amr-bil-Maroof and Nahy-anil-Munkar.">Ten Obligatory Acts</dfn> (Furu ad-Din &mdash; branches of religion). These include Salah, Sawm, Zakah and Hajj (shared with Sunni Islam), but add several distinctively Shi&rsquo;a duties: <dfn class="term" data-def="A Shi'a Islamic tax of one-fifth (20%) of annual surplus income, paid to religious scholars for charitable and religious purposes. Distinct from Sunni Zakah.">Khums</dfn> (a 20% tax on surplus income), Jihad (striving in the way of Allah), <em>Amr-bil-Maroof</em> (commanding what is right), and <em>Nahy-anil-Munkar</em> (forbidding what is wrong). Khums is particularly significant: Surah 8:41 specifies that a fifth of certain gains should be set aside &ldquo;for Allah and the Messenger,&rdquo; which Shi&rsquo;a Muslims understand as a continuing obligation paid to the Imam&rsquo;s representatives (the religious scholars).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state what Khums is and how it differs from Zakah in terms of percentage and recipients.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Khums (20% of surplus income) is a Shi&rsquo;a-specific obligation distinct from Sunni Zakah (2.5% of savings above nisab). Khums is paid to the Imam&rsquo;s representatives (religious scholars) for religious and charitable purposes. Both Zakah and Khums serve to reduce inequality within the ummah, but they operate on different rates and through different channels.</p>
</div>

The Ten Obligatory Acts of Shi&rsquo;a Islam (Wajibat) include the Five Pillars (Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Hajj, and Shahadah through Walayah) plus additional obligations: Khums (20% levy), Jihad, commanding good, forbidding evil, and love/disavowal (Tawallah and Tabarra). Some scholars count the Ten differently, but Khums is the distinctive Shi&rsquo;a addition. The underlying logic is the same: both Sunni and Shi&rsquo;a Muslims structure their lives around prescribed acts of worship that express submission to Allah.</p>
