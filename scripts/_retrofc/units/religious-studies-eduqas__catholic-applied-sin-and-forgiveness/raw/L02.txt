<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Salvation and Redemption</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="In Catholic theology, the rescue of humanity from sin and its consequences through the life, death, resurrection and ascension of Jesus Christ.">Salvation</dfn> is the central drama of Catholic faith. Human beings, the Church teaches, are created by God in his image (Genesis 1:26&ndash;27), but through Original Sin have become separated from God and subject to death. God&rsquo;s response is the sending of his Son: &ldquo;For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life&rdquo; (John 3:16). Salvation is not something human beings can achieve by their own efforts &mdash; it is a free gift of God&rsquo;s grace, made possible by the life, death, resurrection and ascension of Jesus Christ.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The act of buying back or setting free; in Catholic theology, the liberation of humanity from sin and death through Christ's sacrifice on the Cross.">Redemption</dfn> is the specific theological term for how salvation is achieved. It means &ldquo;buying back&rdquo; or &ldquo;setting free.&rdquo; The Catholic tradition uses several models to understand what the Cross accomplished: Anselm&rsquo;s satisfaction theory (Christ&rsquo;s death satisfied the debt of honour owed to God by human sin); Aquinas&rsquo;s account of Christ as the perfect sacrifice offered to God on humanity&rsquo;s behalf; and the liberation model (Christ&rsquo;s resurrection liberates humanity from the dominion of sin and death). All of these models point to the same conviction: that what human beings could not do for themselves, God did through Christ.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words what redemption means in Catholic theology, naming at least one theological model used to explain how it works.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Redemption means God&rsquo;s act of setting humanity free from sin and death through Christ&rsquo;s life, death, resurrection and ascension. It is a free gift of grace, not something human beings can earn. Key scriptural texts include John 3:16 and John 14:6 (&ldquo;I am the way, the truth and the life; no one comes to the Father except through me&rdquo;).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Grace and Human Freedom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A key question in Catholic theology is the relationship between <dfn class="term" data-def="God's free and undeserved gift of participation in the divine life, which enables human beings to be saved; in Catholic theology, the interior help of God.">grace</dfn> and human freedom. The Church teaches that salvation requires both: God&rsquo;s grace is the prior and necessary gift that makes salvation possible, but human beings must freely respond to it. The Catechism (CCC 1996&ndash;2005) describes grace as &ldquo;the free and undeserved help that God gives us to respond to his call.&rdquo; This means that salvation is not automatic: human beings can freely reject God&rsquo;s offer, which is why the Church affirms the reality of hell as the consequence of finally and freely refusing God&rsquo;s love.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The compatibility of hell and a God of unconditional love is a classic theological challenge. The Catholic response is that God does not send anyone to hell &mdash; rather, hell is the logical consequence of persistently refusing the love and mercy that God offers. God&rsquo;s love is unconditional; human beings can nonetheless use their freedom to reject it. The Catechism (CCC 1033) states that hell is not God&rsquo;s will for anyone: &ldquo;God predestines no one to go to hell.&rdquo; The Church also teaches that God&rsquo;s mercy is universal &mdash; available to every person &mdash; while leaving open the question of how many ultimately accept it.</p>

Pope Francis has repeatedly emphasised the universality of God&rsquo;s mercy &mdash; a theme developed especially in his Bull of Indiction for the Jubilee Year of Mercy, <em>Misericordiae Vultus</em> (2015). While the Church insists that Christ is the unique mediator of salvation (John 14:6), it also teaches that God can bring people to salvation by ways known to him alone (CCC 1257&ndash;1261 &mdash; the principle of &ldquo;subsistit in&rdquo; and the broader understanding of implicit faith). This is a carefully maintained tension: the uniqueness of Christ&rsquo;s redemptive work is affirmed, but so is the breadth of God&rsquo;s saving intention.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Church: One, Holy, Catholic, Apostolic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The Catholic Church describes itself using four attributes, the &ldquo;four marks of the Church,&rdquo; affirmed in the Nicene Creed: &ldquo;one, holy, catholic and apostolic.&rdquo; The Church is <em>one</em> because it is united under one Lord, one faith, one baptism (Ephesians 4:5) and one visible head (the Pope). It is <em>holy</em> because it is sanctified by Christ and called to holiness, though its individual members remain sinners. It is <em>catholic</em> (from the Greek <em>katholikos</em> &mdash; universal) because it is the Church for all people in all places and times. It is <em>apostolic</em> because it stands in direct succession from the apostles: the bishops are the successors of the apostles, and the Pope is the successor of Peter, to whom Christ said, &ldquo;On this rock I will build my Church&rdquo; (Matthew 16:18).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Church as Body of Christ and People of God</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Second Vatican Council&rsquo;s <em>Lumen Gentium</em> (1964) offered two complementary images of the Church: the <dfn class="term" data-def="A scriptural image of the Church (1 Corinthians 12; Romans 12) in which Christ is the head and all baptised believers are members, each with different gifts serving the whole.">Body of Christ</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Vatican II image of the Church emphasising that all baptised Catholics, clergy and laity alike, share equally in the dignity of God's people and the mission of the Church.">People of God</dfn>. The Body of Christ image (from 1 Corinthians 12 and Romans 12) emphasises the organic unity of the Church: different members with different gifts, all necessary, all serving the one body of which Christ is the head. The People of God image emphasises the shared dignity and vocation of all the baptised &mdash; clergy and laity alike &mdash; in contrast to a purely hierarchical vision of the Church.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The Catechism (CCC 846&ndash;848) addresses the ancient maxim &ldquo;outside the Church there is no salvation&rdquo; (<em>extra ecclesiam nulla salus</em>, attributed to Cyprian of Carthage, third century). The Catechism teaches that this does not mean that non-Catholics are automatically damned. Rather, it means that all salvation comes through Christ and his Church &mdash; but God is not &ldquo;bound&rdquo; to his sacraments and can give grace to those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ. The Church is the ordinary means of salvation, but God&rsquo;s mercy is not limited to it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what the Catechism (CCC 846–848) teaches about the saying 'outside the Church there is no salvation'. Does it mean all non-Catholics are damned?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Catechism (CCC 846&ndash;848) teaches that all salvation comes through Christ and his Church, but that God can give grace to those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ. &ldquo;Outside the Church no salvation&rdquo; does not mean automatic damnation for non-Catholics &mdash; it affirms the Church as the ordinary means of salvation while maintaining God&rsquo;s universal saving will.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Mary as Model of the Church</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><em>Lumen Gentium</em> (chapter 8) presents <dfn class="term" data-def="The title given to Mary in Catholic theology as the perfect model and image of what the whole Church is called to be: responsive to God's grace, faithful and motherly towards all believers.">Mary as model of the Church</dfn>. Mary&rsquo;s &ldquo;fiat&rdquo; &mdash; her willing &ldquo;yes&rdquo; to God at the Annunciation (Luke 1:38) &mdash; is the paradigm of the response that every believer and the whole Church is called to give to God. She accepted God&rsquo;s grace perfectly, without reservation, and bore Christ into the world. In this way she models what the Church is: the community that receives grace, bears Christ within itself and brings him to the world.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Architecture of Catholic Worship: Altar, Font and Orientation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The physical design of a Catholic church is itself a theological statement. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The table at the front of a Catholic church at which the Eucharist (Mass) is celebrated; the centre of Catholic worship, symbolising both the table of the Last Supper and the altar of sacrifice.">altar</dfn> is the focal point of every Catholic church &mdash; the table on which the Eucharist is celebrated. In Catholic theology, the Mass makes present the once-for-all sacrifice of Calvary: the altar is both the table of the Lord&rsquo;s Supper and the altar of sacrifice. The altar&rsquo;s central position reinforces the Catholic conviction that the Eucharist is the &ldquo;source and summit of the Christian life&rdquo; (Vatican II, <em>Lumen Gentium</em> 11).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The basin of water at the entrance of a Catholic church used for baptism; its position at the entrance symbolises that baptism is the gateway into the Church and into salvation.">font</dfn> is typically placed near the entrance of the church, symbolising that baptism is the gateway into the Church &mdash; the sacrament through which a person first dies and rises with Christ and is incorporated into his Body. The traditional orientation of a Catholic church (east-facing, with the altar at the liturgical east) symbolises prayer towards the rising sun as a sign of the risen Christ and the hope of his return. Lady chapels (dedicated to Mary) and confessional boxes (for the sacrament of Reconciliation) are additional architectural features that embody Catholic theology: Marian devotion and the ongoing availability of God&rsquo;s forgiveness to sinners.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20">Every element of a Catholic church&rsquo;s design is intended to facilitate a particular experience of worship and draw the worshipper towards God. The nave (the long main body of the church) draws the eye and the movement of the congregation towards the altar and the sanctuary &mdash; from the world outside into the sacred space. High ceilings and stained glass windows suggest transcendence: God is above and beyond. Statues of saints provide examples of holiness; the Stations of the Cross around the walls enable the faithful to walk meditatively through the Passion narrative. The tabernacle &mdash; where the consecrated Eucharist is reserved &mdash; is marked by a lit sanctuary lamp, indicating the real presence of Christ. In British Catholic churches, all of these elements are typically present, making the building a &ldquo;catechism in stone&rdquo; that teaches the faith even to those who cannot read.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Salvation is God&rsquo;s free gift through the life, death, resurrection and ascension of Christ (John 3:16). Redemption is the act of buying humanity back from sin and death. Grace is God&rsquo;s undeserved help; human beings must freely accept it &mdash; which is why the Church affirms the real possibility of hell as the consequence of freely rejecting God.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The Church is described as one, holy, catholic and apostolic, and as both the Body of Christ (CCC 1 Corinthians 12) and the People of God (<em>Lumen Gentium</em>, Vatican II, 1964). CCC 846&ndash;848 teaches that all salvation comes through Christ and the Church, but that God&rsquo;s mercy is not confined to those who formally belong to it.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The architecture of a Catholic church &mdash; altar, font, orientation, Lady chapel, tabernacle, Stations of the Cross &mdash; embodies Catholic theology and facilitates worship. The altar (Eucharist as source and summit), the font (baptism as gateway) and the tabernacle (real presence) are the three most theologically significant features.</li>
</ul>
