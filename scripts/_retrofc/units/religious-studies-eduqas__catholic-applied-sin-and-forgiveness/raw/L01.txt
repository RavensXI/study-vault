<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Crime and Sin: What Is the Difference?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A fundamental distinction in Catholic moral theology is between <dfn class="term" data-def="An act that breaks the law of the state and may result in legal punishment.">crime</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="An act that breaks the moral law of God, harming one's relationship with God, others and oneself.">sin</dfn>. A crime is an act that violates the law of the state and carries a legal penalty. Sin is an act that violates the moral law of God &mdash; it harms the relationship between the person and God, between the person and others, and within the person themselves (CCC 1849). While many crimes are also sins (murder, theft, fraud), and many sins are also crimes, the two categories are not identical. Some sins &mdash; such as envy or hatred &mdash; are not crimes. Some acts that are legal in a given country may still be judged sinful by the Church (for example, abortion or assisted dying in places where these are permitted by law). This distinction matters because Catholic moral reasoning is not simply the same as law-abidingness: it asks what is right before God, not merely what the state permits.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Catholic moral theology also distinguishes between <dfn class="term" data-def="The view that moral rules are absolute and apply to all people in all situations regardless of consequences.">absolutist approaches to morality</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The view that moral judgements depend on particular circumstances and that no moral rule applies in every situation.">relativistic approaches</dfn>. Natural Law theory (developed especially by Thomas Aquinas) is absolutist: certain acts are intrinsically good or evil, regardless of intention or outcome. The primary precepts of Natural Law &mdash; including the preservation of life and the prohibition of unjust killing &mdash; are held to be binding on all people at all times. Relativism, by contrast, holds that moral judgements are shaped by culture, circumstance and individual choice. The Catholic Church consistently rejects moral relativism as a basis for law or ethics, though it acknowledges that circumstances can affect the gravity of a sinful act.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the difference between crime and sin in Catholic teaching. Give one example of each that the other category does NOT cover.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Crime breaks the law of the state; sin breaks the law of God. The two overlap but are not identical: some sins (envy) are not crimes; some legal acts may be considered sinful. Catholic moral reasoning uses Natural Law to judge actions independently of what the state permits &mdash; an absolutist approach in which some acts are intrinsically wrong regardless of circumstance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Purposes of Punishment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Catholic Church teaches that punishment of criminals can serve several legitimate purposes. The Catechism (CCC 2266) identifies the primary purposes as: protecting society from harmful acts, preserving public order, and providing for the amendment (reform) of the offender. In Catholic moral theology, four rationales for punishment are commonly distinguished:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that punishment is deserved payment for wrongdoing; the offender should face consequences proportionate to the harm caused.">Retribution</dfn> is the view that punishment is a form of just desert &mdash; the offender has done wrong and must face proportionate consequences. The Old Testament principle of &ldquo;an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth&rdquo; (Exodus 21:24) expresses this logic. However, Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount challenged this approach: &ldquo;You have heard that it was said, &lsquo;An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.&rsquo; But I say to you, do not resist an evildoer&rdquo; (Matthew 5:38&ndash;39). Catholic moral theology accepts that retribution has a legitimate role in maintaining justice, but insists it must be proportionate and directed at justice, not mere vengeance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that punishment discourages others (and the offender) from committing similar crimes in future.">Deterrence</dfn> aims to prevent future crime by making the consequences of offending sufficiently unappealing. <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that punishment should aim to reform the offender, enabling them to return to society as a law-abiding member.">Rehabilitation</dfn> (or reformation) is the aim of changing the offender&rsquo;s behaviour and attitudes so that they can live peacefully in society. The Church strongly emphasises rehabilitation, because it treats the offender as a person with an inherent dignity who remains capable of repentance and change. Prison chaplains &mdash; many of them Catholic priests &mdash; play an important role in British prisons in supporting the spiritual welfare and rehabilitation of those in custody.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">A central tension in Christian ethics is the relationship between forgiveness and justice. Jesus placed extraordinary emphasis on forgiveness. When Peter asked how many times he should forgive a brother who sins against him &mdash; suggesting the generous number of seven &mdash; Jesus replied, &ldquo;not seven times, but seventy-seven times&rdquo; (Matthew 18:21&ndash;22). This is usually understood to mean there should be no limit to forgiveness. Matthew 6:14&ndash;15 states: &ldquo;For if you forgive others their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you, but if you do not forgive others their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.&rdquo;</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Yet the Church does not interpret forgiveness as eliminating the need for punishment. Forgiveness operates at the personal and spiritual level &mdash; releasing the victim from bitterness and restoring the possibility of relationship with God &mdash; while justice operates at the social and legal level. A Catholic victim of crime may genuinely forgive their offender and still believe that a just punishment is appropriate. The two are not in conflict: justice ensures the common good; forgiveness heals the soul.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Catholic Teaching on Capital Punishment: The Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Catholic Church&rsquo;s teaching on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The legally authorised execution of a person as punishment for a crime; also called the death penalty.">death penalty</dfn> (capital punishment) has developed significantly over time, and this development is itself an important example of how the ordinary Magisterium can change its emphasis while claiming continuity with underlying principles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In earlier centuries, many Catholic thinkers accepted the death penalty as a legitimate tool of the state. Augustine, in his letters to Macedonius (Letters 134 and 153), argued that the state could rightly execute criminals and that doing so could even be an act of mercy &mdash; preventing further sin. This position was standard Catholic moral teaching for much of the Church&rsquo;s history. The general argument was that society had the right to protect itself, and that the death penalty could serve retributive and deterrent purposes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Pope John Paul II&rsquo;s encyclical <em>Evangelium Vitae</em> (1995), paragraph 56, marked a significant shift. It did not absolutely condemn the death penalty but argued that cases in which it was truly necessary &ldquo;are very rare, if not practically non-existent&rdquo; in modern societies, because modern states have other means of protecting society (such as secure incarceration). The encyclical clearly preferred non-lethal means and presented the death penalty as acceptable only in extreme necessity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state: what did Pope John Paul II teach about capital punishment in Evangelium Vitae 56 (1995), and how did Pope Francis update this teaching in 2018?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pope John Paul II (<em>Evangelium Vitae</em> 56, 1995) taught that capital punishment was acceptable only in extreme necessity and is practically never needed in modern societies. In 2018, Pope Francis updated the Catechism paragraph 2267 to declare the death penalty &ldquo;inadmissible&rdquo; in all circumstances, as an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the human person. This is the current Catholic position.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Current Catholic Position: CCC 2267 (2018)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In August 2018, Pope Francis approved a revision of the Catechism of the Catholic Church paragraph 2267, which now states that the death penalty is &ldquo;inadmissible because it is an attack on the inviolability and dignity of the person&rdquo; and that the Church works for its abolition worldwide. This represents a definitive development of the Church&rsquo;s ordinary Magisterium on this question. The reasoning given is that modern understanding of human dignity (itself a development rooted in the Catechism&rsquo;s teaching on the &ldquo;imago Dei&rdquo;) makes the execution of criminals incompatible with the Gospel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">This means that Catholic students must be careful: older formulations of the Catholic position on capital punishment (including the pre-2018 version of CCC 2267) are now superseded. The current position is clear &mdash; the death penalty is inadmissible &mdash; while the Church also acknowledges that this is a development from earlier positions held for much of its history.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n19">Arguments sometimes advanced in favour of capital punishment include: the Old Testament principle of proportionate justice (&ldquo;Whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed,&rdquo; Genesis 9:6, cited by some as a divine mandate for the death penalty); the deterrent effect on potential offenders; the permanent protection of society from those who cannot be safely contained; and the classical argument associated with Augustine that the state acts as God&rsquo;s servant in executing justice (Romans 13:4).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Arguments against capital punishment &mdash; now the position of the Catholic Church &mdash; include: the dignity of every human person as made in God&rsquo;s image cannot be forfeited, even by the most serious crime; execution is irreversible and miscarriages of justice (the execution of innocent people) cannot be corrected; modern secure incarceration achieves the protective function without killing; execution prevents the possibility of repentance and reform (which the Church holds open even for the most serious offenders); and non-religious critics note that statistical evidence does not support the deterrent effect of the death penalty. From a secular human-rights perspective, capital punishment is widely regarded as a violation of the right to life.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Crime breaks the law of the state; sin breaks the law of God. The Catholic Church uses Natural Law to judge acts independently of what the state permits. Punishment can serve retributive, deterrent and rehabilitative purposes, with the Church strongly emphasising rehabilitation and the offender&rsquo;s dignity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Jesus taught unlimited forgiveness (Matthew 18:21&ndash;22; Matthew 6:14&ndash;15), but forgiveness and justice are not the same: the Church supports just punishment while insisting on the offender&rsquo;s inherent dignity and the possibility of reform.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The current Catholic position on capital punishment is that it is &ldquo;inadmissible&rdquo; in all circumstances (CCC 2267, updated 2018 by Pope Francis), because it violates the inviolable dignity of the human person. This is a development from the earlier position in <em>Evangelium Vitae</em> 56 (Pope John Paul II, 1995), which allowed for its use in extreme necessity.</li>
</ul>
