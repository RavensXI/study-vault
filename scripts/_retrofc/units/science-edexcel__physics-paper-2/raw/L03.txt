--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Waves?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. Examples include sound waves, water waves, and electromagnetic waves.">wave</dfn> is a disturbance that transfers energy and information from one place to another without transferring matter. When a wave passes through water, the water molecules oscillate up and down, but the water itself does not travel with the wave — it is the wave pattern that moves.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Evidence for this comes from watching a floating object on water: it bobs up and down as waves pass, but it does not move forward with the waves. Similarly, sound waves cause air particles to vibrate back and forth, but the air itself does not travel from the source to the listener.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What do waves transfer, and what do they not transfer?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Waves transfer energy and information without transferring matter. The particles of the medium oscillate about a fixed position.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Wave Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">All waves can be described using these key terms:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum displacement of a point on a wave from its rest (equilibrium) position. Measured in metres (m). Larger amplitude means a wave carries more energy.">Amplitude</dfn> is the maximum displacement from the rest position. <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance from one point on a wave to the equivalent point on the next wave, such as crest to crest or trough to trough. Measured in metres (m).">Wavelength</dfn> (\(\lambda\)) is the distance from one point on a wave to the same point on the next wave (e.g. crest to crest). <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of complete waves passing a point per second. Measured in hertz (Hz), where 1 Hz equals one wave per second.">Frequency</dfn> (\(f\)) is the number of complete waves passing a point per second, measured in hertz (Hz). <dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken for one complete wave to pass a given point. Measured in seconds (s). Period = 1 / frequency.">Period</dfn> is the time for one complete wave to pass a point.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The speed at which a wave travels through a medium. Measured in metres per second (m/s). Wave speed = frequency × wavelength.">Wave speed</dfn> (\(v\)) is how fast the wave travels. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An imaginary line joining points on a wave that are in phase (e.g. all the crests). Wavefronts are perpendicular to the direction of wave travel.">wavefront</dfn> is an imaginary line joining all the points at the same phase of a wave.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Transverse and Longitudinal Waves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">There are two types of wave:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the oscillations (vibrations) are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Examples include electromagnetic waves, water surface waves, and waves on a string.">transverse wave</dfn>, the oscillations are perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of energy transfer. Examples include all electromagnetic waves, water surface waves, and waves on a string.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A wave where the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. The wave consists of compressions and rarefactions. Sound is a longitudinal wave.">longitudinal wave</dfn>, the oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer. The wave creates alternating <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are pushed closer together, creating an area of higher pressure.">compressions</dfn> (where particles are pushed together) and <dfn class="term" data-def="A region in a longitudinal wave where particles are spread further apart, creating an area of lower pressure.">rarefactions</dfn> (where particles are spread apart). Sound is a longitudinal wave. Seismic P-waves are also longitudinal.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">$$v = f \times \lambda$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">where \(v\) is wave speed in m/s, \(f\) is frequency in Hz, and \(\lambda\) is wavelength in metres (m). This equation applies to all types of wave.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Wave speed can also be calculated using:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">$$v = \frac{x}{t}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">where \(x\) is the distance travelled in metres and \(t\) is the time taken in seconds.</p>
  </div></div>

  <p>You must recall two wave equations: \(v = f \times \lambda\) and \(v = \frac{x}{t}\). Both are used in the exam and must be recalled from memory.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Electromagnetic Spectrum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuous range of electromagnetic waves ordered by wavelength and frequency. From longest wavelength to shortest: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays.">electromagnetic (EM) spectrum</dfn> is a continuous range of waves. All EM waves are transverse, transfer energy from source to observer, and travel at the same speed in a vacuum — the speed of light, approximately \(3 \times 10^8\) m/s.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The spectrum is ordered from longest wavelength (lowest frequency) to shortest wavelength (highest frequency):</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Radio waves → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Our eyes can only detect a narrow range — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The small part of the electromagnetic spectrum that human eyes can detect, ranging from red (longest wavelength, ~700 nm) to violet (shortest wavelength, ~400 nm).">visible spectrum</dfn>, which ranges from red (longest wavelength) through orange, yellow, green, blue, and indigo to violet (shortest wavelength).</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Radio waves:</strong> broadcasting, communications, and satellite transmissions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Microwaves:</strong> cooking, mobile phone communications, and satellite transmissions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Infrared:</strong> cooking, thermal imaging, short-range communications, optical fibres, TV remote controls, and security systems.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Visible light:</strong> vision, photography, and illumination.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Ultraviolet:</strong> security marking, fluorescent lamps, detecting forged banknotes, and disinfecting water.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>X-rays:</strong> medical imaging, airport security scanners, and observing internal structures.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Gamma rays:</strong> sterilising food and medical equipment, detecting cancer, and cancer treatment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Microwaves:</strong> internal heating of body cells. <strong>Infrared:</strong> skin burns. <strong>Ultraviolet:</strong> damage to surface cells and eyes, potentially leading to skin cancer and eye conditions. <strong>X-rays and gamma rays:</strong> can cause mutation or damage to cells inside the body.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Refraction of Waves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35">When a wave crosses the boundary between two different substances, its speed changes. If the wave hits the boundary at an angle (not along the normal), this change in speed causes a change in direction — this is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The change in direction of a wave as it passes from one medium to another, caused by a change in speed. Waves bend towards the normal when slowing down and away from the normal when speeding up.">refraction</dfn>.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n36">Different substances may absorb, transmit, refract, or reflect waves in ways that vary with wavelength. This is why a glass prism can split white light into the visible spectrum — different colours (wavelengths) are refracted by different amounts.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n37" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: List the 7 types of EM wave in order from longest to shortest wavelength.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum (\(3 \times 10^8\) m/s), are transverse, and can be reflected, refracted, and absorbed. The danger increases with frequency.</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n38">
  <p><strong>Exam Tip:</strong> When describing the EM spectrum, state the correct order and mention that all EM waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum. For wave calculations, make sure you rearrange \(v = f \lambda\) correctly and convert units (e.g. kHz to Hz, nm to m) before substituting.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion" data-narration-id="n39">
  <p>Waves transfer energy without transferring matter and come in two types: transverse (oscillations perpendicular to direction) and longitudinal (oscillations parallel to direction). The wave equation \(v = f \lambda\) links speed, frequency, and wavelength. The electromagnetic spectrum is a continuous range from radio waves to gamma rays, all travelling at the speed of light in a vacuum but differing in wavelength, frequency, uses, and dangers.</p>
</div>
