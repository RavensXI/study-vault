--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Force?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A push or a pull that acts on an object due to the interaction with another object. Forces are measured in newtons (N) and are vector quantities.">force</dfn> is a push or a pull that acts on an object. Forces are measured in newtons (N). Every force arises because two objects are interacting — either by touching or at a distance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Forces that act between objects that are physically touching, such as friction, normal contact force, tension, and air resistance.">Contact forces</dfn> act when objects are physically in contact. Examples include friction, normal contact force, tension, and air resistance. <dfn class="term" data-def="Forces that act between objects without them touching, such as gravitational, electrostatic, and magnetic forces. Each is linked to a field around the object.">Non-contact forces</dfn> act at a distance through fields — gravitational force, electrostatic force, and magnetic force are all non-contact forces.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: What are the three types of non-contact force?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Forces always come in pairs. When two objects interact, each exerts a force on the other. These interaction pairs can be represented as vectors.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Scalars and Vectors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has magnitude (size) only, with no direction. Examples include mass, speed, distance, energy, and temperature.">scalar</dfn> quantity has magnitude only. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has both magnitude (size) and a specific direction. Examples include force, velocity, displacement, acceleration, and momentum.">vector</dfn> quantity has both magnitude and direction. Force is a vector — it has a size and acts in a particular direction. Mass is a scalar — it has only size.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Key vector quantities include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, and momentum. Key scalar quantities include distance, speed, mass, energy, and temperature.</p>

The length of each arrow represents the magnitude of the force and the direction shows which way it acts.">free body diagram</dfn> shows all the forces acting on a single object. Each force is drawn as an arrow starting from the object. The length of the arrow represents the size of the force, and the direction of the arrow shows which way the force acts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">When the arrows are equal and opposite, the forces are balanced and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The single force that has the same effect as all the individual forces acting on an object combined. Found by adding forces in the same direction and subtracting forces in opposite directions.">resultant force</dfn> is zero. When they are unequal, there is a net (resultant) force and the object will accelerate.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Newton's First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="If the resultant force on an object is zero, a stationary object stays still and a moving object continues at a constant velocity. If the resultant force is not zero, the object accelerates.">Newton's first law</dfn> states that if the resultant force acting on an object is zero, the object will remain stationary or continue to move at a constant velocity in a straight line. If the resultant force is not zero, the object will accelerate — its speed or direction (or both) will change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: What happens to an object when the resultant force is zero, and when it is not zero?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An object moving at constant velocity has a resultant force of zero. It does not need a force to keep it moving — only to change its motion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Newton's Second Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Newton's second law tells us that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">$$F = m \times a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">where \(F\) is the resultant force in newtons (N), \(m\) is the mass in kilograms (kg), and \(a\) is the acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Weight and Gravitational Field Strength</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The force acting on an object due to gravity. It is measured in newtons (N) and depends on mass and gravitational field strength. Weight = mass × gravitational field strength.">Weight</dfn> is the force of gravity acting on an object. It is a vector quantity measured in newtons using a newton meter (spring balance). The equation is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">$$W = m \times g$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">where \(W\) is weight in newtons (N), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(g\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of gravity per unit mass at a point in a gravitational field, measured in newtons per kilogram (N/kg). On Earth, g is approximately 9.8 N/kg.">gravitational field strength</dfn> in N/kg. On Earth, \(g \approx 9.8\) N/kg.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Mass is a scalar — it stays the same everywhere. Weight is a vector — it changes depending on the gravitational field strength.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Newton's Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Newton's third law states that whenever two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. These forces act on different objects, are the same type of force, and are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">For example, when you push against a wall, the wall pushes back on you with an equal force. When Earth pulls you downward with gravity, you pull Earth upward with the same gravitational force.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Give two examples of Newton's third law pairs and state what type of force each pair involves.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Newton's third law pairs are always the same type of force, act on two different objects, are equal in size, and act in opposite directions.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
      <p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of an object. Defined as the ratio of force to acceleration (m = F/a). Objects with greater inertial mass need a larger force to achieve the same acceleration.">Inertial mass</dfn> is a measure of how difficult it is to change the velocity of an object. It is defined as the ratio of force over acceleration:</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n26">$$m = \frac{F}{a}$$</p>
      <p data-narration-id="n27">An object with a large inertial mass requires a large force to produce even a small acceleration. This is why it is much harder to push a loaded trolley than an empty one.</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Circular Motion</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n29">An object moving in a circle at constant speed is constantly changing direction, so its velocity is changing. This means the object is accelerating, even though its speed stays the same.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">For an object to move in a circle, there must be a resultant force acting towards the centre of the circle. This force is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The resultant force that acts towards the centre of a circle, keeping an object moving in a circular path. Without it, the object would travel in a straight line.">centripetal force</dfn>. Without this force, the object would move in a straight line (Newton's first law). The centripetal force could be provided by tension, gravity, friction, or another force depending on the situation.</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n31">
  <p><strong>Exam Tip:</strong> When asked about Newton's laws, always identify the forces involved and state whether the resultant force is zero or not. For F = ma calculations, remember that F is the <em>resultant</em> force, not just any single force. Show your working clearly and include units in every step.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion" data-narration-id="n32">
  <p>Forces are vector quantities that arise from interactions between objects. Newton's three laws explain how forces affect motion: the first law links zero resultant force to constant velocity, the second law quantifies the relationship \(F = ma\), and the third law states that forces always come in equal and opposite pairs. Understanding free body diagrams and the difference between mass and weight is essential for tackling force calculations in the exam.</p>
</div>
