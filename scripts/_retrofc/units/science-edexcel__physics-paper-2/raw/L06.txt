<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Motor Effect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a conductor carrying an electric current is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a force. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.">motor effect</dfn>. The force arises because the magnetic field around the current-carrying wire interacts with the external magnetic field from the permanent magnet. An equal and opposite force also acts on the magnet itself — this is Newton's third law in action.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The motor effect only occurs when the current and the magnetic field are not parallel. The force is greatest when the current is at right angles (perpendicular) to the magnetic field. If the current is parallel to the field, there is no force.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three things does Fleming's left-hand rule represent, and which finger is which?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force. The direction of this force can be found using Fleming's left-hand rule.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Fleming's Left-Hand Rule</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A rule used to find the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field: the thumb represents force (motion), the first finger represents the magnetic field, and the second finger represents current.">Fleming's left-hand rule</dfn> allows you to work out the direction of the force (and therefore the direction the conductor moves) when the current and magnetic field directions are known. Hold your left hand so that the thumb, first finger, and second finger are all at right angles to each other:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Thumb</strong> = direction of the <strong>Force</strong> (motion)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>First finger</strong> = direction of the magnetic <strong>Field</strong> (north to south)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Second finger</strong> = direction of the <strong>Current</strong> (conventional, positive to negative)</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n10">If either the current or the magnetic field direction is reversed, the force reverses too. If both are reversed, the force stays the same.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Calculating the Force on a Conductor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The size of the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field can be calculated using the equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">$$F = B \times I \times l$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Where \(F\) is the force in newtons (N), \(B\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the strength of a magnetic field, measured in tesla (T).">magnetic flux density</dfn> in tesla (T), \(I\) is the current in amperes (A), and \(l\) is the length of the conductor in the field in metres (m).</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A wire of length 0.25 m carries a current of 4 A at right angles to a magnetic field of flux density 0.3 T. Calculate the force on the wire.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">\(F = B \times I \times l = 0.3 \times 4 \times 0.25 = 0.3\) N</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The force on the wire is 0.3 N.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the equation F = BIl and state what each symbol stands for, including units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>\(F = B \times I \times l\) — force (N) = magnetic flux density (T) × current (A) × length (m). This equation only applies when the current is at right angles to the magnetic field.</p>
</div>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">How an Electric Motor Works</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that converts electrical energy into kinetic energy using the motor effect — a current-carrying coil rotates in a magnetic field.">electric motor</dfn> uses the motor effect to produce rotation. A rectangular coil of wire is placed between the poles of a permanent magnet. When current flows through the coil, one side of the coil is pushed up and the other side is pushed down (by Fleming's left-hand rule), making the coil spin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A split-ring device in a motor that reverses the direction of current every half turn, keeping the coil spinning in the same direction.">split-ring commutator</dfn> reverses the direction of the current every half turn, ensuring that the forces on each side of the coil continue to push in the same rotational direction. Without the commutator, the coil would stop and reverse after each half turn.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Electromagnetic Induction</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of generating a potential difference (voltage) across a conductor by changing the magnetic field around it or by moving the conductor through a magnetic field.">Electromagnetic induction</dfn> is the process of generating a potential difference (and, if the circuit is complete, a current) by moving a conductor through a magnetic field, or by changing the magnetic field around a conductor. This is the principle behind generators and transformers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The size and direction of the induced potential difference depend on several factors:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The <strong>speed of movement</strong> — faster movement induces a larger potential difference</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The <strong>strength of the magnetic field</strong> — a stronger field induces a larger potential difference</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">The <strong>number of turns</strong> on the coil — more turns induce a larger potential difference</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The <strong>direction of movement</strong> or field change — reversing the direction reverses the induced potential difference</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The magnetic field produced by the induced current always <strong>opposes the original change</strong> that caused it. This is known as <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that an induced current always flows in a direction that opposes the change producing it.">Lenz's law</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">Transformers and the National Grid</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n31">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that uses electromagnetic induction to change the size of an alternating voltage, consisting of two coils of wire wound around an iron core.">transformer</dfn> uses electromagnetic induction to change the size of an alternating voltage. It consists of two coils of wire — a <dfn class="term" data-def="The coil of a transformer connected to the input (supply) voltage.">primary coil</dfn> and a <dfn class="term" data-def="The coil of a transformer connected to the output voltage.">secondary coil</dfn> — wound around a shared iron core. An alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field in the iron core, which induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that increases voltage — it has more turns on the secondary coil than on the primary coil.">step-up transformer</dfn> has more turns on the secondary coil than the primary, so the output voltage is higher than the input. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that decreases voltage — it has fewer turns on the secondary coil than on the primary coil.">step-down transformer</dfn> has fewer turns on the secondary coil, so the output voltage is lower.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why is electricity transmitted at high voltage in the national grid?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the national grid, step-up transformers increase the voltage at power stations to reduce the current. Lower current means less energy is wasted as heat in the transmission cables. Step-down transformers then reduce the voltage to safe levels for homes and businesses.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Transformer Power Equation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34">For a 100% efficient transformer, the power input equals the power output. This gives the equation:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35">$$V_P \times I_P = V_S \times I_S$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36">Where \(V_P\) and \(I_P\) are the voltage and current in the primary coil, and \(V_S\) and \(I_S\) are the voltage and current in the secondary coil. If a transformer steps up the voltage, it must step down the current by the same factor (and vice versa), so that power is conserved.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Example:</strong> A step-up transformer has a primary voltage of 230 V and a primary current of 10 A. The secondary voltage is 11,500 V. What is the secondary current?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38">\(V_P \times I_P = V_S \times I_S\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39">\(230 \times 10 = 11{,}500 \times I_S\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n40">\(I_S = \frac{2300}{11500} = 0.2\) A</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>
