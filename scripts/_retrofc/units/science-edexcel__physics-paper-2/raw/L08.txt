<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Stretching, Bending, and Compressing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When forces act on an object, they can change its shape. An object can be <dfn class="term" data-def="Pulling an object so that it becomes longer than its original length.">stretched</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="Applying forces that cause an object to curve or change its straight shape.">bent</dfn>, or <dfn class="term" data-def="Pushing an object so that it becomes shorter or more compact than its original size.">compressed</dfn>. In every case, more than one force is required to change the shape — a single force would simply cause the object to accelerate rather than deform. For example, to stretch a spring you must pull both ends in opposite directions, or fix one end and pull the other.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why do you need more than one force to change the shape of an object?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stretching, bending, or compressing an object always requires more than one force. A single force would cause acceleration, not deformation.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Elastic and Inelastic Distortion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">When a force deforms an object, the deformation can be either <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of deformation where the object returns to its original shape and size when the deforming force is removed.">elastic distortion</dfn> or <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of deformation where the object does not return to its original shape when the deforming force is removed — it is permanently changed.">inelastic distortion</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In <strong>elastic distortion</strong>, the object returns to its original shape and size once the force is removed. A stretched rubber band that springs back to its original length is an example of elastic behaviour. In <strong>inelastic distortion</strong>, the object is permanently deformed — it does not return to its original shape. Bending a paperclip is an example of inelastic distortion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A spring behaves elastically as long as it is not stretched beyond its <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum force or extension that can be applied to a spring while it still behaves elastically and returns to its original length.">elastic limit</dfn>. Beyond this point, the spring undergoes inelastic distortion and will not return to its original length when the force is removed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Hooke’s Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The law stating that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded.">Hooke’s law</dfn> states that the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, as long as the elastic limit is not exceeded. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$F = k \times x$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Where \(F\) is the force applied in newtons (N), \(k\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of how stiff a spring is, equal to the force needed to extend the spring by one metre, measured in N/m.">spring constant</dfn> in newtons per metre (N/m), and \(x\) is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The increase in length of a spring or object beyond its natural (unstretched) length.">extension</dfn> in metres (m).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A stiffer spring has a larger spring constant — it requires more force to produce the same extension. The spring constant can be found by rearranging the equation: \(k = \frac{F}{x}\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down Hooke's law equation, state what each symbol means, and explain what happens beyond the elastic limit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Hooke’s law: \(F = k \times x\). The extension is directly proportional to the force, but only up to the elastic limit. Beyond this point, the relationship is no longer linear and the spring does not return to its original length.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Force-Extension Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph plotting force (y-axis) against extension (x-axis) for a material being stretched, used to identify elastic and inelastic behaviour.">force-extension graph</dfn> shows the relationship between force and extension for a material. For a spring obeying Hooke’s law, the graph is a straight line through the origin — this is a <strong>linear</strong> relationship. The gradient of the straight-line section equals the spring constant \(k\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">If the spring is stretched beyond its elastic limit, the graph curves — the extension increases more quickly for each additional unit of force. This is a <strong>non-linear</strong> relationship. The point where the line starts to curve marks the elastic limit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Different materials produce different shaped graphs. A rubber band, for example, does not obey Hooke’s law — its force-extension graph is curved from the start. It still returns to its original shape (elastic), but the relationship between force and extension is non-linear.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Energy Stored in a Stretched Spring</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">When a spring is stretched (or compressed) within its elastic limit, work is done on the spring, and energy is stored as <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy stored in a stretched or compressed elastic object, sometimes called elastic potential energy.">elastic potential energy</dfn>. The amount of energy stored can be calculated using the equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">$$E = \frac{1}{2} \times k \times x^2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Where \(E\) is the energy transferred in stretching (joules, J), \(k\) is the spring constant (N/m), and \(x\) is the extension (m). This equation only applies when the spring obeys Hooke’s law — within the elastic limit.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n27">The elastic potential energy stored is 0.4 J.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Write the equation for elastic potential energy and explain why it only works within the elastic limit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elastic potential energy: \(E = \frac{1}{2} k x^2\). This equation applies only when the spring obeys Hooke’s law (within the elastic limit). Notice that energy depends on extension <em>squared</em> — doubling the extension quadruples the energy stored.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Core Practical: Investigating Springs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">In this required practical, you investigate the relationship between force and extension for a spring and calculate the work done.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Method:</strong> Clamp a spring vertically. Measure its natural (unstretched) length with a ruler. Add masses one at a time, recording the new length each time. Calculate the extension (new length minus original length) for each mass.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Variables:</strong> Independent variable = force (weight of masses). Dependent variable = extension. Control variables = same spring, same starting position, same measuring equipment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Analysis:</strong> Plot a force-extension graph. The straight-line region confirms Hooke’s law. The gradient equals the spring constant. If the line curves, the elastic limit has been exceeded.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Accuracy:</strong> Read the ruler at eye level to avoid parallax error. Wait for the spring to stop oscillating before reading. Repeat readings and calculate a mean to improve reliability.</li>
</ul>
