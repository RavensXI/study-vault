--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Momentum?</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A property of a moving object equal to its mass multiplied by its velocity. Momentum is a vector quantity measured in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s).">Momentum</dfn> is a property of any moving object. It depends on both the mass and velocity of the object. The equation for momentum is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$p = m \times v$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">where \(p\) is momentum in kg m/s, \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(v\) is velocity in m/s. Because velocity is a vector, momentum is also a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Write the momentum equation and state its units.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Momentum = mass × velocity. A 50 kg skater moving at 4 m/s has a momentum of 200 kg m/s. Doubling the velocity doubles the momentum.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Conservation of Momentum</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n7">In a closed system (one where no external forces act), the total momentum before an event is equal to the total momentum after it. This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="In a closed system, the total momentum before a collision or explosion equals the total momentum after it. This is a fundamental law of physics.">conservation of momentum</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">This principle applies to all collisions and explosions. For a collision between two objects:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'$$</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Examples of Momentum in Collisions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A collision where the objects stick together after impact and move as a single object. Some kinetic energy is converted to other forms, but momentum is conserved.">perfectly inelastic collision</dfn>, the two objects stick together and move as one after the collision. For example, a 2 kg trolley moving at 3 m/s collides with a stationary 1 kg trolley. They stick together:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Total momentum before = \(2 \times 3 + 1 \times 0 = 6\) kg m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Total momentum after = \((2 + 1) \times v = 6\), so \(v = 2\) m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">In an explosion, the total momentum before is zero (everything is stationary). After the explosion, the momentum of each part must add to zero — the parts move in opposite directions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Newton’s Second Law and Momentum</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">Newton’s second law can be written in terms of momentum. The resultant force on an object equals the rate of change of momentum:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$F = \frac{mv - mu}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">where \(F\) is the force in newtons (N), \(mv - mu\) is the change in momentum in kg m/s, and \(t\) is the time in seconds (s). This form of the equation is particularly useful for understanding collisions and <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that plots force against time. The area under the curve represents the change in momentum (impulse) of the object.">force-time graphs</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: What does the area under a force-time graph represent?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The area under a force-time graph equals the change in momentum (impulse). A larger force applied for a shorter time produces the same change in momentum as a smaller force applied for a longer time.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Stopping Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total distance a vehicle travels from the moment the driver sees a hazard to when the vehicle stops completely. It equals thinking distance plus braking distance.">stopping distance</dfn> of a vehicle is the sum of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance a vehicle travels during the driver’s reaction time, before the brakes are applied. It depends on the driver’s speed and reaction time.">thinking distance</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance a vehicle travels after the brakes are applied until it comes to a complete stop. It depends on speed, mass, brake condition, road surface, and tyre grip.">braking distance</dfn>:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$\text{stopping distance} = \text{thinking distance} + \text{braking distance}$$</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting Stopping Distance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Thinking distance</strong> is affected by the driver’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The time between a stimulus (such as seeing a hazard) and the start of a response (such as pressing the brake). Typical human reaction time is 0.2–0.9 seconds.">reaction time</dfn>. Factors that increase reaction time include tiredness, alcohol, drugs, distractions (such as using a mobile phone), and poor visibility. A typical reaction time is 0.2–0.9 seconds.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Braking distance</strong> is affected by: the speed of the vehicle (higher speed means longer braking distance), the mass of the vehicle, the condition of the brakes, the condition of the road surface, and the amount of friction between the tyres and the road.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Increasing speed has a dramatic effect on stopping distance because both thinking distance and braking distance increase with speed. Braking distance increases with the square of the speed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Large Decelerations and Safety</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n26">When a vehicle stops very quickly, the deceleration is very large. Using \(F = \frac{mv - mu}{t}\), if the time is very short, the force is very large. This can cause serious injuries to passengers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="Zones at the front and rear of a vehicle designed to crumple and deform in a collision. They increase the time over which the vehicle decelerates, reducing the force on the occupants.">Crumple zones</dfn>, seat belts, and airbags all work on the same principle: they increase the time over which the momentum changes, which reduces the force experienced by the passengers. This is a direct application of the equation \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{t}\) — increasing \(t\) decreases \(F\) for the same change in momentum.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How do crumple zones reduce injury in a crash?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Safety features such as crumple zones, seat belts, and airbags increase the time of deceleration, reducing the force on passengers for the same change in momentum.</p>
</div>

<div class="exam-tip" data-narration-id="n31">
  <p><strong>Exam Tip:</strong> For momentum calculations, always assign a direction as positive and stick with it. Objects moving in opposite directions have opposite signs for velocity. When using \(F = \frac{mv - mu}{t}\), remember to subtract the initial momentum from the final momentum, not the other way round.</p>
</div>

<div class="conclusion" data-narration-id="n32">
  <p>Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity and is always conserved in a closed system. Newton’s second law links force to the rate of change of momentum, and this explains why safety features like crumple zones reduce the force in a collision by increasing the stopping time. Stopping distance depends on both thinking distance and braking distance, each affected by different factors.</p>
</div>
