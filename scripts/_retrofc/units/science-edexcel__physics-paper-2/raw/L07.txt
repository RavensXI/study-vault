<h2 data-narration-id="n1">States of Matter and the Particle Model</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) that are constantly moving. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A simplified description of matter that explains the properties of solids, liquids, and gases in terms of the arrangement, movement, and energy of particles.">particle model</dfn> (also called the kinetic theory model) explains the differences between the three states of matter — <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter with a fixed shape and volume, where particles are closely packed in a regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions.">solids</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter with a fixed volume but no fixed shape, where particles are close together but free to move past each other.">liquids</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of matter with no fixed shape or volume, where particles are far apart and move randomly at high speeds.">gases</dfn> — in terms of how the particles are arranged and how they move.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Solids:</strong> particles are closely packed in a regular pattern. They vibrate about fixed positions but do not move from place to place. Strong forces of attraction hold them together. This is why solids have a fixed shape and a fixed volume.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Liquids:</strong> particles are close together but arranged randomly. They can slide past each other, which is why liquids flow and take the shape of their container. They have a fixed volume but no fixed shape.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Gases:</strong> particles are far apart and move rapidly in random directions. The forces between them are very weak. Gases have no fixed shape and no fixed volume — they spread out to fill any container.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Describe the arrangement and movement of particles in each of the three states of matter.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In solids, particles vibrate in fixed positions. In liquids, particles move past each other. In gases, particles move randomly at high speed. The strength of forces between particles decreases from solid to liquid to gas.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Density</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The mass per unit volume of a substance, calculated using the equation density = mass ÷ volume, measured in kg/m³ or g/cm³.">Density</dfn> tells you how much mass is packed into a given volume. It is calculated using the equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Where \(\rho\) is density in \(\text{kg/m}^3\), \(m\) is mass in kg, and \(V\) is volume in \(\text{m}^3\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Because particles are packed most closely in solids, solids generally have the highest density. Gases have the lowest density because their particles are spread far apart. For example, the density of iron is about \(7874 \text{ kg/m}^3\), water is \(1000 \text{ kg/m}^3\), and air is only about \(1.2 \text{ kg/m}^3\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Changes of State</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">When a substance changes state — for example, from solid to liquid (<dfn class="term" data-def="The change of state from solid to liquid, which occurs at the melting point.">melting</dfn>) or from liquid to gas (<dfn class="term" data-def="The rapid change of state from liquid to gas that occurs throughout the liquid at the boiling point.">boiling</dfn>) — the mass is <strong>conserved</strong>. No particles are gained or lost; only the arrangement and energy of the particles change.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The key changes of state are:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Melting</strong> (solid → liquid) and <strong>freezing</strong> (liquid → solid)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Boiling/evaporation</strong> (liquid → gas) and <strong>condensation</strong> (gas → liquid)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Sublimation</strong> (solid → gas directly, without passing through the liquid state)</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n21">These are <strong>physical changes</strong> — unlike chemical changes, the original substance can be recovered if the change is reversed. The particles themselves do not change; only their spacing and movement alter.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Internal Energy, Specific Heat Capacity, and Latent Heat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">When you heat a substance, the energy stored inside it increases. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles in a system.">internal energy</dfn>. If the substance is not changing state, the added energy raises its temperature — the particles move faster. If the substance is changing state (e.g. melting or boiling), the temperature stays constant while the energy is used to break bonds between particles.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C, measured in J/kg °C.">Specific heat capacity</dfn> (\(c\)) tells you how much energy is needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">$$\Delta Q = m \times c \times \Delta \theta$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Where \(\Delta Q\) is the change in thermal energy (J), \(m\) is mass (kg), \(c\) is specific heat capacity (J/kg °C), and \(\Delta \theta\) is the change in temperature (°C).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Write out both equations for thermal energy (specific heat capacity and specific latent heat) and state what each symbol means.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without changing its temperature, measured in J/kg.">Specific latent heat</dfn> (\(L\)) is the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without any change in temperature. The equation is: \(Q = m \times L\).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Reducing Unwanted Energy Transfer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><dfn class="term" data-def="The use of materials or methods to reduce the rate of energy transfer by conduction, convection, or radiation.">Thermal insulation</dfn> reduces unwanted energy transfers from buildings and objects. Methods include using materials with low thermal conductivity (such as foam, fibreglass, or trapped air), double glazing (a layer of air or vacuum between two panes of glass reduces conduction and convection), cavity wall insulation, loft insulation, and reflective surfaces to reduce radiation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Gas Pressure and Temperature</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Gas <dfn class="term" data-def="The force per unit area exerted by gas particles colliding with the walls of their container, measured in pascals (Pa).">pressure</dfn> is caused by gas particles colliding with the walls of their container. The more frequent and forceful these collisions, the higher the pressure. When the temperature of a gas increases, its particles gain kinetic energy and move faster. If the volume is kept constant, the faster-moving particles hit the walls more frequently and with greater force, so the pressure increases.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33"><dfn class="term" data-def="The lowest possible temperature (−273 °C or 0 K), at which particles have the minimum possible kinetic energy and effectively stop moving.">Absolute zero</dfn> is the temperature at which particles have the minimum possible kinetic energy and effectively stop moving. It is −273 °C, which equals 0 kelvin (0 K). To convert between Celsius and kelvin, use:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">$$\text{temperature in K} = \text{temperature in} \degree\text{C} + 273$$</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n35" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: convert 25 degrees Celsius to kelvin, then convert 350 K to Celsius.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Absolute zero is −273 °C (0 K). At this temperature, particles have minimum kinetic energy. To convert: K = °C + 273.</p>
</div>
