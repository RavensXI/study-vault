--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dark Places — An Introduction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not all poems in the Time and Place anthology celebrate beauty. Some use place to expose suffering, inequality and isolation. In this lesson we study three poems that present “dark places”: Blake’s “London,” Afrika’s “Nothing’s Changed” and Davidson’s “In Romney Marsh.” Each poet uses a specific location to make a broader point about power, injustice or the human condition.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">London — William Blake (1794)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Blake published “London” in his <em>Songs of Experience</em> (1794). The speaker walks through London’s streets and sees suffering everywhere: “Marks of weakness, marks of woe” on every face. The poem attacks institutions — the Church, the monarchy, the marriage laws — for allowing this misery. Blake uses the word “<dfn class="term" data-def="Mapped out, legally controlled or restricted. Blake uses it to suggest that even the streets and the river are owned and controlled by the powerful.">charter’d</dfn>” twice in the opening stanza (“charter’d street,” “charter’d Thames”) to suggest that the city’s streets and even its river are owned and controlled.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Key quotations: “In every cry of every Man, / In every Infant’s cry of fear, / In every voice: in every ban” — the <dfn class="term" data-def="The deliberate repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive lines or clauses for emphasis.">anaphora</dfn> of “every” hammers home the universality of suffering. “The mind-forg’d manacles” is a powerful metaphor: people are imprisoned not by physical chains but by mental ones — by acceptance of authority. The final stanza’s “Marriage hearse” is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech combining two contradictory terms, such as 'living death' or 'deafening silence.'">oxymoron</dfn> linking love with death, suggesting that even marriage is corrupted by society.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain both sides of the contrast described here from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Blake’s “mind-forg’d manacles” suggests that oppression is partly psychological — people are trapped by their own acceptance of authority. This makes “London” a poem about mental as well as physical suffering, and connects it to poems about identity and belonging elsewhere in the anthology.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Nothing’s Changed — Tatamkhulu Afrika (1998)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Afrika was a South African poet of mixed heritage who chose to be classified as Black under <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of racial segregation enforced by the South African government from 1948 to 1994, which separated people by race and denied rights to non-white citizens.">apartheid</dfn>. He wrote “Nothing’s Changed” after visiting District Six in Cape Town — a neighbourhood bulldozed under apartheid to remove its non-white residents. Although apartheid officially ended in 1994, Afrika found that nothing had really changed: a new whites-only restaurant had appeared alongside a “working man’s café” where Black South Africans ate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The poem is written in short, clipped stanzas with no regular rhyme, mirroring the speaker’s controlled anger. Physical details build tension: “my feet know / and my hands,” “the hot, white, inward turning / anger of my eyes” — the body itself remembers injustice. The contrast between the restaurant (“linen falls, / the single rose”) and the café (“plastic table’s top, / wipe your fingers on your jeans”) exposes continuing inequality through place.</p>

In 1966, the apartheid government declared it a whites-only area and forcibly removed over 60,000 residents, bulldozing their homes. By 1998 when Afrika wrote this poem, apartheid had legally ended, but economic and social segregation persisted. The poem’s title — “Nothing’s Changed” — is a bitter summary: laws changed, but lived reality did not.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">In Romney Marsh — John Davidson (1899)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Davidson presents Romney Marsh — a flat, windswept coastal area in Kent — as a place of wild, almost aggressive natural beauty. The poem describes a walk along the coast at night, with the sea, stars and wind creating an overwhelming sensory landscape. Unlike the Romantic poets’ comfortable harmony with nature, Davidson’s speaker seems dwarfed and isolated by the vast, indifferent landscape.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Key quotations: “The beach with all its organ stops / Pealing a low-tide psalm” — the extended metaphor of the sea as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A church organ, used here as a metaphor for the powerful, resonant sounds of the sea.">cathedral organ</dfn> gives the landscape a religious grandeur. “Masts in the offing wagged their tops; / The swinging waves pealed on the shore” — personification makes the ships part of the natural scene. The poem’s regular quatrains and ABAB rhyme scheme create a hymn-like quality, as though the speaker is worshipping the landscape even as it overwhelms them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe Davidson development across the text in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Davidson’s extended metaphor of the sea as a cathedral organ transforms the landscape into something sacred but impersonal. Unlike Keats or Wordsworth, who feel at ease in nature, Davidson presents a place that inspires awe through its indifference to humans.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Blake vs Afrika:</strong> Both use place to expose social injustice. Blake’s London is universal (“every”), Afrika’s Cape Town is specific and personal (“my feet know”). Blake writes in regular quatrains with a controlled, prophetic voice; Afrika uses fragmented, clipped lines mirroring suppressed rage.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Blake vs Davidson:</strong> Both present places that are oppressive, but Blake’s oppression is human-made (institutions, laws) while Davidson’s is natural (the vast, indifferent landscape). Blake’s tone is angry; Davidson’s is reverential but awed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Afrika vs Davidson:</strong> Both poets feel small in their landscapes, but for different reasons — Afrika is excluded by racial injustice, Davidson is dwarfed by nature’s scale. Place in Afrika’s poem is a site of human cruelty; in Davidson’s, it is indifferent to humanity altogether.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Blake’s “London” uses anaphora and metaphor (“mind-forg’d manacles”) to present the city as a place of universal oppression by powerful institutions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Afrika’s “Nothing’s Changed” uses physical, bodily language and contrasting descriptions to expose the persistence of racial inequality in post-apartheid South Africa.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Davidson’s “In Romney Marsh” presents nature as grand but indifferent, using the extended organ metaphor to create a sense of awe mixed with isolation.</li></ul>
