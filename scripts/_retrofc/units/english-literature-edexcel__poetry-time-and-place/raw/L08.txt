<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Technique — Anthology Comparison</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">This lesson covers the practical exam skills you need for the anthology comparison question: the typical format, timing, planning method and a bank of key quotations for all 15 poems in the Time and Place collection. Since this is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An exam in which students are not permitted to bring copies of the set texts into the examination room.">closed-book</dfn> exam for your chosen poem (the named poem is printed), learning quotations is essential.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Question Format</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The anthology comparison question typically prints one named poem from the Time and Place collection and asks you to compare it with another poem of your choice from the same collection. A question might ask you to compare the ways the poets present feelings about a specific place, and to address the poets’ use of language, form and structure along with the influence of context on the poems. Your answer is assessed on AO2 (language, form, structure) and AO3 (context). Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the precise rubric wording and mark allocation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The named poem is reprinted in the question paper, so you can quote directly from it. Your chosen poem is from memory — you must learn quotations. Aim for 2–3 short, versatile quotations per poem. Short quotations (3–6 words) are easier to memorise and more effective than long ones — they show you can select precisely.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Timing & Planning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Treat this question as a proportion of the whole paper — allocate time based on its share of the total marks. Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact timing; the breakdown below works for any block of roughly 35–40 minutes, but you can scale it to the time you are given.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>First sixth of your time:</strong> Read the named poem carefully. Choose your second poem. Write a one-sentence thesis. Jot down 3 comparison points with quotations for each poem.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Middle section (writing):</strong> Write 3–4 paragraphs of integrated comparison. Each paragraph = one comparison point, weaving both poems together, with AO2 analysis dominant and AO3 woven in.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Final few minutes:</strong> Re-read your answer. Check you have addressed both AO2 and AO3. Add any missed comparative connectives. Fix spelling errors in quotations.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>2. Point 1 (Language):</strong> Name one language feature per poem (e.g. Blake’s anaphora “every” / Afrika’s bodily language “my feet know”). Note the effect of each.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>3. Point 2 (Form/Structure):</strong> Name the form of each poem and explain what it does (e.g. Blake’s quatrains = controlled rage / Afrika’s clipped free verse = suppressed anger).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>4. Point 3 (Context):</strong> One context point per poem that explains WHY the poet presents place this way.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>5. Chosen poem quotations:</strong> Write out 2–3 key quotations from memory so you don’t forget them mid-essay.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Key Quotation Bank — All 15 Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Learn 2–3 short quotations per poem. Focus on quotations that contain analysable language features — metaphors, imagery, structural devices — not plain narrative statements.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>To Autumn (Keats):</strong> “Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness” (sibilance, sensory richness) • “Where are the songs of Spring?” (rhetorical question, acceptance of time passing) • “soft-dying day” (gentle euphemism for decline/death)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Composed upon Westminster Bridge (Wordsworth):</strong> “Earth has not anything to show more fair” (hyperbole, awe) • “like a garment, wear / The beauty of the morning” (simile, city clothed in nature) • “the very houses seem asleep” (personification, peace)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>London (Blake):</strong> “charter’d street” / “charter’d Thames” (repetition, control) • “mind-forg’d manacles” (metaphor, psychological oppression) • “Marriage hearse” (oxymoron, corruption)</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>I started Early – Took my Dog (Dickinson):</strong> “He followed – close behind” (personification, pursuit) • “I felt His Silver Heel / Upon my Ankle” (sensory, threatening intimacy) • Hymn metre creating unsettling contrast</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Where the Picnic was (Hardy):</strong> “the charred stick-ends of our last year’s fire” (symbol, dead memory) • “winter mire” (pathetic fallacy) • “they are scattered” (loss of community)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Adlestrop (Thomas):</strong> “Yes, I remember Adlestrop” (conversational, intimate) • “all the birds / Of Oxfordshire and Gloucestershire” (expansion, pastoral beauty) • “no one left and no one came” (isolation, stillness)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Home Thoughts from Abroad (Browning):</strong> “Oh, to be in England / Now that April’s there!” (exclamatory longing) • “the wise thrush; he sings each song twice over” (personification, joy) • “gaudy melon-flower” (foreign beauty dismissed)</p>

    <span>Contemporary: Fanthorpe, Adcock, Alvi, Nichols, Afrika, Hannah, Davidson, Jennings</span>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>First Flight (Fanthorpe):</strong> Landscape shrinking during takeoff (perspective shift as metaphor) • Familiar details becoming abstract (loss through distance)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Stewart Island (Adcock):</strong> precise observational detail (sandflies, a seagull attack, the hotel manager’s wife) • Speaker as visitor, not native • Free verse reflecting casual, detached exploration</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Presents from my Aunts in Pakistan (Alvi):</strong> “of no fixed nationality” (<dfn class="term" data-def="The experience of existing between two cultures, languages or national identities.">dual identity</dfn>) • “peacock-blue” / “apple-green” (colour imagery for Pakistani culture) • “longed / for denim and corduroy” (English items colourless)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Hurricane Hits England (Nichols):</strong> “the earth is the earth is the earth” (<dfn class="term" data-def="The immediate repetition of a word or phrase for emphasis.">epizeuxis</dfn>, universal connection) • “Huracan,” “Oya,” “Shango” (invocation of Caribbean gods) • Pronoun shift from “her” to “I”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Nothing’s Changed (Afrika):</strong> “my feet know / and my hands” (bodily memory) • “the hot, white, inward turning / anger of my eyes” (controlled rage) • Restaurant vs café contrast (inequality made visible)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Postcard from a Travel Snob (Hannah):</strong> Persona voice (dramatic irony) • Rhyming couplets (polished surface mirroring pretension) • Satirical tone exposing how place is used for ego</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>In Romney Marsh (Davidson):</strong> “organ stops / Pealing a low-tide psalm” (extended organ metaphor) • ABAB quatrains (hymn-like, worshipful) • Nature as grand and indifferent</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Absence (Jennings):</strong> “It was because the place was just the same / That made your absence seem a savage force” (grief changes the landscape) • The unchanged setting intensifies loss • Tight tercets (compressed emotion)</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe the structural feature and explain why the writer chose it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You do not need to memorise long passages. Short, precise quotations (3–6 words) are more effective because you can embed them into your sentences: “Blake’s ‘mind-forg’d manacles’ suggests…” is smoother and more analytical than copying out a full stanza. Accuracy matters — a slightly misquoted word can undermine your credibility.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n34">Choosing Your Comparison Poem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n35">Before the exam, prepare 3–4 “go-to” poems that you know in depth. For each, know: the form, 2–3 key quotations, 2 language features, 1 context point. The table below suggests strong pairings by theme:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Celebrating place:</strong> To Autumn + Westminster Bridge + Adlestrop + Home Thoughts from Abroad</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Social criticism:</strong> London + Nothing’s Changed</p>
<p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Displacement:</strong> Presents from my Aunts + Hurricane Hits England + Stewart Island</p>
<p data-narration-id="n39"><strong>Memory and loss:</strong> Where the Picnic was + First Flight + Absence + Adlestrop</p>
<p data-narration-id="n40"><strong>Nature’s power:</strong> I started Early + In Romney Marsh + To Autumn + Hurricane Hits England</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n42">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n43">Section B Part 1 is worth 20 marks (AO2: 15 + AO3: 5). Allocate 35–40 minutes. The named poem is printed; your chosen poem is from memory.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n44">Learn 2–3 short, analysable quotations per poem. Short quotations (3–6 words) embedded in sentences are more effective than long copied passages.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n45">Prepare 3–4 “go-to” poems in depth so you can adapt to whatever named poem the exam gives you. Know the form, quotations, methods and context for each.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n41">In the final minutes, scan your answer for three things: (1) Have you used comparative connectives in every paragraph? (2) Have you analysed effects, not just identified techniques? (3) Have you included at least two context points across the whole answer? If any are missing, add a sentence. These quick additions can push you up a mark band.</p>
