How to build a high-scoring comparison: thesis, structure, AO2 and AO3 integration.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Comparison Skills — The Core of Section B Part 1</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Section B Part 1 question on Component 2 is worth 20 marks and requires you to <strong>compare</strong> two poems from the Time and Place anthology. One poem is printed in the question paper (the “named poem”); you choose the second from memory. The question will ask how both poets present a specific theme, idea or feature of place. Marks are awarded for AO2 (language, form and structure — 15 marks) and AO3 (context — 5 marks). This lesson teaches you how to structure a comparison that integrates both assessment objectives effectively.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Step 1 — Read the Question Carefully</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The question will always specify a focus — for example, “Compare how the poets present feelings about a particular place” or “Compare how the poets use language and structure to present the power of nature.” Everything in your answer must connect to this focus. A common mistake is to write everything you know about both poems without answering the actual question. Underline the key words in the question and refer back to them throughout your response.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain both sides of the contrast described here from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You must choose your second poem wisely. Pick the poem that gives you the richest comparison with the named poem — ideally one that shares the theme but uses different methods. For example, if “London” is the named poem and the focus is “social criticism,” choosing “Nothing’s Changed” gives you strong parallels (both expose inequality through place) with clear differences (Blake’s abstract metaphors vs Afrika’s physical, bodily language).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Step 2 — Plan a <dfn class="term" data-def="A central argument or interpretive claim that runs through the entire response, giving it focus and direction.">Thesis</dfn></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Before writing, decide on a central argument. A thesis is not a summary (“Both poems are about place”); it is an interpretive claim that you will develop through the essay. For example: “Both Blake and Afrika use place to expose inequality, but Blake presents oppression as universal and psychological while Afrika presents it as specific and physical.” A strong thesis gives your answer direction and prevents it from becoming a list of disconnected observations.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Step 3 — Structure Your Response</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The most effective structure weaves both poems together rather than writing about Poem A and then Poem B. Use <dfn class="term" data-def="Comparing two poems point by point throughout the essay, rather than discussing one poem completely before moving to the other.">integrated comparison</dfn> — each paragraph discusses a point of comparison and moves between both poems. A typical high-scoring response has 3–4 paragraphs:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Paragraph 1:</strong> Introduction — state your thesis and briefly introduce both poems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Paragraphs 2–3:</strong> Comparative analysis. Each paragraph focuses on one point of comparison (e.g. imagery, form, tone). Within each paragraph: analyse the named poem (AO2), then compare with your chosen poem (AO2), then add context for one or both (AO3).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Paragraph 4:</strong> Final comparison point or conclusion — draw your argument together and link back to the thesis.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Integrated comparison means every paragraph discusses <strong>both</strong> poems. Use comparative connectives: “similarly,” “in contrast,” “whereas,” “both poets,” “however,” “unlike.” Avoid the “Poem A then Poem B” structure, which makes it harder to compare and often results in retelling rather than analysis.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Step 4 — Integrate AO2 and AO3</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">AO2 (15 marks) is worth three times as much as AO3 (5 marks), so language, form and structure analysis should dominate your answer. But context should be woven in naturally, not bolted on as a separate paragraph. The best approach is to use context to <strong>explain</strong> a method or effect. For example: “Keats personifies Autumn as a human figure, creating a sense of intimate familiarity with the season. <strong>Context:</strong> writing this in 1819 while aware of his own failing health, this personification suggests Keats was trying to hold onto life’s fleeting beauty.”</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">“Both Blake and Afrika use place to expose social inequality, but they do so through different registers. Blake’s anaphora — ‘In every cry of every Man, / In every Infant’s cry of fear’ — creates a prophetic, universal voice, suggesting that suffering in London is inescapable and affects everyone. The repetition of ‘every’ hammers home the totality of oppression. In contrast, Afrika’s language is physical and personal: ‘my feet know / and my hands.’ The possessive pronoun ‘my’ makes the injustice bodily and individual — this is not abstract suffering but one person’s lived experience of racial segregation. Blake, writing as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who holds unconventional political or social views, especially about equality, freedom and justice.">radical</dfn> in 1794, attacks the institutions of Church and monarchy that permit suffering; Afrika, writing in 1998 after the formal end of apartheid, attacks the persistence of inequality despite legal change. Both poets therefore use place not as a setting but as evidence of injustice — though Blake’s evidence is metaphorical (‘mind-forg’d manacles’) while Afrika’s is concrete (the contrasting restaurant and café).”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Retelling:</strong> Don’t summarise what happens in each poem. The examiner knows the poems. Focus on <strong>how</strong> and <strong>why</strong> — the methods and their effects.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Feature-spotting:</strong> Don’t list techniques without explaining their effect. “Blake uses alliteration” earns almost nothing. “Blake’s alliteration in ‘Marks of weakness, marks of woe’ creates a heavy, mournful rhythm that emphasises the weight of suffering” is analysis.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Context dump:</strong> Don’t write a paragraph of pure context. Weave it into your analysis: “Writing during the Industrial Revolution, Blake’s use of ‘charter’d’ reflects his anger at the commercialisation of public spaces.”</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Choose your second poem for the richest comparison — shared theme, different methods.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Write a thesis (central argument) before you start writing, then develop it through 3–4 paragraphs of integrated comparison using comparative connectives.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">AO2 (language, form, structure) is worth 15 marks — make it the focus. Weave AO3 (context) in naturally to explain methods and effects, not as a separate paragraph.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">You have approximately 35–40 minutes for Section B Part 1. Spend 5 minutes planning (choose your second poem, write a thesis, note 3 comparison points) and 30–35 minutes writing. Aim for 3–4 substantial paragraphs with integrated comparison. Always leave 2 minutes to re-read and check you have addressed both AO2 and AO3. Quality of analysis matters more than length — three well-developed paragraphs beat five thin ones.</p>
