--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Turnly cross-cuts between Japan and North Korea using stage directions and the sea.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Countries, One Stage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Francis Turnly&rsquo;s <em>The Great Wave</em> takes place across two countries and 24 years. To hold those two worlds simultaneously, Turnly relies on a single structural engine: <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique where a playwright cuts rapidly between two or more scenes happening in different times or places, keeping both storylines alive in the audience&rsquo;s mind at once.">cross-cutting</dfn>. Scenes in Japan and North Korea alternate in quick succession, so the audience is never allowed to settle entirely into one world before the other pulls them back. This is not accidental restlessness &mdash; it is Turnly&rsquo;s way of insisting that both lives are happening at the same time, that Hanako&rsquo;s captivity and her family&rsquo;s grief are not separate stories but a single wound.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why cross-cutting between Japan and North Korea is a deliberate structural choice, not just a storytelling convenience.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cross-cutting in <em>The Great Wave</em> creates <strong>parallel time</strong>: the audience watches Hanako adapting to life in North Korea at exactly the same moment that Reiko and Etsuko in Japan are refusing to accept she is dead. The two timelines are always in tension.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Stage Directions and the Sea</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Turnly&rsquo;s stage directions do far more than describe the set. They specify the <em>sound</em> of the sea as a recurring presence &mdash; waves are not decorative background noise but a reminder of the beach where Hanako disappeared. A director staging the play must decide how to realise this: a <dfn class="term" data-def="A layered design of sound &mdash; recorded or live &mdash; that creates the atmosphere of a place or moment, using multiple audio elements simultaneously.">soundscape</dfn> of waves can fade in and out to mark every transition between storylines, functioning as an audible signal that the two worlds are connected by that same sea. The stage direction does not merely set the scene &mdash; it instructs the entire company about the play&rsquo;s emotional geography.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For a designer, realising the sea means a choice between approaches. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A broad wash of light covering the whole stage area, often used to create atmosphere or suggest an environment rather than illuminate specific actors.">lighting wash</dfn> of shifting blue-grey across the cyclorama can evoke the sea&rsquo;s restlessness without requiring literal scenic waves. Some productions have used <dfn class="term" data-def="A metal or glass plate placed in front of a lantern to project a pattern or texture of light onto the stage surface.">gobos</dfn> to throw fragmented light patterns suggesting water moving across the floor &mdash; so even the North Korean compound scenes carry a visual trace of the sea the captive cannot reach.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How transitions signal a leap in time</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Because the play spans 1979 to 2003, transitions between scenes must carry not just geographical shift (Japan to North Korea) but also <strong>temporal shift</strong> &mdash; sometimes several years pass in a single cut. Turnly&rsquo;s stage directions use a combination of signals: a change in <dfn class="term" data-def="The complete picture of light on stage at a given moment &mdash; its colour, direction, intensity and coverage.">lighting state</dfn>, a costume change visible to the audience, and a change in the physical bearing of the performers. A character who was upright and defiant in the preceding scene may enter the next one carrying the weight of another two years in their posture and pace.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">For the director and designer this means agreeing a consistent vocabulary for transitions. If a blackout followed by a surge of wave sound means &ldquo;time has passed,&rdquo; that convention must be established early and held consistently &mdash; otherwise the audience loses its bearings across the play&rsquo;s long arc. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern of movement and positioning of performers on stage, planned by the director to create meaning and guide the audience&rsquo;s eye.">blocking</dfn> at the start of each new scene can itself signal time: a performer moving quickly to a new position suggests arrival in a new moment, while stillness as the lights rise signals that this scene begins mid-thought.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Code-Switching and Language as Subtext</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">One of Turnly&rsquo;s most striking dramatic methods is the use of language as a marker of identity and coercion. In the North Korean scenes, English stands in for Japanese (the language Hanako speaks at home and later teaches) and stylised Korean signals that she is now living inside a system that wants to erase who she is. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Moving between two languages or registers within a scene or even a single exchange, used here to signal identity, power, and the pressure to conform.">code-switching</dfn> is a form of subtext: every time Hanako speaks in the language she has been forced to learn, the audience feels the cost of her survival.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For a performer playing Hanako, the challenge is to carry the weight of that language shift in the body as well as the voice. A shift to Korean might be accompanied by a slight change in posture &mdash; a contained stillness, <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical and spatial relationship between two performers on stage, used to communicate status, emotional connection, or threat.">proxemics</dfn> that keep her at a formal distance from the officials around her &mdash; so that the audience reads the language change as a kind of performance within performance, Hanako surviving by playing a role.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name two dramatic methods Turnly uses to move between the Japan and North Korea storylines, and for each one say what effect it creates for the audience.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The play&rsquo;s <strong>documentary realism</strong> roots &mdash; Turnly&rsquo;s years of research with abductee families &mdash; shape the dramatic methods. The stage directions specify moments of bureaucratic procedure (interrogations, declarations, diplomatic meetings) with a precision that signals: <em>this really happened to real people</em>. That weight sits underneath every theatrical choice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Wave as Structural Metaphor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The play&rsquo;s title is not set dressing. A wave is something that cannot be stopped once it begins; it moves people without asking permission; it appears to recede but leaves a changed landscape behind. Turnly uses this image structurally: the cross-cutting itself <em>moves</em> like a wave &mdash; rising in intensity as diplomatic events accelerate in the play&rsquo;s second half, and finally cresting in the scenes around the 2002 Pyongyang Declaration. A director staging the play needs to feel and shape this rhythmic architecture, using the pace of scene transitions to build that wave towards its final breaking point.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Common misconception: treating cross-cutting as just an editing device</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Students sometimes describe cross-cutting as though it were simply a way to &ldquo;keep the story moving.&rdquo; Examiners reward much more than this. Cross-cutting in <em>The Great Wave</em> is a <strong>political act</strong>: by refusing to let the audience follow only Hanako&rsquo;s story or only the family&rsquo;s story, Turnly forces us to hold both in mind simultaneously &mdash; to feel the injustice of the gap between a family waiting for news and a daughter alive but unreachable. The technique creates dramatic irony and moral pressure on the audience, not just narrative variety.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">When you discuss cross-cutting in an extended-response question, always explain its <strong>effect on the audience&rsquo;s emotions and understanding</strong>, not just its technical description. Ask yourself: what does the audience know in this scene that the characters on stage do not? How does that knowledge create tension or grief?</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Cross-cutting between Japan and North Korea is Turnly&rsquo;s central structural method: it forces the audience to experience both storylines simultaneously, creating dramatic irony and mounting moral pressure as the years pass.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Stage directions specify the sea &mdash; as sound, light and metaphor &mdash; as a recurring presence that connects the two worlds. Designers can realise this through a wave soundscape, a shifting blue-grey lighting wash, or gobo water-patterns that appear in both locations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Code-switching between languages marks Hanako&rsquo;s identity under coercion; a performer can carry this in their physicality and proxemics as well as their voice, making the language shift a visible as well as audible event.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For &ldquo;Analyse the playwright&rsquo;s intentions&rdquo; questions (12 marks), the most common error is describing what happens in the play rather than analysing <strong>what Turnly wants the audience to think or feel</strong> at that moment. For cross-cutting and stage direction questions: never say &ldquo;the playwright uses cross-cutting to show both storylines.&rdquo; Instead say: &ldquo;Turnly uses rapid cross-cutting to force the audience to hold both worlds simultaneously, creating a growing sense of moral outrage &mdash; the audience knows Hanako is alive while her family grieves, and that gap is the play&rsquo;s central wound.&rdquo; For extended staging responses (20 marks), always translate the playwright&rsquo;s method into a specific design or performer choice: &ldquo;I would use a wave-soundscape cross-fade as the transition cue, building in volume towards Act 2, so the audience physically feels the pressure of time passing.&rdquo;</p>
