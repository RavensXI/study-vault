<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Worlds on One Stage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The design challenge of <em>The Great Wave</em> is fundamental: how do you put Japan and North Korea on the same stage without either location feeling vague? Turnly&rsquo;s play demands a design language that can differentiate between two politically and emotionally distinct worlds, often within the same scene transition. The solution most productions reach for is not naturalistic scene-painting but a <dfn class="term" data-def="A staging approach where two distinct locations are represented simultaneously on stage, each visible to the audience at the same time, often separated by light or level.">split staging</dfn> strategy: a stage divided so that the Japanese household and the North Korean compound can each claim a permanent location, with lighting doing the work of telling the audience where they are.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For a <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall shape and configuration of a performance space: how stage and audience are arranged relative to each other.">staging configuration</dfn>, an end-on or <dfn class="term" data-def="A stage that extends into the audience on three sides, with spectators on three sides and the backstage on the fourth.">thrust stage</dfn> both offer advantages for this play. A thrust configuration brings the audience close to both worlds simultaneously, collapsing any comfortable distance between spectator and the injustice they are watching &mdash; a Brechtian effect even in a predominantly naturalistic production.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe in two sentences how a lighting designer could use just two contrasting lighting states to distinguish the Japan scenes from the North Korea scenes.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The two locations demand contrasting design vocabularies: Japan evokes domestic warmth and coastal openness &mdash; natural light, timber, the blue-grey of the sea. North Korea evokes state control &mdash; flat, harsh overhead light, institutional walls, the absence of the natural world. Every design choice should be readable as belonging to one or the other.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lighting Design: From Coastal Softness to State Searchlight</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The Japan scenes can be lit with a warm amber-tinted <dfn class="term" data-def="A broad wash of light covering the whole stage area, often used to create atmosphere or suggest an environment rather than illuminate specific actors.">lighting wash</dfn> that evokes domestic interior light and coastal afternoon. For the North Korean compound, the design logic shifts entirely. A harsh overhead rig &mdash; profile lanterns angled steeply downward, producing sharp shadows &mdash; communicates institutional control without a single piece of dialogue. Hanako is <em>surveilled</em>; the light does not flatter or warm, it locates and exposes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The &ldquo;searchlight&rdquo; image referred to in the play&rsquo;s design history extends this logic: a tight <dfn class="term" data-def="A single focused lantern used to illuminate one specific area or performer, creating contrast against the darker surrounding stage.">lighting special</dfn> that picks out a performer centre stage, surrounded by darkness, functions as both interrogation lighting and a visual metaphor for isolation. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A metal or glass plate placed in front of a lantern to project a pattern of light onto the stage &mdash; here used to suggest a window frame, bars, or fragmented light.">gobo</dfn> patterned with a window-frame or horizontal bars, projected across Hanako&rsquo;s body, makes her captivity legible without any set-piece prison construction.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">A common student error is to write: &ldquo;I would use cold blue lighting for the North Korea scenes to show it is cold and unfriendly.&rdquo; Examiners need much more than this. Specify: <strong>which lantern type</strong> (profile spots for hard-edged shadows; Fresnels for a softer, more diffused chill), <strong>what intensity</strong> (70% rather than full, so the actors are readable but the environment feels leached of warmth), <strong>what colour gel</strong> (a Lee 201 &ldquo;Full C.T. Blue&rdquo; or similar cold wash), and <strong>what directional angle</strong> (steep top-light creates institutional surveillance rather than the enveloping cold of a side or front wash). Each specific choice carries meaning; a generic description carries none.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Sound Design: Waves and Propaganda</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The play&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="A layered design of sound &mdash; recorded or live &mdash; that creates the atmosphere of a place or moment, using multiple audio elements simultaneously.">soundscape</dfn> moves between two very different audio environments. The Japan scenes carry the sound of the sea &mdash; waves that begin as natural beach noise and gradually, across the play, become something the family has to choose to hear or not. A sound designer might position the sea sound binaurally (from the sides of the auditorium) in early scenes, then bring it to a mono centre speaker in later scenes, as if the sea has retreated into memory.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The North Korean compound scenes can be underscored by institutional sound &mdash; a low, steady drone that belongs to the building, not to nature; or, more specifically, by occasional fragments of state propaganda broadcast &mdash; distant music, an announcer&rsquo;s voice &mdash; that arrive not as dramatic set-pieces but as background texture, as ordinary as traffic. This is the sound of a system that does not need to shout because it is simply the air you breathe. A <dfn class="term" data-def="Music or sound played quietly beneath dialogue or action to support the emotional atmosphere without drawing attention to itself.">soundscape underscore</dfn> of this kind is more unsettling than a dramatic sound cue precisely because it feels unremarkable.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, describe one specific sound design choice for the Japan scenes and one for the North Korea scenes, naming the effect each creates for the audience.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The sea functions as a <strong>controlling sound motif</strong>: it begins the play (the beach storm where Hanako disappears) and it is always available to the sound designer as a marker of the original loss. In the North Korean scenes, the absence of the sea &mdash; or its replacement by state sound &mdash; measures how far Hanako has been taken from herself.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Set Design: Minimalism Across Decades</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Because the play covers 24 years and two countries, a realistic naturalistic set would require constant scene changes and would slow the play&rsquo;s momentum fatally. Turnly&rsquo;s play calls for a <dfn class="term" data-def="An approach to set design that uses as few pieces of furniture and scenery as possible, creating location through light, sound and performer action rather than literal construction.">minimalist set</dfn>: a stage that holds both locations simultaneously, with a handful of key elements that can be rearranged or relit to signal transitions. A low platform <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of different heights on stage &mdash; platforms, rostra, ramps &mdash; to create visual interest, signal status, or differentiate locations.">levels</dfn> structure can separate the domestic Japanese household at one depth from the clinical compound surfaces at another, without a scene change.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Projections on the <dfn class="term" data-def="A large curved or flat screen at the back of the stage, used for projected images or as a background for lighting.">cyclorama</dfn> can carry the sea without requiring a physical construction: a rolling ocean image projected behind the Japan scenes gives way to a blank institutional wall in the North Korean compound sequences. This also allows the projection to evolve &mdash; the sea becoming stormier or calmer &mdash; in response to the emotional arc of the family&rsquo;s scenes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Costume: Decade, Culture, Coercion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Costume in <em>The Great Wave</em> carries enormous narrative load because it must show the passage of decades (1979 to 2003) and the erasure of Hanako&rsquo;s Japanese identity within the North Korean state. The design must signal two things simultaneously: the historical period (1980s Japanese civilian clothing moves through decades) and the institutional pressure on identity (Hanako&rsquo;s costume in North Korea becoming less her own over time &mdash; more muted, more uniform, less personal).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For the Japanese family scenes, costume colour and silhouette can reflect period fashion while also tracking emotional states: Etsuko&rsquo;s palette might grow greyer as the years pass, her costume gradually losing the bright accent colours of the early scenes. For Hanako in captivity, a DPRK-inflected institutional grey or khaki &mdash; plain fabric, functional cut &mdash; replaces the personal clothing of the beach scene. The contrast between what she wore at 17 and what she wears a decade later can be read by the audience as the visual measure of what she has lost.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Design choices only work if the performer engages with them. A performer playing Hanako in the compound scenes can use <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical and spatial relationship between two performers on stage, used to communicate status, emotional connection, or threat.">proxemics</dfn> to reinforce the costume&rsquo;s meaning: keeping a formal, controlled distance from officials, never allowing her body to claim the space as her own &mdash; the body contained within the uniform rather than inhabiting it. When Hanako has a rare private moment, a small physical release &mdash; dropping the shoulders, letting the hands open &mdash; can signal the self that still exists underneath the institutional costume. These micro-choices are what separate a performance that merely wears the costume from one that lives in it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">A split staging strategy differentiates Japan and North Korea without constant scene changes: contrasting lighting states (warm amber wash versus steep overhead profile spot with cold gel), distinct sound environments (sea soundscape versus institutional drone), and minimalist set elements that can be relit to shift location.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Specific design vocabulary is essential in exam answers: name the lantern type, gel colour, gobo pattern, fabric, and directional angle for every design choice &mdash; not just &ldquo;cold blue lighting&rdquo; but exactly which lantern, what gel, at what intensity, from what angle, and what effect on the audience at a named moment.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Costume tracks both historical period and identity erosion: Hanako&rsquo;s clothing in North Korea becomes progressively more institutional over the decades, providing a visual measure of what the state has taken from her. Performers can reinforce costume meaning through proxemics and physical choices.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n20">For &ldquo;How would you (as a designer) design&rdquo; questions (8 marks), the single most penalised error is <strong>generic description</strong>: &ldquo;I would use dark lighting to show North Korea is oppressive.&rdquo; Examiners need: the specific lantern type (profile spot for hard-edged shadows), the gel colour (a cold Lee 201 blue or similar), the angle (steep overhead to create institutional surveillance), the intensity (70% to leach warmth without losing visibility), and the effect on the audience at a named moment (when Hanako first arrives in the compound, the harsh overhead light makes her look exposed and vulnerable, signalling to the audience that she has no protection here). The same discipline applies to sound: name the specific audio element (recorded wave sound binaural from side speakers) and the specific effect (the audience locates themselves at the Japanese coast immediately). Design questions reward precision &mdash; general ideas score at Developing; concrete named choices score at Mastering.</p>
