--- DESCRIPTION ---
The twelve factors that shape self-concept and six evidence-based strategies for building resilience.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Shapes Self-Concept?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Self-concept &mdash; how an individual thinks about, evaluates and perceives themselves &mdash; is not fixed at birth. It is constantly shaped and reshaped by a wide range of factors throughout life. Some of these factors have a positive influence, strengthening an individual&rsquo;s sense of worth and identity. Others have a negative influence, undermining self-esteem and making it harder for someone to see their own value. The specification identifies twelve factors that affect self-concept, and understanding how each one works &mdash; and how they interact &mdash; is central to this topic.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Twelve Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The twelve factors can be grouped to make them easier to remember. The first group relates to personal characteristics and experiences: <strong>age</strong>, <strong>appearance</strong>, <strong>gender</strong> and <strong>sexuality</strong>. The second group relates to social and relational influences: <strong>relationships with others</strong>, <strong>culture</strong> and <strong>education</strong>. The third group relates to adversity and discrimination: <strong>abuse</strong>, <strong>stereotyping and discrimination</strong>, <strong>unconscious bias</strong> and <strong>social media</strong>. The twelfth factor &mdash; <dfn class="term" data-def="Significant events and experiences that have occurred during an individual&rsquo;s life, which shape how they see themselves and interact with the world.">life experiences</dfn> &mdash; acts as an overarching category that can encompass many of the others.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write all twelve factors from memory. Then mark which you have written positively and negatively in terms of their typical effect on self-concept.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The twelve factors affecting self-concept are: <strong>abuse, age, appearance, culture, education, emotional development, gender, income, life experiences, relationships with others, sexuality, social media, stereotyping and discrimination, and unconscious bias.</strong> (Note: some versions of the spec list 12 distinct factors; always learn from the most recent published version and your notes.)</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Positive and Negative Influences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Each factor can operate as a positive <em>or</em> negative influence depending on the individual&rsquo;s circumstances. <dfn class="term" data-def="The way an individual or group are treated as inferior or different based on a characteristic such as age, gender, race or sexuality, regardless of their actual behaviour or abilities.">Stereotyping and discrimination</dfn> almost always harm self-concept &mdash; when a person is repeatedly told, either explicitly or through others&rsquo; behaviour, that they are &ldquo;less than&rdquo;, they begin to internalise that message. Research consistently links experiences of racism, sexism or homophobia to lower self-esteem and poorer mental health outcomes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By contrast, positive <strong>relationships with others</strong> are among the most powerful constructive forces on self-concept. When a parent consistently encourages a child, when a teacher notices and names a student&rsquo;s strengths, or when a care worker treats a service user with genuine respect, the individual&rsquo;s sense of their own worth is reinforced. Consider 14-year-old Daniil, who has just moved schools. His new form tutor regularly praises his contributions in class and pairs him with a supportive peer mentor. Within a few weeks, Daniil&rsquo;s self-confidence has improved noticeably. Positive relationships at a critical life stage can offset the negative effects of other factors.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Age</strong> affects self-concept in different ways at different life stages. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to self-concept fluctuations as they negotiate their identity. Older adults in later adulthood may face ageist assumptions about their capabilities &mdash; being spoken to slowly, being left out of decisions &mdash; which can damage their sense of self-worth.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Appearance</strong> is significant because society attaches value to physical looks, particularly in adolescence. A young person whose appearance does not match cultural ideals of attractiveness may experience lower self-esteem. Positive feedback about appearance from trusted people can counteract this.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Social media</strong> is a contemporary factor with significant negative potential. Constant comparison to curated, filtered images of others can lead individuals to feel their own lives or bodies are inadequate. For young people in particular, the social validation of likes and followers can become a measure of self-worth &mdash; making self-concept unstable and dependent on external approval.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Assumptions or preferences about individuals or groups that operate below conscious awareness, influencing behaviour and judgements without the person realising.">Unconscious bias</dfn> occurs when practitioners or peers make automatic assumptions about individuals based on characteristics such as race, gender or disability &mdash; without realising they are doing so. For the service user, repeated experiences of unconscious bias can lead to internalised negative self-concept: they begin to believe they are less capable or less valued than others.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">How Factors Interrelate</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The twelve factors do not operate in isolation. <strong>Income</strong>, for instance, affects the environment a person grows up in, the <strong>education</strong> they can access, their <strong>appearance</strong> (including the clothes and products available to them), and the <strong>relationships</strong> they are able to maintain. A child growing up in poverty may face multiple simultaneous negative influences on their self-concept. Conversely, strong <strong>emotional development</strong> &mdash; developed through secure early attachments &mdash; can buffer an individual against the harmful effects of other factors. Mark schemes on this topic often reward candidates who can identify two factors and explain how <em>each</em> affects self-concept, with examples.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: pick any two of the twelve factors. Write a chain showing how each one could negatively affect self-concept. Then write how each could work positively instead.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Factors affecting self-concept can be <strong>positive</strong> (supportive relationships, educational achievement, positive life experiences) or <strong>negative</strong> (abuse, discrimination, social media comparison, unconscious bias). The same factor can work in either direction depending on the individual&rsquo;s circumstances.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Six Strategies for Developing Resilience</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Resilience &mdash; the ability to adapt successfully to life&rsquo;s unexpected challenges &mdash; can be developed. The specification identifies six named strategies that support resilience-building. Practitioners in care settings use these strategies to help service users manage adversity more effectively.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Six Resilience Strategies Explained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>1. Self-reflection and learning from past difficulties.</strong> Individuals who take time to reflect on how they have managed previous challenges begin to recognise patterns in their own resilience. A social worker might encourage a care leaver to think about a difficult transition they have already navigated and identify what helped them at the time.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>2. Recognising personal strengths and qualities.</strong> Many individuals who have experienced adversity struggle to identify their own strengths. Practitioners support resilience by helping individuals name their capabilities &mdash; &ldquo;you managed that difficult conversation really well; that took courage.&rdquo; Recognising strengths provides a foundation for self-concept and for facing future challenges.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>3. Positive interactions with others.</strong> Maintaining supportive relationships &mdash; with friends, family, peers or practitioners &mdash; provides the emotional scaffolding that helps individuals manage difficult times. Feeling heard and valued by others reinforces a positive self-concept, which in turn supports resilience.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>4. Participating in new activities.</strong> Taking on new experiences &mdash; joining a sports club, starting a course, volunteering &mdash; builds competence and confidence. Each successful new experience adds to an individual&rsquo;s evidence that they are capable, which strengthens both self-concept and the capacity to adapt.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>5. Achievements, praise and rewards.</strong> External recognition matters, particularly during periods of low self-esteem. When a care worker acknowledges that a service user has reached a goal &mdash; however small &mdash; it reinforces positive self-concept and motivates continued effort. This is not about empty praise, but about honest recognition of genuine progress.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>6. Understanding personal limits and setting realistic goals.</strong> Resilience is not about pushing through regardless of cost. Individuals who understand their own boundaries &mdash; who can say &ldquo;I need a break&rdquo; or &ldquo;that is too much for me right now&rdquo; &mdash; are less likely to experience burnout or crisis. Setting realistic, achievable goals means progress is possible and self-concept is maintained, rather than being repeatedly undermined by unachievable targets.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Twelve factors shape self-concept &mdash; including age, appearance, relationships, culture, income, abuse, social media, stereotyping and discrimination, and unconscious bias &mdash; and each can work positively or negatively depending on circumstances.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">The six resilience strategies are: self-reflection on past difficulties, recognising personal strengths, positive interactions with others, participating in new activities, achievements and praise, and setting realistic goals.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Factors do not operate in isolation &mdash; income, education, relationships and emotional development interact, and high-tariff exam answers show how multiple factors compound each other.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">On a 4-mark question asking you to identify two factors affecting self-concept and explain how each works, the most common error is giving two factors that are so similar they cannot be awarded two separate marks &mdash; for example, &ldquo;discrimination&rdquo; and &ldquo;stereotyping&rdquo;, which are listed as one combined factor. Make sure your two chosen factors are genuinely distinct items from the list of twelve. Then for each factor, use the word &ldquo;because&rdquo; to show <em>how</em> it affects self-concept, not just that it does. For example: &ldquo;Social media negatively affects self-concept because young people compare themselves to filtered images of others, leading them to feel inadequate, therefore lowering their self-esteem.&rdquo;</p>
