<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Current UK Health Campaigns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The specification requires students to know named, current UK health campaigns across a range of themes. Exam questions often ask students to &ldquo;name a current campaign&rdquo; or &ldquo;give an example of a campaign that promotes [specific health behaviour]&rdquo; &mdash; a vague answer like &ldquo;a healthy eating campaign&rdquo; will not score marks. The campaigns below are grouped by theme, as they appear in the specification, and reflect initiatives that are active in the 2025&ndash;26 academic year. Note that campaign names change: <dfn class="term" data-def="A series of public health campaigns run by NHS England, initially launched as Change4Life in 2009, rebranded to Better Health in 2020, promoting healthy eating, physical activity and tobacco cessation across England.">Change4Life (now NHS Better Health)</dfn> is one such example &mdash; students should know both names.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Campaigns by Theme</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Alcohol awareness:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="An annual public health campaign run each January by Alcohol Change UK, encouraging people to take a month off alcohol to improve their health and raise money for charity.">Dry January</dfn> (Alcohol Change UK) runs every January and encourages participants to abstain from alcohol for the month. It has grown significantly since its launch in 2013, with millions of participants annually. Research consistently shows short-term improvements in liver function, blood pressure and sleep quality among participants.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Smoking cessation:</strong> <strong>No Smoking Day</strong> is an annual awareness day held each March, raising awareness of the harms of smoking and signposting support services including NHS Stop Smoking services. It has been running since 1984. <strong>Stoptober</strong> runs each October and encourages smokers to quit for the month of October, with evidence that those who stop for 28 days are five times more likely to quit for good.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Men&rsquo;s health:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="An annual campaign held each November by the Movember Foundation, raising awareness of men&rsquo;s health issues including prostate cancer, testicular cancer and mental health, through sponsored moustache-growing and fundraising.">Movember</dfn> takes place every November, targeting men&rsquo;s health issues including prostate cancer, testicular cancer, and men&rsquo;s mental health. By focusing on a visible and engaging activity (sponsored moustache-growing), it reaches men who might not otherwise engage with health services.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Mental health:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="An annual mental health awareness day, held on the first Thursday of February, run jointly by Mind and Rethink Mental Illness, encouraging open conversation about mental health to reduce stigma.">Time to Talk Day</dfn> is held each February and is run jointly by Mind and Rethink Mental Illness. It encourages people to have conversations about mental health to reduce stigma and encourage help-seeking. <dfn class="term" data-def="A framework developed by the New Economics Foundation (NEF) and adopted by NHS and public health bodies, identifying five evidence-based actions shown to improve well-being: connect, be active, take notice, keep learning, and give.">Five Ways to Well-being</dfn> was developed by the New Economics Foundation (NEF) for the government and has been widely adopted by NHS and local authority well-being programmes. The five actions are: connect (with people around you), be active, take notice (of the world around you), keep learning, and give (to others).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Physical activity:</strong> <strong>Cycle to Work Day</strong> encourages employees to cycle to work as a healthy and sustainable commuting choice. It is run in partnership with employers and local authorities. <strong>This Girl Can</strong> (Sport England) uses authentic, diverse imagery to encourage women and girls to be physically active regardless of their fitness level or body shape, specifically addressing the barriers of self-consciousness and fear of judgement.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Community health and safety:</strong> <strong>Anti-Bullying Week</strong> takes place each November, run by the Anti-Bullying Alliance, promoting positive relationships in schools and online. <strong>Keeping Warm in Winter</strong> campaigns (coordinated by NHS and local councils) target older adults and those with chronic conditions, reducing the health risks of cold homes including respiratory illness and cardiovascular events. <strong>Catch It, Bin It, Kill It</strong> is a long-running respiratory hygiene campaign reminding people to catch sneezes and coughs in a tissue and dispose of it promptly, reducing the spread of respiratory infections.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name one campaign for each theme: alcohol, smoking, men's health, mental health, physical activity, and community health. Write the campaign name and one fact about it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Current named campaigns include: <strong>Dry January</strong> (alcohol); <strong>No Smoking Day</strong> and <strong>Stoptober</strong> (smoking); <strong>Movember</strong> (men&rsquo;s health); <strong>Time to Talk Day</strong> and <strong>Five Ways to Well-being</strong> (mental health); <strong>Cycle to Work Day</strong> and <strong>This Girl Can</strong> (physical activity); <strong>Anti-Bullying Week</strong> (community). Knowing the campaign name, its theme, and one key feature is the minimum required to score marks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Methods Used to Deliver Health Campaigns</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The specification identifies six main methods through which health campaigns reach their audiences. Understanding each method allows students to evaluate how campaigns are delivered and to assess their likely effectiveness for different target groups.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Six Delivery Methods</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>1. Printed materials (leaflets and posters):</strong> Traditional and cost-effective. Leaflets in GP surgeries, pharmacies and community centres reach people at moments when they may be thinking about their health. Posters in public spaces (bus stops, schools, workplaces) build repeated exposure to a message. Limitation: can be ignored, may not reach those with low literacy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>2. Websites and online resources:</strong> The NHS and campaign organisations maintain websites with in-depth information, self-assessment tools, and signposting to support services. Websites allow people to access information privately, at a time of their choosing. Limitation: depends on internet access and digital literacy, which are not universal.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>3. Television advertisements:</strong> Reach a mass audience quickly and can use emotional storytelling to change attitudes. NHS campaigns (including anti-smoking and mental health awareness) have used TV advertising effectively. Limitation: expensive to produce; audiences can skip or tune out.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>4. Celebrities, soap operas and drama:</strong> Storylines in popular soap operas (such as EastEnders and Emmerdale) have addressed topics including domestic abuse, cancer diagnosis, addiction, and dementia, raising awareness among audiences who might not actively seek health information. Celebrity involvement in campaigns (such as sportspeople promoting exercise or mental health advocates speaking publicly) increases reach and relatability. Limitation: entertainment content is not always factually accurate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>5. Social media, internet and pop-ups:</strong> Social media platforms allow campaigns to reach specific demographic groups with targeted messaging. Movember and Time to Talk Day both use social media to encourage sharing and peer-to-peer advocacy. Pop-up adverts on websites and apps can target individuals based on search behaviour. Limitation: algorithm-driven platforms may not reach those most in need; misinformation spreads rapidly online.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>6. Guest speakers in schools and colleges:</strong> A health professional, service user or campaign representative speaking directly to young people can be more impactful than printed materials, because it allows for questions, personal testimony and two-way dialogue. Limitation: resource-intensive; effectiveness depends heavily on the quality of the speaker and session.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name one strength and one limitation for each of these three campaign delivery methods: social media, television advertising, and guest speakers.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six delivery methods are: <strong>printed materials</strong> (leaflets, posters); <strong>websites</strong>; <strong>television advertisements</strong>; <strong>celebrities and soap operas</strong>; <strong>social media and internet pop-ups</strong>; <strong>guest speakers</strong> in schools and colleges. Each method has different reach, cost, and effectiveness for different target groups.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Reliability and Validity of Health Campaign Information</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Not all health information is equally trustworthy. A key skill in health and social care is being able to assess whether information is <dfn class="term" data-def="The consistency of information or data &mdash; a reliable source produces consistent information that other credible sources agree with.">reliable</dfn> (consistent, produced by a credible organisation, confirmed by other sources) and <dfn class="term" data-def="The accuracy and appropriateness of information &mdash; valid information is truthful, based on good evidence, and relevant to the individual&rsquo;s specific situation.">valid</dfn> (accurate, based on sound evidence, and appropriate for the individual).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">When evaluating a health campaign, students should consider: <strong>who produced the information</strong> (is it an NHS body, a recognised charity such as Mind or Cancer Research UK, or a commercial organisation with a financial interest?); <strong>what evidence underpins the claims</strong> (is it peer-reviewed research, or anecdote?); <strong>when it was produced</strong> (health guidance changes as new evidence emerges; an older campaign may contain outdated advice); and <strong>whether it is appropriate for the target group</strong> (a campaign aimed at young men that uses text-heavy leaflets may be less effective than a video on social media).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n23">Compare two sources of information about stopping smoking. Source A is an NHS Better Health Stop Smoking page, citing clinical trials showing the effectiveness of nicotine replacement therapy, updated in 2024 and reviewed by NHS medical advisers. Source B is a blog post by a supplement company recommending a proprietary product to &ldquo;curb nicotine cravings naturally&rdquo;, with testimonials but no clinical citations. Source A is more reliable: it is produced by a public health body with no commercial interest, is based on peer-reviewed clinical evidence, and is regularly updated. Source B has lower reliability: the producer has a commercial interest in selling a product, and the evidence is anecdotal rather than clinical. In a care setting, only Source A would be appropriate to share with a service user who wants to stop smoking.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Named current UK campaigns include: Dry January (alcohol), No Smoking Day and Stoptober (smoking), Movember (men&rsquo;s health), Time to Talk Day and Five Ways to Well-being (mental health), This Girl Can and Cycle to Work Day (physical activity), and Anti-Bullying Week (community). Always learn the campaign name, not just the theme.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Six delivery methods are used by campaigns: printed materials, websites, television advertising, celebrities and soap operas, social media and pop-ups, and guest speakers in schools and colleges. Each has strengths and limitations.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Reliable and valid health information comes from NHS bodies, registered charities, and government organisations, is underpinned by clinical evidence, is recently updated, and is appropriate for the target group. Always consider who produced the information and why.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">Exam questions on campaigns often ask you to &lsquo;name&rsquo; or &lsquo;identify&rsquo; a current health campaign &mdash; always give the specific name (e.g. Dry January, not just &ldquo;an alcohol awareness campaign&rdquo;). For questions about reliability and validity, avoid vague answers like &ldquo;I would check it is from a good source&rdquo;. Be specific: name the type of organisation (e.g. NHS, registered charity, government body), the type of evidence (clinical trials rather than testimonials), and the currency of the information (recently updated rather than several years old). The command word &lsquo;Evaluate&rsquo; on a campaign question requires you to consider both strengths and limitations of a method or campaign &mdash; a one-sided answer cannot reach the top band.</p>
