<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Spaces, One Stage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The staging challenge at the heart of <em>Things I Know to Be True</em> is how to hold two fundamentally different spaces on stage simultaneously: the suburban kitchen, Fran&rsquo;s practical domestic territory, and the rose garden, Bob&rsquo;s emotional refuge. Both spaces must feel inhabited throughout the play; characters move between them, and that movement is always meaningful. A <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a stage is physically organised, including platforms, levels, furniture and architectural elements, to create environment and control where the audience looks.">set design</dfn> solution for this play must therefore do two things at once: establish the domestic realism of a contemporary Australian home and signal, through the choice of materials, <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of raised or lowered areas of the stage to create visual interest, suggest status and organise the playing space.">levels</dfn> and visual contrast, that the garden carries a weight beyond the literal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Many production approaches use a largely bare stage with carefully chosen scenic elements: a kitchen table and chairs upstage to one side, a patch of soil or a single rose bush to the other. The simplicity prevents the design from competing with the play&rsquo;s emotional register. The audience is asked to invest imaginatively in the spaces, and they will do so provided the key scenic signatures are clear: the hardness of the kitchen floor against the softness of the garden earth, the enclosed quality of the interior against the open expanse of the outdoor space.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two specific set design choices a designer could make to distinguish the kitchen from the garden space on a shared stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The kitchen and the rose garden are the play&rsquo;s twin centres. A designer&rsquo;s task is to make them coexist on a shared stage while keeping their distinct qualities clear: the kitchen is practical and enclosed; the garden is open, symbolic and emotionally loaded.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lighting the Seasons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Because the play moves through a full year in four seasons, a lighting designer has an unusually rich palette to work with. The seasonal arc creates a ready-made visual narrative: the lighting should track the emotional temperature of the play as much as the literal time of day. In spring, a warm, hopeful <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality, colour and intensity of light on stage at a given moment, created by the combination of all active lanterns.">lighting state</dfn> makes sense &mdash; perhaps a yellow-gold wash from a front-of-house wash bar, suggesting the optimism of new beginnings. By winter, the same space should feel colder and more exposed: a shift to a bluer, flatter wash, with reduced intensity, creates the emotional chill of the play&rsquo;s ending without needing any text to announce it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The garden, as an outdoor space, invites naturalistic lighting choices. Australian summer light is bright, high and unforgiving: a strong overhead source, perhaps a single <dfn class="term" data-def="A lantern that produces a hard-edged, focused beam of light, used for sharp specials and tight isolation of performers.">profile lantern</dfn> at steep angle to suggest midday sun, combined with a warm <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of lantern that produces a soft-edged, diffused wash of light, used for general cover and for warm fill.">Fresnel</dfn> fill from the side, would give the summer garden its harsh brightness. When the play moves into autumn and winter, the overhead angle softens; the fills are withdrawn. The physical sense of light shrinking is inseparable from the emotional sense of the family&rsquo;s certainties contracting.</p>

A leaf-break or branch-pattern gobo projected across the garden area would dapple the ground and the performers with shifting light and shadow, suggesting the presence of trees or foliage without any physical set element needing to represent it. In the spring and summer garden scenes, this gobo might be used with a warm amber gel; by autumn, the same gobo with an orange-red gel creates the impression of dying leaves. By winter, the gobo is withdrawn entirely: bare, flat light for a bare garden. The gobo therefore participates in the seasonal arc of the whole design.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Sound Design: Cicadas, Birdsong and the Kitchen Underscore</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Sound design in this play must distinguish between two acoustic environments: the inside of the kitchen and the outside of the garden. This distinction is not just about realism; it is about the play&rsquo;s emotional grammar. The kitchen is the space of direct confrontation &mdash; Fran&rsquo;s world, where practical truths are spoken. The garden is the space of private feeling and slower time. A sound designer who understands this will create two distinct sonic environments and manage the transition between them carefully.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">For the garden, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A continuous layer of ambient sound constructed from multiple recorded or live elements to create atmosphere or place.">soundscape</dfn> of cicadas and birdsong establishes the Australian summer setting while also carrying an emotional quality: the warmth and continuity of the natural world contrasting with the disruptions the children bring. As the seasons turn, the soundscape changes: autumn might bring a wind underscore, winter a near-silence broken only by rain. For the kitchen scenes, the sound design can afford to be more minimal &mdash; perhaps a low, barely perceptible <dfn class="term" data-def="Background music that plays beneath dialogue or action, supporting the emotional tone without drawing attention to itself.">underscore</dfn> in the most emotionally charged confrontations, or simply the ambient sounds of a domestic interior: the distant hum of a refrigerator, the sound of water from a tap. These diegetic sounds ground the audience in the domestic reality of the space.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, describe how you would use sound to distinguish the kitchen from the garden, naming at least one specific sound element for each space.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The sound design must create two distinct acoustic environments. The garden: a seasonal soundscape (cicadas in summer, wind in autumn, near-silence in winter). The kitchen: minimal underscore or diegetic domestic sounds. The shift between these environments should be part of every transition between the two spaces.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Costume Design: Season and Character</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Costume in this play operates primarily in a naturalistic register: the Price family are a contemporary suburban Australian family, and their clothing should reflect that. Jeans, casual shirts, the particular textures of modern working life. However, the seasonal structure gives a costume designer the opportunity to make the passage of time visible through clothing. Pip&rsquo;s spring arrival might be in light layers &mdash; linen or cotton in pale colours suggesting something new and tentative. Mark&rsquo;s summer scenes might carry the heat of the season in warmer fabrics and colours. By winter, the clothing grows heavier, more protective.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The more significant costume choice concerns Mark&rsquo;s arc as he transitions to Mia. The play tracks this across the summer season. A costume designer must collaborate closely with the director and, above all, with the performer to make choices that are specific, respectful and character-driven rather than generalised. Early in the summer season, Mark&rsquo;s clothing might reflect a tentative, not yet fully expressed identity. As the season reaches its peak &mdash; the moment of revelation with his parents &mdash; costume choices can carry the weight of that honesty: a clarity and simplicity of dress that signals that a decision has been made. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The overall outline and shape created by a garment on the body, used to signal character, period and emotional state.">silhouette</dfn> matters: a costume that fits the body honestly communicates something very different from one that is performing concealment.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Imagine a scene in the winter garden in which Bob tends the rose bush alone before Rosie arrives to speak with him. As a lighting designer, you would begin with the winter state established earlier: a cold, blue-white wash from a single overhead Fresnel, shadows long and hard, the gobo withdrawn so that only bare, flat light reaches the stage. The rose bush itself &mdash; a physical set element, bare-stemmed in winter &mdash; is lit separately by a downstage floor birdies unit with a pale amber gel, so that it glows slightly warmer than the space around it: still alive, still present. Bob is in the garden&rsquo;s main wash. When Rosie enters from the kitchen side, the kitchen&rsquo;s interior warm light briefly spills into the garden area before the door closes: a cross-fade that registers her arrival as a change in the light. As a sound designer, you would fade the winter soundscape (a low wind underscore, near-silence) to near-nothing as their conversation begins, so that the words land in absolute acoustic clarity. As a costume designer, Bob wears a heavier, darker jacket &mdash; navy or charcoal &mdash; and Rosie&rsquo;s winter coat in a contrasting lighter fabric: they are the same family, but the visual contrast of their costumes keeps them subtly individualised. Every design choice in this moment supports the emotional content: the coldness of the season, the fragility of the rose, the weight of what has not yet been said.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">The kitchen and the garden must coexist on a shared stage with clearly distinct qualities: the kitchen is practical and enclosed; the garden is open, symbolic and emotionally loaded. Set, lighting and sound all play a role in maintaining this distinction.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The seasonal arc (spring to winter) gives the lighting designer a structural palette: warm, high and bright in spring and summer; colder, flatter and more stripped by autumn and winter. A gobo can track this arc without any physical scene change.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Sound design uses two distinct environments: a seasonal soundscape for the garden (cicadas, wind, near-silence) and a minimal or diegetic domestic acoustic for the kitchen. Costume reflects both naturalistic character and the seasonal passage of time, with Mark&rsquo;s arc demanding particular sensitivity and specificity.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For the 8-mark &lsquo;Interpret as Designer&rsquo; question, the most common error is describing design ideas in general terms: &ldquo;I would use dark lighting to create a sad mood.&rdquo; This will not reach the top band. You must name specific elements: which lantern (profile, Fresnel, floor birdie), which colour (gel number or descriptive name &mdash; amber, cool blue, steel grey), at what intensity, from which direction, and what specific effect this creates for the audience at a named moment. The mark scheme rewards concreteness. &ldquo;I would use a tight profile spot with a pale amber gel overhead at steep angle to suggest the harsh Australian sun in the summer garden scene, creating a sense of exposure that mirrors the character&rsquo;s emotional vulnerability&rdquo; is the kind of answer that earns Mastering marks.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For the 20-mark extended staging response, spread your answer across multiple design disciplines: set, lighting, sound AND costume. If you focus only on lighting, you are missing a significant portion of the marks. Each discipline should address the same named moment from a different angle, and you should explain how the design choices work together to create a unified effect on the audience.</p>
