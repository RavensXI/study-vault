<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What the Play Asks Us to Believe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Things I Know to Be True</em> draws its title from a phrase that challenges certainty. The things we know to be true about our families &mdash; their loyalty, their stability, their love &mdash; are exactly what the play systematically tests. Andrew Bovell structures the play's themes around the tension between what the Price family projects outwardly (a settled suburban life, parental pride, sibling solidarity) and what is actually concealed beneath that surface. For a dramatist, this gap between appearance and reality is a rich seam of <dfn class="term" data-def="The emotional tension created when the audience has information that one or more characters on stage do not.">dramatic irony</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Family: Conditional and Unconditional Love</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The dominant theme of the play is family itself: what it can absorb, what it cannot, and who pays the cost of loyalty. Bovell explores two competing models of parental love through Bob and Fran. Fran's love is fierce and vocal; Bob's is quieter and harder to read. The play tests whether parental love is truly unconditional &mdash; offered regardless of what a child does or who they are &mdash; or whether it carries conditions that only a revelation can expose.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In staging this theme, a director working with the <dfn class="term" data-def="The position and arrangement of performers relative to each other and to the audience, used to express status, relationship and emotional state.">proxemics</dfn> of the family group can communicate volumes before dialogue begins. When all six characters occupy the stage together, the distances between them &mdash; who stands near whom, who gravitates towards a parent, who is physically at the margin of the group &mdash; form an instant visual map of the family's emotional geography. A production using a proscenium arch configuration might arrange the family in a tableau that places Bob and Fran at the apex, with the four children arranged at varying distances, so that the staging enacts the theme of parental expectation as a gravitational force.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name three revelations in the play that test whether the Price family's love is truly unconditional.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The central question of the play is whether the family's love is truly unconditional. Each of the four children's revelations tests this question from a different direction: departure, identity, criminality and grief.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Identity: Gender, Self-Determination and the Family's Gaze</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The theme of identity runs through every character arc. For Mark, identity is the most explicit subject: the summer season stages the journey of a character coming forward with a truth about who they are in gender terms. Bovell handles this arc with dramatic care, showing the parents' different responses and the sibling dynamics around the revelation. For a performer, the challenge is to play the specific mixture of assertion and vulnerability that characterises a moment of authentic self-disclosure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Frantic Assembly's movement sequences are structurally significant here. Contact improvisation and the round-by-through-under vocabulary can express the bodily dimension of identity &mdash; the sense of inhabiting a self more fully &mdash; in a way that pure dialogue cannot. A designer working on this section of the play might use <dfn class="term" data-def="Lighting using gradual transitions between states to suggest changing emotional atmosphere.">cross-fading</dfn> between lighting states to mark the transition between the family's before-and-after relationship with Mark / Mia, rather than a single sudden shift.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">For Pip, identity is about self-determination: leaving a marriage and a city is an act of individual will that the family reads as abandonment. For Rosie, identity is the most existential: she is the character who must find meaning after heartbreak and who speaks from the play's narrative edge. Identity is also a theme for the parents themselves: Bob's late-revealed secret reframes who he is in the audience's understanding, retroactively altering the identity they believed they had witnessed across the whole play.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Truth, Secrets and the Things We Don't Know</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Alongside the children's revelations, the play harbours two parental secrets: Bob's concealed infidelity and the late-play shock that reframes his character. These secrets introduce the theme of truth itself as a complex, unstable thing. What the family has built together &mdash; their shared history, their mutual understanding &mdash; turns out to rest on partial information. Bovell suggests that all families are, to some degree, fictions that each member maintains for the others.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The rose garden carries this theme in physical form. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An object, image or element of staging that carries significance beyond its literal function, representing an idea, relationship or emotional state.">prop as symbol</dfn>, the roses represent the beauty Bob cultivates and the concealment required to maintain it: a garden needs tending, work, constant management, just as a long marriage does. As a set designer, using the garden's seasonal change &mdash; spring blossom, summer fullness, autumn decay, bare winter stems &mdash; to mirror the play's structural arc makes the theme of concealed truth visible across the whole production.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Money, Ageing and Australian Suburbia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Ben's arc introduces the theme of financial wrongdoing and the gap between a child's projected success and their private reality. The theme here is the pressure of family expectation: the child who is supposed to have succeeded and has not. A performer playing Ben must communicate the layered quality of this shame &mdash; personal guilt, dread of a father's disappointment, awareness that his crime will affect the whole family.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Bovell also explores the theme of ageing parents and the moment when adult children see their parents as fallible. The Price parents are settled into late middle age when the play begins; by its end, they are irrevocably changed. The Australian suburban setting &mdash; a comfortable home, a tended garden, the domestic certainties of middle-class life in contemporary Adelaide &mdash; provides the <dfn class="term" data-def="The social, historical and cultural circumstances that surround the creation of a play and that shape how audiences understand it.">context</dfn> against which every destabilising revelation lands. The ordinariness of the setting is the point: the play insists that its themes are universal.</p>

Family love is tested by Pip's departure just as much as by Ben's arrest. Strong exam answers draw thematic connections across the play rather than treating each season in isolation.</p>
