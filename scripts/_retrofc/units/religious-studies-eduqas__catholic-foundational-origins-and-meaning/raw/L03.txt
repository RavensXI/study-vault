<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Imago Dei as the Foundation of Catholic Social Teaching</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Catholic understanding of what it means to love and serve others begins with a single conviction: every human being is made in the image of God &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Catholic teaching that every human being bears the image of God, giving each person inherent dignity that demands respect and justice.">imago Dei</dfn>. Because human dignity is God-given, not earned or granted by governments, it cannot be stripped away. This conviction shapes the whole tradition of <dfn class="term" data-def="The body of Catholic teaching on the moral dimensions of economic, political and social life, rooted in the dignity of every person.">Catholic Social Teaching</dfn> (CST), which has developed over more than a century of papal encyclicals and Church documents.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Second Vatican Council&rsquo;s <em>Gaudium et Spes</em> (&ldquo;Joy and Hope&rdquo;, 1965) is one of the most important documents in this tradition. Paragraph 29 teaches that all human beings possess the same dignity regardless of race, sex, social condition or religion, because all are made in God&rsquo;s image. Paragraph 78 connects this dignity to the call for justice, peace and reconciliation: the Church&rsquo;s mission is not only spiritual but also social &mdash; to transform the structures of society so that they reflect the dignity of every person.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences why imago Dei is the foundation of Catholic Social Teaching. Then name the document and paragraph that teaches universal human dignity.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>Gaudium et Spes</em> (Second Vatican Council, 1965) at paragraph 29 teaches that all people share equal dignity because all are made in the image of God. Paragraph 78 links this dignity to the Church&rsquo;s work for justice, peace and reconciliation in society.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Principles of Catholic Social Teaching</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Catholic Social Teaching rests on several interlocking principles. <strong>Human dignity</strong> is the foundation: every person deserves respect because they bear the image of God. <strong>The common good</strong> means that society must be organised so that everyone can flourish &mdash; not just the wealthy or powerful. <strong>Solidarity</strong> calls Catholics to see themselves as members of one human family and to stand with those who suffer. <strong>Subsidiarity</strong> means that decisions should be taken at the most local level possible, with higher authorities supporting rather than overriding local communities. <strong>Care for creation</strong> recognises that the earth is a gift from God to be stewarded, not exploited &mdash; a theme developed especially in Pope Francis&rsquo;s encyclical <em>Laudato Si&rsquo;</em> (2015).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">These principles are not abstract theories. They have generated a rich tradition of Catholic activism: trade unions, schools, hospitals, credit unions, housing associations and international development organisations. The call to <strong>love one&rsquo;s neighbour</strong> (Luke 10:25&ndash;37, the parable of the Good Samaritan) is understood as demanding concrete social action, not merely private kindness.</p>

God declared creation &ldquo;very good&rdquo; (Genesis 1:31) and entrusted it to human care. Pope Francis&rsquo;s <em>Laudato Si&rsquo;</em> (2015) extended Catholic Social Teaching explicitly to the environment &mdash; arguing that care for the poor and care for the earth are inseparable. The encyclical calls for an &ldquo;ecological conversion&rdquo;: a change of heart that sees the planet as a shared home entrusted to all of humanity, not a resource to be consumed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This ecological dimension reflects a deeper point: CST is not just about human-to-human justice. It sees all created reality as caught up in God&rsquo;s purposes. Catholics are called to respect creation as an expression of respect for its Creator and for future generations who will inherit the earth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">CAFOD: Catholic Action for Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, the official Catholic aid and development agency of England and Wales, working to end poverty worldwide.">CAFOD</dfn> (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development) is the official aid and development agency of the Bishops&rsquo; Conference of England and Wales. Founded in 1962, CAFOD works in over 40 countries worldwide, addressing poverty, hunger, displacement and injustice. Its work is explicitly grounded in Catholic Social Teaching &mdash; the belief that poverty is not just a practical problem but a moral one, since it violates the dignity of people made in God&rsquo;s image.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">CAFOD operates through local partners in the communities it serves, embodying the principle of subsidiarity. It campaigns for systemic change as well as delivering emergency aid: poverty is not just about immediate suffering but about unjust structures that keep people poor. Catholics in Britain support CAFOD through parish fundraising, Fast Days (giving up a meal and donating the money) and advocacy campaigns. CAFOD works alongside other aid organisations while maintaining its Catholic identity and theological motivation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain what CAFOD is, when it was founded, and what Catholic Social Teaching principle most directly motivates its work.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development) was founded in 1962 and is the official Catholic development charity of England and Wales. It works in over 40 countries, motivated by the Catholic Social Teaching principle that every person&rsquo;s dignity must be respected and that poverty is a moral injustice, not merely a practical problem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The St Vincent de Paul Society: Local Charity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">While CAFOD operates internationally, the <dfn class="term" data-def="An international Catholic voluntary organisation founded in 1833, working in local communities to provide practical help to people in need.">St Vincent de Paul Society</dfn> (SVP) focuses on local, face-to-face service. Founded in Paris in 1833 by a group of students inspired by the model of the seventeenth-century saint Vincent de Paul, the SVP now operates in over 150 countries. In Britain, SVP members visit those who are lonely or housebound, support people in poverty and crisis, and run food banks, prison visiting programmes and holiday schemes for children in deprived areas.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The SVP embodies the principle of solidarity: its members do not simply write cheques but enter into personal friendship with those they serve. The model is the Good Samaritan &mdash; not merely providing material help but accompanying the person in need. The SVP understands its work as part of what the Catechism calls the <em>corporal works of mercy</em> (feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, visiting the sick and imprisoned), rooted in Matthew 25:31&ndash;46, where Jesus identifies himself with the poor and vulnerable.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">The Catholic Church&rsquo;s commitment to <dfn class="term" data-def="Respectful dialogue and cooperation between people of different religious traditions, seeking mutual understanding without abandoning one&rsquo;s own beliefs.">inter-faith dialogue</dfn> grew significantly through the Second Vatican Council. The declaration <em>Nostra Aetate</em> (1965) acknowledged the truth and goodness found in other religious traditions &mdash; in Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam, Judaism and others &mdash; while insisting that Christ is the fullness of God&rsquo;s revelation. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">In Britain, the Catholic Bishops&rsquo; Conference maintains inter-faith advisers who facilitate dialogue with Muslim, Jewish, Hindu, Sikh and Buddhist communities. Bodies such as the Council of Christians and Jews and the Christian-Muslim Forum bring religious communities together to address shared concerns: social justice, religious freedom and community cohesion. This engagement reflects the conviction that the imago Dei is found in every human being, regardless of their religion, and that working together for the common good is itself an expression of love for one&rsquo;s neighbour. Britain&rsquo;s own diversity of religious traditions &mdash; Christianity in its many denominations, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism and Buddhism &mdash; makes inter-faith dialogue not an abstract ideal but a daily necessity in parish and school life.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Catholic Social Teaching is grounded in the imago Dei: every person&rsquo;s dignity is God-given. <em>Gaudium et Spes</em> 29 declares all people equal in dignity; paragraph 78 links this to justice, peace and reconciliation.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">CAFOD (international development, founded 1962) and the St Vincent de Paul Society (local face-to-face service, founded 1833) are the two key Catholic charities in Britain. Both are motivated by Catholic Social Teaching rather than simply humanitarian concern.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Inter-faith dialogue is part of the Church&rsquo;s mission: Britain&rsquo;s religious diversity makes dialogue with Judaism, Islam, Hinduism, Sikhism and Buddhism a practical and theological necessity, grounded in the recognition that the imago Dei is present in all people.</li>
</ul>
