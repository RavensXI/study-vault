--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Sedimentary Rocks: Reading the Past</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sedimentary rocks are an archive of Earth&rsquo;s history. Every layer &mdash; its grain size, composition, fossils and sedimentary structures &mdash; carries information about the environment where it was deposited. The specification asks you to identify six main rock types and to use their properties plus sedimentary structures to interpret the environment in which they formed. This lesson covers both the classification of the rocks and the toolkit of structures you use to read them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Clastic Sedimentary Rocks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Sedimentary rocks formed from fragments (clasts) of pre-existing rock that have been weathered, transported and deposited.">Clastic rocks</dfn> are built from fragments of older rocks. Their classification is based primarily on grain size. In order from coarsest to finest:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Breccia</strong> &mdash; coarse angular fragments (clasts larger than 2 mm, angular shape). The angularity shows short transport distance, suggesting deposition near the source &mdash; for example, in a scree at the foot of a cliff or as a fault breccia produced by crushing along a fault plane.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Conglomerate</strong> &mdash; coarse rounded fragments (clasts larger than 2 mm, rounded shape). Rounded clasts indicate long transport by a powerful agent such as a braided river or a high-energy beach. Old Red Sandstone conglomerates in Wales and Scotland are classic examples from the Devonian.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Sandstone</strong> &mdash; medium-grained (0.06&ndash;2 mm), clasts of quartz and rock fragments. Can form in rivers, beaches, dunes or shallow seas. The grain shape and sorting reveal the specific environment: well-rounded frosted grains = aeolian (desert) origin; angular to sub-rounded, poorly sorted = river origin.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Shale</strong> &mdash; very fine-grained (clay and silt particles, below 0.06 mm). Shale is the most abundant sedimentary rock, forming in low-energy quiet-water environments: deep sea, lake bottoms, tidal mudflats. It splits along thin parallel <dfn class="term" data-def="Horizontal planar surfaces along which sedimentary rock splits, representing successive layers of sediment deposition.">bedding</dfn> planes called laminations. Black shale contains abundant organic carbon and can be a source rock for petroleum.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what grain property distinguishes breccia from conglomerate? What does each tell you about transport distance?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Breccia = coarse angular clasts (short transport). Conglomerate = coarse rounded clasts (long transport). Sandstone = medium grains. Shale = fine clay/silt particles, low-energy environment. Grain size decreases as transport energy decreases.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Chemical and Biochemical Sedimentary Rocks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Sedimentary rocks formed by the precipitation of minerals from solution, including evaporites and some limestones.">Chemical sedimentary rocks</dfn> do not form from transported fragments but from ions precipitated directly from water. <strong>Evaporites</strong> &mdash; halite (rock salt) and gypsum &mdash; form when seawater or lake water evaporates in hot, arid conditions, leaving crystals behind. The presence of an evaporite sequence in the rock record is strong evidence for a palaeo-arid environment, such as a desert sabkha or an enclosed basin cut off from the open ocean.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Limestone</strong> is mostly calcium carbonate and can form in two ways: biochemically (from the shells and skeletons of marine organisms &mdash; corals, molluscs, echinoderms) or by direct chemical precipitation from supersaturated warm seawater. Most British limestones are biochemical in origin. Carboniferous Limestone in the Pennines formed in a warm, shallow tropical sea teeming with corals and crinoids. Chalk (Cretaceous Limestone) formed from the accumulation of microscopic calcareous algae in a deeper, clearer sea.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: name two evaporite minerals and state the palaeoenvironment they indicate. Which British limestone formed in a tropical reef setting?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Evaporites (halite, gypsum) = hot, arid, evaporating basin. Carboniferous Limestone (Pennines) = warm shallow tropical sea. Chalk = deeper, clearer Cretaceous sea. Limestone generally = marine environment with calcium carbonate source.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Sedimentary Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Sedimentary structures are primary features formed at the time of deposition and preserved in the rock record. They add vital information about transport direction, water depth and energy that grain size alone cannot give.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Layers of sediment inclined to the main horizontal bedding plane, formed by migration of bedforms such as dunes or ripples.">Cross-bedding</dfn> forms when sand is moved by a flowing current (river or wind) and builds up as migrating dunes. The internal laminae are inclined in the direction of flow. Cross-beds in a desert sandstone are large-scale (metres high) and curved; river cross-beds are smaller-scale. The dip direction of cross-beds gives the palaeoflow direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A vertical size gradient within a single bed, with coarsest grains at the base and finest at the top, formed by density settling from a turbidite current.">Graded bedding</dfn> shows coarse grains at the base of a bed fining upward to fine sand or silt at the top. It forms when a <dfn class="term" data-def="A submarine density current carrying a mixture of sediment and water that flows rapidly down a continental slope, depositing a graded bed.">turbidite</dfn> current &mdash; an underwater avalanche of sediment &mdash; flows down the continental slope. The heaviest grains settle first; fine particles drift down last. Graded beds are therefore a clear signature of deep-water turbidite environments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Ripple marks in a rock surface indicate shallow water or a current at the time of deposition. Asymmetric ripples (one steep face, one gentle face) indicate a one-directional current; symmetric ripples indicate wave oscillation. Desiccation cracks (mud cracks) form when wet sediment exposed to air dries and shrinks &mdash; they indicate an environment that was periodically exposed, such as a tidal flat or the margin of a desert lake.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Tillite</strong> is a lithified (turned to rock) glacial till &mdash; an unsorted, unlayered mixture of clay to boulder-sized clasts. Tillites in the rock record are evidence of past glaciation. The presence of striated clasts (scratched by glacial transport) confirms a glacial origin.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Coal</strong> forms from the compaction and burial of plant material in swampy, waterlogged conditions where organic matter accumulates without full decomposition (low oxygen). Carboniferous coal seams in South Wales and Yorkshire formed in tropical delta swamps. The sequence coal &mdash; seat earth (fossil soil) &mdash; shale &mdash; limestone, repeating, is the classic Carboniferous cyclothem.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Black shale</strong> is organic-rich, formed in oxygen-poor (anoxic) deep water where organic matter cannot fully decompose. It can be a source rock for oil and gas. Desert sandstone (cross-bedded, well-rounded frosted grains, red from iron oxide) records a hot, arid continental environment.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n22">A graphic log is a vertical column drawn to scale showing rock types and sedimentary structures from bottom (oldest) to top (youngest). Grain size is plotted on the horizontal axis &mdash; fine to the left, coarse to the right. A coarsening-upward sequence (getting coarser toward the top) indicates an increase in energy over time, such as a delta advancing into a basin. A fining-upward sequence indicates a decrease in energy, such as a river channel migrating away. Structures such as cross-beds, graded beds and ripple marks are shown as symbols within the relevant bed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Exam questions on graphic logs ask you to identify rock types, structures, or changes in environment reading upward through the sequence. Practice naming the structures and linking them to environments: graded bed = turbidite; desiccation cracks = exposure/tidal flat; large-scale cross-beds = aeolian dune.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n24">Together, rock type, grain properties, sedimentary structures and fossils build a complete picture of the ancient environment. The fossils found in sedimentary rocks are the focus of a later unit; for now, it is enough to know that reef corals in a limestone confirm a warm, shallow, well-oxygenated marine setting, while trace fossils (burrows) in shale indicate soft, waterlogged sea-floor sediment. This lesson completes the classification of sedimentary rocks; the next moves to metamorphic rocks and the conditions that transform existing rock without melting it.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Six spec sedimentary rock types: breccia (coarse, angular), conglomerate (coarse, rounded), sandstone (medium), shale (fine, laminated), evaporites (halite/gypsum &mdash; arid basins), limestone (carbonate &mdash; marine).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Sedimentary structures provide palaeo-environment evidence: cross-bedding (current direction), graded bedding (turbidite/deep sea), ripple marks (shallow current or wave), desiccation cracks (periodic exposure).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Graphic logs read from base (oldest) to top (youngest); a coarsening-upward sequence indicates increasing energy; a fining-upward sequence indicates decreasing energy.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Questions on sedimentary rocks frequently ask you to &ldquo;interpret the environment of deposition&rdquo; from one or more pieces of evidence. Give at least two pieces of evidence and link each explicitly to the environment. For example: &ldquo;The presence of graded bedding indicates a turbidite current in a deep-marine setting; the fine grain size of the mudstone interbeds supports a deep, low-energy environment.&rdquo; For graphic log questions, read upward from base to top &mdash; don&rsquo;t assume the oldest is at the top. Cross-bedding dip direction questions always ask you to use the inclined laminae to determine flow direction, not the overall bed attitude. For evaporites, state both the mineral present AND the process (evaporation in hot, arid conditions).</p>
