Lenses, colour, specular reflection and how sound travels through solids.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Specular Reflection and Diffuse Scattering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a beam of light hits a smooth, polished surface such as a mirror, all the rays reflect at the same angle &mdash; the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the <dfn class="term" data-def="An imaginary line drawn perpendicular to a surface at the point where a ray of light meets it, used as the reference line for measuring angles of incidence and reflection.">normal</dfn>. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="Reflection from a smooth surface in which parallel rays all reflect at the same angle, producing a clear image.">specular reflection</dfn>. Because all rays reflect uniformly, a clear image is formed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Most everyday surfaces are not perfectly smooth. An uneven surface scatters incoming light in many different directions &mdash; this is <dfn class="term" data-def="Reflection from an uneven surface in which light is scattered in many directions, so no clear image is formed.">diffuse reflection</dfn>. This is why you can see a piece of paper from any angle but cannot see your reflection in it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Refraction, Prisms and Dispersion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Light is refracted when it changes speed at a boundary. When white light passes through a glass prism, the different colours within it travel at slightly different speeds in glass, so they refract by slightly different amounts. Violet light (shortest visible wavelength) is refracted the most; red light (longest visible wavelength) is refracted the least. The beam spreads into the colours of the spectrum. This spreading is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The separation of white light into its component colours by a prism, caused by different wavelengths refracting by different amounts.">dispersion</dfn>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down which colour refracts most and which refracts least through a prism, and explain why.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>White light is a mixture of all visible colours, ranging from violet (shortest wavelength) to red (longest wavelength). A prism disperses white light into a spectrum because different wavelengths travel at different speeds in glass, causing different amounts of refraction. Violet refracts the most; red refracts the least.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Colour, Absorption and Transmission</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transparent coloured material that transmits light of certain wavelengths and absorbs others, used to select particular colours from white light.">coloured filter</dfn> works by transmitting light of some wavelengths and absorbing light of others. A red filter transmits red wavelengths and absorbs all others, which is why only red light passes through it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">An object&rsquo;s apparent colour depends on which wavelengths it <em>scatters</em> (reflects diffusely). An object appears white if it scatters all colours equally; it appears black if it absorbs all colours and scatters none. A red tomato scatters red wavelengths and absorbs the rest. This means the colour you perceive depends on the light falling on the object: a red object under only blue light will appear dark (because there is no red light to scatter).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thicker in the middle than at the edges; it causes parallel rays of light to converge (meet) at a focal point.">convex lens</dfn> (converging lens) is thicker at the centre. It refracts parallel rays inwards so they meet at a focal point on the far side. Convex lenses are used in cameras, eyes and magnifying glasses. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thinner in the middle than at the edges; it causes parallel rays to diverge, appearing to spread from a virtual focal point.">concave lens</dfn> (diverging lens) is thinner at the centre. It refracts parallel rays outwards so they appear to spread from a point on the same side as the incoming light. Concave lenses are used in glasses to correct short-sightedness.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An image that is formed where rays of light actually meet, so it can be projected onto a screen.">real image</dfn> is formed when refracted rays actually converge at a point; it can be projected onto a screen. A <dfn class="term" data-def="An image that appears to be at a point from which rays seem to diverge, but cannot be projected onto a screen.">virtual image</dfn> appears to come from a point but the rays do not actually meet there; it cannot be projected.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sound Transmission Through Solids</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n13">Sound travels as a longitudinal wave, and it travels better through solids and liquids than through air &mdash; because the particles are closer together and transmit vibrations more efficiently. This is why you can hear sound through a wall more easily when you press your ear against it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The bones in the middle ear are a biological example of sound transmission through a solid. The three small bones (malleus, incus and stapes) transmit vibrations from the eardrum to the fluid of the inner ear. These bones transmit sound most efficiently in the frequency range from about 1 kHz to about 3 kHz &mdash; which corresponds well with the frequencies most important for understanding speech. This limited frequency response is the reason human hearing has the range it does.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in two sentences why sound travels better through solids than air, linking to particle spacing.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sound travels faster and more efficiently through solids and liquids than through gases, because particles are closer together and transmit vibrations more readily. The bones in the middle ear transmit sound vibrations most efficiently in the 1 kHz to 3 kHz range, which is the frequency range of normal speech.</p>
</div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">The ways in which sound waves are transmitted, reflected and refracted as they pass through different materials are exploited in several technologies. In medical <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of high-frequency sound waves above the human hearing range to image internal body structures, by detecting the reflections from boundaries between tissues.">ultrasound imaging</dfn>, high-frequency sound waves above the range of human hearing are directed into the body. Reflections from boundaries between different tissue types return to a detector and are used to build up an image.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">SONAR (Sound Navigation And Ranging) works on a similar principle in water: pulses of sound are sent out from a vessel and the time for reflections to return is used to calculate the depth of the sea floor or the position of objects such as fish shoals. Earthquake (seismic) waves, which travel through the Earth, have revealed details of the Earth&rsquo;s layered internal structure because different types of seismic wave are reflected and refracted differently at boundaries between rock layers. The differences in velocity, absorption and reflection between wave types allow scientists to map structures hidden from direct observation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Transmission, Absorption and Scattering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">When a wave meets a material, three things can happen: it may be <strong>transmitted</strong> (pass through), <strong>absorbed</strong> (its energy transferred to the material) or <strong>reflected and scattered</strong>. Most real encounters involve a combination of all three, in proportions that depend on the wavelength of the wave and the nature of the material. A clear glass window transmits visible light but absorbs some infrared; a coloured glass transmits selected wavelengths and absorbs others; an opaque wall absorbs all visible light and transmits none.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The principle extends to sound. Soft furnishings in a concert hall absorb sound, reducing echo and reverberation. Hard surfaces reflect sound, which can cause unwanted echoes. Acoustic engineers designing concert halls and recording studios use knowledge of sound transmission, absorption and reflection to control how sound behaves in a space. The same considerations apply to building construction: thick, dense walls transmit less sound than thin partitions because more of the wave energy is absorbed within the material.</p>

For questions about lenses, remember that a convex lens converges rays (they meet on the far side) and produces a real image that can be projected, while a concave lens diverges rays and produces a virtual image. In questions on sound transmission, be specific: explain that sound travels faster in solids because particles are closer together, not just &lsquo;because it&rsquo;s denser&rsquo;. For questions on the ear, state the frequency range (approximately 1&ndash;3 kHz) explicitly if the mark scheme looks numerical.</p>
