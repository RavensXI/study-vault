--- DESCRIPTION ---
Women who fought — the Amazons of Greek myth and art, Penthesilea, and Camilla in Virgil's Aeneid.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Reversing the Natural Order</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Both the Greeks and the Romans showed a persistent fascination with the figure of the warrior woman: a female who took up arms, stepped onto the battlefield, and excelled in the role that ancient culture most exclusively assigned to men. These figures appeared in myth, in epic poetry, and on the walls of public buildings. They were rarely celebrated without ambivalence. A woman who fought was, by definition, doing something she was not supposed to do &mdash; and ancient sources show both admiration for her courage and relief when the social order was restored. Studying warrior women in the ancient world reveals as much about the boundaries imposed on real women as it does about the warriors themselves.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Amazons in Greek Myth and Art</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A race of warrior women in Greek mythology, said to live in the region of the Black Sea and to be the equals of men in battle.">Amazons</dfn> were a mythological nation of female warriors, said to live somewhere on the fringes of the known world &mdash; in early sources near the Black Sea, later in Libya or other remote regions. Greek myth stressed what made them different from ordinary women: they allegedly cut off one breast to improve their aim with a bow (the name &ldquo;Amazon&rdquo; was popularly but incorrectly derived from &ldquo;without breast&rdquo; in Greek); they rejected men except for the purpose of reproduction; they chose to fight rather than to weave.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A battle between Greek heroes and the Amazons, a popular subject in Greek art representing the triumph of Greek civilisation over barbarism.">Amazonomachy</dfn> (battle against the Amazons) became one of the most popular subjects in Greek public art precisely because it stood for something: the victory of Greek order over barbarism, of civilised men over these dangerous, inverted women. The Bassae Frieze (now in the British Museum) is one of the prescribed visual sources for this subject. This continuous marble frieze originally decorated the interior of the temple of Apollo at Bassae in Arcadia, constructed in the fifth century BC. The frieze, carved in high relief, depicts two battles side by side: on one set of slabs, Lapiths fight Centaurs (the Lapith Centauromachy — the battle at the wedding feast of Peirithoos); on another, Greeks fight Amazons (the Amazonomachy). In both battles, the Greeks are depicted as the forces of order triumphing over chaos, whether that chaos takes the form of half-human monsters or warrior women who have stepped outside their proper role.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the Bassae Frieze, where was it originally located, and what two battles does it depict? What does the combination of subjects tell us?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Bassae Frieze is a marble relief from the interior of the Temple of Apollo at Bassae (Arcadia), dating to the fifth century BC, now in the British Museum. It depicts two battles: the Centauromachy (Lapiths versus Centaurs — the battle at the wedding feast of Peirithoos) and the Amazonomachy (Greeks versus Amazons). Both battles symbolise the triumph of Greek civilised order over savage disorder.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Penthesilea: the Amazon Queen at Troy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The queen of the Amazons in Greek mythology; she fought for Troy against the Greeks and was killed by Achilles.">Penthesilea</dfn> was the most celebrated individual Amazon: the queen who led her warriors to Troy to fight for the Trojans against the Greeks. Her story does not appear in the prescribed books of Homer&rsquo;s <em>Iliad</em>, but it was well known in antiquity from other sources, and it was depicted on Greek vases. According to the myth, Penthesilea fought with great courage before being killed by Achilles. At the moment of her death Achilles is said to have fallen in love with her &mdash; a paradox that ancient artists found compelling. A famous red-figure cup attributed to the Penthesilea Painter (c. 460 BC) shows Achilles driving his spear into Penthesilea&rsquo;s throat at the very moment their eyes meet, making the scene simultaneously an act of violence and a moment of recognition between equals.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">What does Penthesilea&rsquo;s death tell us about Greek attitudes to the warrior woman? The pattern is revealing: Penthesilea is given heroic qualities but is ultimately killed by the greatest Greek hero. The Amazon challenge is real but it is contained. The <em>Amazonomachy</em> always ends with the Greeks winning. The warrior woman is admired for her courage but the proper order is restored: men on top, women in their place. This pattern appears throughout Greek culture, from the Bassae Frieze to the metopes of the Parthenon (which also depicted an Amazonomachy on the west side of the temple) to the great mythological battles on Attic vases.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Modern archaeology has found evidence in the steppes of Central Asia (modern Ukraine, Russia, and Kazakhstan) of women buried with weapons, horses, and armour alongside male warriors in nomadic burial mounds (kurgans) dating from around the fifth to the third centuries BC. Some scholars argue these are the historical reality behind the Amazon myth &mdash; Greek travellers and traders heard stories of Scythian or Sarmatian women who rode and fought, and converted these into a mythological nation. The ancient Greek historian Herodotus describes the Sauromatae as descended from Amazons and Scythians, and notes that their women rode and hunted. This does not prove that the Amazons of Greek myth were historical, but it does suggest Greek culture was aware that some women in the world beyond Greece did things that Greek women did not.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Camilla in Virgil&rsquo;s Aeneid</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The Roman answer to the Amazon tradition is <dfn class="term" data-def="A warrior maiden in Virgil's Aeneid who fights against Aeneas on behalf of the Italian king Turnus; dedicated as a child to the goddess Diana.">Camilla</dfn>, the warrior maiden who appears in Virgil&rsquo;s <em>Aeneid</em> (Book 11, lines 532&ndash;835, a prescribed source). Camilla leads a band of female warriors who fight alongside Turnus against Aeneas and the Trojans. She is described in heroic terms: fast enough that she could run across a field of grain without bending a single stalk, and fierce in battle, equal to the best male warriors on the field. Her dedication to the virgin goddess Diana (the equivalent of the Greek Artemis) marks her as having chosen a life outside the normal female role of wife and mother.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Virgil&rsquo;s portrayal of Camilla is genuinely admiring: she is brave, skilled, and noble, and she dies heroically. Yet Virgil also provides a cause for her downfall that is significant. She becomes distracted by the sight of a Trojan warrior wearing dazzlingly beautiful armour and gold, and in her desire to strip it from him she breaks off from the battle and is killed from behind. Some ancient and modern readers see this as a gendered comment: Camilla falters because she is distracted by beautiful things, a characteristically &ldquo;feminine&rdquo; weakness. Others see it simply as the standard Homeric device of a hero being killed at the moment of pride (<dfn class="term" data-def="In Greek thought, excessive pride or arrogance that leads to a character's downfall, often by provoking divine punishment.">hubris</dfn>). The ambiguity is part of what makes Camilla such a rich figure for analysis.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state who Camilla is in the Aeneid, which goddess she is dedicated to, and how she dies. Then explain what her death might suggest about Roman attitudes to warrior women.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Camilla is a warrior maiden in Virgil&rsquo;s <em>Aeneid</em> (Book 11), dedicated to Diana. She fights with heroic skill but is killed when she becomes distracted by a warrior&rsquo;s beautiful armour. Her fate can be read as restoring conventional order: the woman warrior&rsquo;s excellence is acknowledged but her downfall reaffirms that the battlefield ultimately belongs to men.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Both Greek and Roman cultures produced warrior women who were admirable but ultimately defeated, and both used the warrior woman to explore the boundaries of acceptable female behaviour. The Amazons in Greek art (Bassae Frieze, Parthenon metopes) are usually foreign or mythological: they are killed by Greek heroes, restoring order. Camilla in Virgil is fighting on the wrong side (against the proto-Roman Aeneas) and is killed by a named Roman soldier. In both traditions, the pattern is the same: excellence acknowledged, death as restoration of order, and a male hero as the agent of that restoration. The key difference is in tone: Greek Amazonomachy tends to be triumphalist, emphasising the victory of civilisation; Virgil&rsquo;s portrait of Camilla is more elegiac, mourning what is lost when such a heroic figure is cut down. The Bassae Frieze is a public, civic monument designed to celebrate Greek victory; the <em>Aeneid</em> is an epic poem that can afford more nuance.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Amazons in Greek myth and art represent warrior women who challenge the social order; the Amazonomachy on the Bassae Frieze and Parthenon depicts Greek heroes defeating them, symbolising the triumph of civilisation over barbarism and the restoration of proper gender roles. The Bassae Frieze also depicts the Lapith Centauromachy alongside the Amazonomachy — both battles symbolise the defeat of savage disorder by civilised order.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Penthesilea, the Amazon queen who fought and died at Troy, appears in Greek vase painting as a simultaneously heroic and tragic figure &mdash; admired for her courage but killed by Achilles, ensuring the Greek hero remains supreme.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Camilla in Virgil&rsquo;s Aeneid (Book 11) is the Roman equivalent: a warrior maiden of genuine excellence who is killed when she allows herself to be distracted. Both Greeks and Romans used warrior women to acknowledge female capability while ultimately reaffirming that the battlefield was a male domain.</li>
</ul>
