<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Rome and the Greek Gods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the Romans encountered the Greek world from around the third century BC onwards, they found a pantheon of deities that mapped closely &mdash; though not perfectly &mdash; onto their own. The Romans absorbed and adapted Greek mythology with extraordinary thoroughness, giving Greek gods Roman names and integrating their stories into Roman religion and literature. Understanding where the correspondence is close, where it differs, and what those differences reveal about each culture is one of the most important analytical skills in this subject.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The key Roman Olympians were: <dfn class="term" data-def="King of the Roman gods, ruler of the sky and thunder; equivalent to the Greek Zeus, but with a more formal, authoritative public role in Roman state religion.">Jupiter</dfn>, Juno, Neptune, Vulcan, Mercury, Mars, Pluto, Apollo, Juno, Venus, Minerva, Diana, Bacchus, Vesta and Ceres. Apollo was adopted directly into Roman religion without a name change &mdash; one of very few deities to retain his Greek name.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this table and write the Roman equivalent of each Greek god: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hephaistos, Ares, Hermes, Artemis, Aphrodite, Demeter, Dionysos, Athena, Hestia, Hades.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The principal Greek&ndash;Roman equivalences: Zeus &rarr; Jupiter; Hera &rarr; Juno; Poseidon &rarr; Neptune; Hephaistos &rarr; Vulcan; Ares &rarr; Mars; Hermes &rarr; Mercury; Artemis &rarr; Diana; Aphrodite &rarr; Venus; Demeter &rarr; Ceres; Dionysos &rarr; Bacchus; Athena &rarr; Minerva; Hestia &rarr; Vesta; Hades &rarr; Pluto. Apollo kept his Greek name in Roman religion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">How Roman Religion Adapted Greek Myth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Roman religion was originally quite different from the Greek tradition. Early Roman religion was highly ritual-focused: the Romans believed that correct performance of religious acts (the right words, the right gestures, the right offerings at the right time) maintained the <dfn class="term" data-def="The relationship of goodwill and harmony between the Roman people and their gods, maintained through correct religious practice.">pax deorum</dfn> &mdash; the &ldquo;peace of the gods&rdquo; &mdash; which guaranteed Rome&rsquo;s prosperity and safety. The gods were powerful forces to be kept on side through meticulous ritual, and initially the Romans were less interested in myths about the gods&rsquo; personal lives than the Greeks were.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">As Rome came into sustained contact with the Greek world &mdash; through trade, conquest and the presence of Greek communities in southern Italy &mdash; Roman writers began actively adopting Greek myths. The poets Virgil and Ovid in particular wove Greek mythology into their Latin masterworks. Virgil&rsquo;s <em>Aeneid</em> traces Rome&rsquo;s origins to the Trojan hero Aeneas, inserting Rome into the whole framework of Greek myth. Ovid&rsquo;s <em>Metamorphoses</em> retold hundreds of Greek mythological stories for a Roman audience, giving the Roman gods Greek names in many episodes.</p>

The Greek god Ares was widely disliked in Greek mythology &mdash; portrayed as brutal, bloodthirsty and often cowardly, mocked even by his fellow Olympians. Mars, by contrast, was second only to Jupiter in Roman religious importance. He was the divine father of Romulus, the founder of Rome, and the protector of Roman armies. His importance to the Roman state was vastly greater than Ares&rsquo;s importance to any Greek city-state. This difference reflects the Romans&rsquo; emphasis on military power and expansion as core national values.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Jupiter, Juno and the Capitoline Triad</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Jupiter was Rome&rsquo;s supreme deity, the equivalent of Zeus as king of the gods and ruler of sky and thunder. Yet Jupiter had a more formal, political dimension in Roman religion than Zeus possessed in Greece. The great Capitoline Temple in Rome was dedicated not just to Jupiter but to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three central Roman deities worshipped together on the Capitoline Hill in Rome: Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, the most important gods of the Roman state.">Capitoline Triad</dfn>: Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, worshipped together as the protective trinity of the Roman state. This formal grouping of three gods as an official state cult has no direct Greek equivalent and represents a distinctly Roman religious development. Juno (equivalent to Greek Hera) was queen of the gods and particularly associated with women, marriage and childbirth, as well as the protection of the Roman state. Unlike the often vindictive and jealous Hera of Greek myth, Juno&rsquo;s official Roman persona was more dignified and her role in state religion was considerably more prominent.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Explain in two sentences why Mars was more important in Roman religion than Ares was in Greek religion. Then give one visual source that shows Roman gods in a monumental context.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In Roman religion, the gods served a strongly civic and military function. Jupiter guaranteed Rome&rsquo;s supremacy; Mars protected her armies; Vesta (goddess of the hearth) maintained the eternal flame symbolic of Rome&rsquo;s continuity. The Capitoline Triad &mdash; Jupiter, Juno, Minerva &mdash; was the heart of official Roman state religion, worshipped on the Capitoline Hill.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Differences in How Gods Were Represented in Roman Art</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Roman artists inherited the Greek tradition of anthropomorphic representation and continued to show gods in human form. However, the emphasis in Roman art shifted. Where Greek art often showed gods in active, mythological moments (Zeus hurling the thunderbolt; Athena emerging from his head), Roman art frequently used gods to make political statements. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A marble statue of Augustus, the first Roman emperor, found at Prima Porta near Rome; Augustus holds a commander's baton and his breastplate depicts the cosmos under Roman order.">Augustus of Prima Porta</dfn> &mdash; a marble statue now in the Vatican Museum &mdash; is the most spectacular example. Augustus stands in a military pose, his arm raised in an oratorical gesture, wearing armour whose breastplate carries a cosmic scene: the sky god (Caelus) above, the earth goddess (Terra) below, and a Roman general receiving back the standards captured by the Parthians. At the base of the statue is Cupid riding a dolphin, a reference to Augustus&rsquo;s claimed divine descent through Venus via his adoptive father Julius Caesar. The statue thus weaves together military victory, cosmic order, divine ancestry and political authority in a single image.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An altar of Augustan peace built in Rome between 13 and 9 BC; its carved relief panels show processions, mythological scenes and allegories celebrating Augustus's rule and Rome's divine blessings.">Ara Pacis</dfn> (Altar of Augustan Peace) offers a complementary example. Completed in 9 BC, it was a marble altar enclosed by carved walls depicting both a solemn procession of the imperial family and mythological relief panels. One panel shows the goddess Roma seated as a warrior; another shows Aeneas making sacrifice at the altar, linking Augustus directly to the Trojan hero and thus to Venus and the gods. The mythological scenes are not entertainment &mdash; they are political theology, embedding Augustus&rsquo;s rule within a divine narrative of Rome&rsquo;s destined greatness.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Greek religion placed great emphasis on myth &mdash; the stories of the gods&rsquo; personalities, quarrels, loves and adventures were central to how ordinary people understood the divine world. The gods in Greek religion were vividly individual and often humanly flawed. Roman religion, particularly in its official state form, was more procedural: the correct performance of ritual (and the maintenance of the pax deorum) was more important than narrative or personal devotion to a specific god&rsquo;s character. A Roman farmer would pray to the appropriate deity for a good harvest not because he believed that deity had an interesting personal history, but because performing the right rite would secure divine favour. This difference does not mean Romans were less religious &mdash; they were deeply so &mdash; but the centre of gravity was different: Greek religion was mythological; Roman religion was ritual.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Most Greek gods had Roman equivalents with new names; the main exception was Apollo, who kept his Greek name. Mars (Ares) is the clearest example of a god whose Roman version was far more important than the Greek original.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Roman religion emphasised the pax deorum &mdash; keeping the gods happy through correct ritual &mdash; rather than the personal mythological dramas that dominated Greek stories about the gods.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Roman rulers used divine imagery in public art (the Augustus of Prima Porta; the Ara Pacis) to make political claims, embedding their authority within a divine narrative of Rome&rsquo;s destiny.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When you are asked to compare Greek and Roman religion or the treatment of a god in both cultures, always move beyond the name-swap. The key differences to show are: Roman gods had a stronger civic and political role (pax deorum; the Capitoline Triad; Mars as father of Rome); Roman art used gods as propaganda in a way Greek art generally did not (Augustus of Prima Porta; the Ara Pacis); the Roman attitude to religion was more ritual-procedural, the Greek more narrative-mythological. Questions often ask you to analyse a source &mdash; if you get a Roman image like the Augustus statue, explain what political message each divine element sends, not just what it shows.</p>
