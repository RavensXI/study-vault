<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Belmont by Moonlight (Act 5, Scene 1)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Act 5 abandons Venice entirely and takes the audience to Belmont, a world of music, moonlight and lovers. Lorenzo and Jessica open with a lyrical exchange of classical references: “In such a night / Troilus methinks mounted the Trojan walls / And sighed his soul toward the Grecian tents.” But Shakespeare’s choice of examples is telling — every love story they cite ended in betrayal, abandonment or tragedy (Troilus and Cressida, Pyramus and Thisbe, Dido and Aeneas). The beauty of the language conceals darkness, just as Belmont’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A seemingly perfect or ideal world. Belmont appears harmonious, but its happiness depends on excluding those who do not belong.">harmony</dfn> depends on excluding those who do not fit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Lorenzo delivers a speech on the power of <dfn class="term" data-def="In Renaissance thought, the concept that the universe was held together by divine harmony. Music symbolises cosmic order; those who cannot appreciate it (like Shylock) are associated with disorder and treachery.">music</dfn>: “The man that hath no music in himself, / Nor is not moved with concord of sweet sounds, / Is fit for treasons, stratagems and spoils.” This appears to be aimed at Shylock, who earlier complained about the “vile squealing of the wry-necked fife.” Shakespeare uses music to mark the boundary between those who belong in Belmont’s world and those who do not. Shylock’s absence from Act 5 haunts the scene: the comedy achieves its harmonious ending by removing the figure whose suffering made that harmony possible.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: Why is Shylock’s absence from Act 5 significant?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shylock never appears in Act 5. Belmont’s moonlit harmony is achieved by excluding the character whose suffering underlies the entire plot. Lorenzo’s speech about music implicitly condemns Shylock as unfit for the community. Shakespeare makes the audience aware of whose absence makes the “happy ending” possible.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Ring Trick</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Portia and Nerissa test their new husbands by demanding the rings they gave away. Bassanio gave his ring to “Balthazar” (Portia in disguise) as payment for saving Antonio; Gratiano gave his to “Balthazar’s clerk” (Nerissa). The wives pretend to be outraged, accusing their husbands of giving the rings to other women. The scene is played for <dfn class="term" data-def="Comedy arising from misunderstanding, deception and wordplay. The ring trick generates comedy because the audience knows the truth while the characters do not.">comedy</dfn> — the audience knows the truth while the husbands squirm.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">But the ring trick also has serious implications. It tests whether Bassanio’s love for Portia or his obligation to Antonio is stronger. At the trial, Bassanio said “life itself, my wife and all the world” are not worth more than Antonio’s life — a declaration that prioritises male friendship over marriage. The ring trick reasserts Portia’s authority: she has saved Antonio, outperformed the male lawyers, and now she demonstrates that she controls the marriage. The resolution gives the rings back, but the power dynamic has been established: Portia is the dominant figure.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Three couples celebrate at the play’s close: Bassanio and Portia, Gratiano and Nerissa, Lorenzo and Jessica. Antonio stands alone. He began the play sad and inexplicably melancholy; he ends it in the same condition, despite having his fortune restored. His intense devotion to Bassanio has been replaced by Bassanio’s marriage to Portia, and Antonio has no equivalent relationship. Like Shylock, Antonio is excluded from the comedy’s resolution — not by cruelty but by the structural logic of a genre that ends in marriage. For those who do not marry, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The conventional happy ending of a Shakespearean comedy, typically featuring marriages, reconciliation and social harmony.">comic resolution</dfn> offers no place.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Comedy or Tragedy? The Genre Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The play’s genre is disputed. It was first published as a comedy, and it does end with marriages and restored fortunes. But the trial scene’s cruelty — Shylock stripped of everything — sits uneasily with Act 5’s celebration. Modern scholars often classify it as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A play that combines comic structure with disturbing or morally complex content, resisting easy classification.">problem play</dfn>. Shakespeare appears deliberately to create this discomfort: the moonlit final act is beautiful precisely because the ugliness has been expelled. The audience must decide whether to accept the comedy at face value or to feel the absence of those it excludes.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three characters who are excluded from the play’s ‘happy ending’ and explain why this matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The play’s “happy ending” excludes Shylock (forced conversion, absent from Act 5), Antonio (alone without a partner), and arguably Jessica (who has lost her father, her faith and her community). Shakespeare’s comedy achieves its resolution only by removing or marginalising those who do not fit.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Jessica’s presence in Belmont is awkward. She has betrayed her father, stolen his money, traded her dead mother’s ring for a monkey, and converted to Christianity. The Christian characters welcome her, but Lorenzo’s earlier description of her as “wise, fair and true” feels hollow given what she has done. In the “in such a night” exchange, Lorenzo and Jessica tease each other with stories of betrayal — a joke that acknowledges the fragility of their own love. Jessica is technically included in the comedy’s resolution, but her assimilation has come at the cost of everything she was.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Belmont’s moonlit harmony depends on excluding Shylock — his absence haunts Act 5, and Lorenzo’s music speech implicitly condemns those who do not belong.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">The ring trick reasserts Portia’s dominance: she has saved Antonio, outperformed the lawyers, and now controls the marriage.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">The comedy’s resolution excludes Shylock, isolates Antonio, and uneasily assimilates Jessica — Shakespeare creates a ‘happy ending’ that the audience cannot fully accept.</li></ul>
