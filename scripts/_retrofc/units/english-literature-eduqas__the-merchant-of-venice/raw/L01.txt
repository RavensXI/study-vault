<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Anti-Semitism in Shakespeare’s England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare wrote <em>The Merchant of Venice</em> around 1596–98. Jews had been expelled from England by Edward I in 1290 and would not be officially readmitted until 1656. Most of Shakespeare’s audience had never met a Jewish person. <dfn class="term" data-def="Prejudice, hostility or discrimination against Jewish people. Elizabethan anti-Semitism was widespread, fuelled by religious stereotypes portraying Jews as Christ-killers and money-lenders.">Anti-Semitism</dfn> was embedded in English culture: Jews were associated with <dfn class="term" data-def="Lending money at interest, condemned by the medieval Church as sinful. Christians were forbidden to charge interest, so money-lending fell to Jewish communities, creating resentment.">usury</dfn> (money-lending at interest), devilry, and the ritual murder of Christian children. The most famous literary Jew before Shylock was Christopher Marlowe’s Barabas in <em>The Jew of Malta</em> (c. 1589) — a cartoonish villain who poisons wells and murders nuns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In 1594, two years before <em>The Merchant</em>, the Queen’s Jewish physician Roderigo Lopez was executed for allegedly plotting to poison Elizabeth I. The trial whipped up anti-Jewish sentiment. Shakespeare wrote his play in this atmosphere, but his treatment of Shylock is far more complex than Marlowe’s Barabas. Shylock is given the play’s most powerful speech (“Hath not a Jew eyes?”) and his grievances are shown to be real. For the Eduqas exam, exploring how Shakespeare both uses and subverts anti-Semitic stereotypes is essential for AO4.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three historical facts about Jews in England help explain the play’s treatment of Shylock?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jews were expelled from England in 1290 and not readmitted until 1656. Most Elizabethans had never met a Jewish person. Anti-Semitic stereotypes were fuelled by the Lopez trial (1594) and Marlowe’s <em>Jew of Malta</em>. Shakespeare both draws on and challenges these stereotypes: Shylock is not a cartoon villain but a complex figure whose suffering the audience is forced to acknowledge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Venice: Commerce, Law & Outsiders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Shakespeare sets the play in <dfn class="term" data-def="A major European trading city in the Renaissance, famous for its wealth, cosmopolitan population, and strict legal system. Venice was a byword for both commercial success and moral ambiguity.">Venice</dfn>, a city-state renowned for its wealth, trade and strict legal system. Venice was one of the few European cities where Jews could live relatively openly, though they were confined to a segregated area called the Ghetto (the word comes from Venice). Shylock exists within this system: tolerated for his economic usefulness (Christians need his money) but despised for his religion. Antonio spits on him, calls him “dog” and “misbeliever,” yet borrows his money when he needs it. This hypocrisy is central to the play.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Belmont, Portia’s estate, provides a <dfn class="term" data-def="A place of beauty, music and romantic love, contrasting with Venice’s commercial harshness. Belmont represents an idealised world, but its harmony excludes those who do not belong.">pastoral</dfn> contrast to Venice’s mercantile harshness. Belmont is a world of music, moonlight and love — but it is also a world that excludes. Shylock is never seen in Belmont. The play’s dual setting creates a structural opposition between commerce and romance, law and love, outsiders and insiders.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><em>The Merchant of Venice</em> was first classified as a comedy, and it does end with marriages and the restoration of social harmony. But the trial scene’s cruelty — Shylock stripped of his wealth, his faith and his dignity — sits uneasily with the moonlit celebration of Act 5. Modern scholars often classify it as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A play that combines elements of comedy and tragedy, resisting easy classification. The Merchant of Venice’s treatment of Shylock makes any purely comic reading impossible.">problem play</dfn>: a work that resists simple classification. An Elizabethan audience may have seen Shylock’s forced conversion as genuinely merciful (saving his soul). A modern audience almost certainly does not. Shakespeare’s own position is deliberately unclear, and this ambiguity is the play’s greatest analytical strength for the Eduqas exam.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Shakespeare’s Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The play draws on two main sources: the “pound of flesh” story (found in medieval collections including <em>Il Pecorone</em> by Giovanni Fiorentino, 1558) and the “three caskets” story (from the <em>Gesta Romanorum</em>, a medieval Latin collection). Shakespeare combines these separate stories into a single plot, linking the bond to the courtship. He also significantly deepens Shylock: in the sources, the Jewish money-lender is a flat villain. Shakespeare gives him a family (Jessica), a history of persecution, and the devastating “Hath not a Jew eyes?” speech. These additions transform the play from a simple morality tale into a complex exploration of prejudice, justice and mercy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name two ways Shakespeare changed Shylock from the source material and explain why these changes matter.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shakespeare deepened the flat villain of his sources by giving Shylock a daughter (Jessica), a history of persecution (Antonio’s abuse), and the “Hath not a Jew eyes?” speech. These additions force the audience to see Shylock as a human being, not just a stereotype — making the play’s treatment of him far more disturbing and morally complex.</p>
</div>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n13">For The Merchant of Venice, context (AO4) is crucial. Always acknowledge the gap between Elizabethan and modern responses to Shylock. An Elizabethan audience may have laughed at him; a modern audience is more likely to sympathise. The strongest responses explore this tension: “Shakespeare gives Shylock the play’s most powerful argument for common humanity, yet the plot still requires his defeat and humiliation.”</p>
