<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Verse and Prose: Who Speaks What</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare uses the distinction between <dfn class="term" data-def="Poetry with regular metre (iambic pentameter). In The Merchant of Venice, verse is used by high-status characters and in emotionally elevated moments.">verse</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Ordinary unmetrical speech. Shylock speaks prose in his early scenes, reflecting his outsider status in a verse-speaking Christian world.">prose</dfn> to mark social boundaries. The Christian characters — Portia, Bassanio, Antonio — speak predominantly in verse, the language of power, beauty and social authority. Shylock speaks prose in his early scenes, reflecting his outsider status. But in his great speeches (“Hath not a Jew eyes?” and the trial), Shylock shifts into powerful rhetoric that matches and exceeds the Christians’ eloquence. Portia’s mercy speech is in <dfn class="term" data-def="Unrhymed iambic pentameter, Shakespeare’s default verse form. It gives speeches formal authority without the artificial quality of rhyme.">blank verse</dfn>, giving it the formal authority of a legal and moral argument.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The prose/verse distinction also operates between Venice and Belmont. Venice’s commercial conversations often use prose; Belmont’s romantic exchanges use verse. When characters move between settings, their language shifts accordingly. Shakespeare uses form to reinforce the play’s structural opposition between commerce and romance, outsiders and insiders.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What does the prose/verse distinction signal about Shylock’s status, and how does he transcend it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shylock begins in prose (outsider status) but rises to powerful rhetoric in his key speeches, matching the Christians’ verse. This linguistic ascent mirrors his claim to common humanity: if he can speak as eloquently as they can, the prejudice that excludes him is exposed as baseless. For AO2, the prose/verse distinction is a key analytical tool.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Casket Riddles: Reading & Interpretation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The three <dfn class="term" data-def="Inscriptions on the gold, silver and lead caskets that test how suitors interpret language. The caskets are a metaphor for the play’s theme: those who read superficially are deceived.">casket inscriptions</dfn> create a structured test of interpretation. Gold: “Who chooseth me shall gain what many men desire” — Morocco chooses this, thinking “many men desire” Portia, but finds a skull. Silver: “Who chooseth me shall get as much as he deserves” — Arragon chooses this, assuming he deserves Portia, but finds a fool. Lead: “Who chooseth me must give and hazard all he hath” — Bassanio chooses this, accepting risk and humility, and finds Portia’s portrait.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Shakespeare structures the play around acts of reading and <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of understanding meaning from language, symbols or events. The play argues that interpretation is always shaped by the interpreter’s assumptions and desires.">interpretation</dfn>. The caskets test how characters read; the bond tests how courts interpret law; Portia’s disguise tests how people read gender. Those who read superficially (Morocco, Arragon, Claudio in the sister play <em>Much Ado</em>) fail; those who look deeper (Bassanio, Portia-as-Balthazar) succeed. But “all that glisters is not gold” applies to the play itself: even the “correct” readings may be less pure than they appear.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Shylock uses two distinct <dfn class="term" data-def="Methods of constructing an argument through language. Shylock employs both Biblical allusion and strict legal logic.">rhetorical strategies</dfn>. In Act 1, Scene 3, he cites the story of Jacob and Laban from Genesis to justify charging interest — using the Christians’ own scripture against them. In the trial, he shifts to strict legal logic: “I crave the law” and “I stand for judgement.” Shakespeare shows Shylock mastering the Christians’ own tools (scripture, law) and turning them to his advantage. Portia defeats him not by superior moral argument but by finding a legal technicality (flesh but no blood). The play’s resolution is determined by legal manoeuvring, not by the triumph of mercy over justice.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Comic and Tragic Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The play’s structure is a <dfn class="term" data-def="Combining comic and tragic elements in a single work. The Merchant’s comic structure (marriages, restored fortunes) contains tragic content (Shylock’s destruction).">hybrid</dfn>. The comic plot (caskets, wooing, ring trick) follows the conventional structure of Elizabethan comedy: confusion leads to resolution, and the play ends in marriage. The tragic subplot (bond, trial, forced conversion) follows the structure of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A play in which a flawed protagonist is brought low by circumstances and their own choices. Shylock’s trajectory resembles that of a tragic hero.">revenge tragedy</dfn>: a wronged figure seeks retribution and is destroyed in the process. Shakespeare interweaves these two structures so that the comedy’s happy ending depends on the tragedy’s resolution — Belmont’s joy is only possible because Shylock has been defeated and expelled.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how the play combines comic and tragic structure, and why this creates a ‘problem play.’">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The comic structure (caskets, marriage, ring trick) depends on the resolution of the tragic subplot (bond, trial, forced conversion). Belmont’s harmony is built on Shylock’s destruction. This structural dependence — comedy needing tragedy to achieve its happy ending — is what makes the play a “problem play” that resists simple genre classification.</p>
</div>

Portia disguises herself as a male lawyer; on Shakespeare’s stage, she was already a boy actor playing a woman.">disguise</dfn> as Balthazar is the play’s central act of performance. On Shakespeare’s stage, where all female roles were played by boy actors, the layers multiplied: a boy playing a woman playing a man playing a lawyer. This metatheatrical complexity connects to the play’s theme of appearance versus reality. If gender, religion and social status can all be performed, then the categories the play’s society depends on (Christian/Jew, male/female, insider/outsider) are shown to be unstable. Performance does not just deceive — it reveals the constructedness of the social order.</p>
