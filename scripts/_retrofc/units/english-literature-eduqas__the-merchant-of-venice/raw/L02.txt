Acts 1–2: Antonio’s bond, Shylock’s grievances, the three caskets and Bassanio’s quest.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Bond (Act 1, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Antonio, a wealthy Christian <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who trades goods, especially by sea. Antonio’s wealth depends on his ships, making him vulnerable to commercial risk.">merchant</dfn>, borrows 3,000 ducats from Shylock so his friend Bassanio can court the wealthy heiress Portia. Shylock proposes the bond: if Antonio cannot repay within three months, Shylock may take “a pound of flesh” from Antonio’s body. Antonio agrees confidently, saying his ships will return in time. But Shakespeare gives Shylock a crucial speech before the deal is struck: “Signor Antonio, many a time and oft / In the Rialto you have rated me / About my moneys and my usances.” Shylock catalogues the abuse he has suffered — Antonio has spat on him, called him “dog,” and kicked him “as you spurn a stranger cur.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This speech is the play’s first moral challenge. Shylock’s grievances are legitimate: Antonio has been consistently racist and physically abusive. Yet Antonio shows no remorse: “I am as like to call thee so again, / To spit on thee again, to spurn thee too.” Shakespeare forces the audience to see the bond not as unprovoked villainy but as the response of a man who has been systematically humiliated. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive, unreasonable interest charged on a loan. Antonio opposes usury on Christian principle, but his solution is to lend money at no interest, which undermines Shylock’s livelihood.">usury</dfn> debate is not abstract: Antonio’s lending without interest has directly damaged Shylock’s business.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What specific abuses has Antonio committed against Shylock, and why do they matter?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Antonio has spat on Shylock, called him “dog,” kicked him, and undermined his business by lending without interest. Shylock’s grievances are real and specific. Shakespeare presents the bond not as unprovoked villainy but as a response to systematic persecution — complicating any reading of Shylock as a simple villain.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Three Caskets (Act 2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In Belmont, Portia’s dead father has decreed that she must marry whoever chooses the correct <dfn class="term" data-def="Three boxes (gold, silver, lead) containing messages. Suitors must choose correctly to win Portia. Each casket tests the chooser’s values: gold = greed, silver = vanity, lead = humility.">casket</dfn>. The gold casket bears the inscription “Who chooseth me shall gain what many men desire”; the silver reads “Who chooseth me shall get as much as he deserves”; the lead reads “Who chooseth me must give and hazard all he hath.” Morocco chooses gold and finds a death’s head: “All that glisters is not gold.” Arragon chooses silver and finds a fool’s head. Both fail because they read superficially.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The casket test embodies the play’s central theme of <dfn class="term" data-def="The recurring idea that outward appearance does not reflect inner truth. The caskets teach that those who judge by surfaces are deceived.">appearance versus reality</dfn>. The most valuable prize is in the least attractive container. Those who choose by outward show fail; only the one who looks beyond surfaces succeeds. When Bassanio later chooses correctly (lead), he articulates the principle: “The world is still deceived with ornament.” But there is irony here too: Bassanio is a fortune-hunter whose initial motivation for courting Portia is her wealth (“In Belmont is a lady richly left”). Shakespeare does not let even the “correct” chooser escape scrutiny.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Antonio’s opening line — “In sooth, I know not why I am so sad” — establishes an unexplained melancholy that runs throughout the play. He risks his life for Bassanio without hesitation. His devotion to Bassanio is intense and possibly <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to romantic or sexual desire between people of the same sex. Antonio’s attachment to Bassanio has been read by many critics as homoerotic.">homoerotic</dfn>: in the trial scene, he tells Bassanio “Say how I loved you, speak me fair in death.” At the end of the play, Bassanio is married to Portia, but Antonio remains alone, without a partner. Shakespeare leaves the nature of Antonio’s love ambiguous, but his isolation at the play’s close is telling: the comedy’s happy ending excludes him.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Shylock’s “Merry Bond”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Shylock describes the pound of flesh as a “merry bond” — a joke, a <dfn class="term" data-def="Something done in a playful or lighthearted way. Shylock calls the pound of flesh a ‘merry sport,’ but the audience is uncertain whether he is joking or deadly serious.">merry sport</dfn>. Whether he genuinely intends to collect is left ambiguous until Act 3: it is only after Jessica’s betrayal and Antonio’s continued abuse that Shylock’s desire for revenge hardens into determination. Shakespeare presents the bond as a test that depends on circumstances: had Antonio repaid on time, had Jessica not eloped, had the Christians shown Shylock any decency, the “merry bond” might have remained a joke. The tragedy is that every character’s behaviour drives Shylock towards the worst version of himself.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how the casket test relates to the play’s theme of appearance versus reality.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“All that glisters is not gold” — the casket test teaches that outward appearance does not reflect inner worth. This principle applies across the play: Shylock’s villainy is more complex than it appears; the Christians’ virtue is less pure than they claim; Portia’s mercy speech conceals legal ruthlessness.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Portia’s Constraint: The Dead Father’s Will</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Portia is wealthy, intelligent and witty, but her dead father’s will controls her marriage. She cannot choose her own husband — she must accept whoever passes the casket test. She complains: “I may neither choose who I would nor refuse who I dislike.” Like Hero in <em>Much Ado</em> and Katherine in <em>Henry V</em>, Portia is a woman whose fate is determined by male authority. Shakespeare gives her extraordinary agency later in the play (she saves Antonio through legal brilliance), but her initial powerlessness connects to the play’s broader exploration of who has authority and who does not.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n15">Shylock’s grievances against Antonio are real and specific — spitting, name-calling, business sabotage. Shakespeare presents the bond as a response to persecution, not unprovoked villainy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n16">The casket test teaches “all that glisters is not gold” — those who judge by surfaces fail. But even Bassanio, who passes, has mixed motives.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n17">Antonio’s intense devotion to Bassanio and his isolation at the end raise questions about the nature of his love and the comedy’s ability to include everyone.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n13">When writing about Shylock’s grievances in Act 1, Scene 3, always connect them to the trial scene in your Part (ii) response. His desire for the pound of flesh is the direct result of years of abuse that Shakespeare dramatises in this opening scene. The best responses trace a clear line from Antonio’s ‘I am as like to call thee so again’ to Shylock’s ‘I would have my bond.’</p>
