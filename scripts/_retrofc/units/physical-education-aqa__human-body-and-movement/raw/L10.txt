<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Health and Fitness: Not the Same Thing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Most people use the words &ldquo;healthy&rdquo; and &ldquo;fit&rdquo; interchangeably, but in sports science they have precise, separate definitions. <dfn class="term" data-def="A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.">Health</dfn> is defined as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being — not simply the absence of illness. This definition, taken from the World Health Organization, emphasises that a person can feel ill or stressed and still have no diagnosable disease, meaning their health is compromised even if a doctor would not prescribe anything.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to meet the demands of the environment.">Fitness</dfn>, in contrast, is defined as the ability to meet the demands of the environment. This means that fitness is specific to purpose: a sprinter needs explosive power and speed; a marathon runner needs cardiovascular endurance and muscular endurance; a goalkeeper needs agility, reaction time and explosive strength. A person who is highly fit for one sport may be relatively unfit for another.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Crucially, health and fitness do not always go together. Someone with a serious injury (poor health) can still maintain cardiovascular fitness through swimming. Equally, an elite athlete may be very fit but show signs of overtraining — sleep disruption, hormonal imbalance, low mood — which are all health issues. Understanding this relationship is essential when evaluating the role exercise plays in an individual&rsquo;s life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: write the full definition of health and the full definition of fitness from memory. Check both definitions against the spec wording before moving on.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Health = a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being (not merely the absence of disease). Fitness = the ability to meet the demands of the environment. These are two distinct definitions — learn both precisely.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Ten Components of Fitness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The specification identifies ten components of fitness. No single sport requires all ten in equal measure, so a key skill is identifying which components are most relevant to a given activity and justifying why. The ten components are:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to change body position quickly and accurately while moving.">Agility</dfn> — the ability to change body position quickly and accurately while moving. Essential in team sports such as rugby, basketball and hockey, where players must dodge opponents and change direction at speed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to retain the body's centre of mass over its base of support.">Balance</dfn> — the ability to retain the body&rsquo;s centre of mass over its base of support. Can be static (holding a handstand) or dynamic (a gymnast on a balance beam). Important in gymnastics, surfing, skiing and judo.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen to working muscles during sustained exercise.">Cardiovascular endurance</dfn> (also called aerobic power) — the ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen to working muscles during sustained exercise. This is the foundation of performance in long-distance running, rowing, cycling, football and many other activities.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to use two or more body parts together smoothly and efficiently.">Coordination</dfn> — the ability to use two or more body parts together smoothly and efficiently. Vital in cricket (eye-hand coordination for batting), tennis (eye-hand for racket sports) and swimming (synchronising arms and legs).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The range of movement possible at a joint.">Flexibility</dfn> — the range of movement possible at a joint. Crucial in gymnastics, dance, swimming (stroke efficiency) and martial arts. Poor flexibility can limit performance and increase injury risk.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a muscle to undergo repeated contractions over a long period without tiring.">Muscular endurance</dfn> — the ability of a muscle to undergo repeated contractions over a long period without tiring. Important in rowing, cycling and long-distance swimming, where the same muscle groups perform thousands of repeated actions.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to exert maximum force in as short a time as possible; a combination of strength and speed.">Power</dfn> (also called explosive strength or anaerobic power) — the ability to exert maximum force in as short a time as possible; it combines strength and speed. Power is critical in sprinting, shot-put, jumping events and boxing.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="The time taken to initiate a response to a stimulus.">Reaction time</dfn> — the time taken to initiate a response to a stimulus. A sprinter reacting to the starting gun, a goalkeeper responding to a shot, and a batsman reading a delivery all depend on a fast reaction time.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17">Strength — the maximum force a muscle or muscle group can exert in a single effort. The specification identifies four types: maximal strength (the greatest force in a single effort), static strength (force exerted against an immovable object), dynamic strength (force produced through a full range of movement repeatedly) and explosive strength (maximum force in the shortest time, which overlaps with power).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Speed — the maximum rate at which an individual is able to perform a movement or cover a distance in a period of time. Speed is relevant not just in sprinting but in any sport where rapid movement is an advantage — a winger in football breaking past a defender, for example.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and list all ten components of fitness from memory. Use the mnemonic device of grouping them: 3 body-composition/endurance (cardiovascular endurance, muscular endurance, flexibility), 4 skill-related (agility, coordination, balance, reaction time), 3 power-related (strength, speed, power).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The ten components: agility, balance, cardiovascular endurance, coordination, flexibility, muscular endurance, power, reaction time, strength, speed. Questions often ask you to select components and justify their importance for a specific sport — always name the component AND give a sporting reason.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n20">A 100m sprinter needs: (1) <strong>reaction time</strong> — to leave the blocks the instant the starter&rsquo;s pistol fires, reducing overall race time; (2) <strong>power</strong> — to accelerate explosively from the blocks during the drive phase; (3) <strong>speed</strong> — to maintain maximum velocity during the top-speed phase; and (4) <strong>muscular endurance</strong> — to sustain near-maximal effort for the full 10&ndash;12 seconds before significant lactic acid accumulation. Cardiovascular endurance is less important than in a marathon because the race duration is too short for the aerobic system to be the limiting factor. In the exam, always justify <em>why</em> the component matters for that specific sport — not just list its name.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Health is complete physical, mental and social well-being; fitness is the ability to meet the demands of the environment — these are separate concepts and a person can be fit but unhealthy, or healthy but unfit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">There are ten components of fitness: agility, balance, cardiovascular endurance, coordination, flexibility, muscular endurance, power, reaction time, strength and speed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Different sports require different combinations of fitness components; exam questions always require you to name a component and justify its relevance with a specific sporting example.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">On questions asking you to &ldquo;define&rdquo; health or fitness, quote the specification definitions precisely — &ldquo;complete physical, mental and social well-being&rdquo; for health, and &ldquo;ability to meet the demands of the environment&rdquo; for fitness. Both words are needed for full marks on a definition question. On &ldquo;explain&rdquo; or &ldquo;analyse&rdquo; questions about components, you must name the component AND justify its relevance with a sporting example. Simply listing components without explanation is a very common error that loses marks. If asked to justify which components are most important for a sport, avoid saying &ldquo;all components are important&rdquo; — the question is asking you to discriminate and prioritise with reasons.</p>
