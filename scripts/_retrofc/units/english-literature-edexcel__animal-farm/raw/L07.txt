<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Allegory: The Layer Beneath the Story</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every element of Animal Farm operates on two levels: the surface narrative (animals on a farm) and the <dfn class="term" data-def="A story with a hidden political or moral meaning, where characters and events represent real-world people and situations.">allegorical</dfn> meaning (the Russian Revolution and Soviet Union). For the Edexcel exam, you need to move fluently between these two levels. When you discuss a character’s actions, connect them to their historical counterpart. When you discuss an event, explain what real-world event it represents and what Orwell is saying about it.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down all seven character-to-figure mappings from memory (Old Major, Napoleon, Snowball, Squealer, Boxer, Benjamin, Mollie, Moses).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The key character mappings are: Old Major = Marx/Lenin, Napoleon = Stalin, Snowball = Trotsky, Squealer = Pravda (Soviet propaganda), Boxer = the loyal proletariat, Benjamin = cynical intellectuals, Mollie = the bourgeoisie, Moses = the Russian Orthodox Church, the dogs = secret police (NKVD), the sheep = citizens who repeat propaganda, Mr Jones = Tsar Nicholas II, Frederick = Hitler, Pilkington = the Western capitalist powers (Britain/USA).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Windmill: Promise and Exploitation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The structure that Snowball designs and the animals build, representing Stalin's Five-Year Plans for Soviet industrialisation.">windmill</dfn> is one of the novella’s central symbols. Snowball designs it as a project that will bring electricity, reduce working hours and improve the animals’ quality of life. After Snowball’s expulsion, Napoleon claims the idea as his own. The windmill represents Stalin’s Five-Year Plans — ambitious <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of developing industries in a country on a wide scale, often at great cost to the working population.">industrialisation</dfn> programmes that promised to modernise the Soviet Union but were built on the brutal exploitation of workers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The windmill is built, destroyed in a storm, rebuilt, destroyed by Frederick’s dynamite, and rebuilt again. Each cycle costs the animals more suffering while delivering less benefit. Orwell uses this repetition to show that the promise of a better future is the tool used to justify present suffering — and that the better future never actually arrives for ordinary citizens.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">After the Rebellion, the animals agree that the farmhouse should be “preserved as a museum” — a symbol of the old regime that no animal should inhabit. But gradually, the pigs move in. They sleep in beds, eat from the best china, and eventually host dinner parties with humans. The farmhouse symbolises the <dfn class="term" data-def="The seat of Russian government in Moscow, representing political power and the privileges of the ruling elite.">Kremlin</dfn> — once a symbol of revolutionary change, it becomes the private residence of a new elite. The distance between the farmhouse and the barn represents the growing gap between the rulers and the ruled.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Seven Commandments: A Constitution Betrayed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Seven Commandments function as Animal Farm’s constitution — the founding principles of the revolution. Their gradual alteration is Orwell’s most effective <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring element (image, phrase, idea or action) that develops meaning throughout a text.">motif</dfn>. Each change adds a qualifying clause that legalises the pigs’ behaviour: “with sheets”, “to excess”, “without cause.” The final reduction to “All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others” replaces seven principles with one self-contradicting statement. This mirrors the Soviet constitution of 1936, which guaranteed freedoms (speech, press, assembly) that were systematically violated during the purges happening at the same time.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how controlling language controls reality in Animal Farm — give one specific example of a commandment change.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Orwell shows that controlling language means controlling reality. If the commandments say the pigs <em>can</em> sleep in beds, then sleeping in beds is no longer a betrayal of the revolution. This connects to Orwell’s lifelong concern about political language, which he explored further in his essay ‘Politics and the English Language’ (1946) and in the concept of <em>Newspeak</em> in <em>Nineteen Eighty-Four</em> (1949).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sugarcandy Mountain: The Opium of the People</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Moses the raven tells the animals about Sugarcandy Mountain — a paradise where animals go after death, where “it was Sunday seven days a week, clover was in season all the year round, and lump sugar and linseed cake grew on the hedges.” This represents religion, specifically the Russian Orthodox Church. Karl Marx famously called religion “the <dfn class="term" data-def="Karl Marx's description of religion — he argued it numbed the working class to their suffering by promising rewards in the afterlife.">opium of the people</dfn>” — a drug that numbs the working class to their suffering by promising rewards in the afterlife.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">The pigs initially chase Moses away because he does no work and spreads distracting stories. But later, Napoleon allows Moses to return and even gives him a ration of beer. This parallels Stalin’s shifting relationship with the Russian Orthodox Church. Initially, the Bolsheviks persecuted the Church, but during World War II, Stalin rehabilitated it to boost morale and national unity. Orwell shows that totalitarian regimes will co-opt any institution — including religion — if it helps maintain control.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">‘Beasts of England’: A Symbol of Lost Hope</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The song ‘Beasts of England’ functions as a powerful symbol that tracks the trajectory of the revolution. Taught by Old Major, it represents the utopian dream of freedom and equality — an anthem that unites all the animals in shared hope. Its banning by Napoleon once he consolidates power is deeply significant: the dictator needs to erase the original vision of the revolution because it would expose how far the new reality has fallen from the original promise. The replacement song, which praises Napoleon personally, mirrors Stalin’s cult of personality, where art, music and literature were co-opted to glorify the leader rather than the collective ideals of socialism.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Neighbouring Farms: The Cold War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Foxwood, owned by the easy-going Mr Pilkington, represents Britain and the Western democracies. Pinchfield, owned by the cunning, hard-driving Frederick, represents Nazi Germany. The shifting alliances between the farms mirror the complex diplomacy of the 1930s and 1940s — including the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 1939 (when Frederick and Napoleon briefly trade) and the subsequent German invasion (the Battle of the Windmill). The final scene, where Napoleon plays cards with Pilkington, represents the Tehran and Yalta conferences where Stalin, Churchill and Roosevelt divided up post-war Europe.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Every character, event and setting in Animal Farm has both a narrative function and an allegorical meaning — always work on both levels in your exam.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Key symbols: the windmill (industrialisation and false promises), the commandments (a betrayed constitution), the farmhouse (the privileges of the ruling elite), Sugarcandy Mountain (religion as a tool of control).</li><li data-narration-id="n22">Orwell’s allegory is not a simple one-to-one mapping — it is a sophisticated argument about how power corrupts, propaganda deceives, and revolution can be betrayed.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When writing about allegory and symbolism, avoid simply listing what each element represents. Instead, explain <em>why</em> Orwell chose these specific symbols and what they add to his argument. For example, the windmill is not just a symbol of industrialisation — its repeated destruction and rebuilding shows how authoritarian regimes use the promise of future prosperity to justify present suffering. This kind of analytical depth, linked to context, is what distinguishes Level 5 responses on the Edexcel mark scheme.</p>
