--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Power Struggle: Snowball vs Napoleon</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After the Rebellion, two pigs emerge as leaders: <dfn class="term" data-def="The intelligent, idealistic pig who represents Leon Trotsky and is expelled by Napoleon.">Snowball</dfn> and Napoleon. They disagree on virtually everything. Snowball is energetic, eloquent and full of ideas — he organises committees, tries to teach the animals to read, and draws up plans for a <dfn class="term" data-def="The structure Snowball designs to modernise the farm, representing Soviet industrialisation programmes.">windmill</dfn> that will bring electricity to the farm and reduce the animals’ working hours. Napoleon, by contrast, says little in public debates but works behind the scenes, quietly building his own power base.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the character Snowball represents and the character Napoleon represents, then list two contrasting qualities of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Snowball represents Leon Trotsky: an intellectual, a brilliant speaker, and a genuine believer in spreading revolution. Napoleon represents Joseph Stalin: quiet, calculating, and focused on consolidating personal power rather than advancing revolutionary ideals.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The windmill debate is the key conflict. Snowball argues passionately that the windmill will transform the animals’ lives. Napoleon opposes it — not because he has a better plan, but because he cannot allow Snowball to gain popularity. This mirrors the real disagreement between Stalin and Trotsky over industrialisation, but Orwell shows that for Napoleon, the debate is really about power, not policy.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Expulsion of Snowball</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Napoleon’s masterstroke comes at the crucial meeting about the windmill. Just as Snowball is delivering his most persuasive speech, Napoleon gives a “high-pitched whimper” and nine enormous dogs — the puppies he secretly took from their mothers and raised in isolation — burst into the barn and chase Snowball from the farm. This is one of the novella’s most shocking moments. Violence, not argument, decides the power struggle.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">The nine dogs represent Stalin’s secret police (the NKVD, later the KGB). Napoleon takes them as puppies and raises them “in a loft” away from the other animals, training them to be loyal only to him. This detail is crucial: Napoleon plans his violent seizure of power long before the windmill debate. The dogs enforce Napoleon’s will through fear, just as Stalin used the secret police to silence, imprison and execute opponents. After Snowball’s expulsion, the dogs sit around Napoleon “wagging their tails at him in the same way as the other dogs had been used to do to Mr Jones” — a devastating detail showing that Napoleon has simply replaced one form of tyranny with another.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Squealer: The Voice of Propaganda</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="The pig who acts as Napoleon's propagandist, manipulating the truth to justify the pigs' actions, representing Soviet state propaganda.">Squealer</dfn> is Napoleon’s most important tool. Described as a pig who “could turn black into white,” Squealer represents the Soviet propaganda machine, particularly the state newspaper <em>Pravda</em> (which, ironically, means ‘truth’). After every new injustice, Squealer appears with “explanations” that twist reality. When the pigs take the milk and apples, Squealer claims it is for the animals’ benefit: “Do you know what would happen if we pigs failed in our duty? Jones would come back!”</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name four propaganda techniques Squealer uses, with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Squealer uses several propaganda techniques: false statistics (“production has increased by 200 per cent”), appeals to fear (the threat of Jones’s return), rewriting history (claiming Snowball was always a traitor), and exploiting the animals’ inability to read or remember. Orwell shows that propaganda works not because it is believable, but because those in power control all sources of information.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>“Napoleon Is Always Right”</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">After Snowball’s expulsion, Boxer adopts the personal motto “Napoleon is always right” alongside his existing “I will work harder.” This is one of the novella’s most poignant moments. Boxer is loyal, hardworking and trusting — exactly the qualities that make him vulnerable to exploitation. His blind faith in Napoleon represents how ordinary Soviet citizens accepted Stalin’s authority, partly out of genuine belief and partly because questioning the leader was dangerous. Orwell makes Boxer sympathetic precisely so the reader feels the injustice of his exploitation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Rewriting History</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">One of Napoleon’s most insidious tactics is the systematic rewriting of the past. After Snowball’s expulsion, Squealer gradually transforms Snowball from a hero of the Rebellion into a traitor who was “Jones’s secret agent all the time.” The animals’ memories of Snowball’s bravery at the Battle of the Cowshed are replaced with a new version where Napoleon was the real hero and Snowball had actually been fighting on Jones’s side. This mirrors how Stalin had Trotsky’s image literally erased from photographs and official records. In the Soviet Union, history was not a fixed record but a tool of power — whoever controlled the narrative controlled the truth. Orwell shows that when citizens cannot verify facts for themselves, they become prisoners of whatever story those in power choose to tell.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Windmill: Promise and Exploitation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In a typically cynical move, Napoleon reverses his position on the windmill after expelling Snowball. He announces that the windmill will be built after all — and claims it was his idea all along. Squealer explains that Napoleon only <em>seemed</em> to oppose it as a “manoeuvre” to get rid of Snowball. The windmill represents Soviet <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of developing industries in a country on a wide scale, often at great cost to the working population.">industrialisation</dfn> under Stalin’s Five-Year Plans — ambitious projects that promised a better future but were built on the brutal exploitation of workers. The animals labour exhaustingly to build the windmill, but the pigs do none of the physical work.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The Snowball-Napoleon conflict mirrors Stalin vs Trotsky — Napoleon wins through violence and scheming, not through better ideas.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The dogs represent Stalin’s secret police; their use shows that Napoleon planned his violent takeover well in advance.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Squealer’s propaganda techniques — false statistics, appeals to fear, rewriting history — show how totalitarian regimes control truth itself.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">When writing about Napoleon’s rise to power, focus on the <em>methods</em> he uses rather than just describing events. Napoleon succeeds through violence (the dogs), propaganda (Squealer), rewriting history (claiming the windmill was his idea), and eliminating opposition (expelling Snowball). For each method, make a clear link to how Stalin consolidated power in the Soviet Union. This demonstrates the contextual understanding (AO3) that Edexcel rewards heavily in Section B.</p>
