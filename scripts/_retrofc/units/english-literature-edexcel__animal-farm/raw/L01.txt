Orwell's political purpose, the Russian Revolution, and why Animal Farm is an allegorical satire.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Did Orwell Write Animal Farm?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">George Orwell (born Eric Arthur Blair, 1903–1950) was a <dfn class="term" data-def="A political belief that society should be run democratically with equal rights and shared wealth, without authoritarian control.">democratic socialist</dfn> who believed passionately in equality and freedom. He was not opposed to socialism itself — he was opposed to what happened when revolutionary ideals were hijacked by power-hungry leaders. His experience fighting in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) was the turning point: he witnessed Soviet-backed communists betraying and attacking their own allies, showing him that revolutionary language could be used to disguise <dfn class="term" data-def="A political system where the state holds total authority over society and suppresses all opposition.">totalitarianism</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when was Animal Farm written and published, and what was the ironic subtitle Orwell gave it?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Orwell wrote Animal Farm in 1943–1944, published in 1945. He subtitled it ‘A Fairy Story’ — an ironic joke, since the novella is a dark political warning about how revolutions can be betrayed by those who seize power.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Orwell struggled to get Animal Farm published because Britain was allied with the Soviet Union during World War II. Several publishers rejected it, fearing it would damage the wartime alliance. This censorship only strengthened Orwell’s belief that uncomfortable truths must be told.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Russian Revolution: Essential Background</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Before 1917, Russia was ruled by Tsar Nicholas II, an autocratic emperor who held absolute power while most of the population lived in desperate poverty. Anger over inequality, food shortages, and Russia’s catastrophic losses in World War I led to two revolutions in 1917. In February, mass protests forced the Tsar to abdicate. In October, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A member of the radical socialist party led by Lenin that seized power in Russia in October 1917.">Bolsheviks</dfn>, led by Vladimir Lenin, seized power in a coup. They were inspired by the political philosophy of Karl Marx, who argued that the working class should overthrow the ruling class and share wealth equally.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Lenin led the October Revolution and established the Soviet Union, but he died in 1924 before the new state was fully established. A power struggle followed between Joseph Stalin and Leon Trotsky. Stalin was a cunning political operator who built alliances behind the scenes, while Trotsky was an intellectual and brilliant orator who believed in spreading revolution worldwide. Stalin won, expelled Trotsky from the country in 1929, and eventually had him assassinated in Mexico in 1940. Stalin then ruled the Soviet Union as a dictator through fear, <dfn class="term" data-def="Information, often biased or misleading, used to promote a particular political viewpoint.">propaganda</dfn>, purges and a cult of personality.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Stalin’s Soviet Union</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Under Stalin, the Soviet Union became a one-party state controlled through terror. The secret police (first the OGPU, then the NKVD) arrested, imprisoned and executed millions. Stalin’s Great Purges of the 1930s targeted anyone suspected of disloyalty — including many of the original Bolsheviks who had fought in the Revolution. Show trials forced victims to confess publicly to fabricated crimes. Meanwhile, state propaganda through the newspaper <em>Pravda</em> (meaning ‘truth’) presented Stalin as a benevolent father figure and rewrote history to erase his rivals, particularly Trotsky. It is this specific pattern — revolution, power struggle, dictatorship, propaganda, purges — that Orwell recreates in Animal Farm.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Allegory, Fable and Satire</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Animal Farm works on three literary levels simultaneously. It is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A story with a hidden political or moral meaning, where characters and events represent real-world people and situations.">allegory</dfn> — every character, event and setting maps onto the history of the Russian Revolution and Soviet Union. It is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A short story, often with animal characters, that teaches a moral lesson.">fable</dfn> — a simple story with animal characters that conveys a moral message. And it is a <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of humour, irony or exaggeration to criticise and ridicule people, institutions or political systems.">satire</dfn> — using dark humour and irony to attack totalitarianism and expose how power corrupts.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does AO3 require you to do with context in an Edexcel essay — separate paragraph or woven through?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For the Edexcel exam, you need to show understanding of context (AO3). This means connecting events and characters in Animal Farm to the Russian Revolution, Orwell’s political beliefs, and the historical period — not in a separate paragraph, but woven throughout your essay.</p>
</div>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A common misconception is that Orwell was attacking communism or socialism. In fact, he described himself as a democratic socialist and wrote in his essay ‘Why I Write’ (1946) that “every line of serious work that I have written since 1936 has been written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic Socialism.” Animal Farm criticises the <em>betrayal</em> of socialist ideals by authoritarian leaders — it is a warning about how power corrupts, not an argument against equality.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Publication Controversy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Orwell completed Animal Farm in February 1944, but it took over a year to find a publisher. T.S. Eliot, then a director at Faber & Faber, rejected it on the grounds that it was “not the right point of view from which to criticise the political situation.” Jonathan Cape initially accepted the manuscript but withdrew after a warning from the Ministry of Information. This censorship demonstrated exactly the kind of political suppression Orwell was warning about — even in a democracy, uncomfortable truths about a powerful ally could be silenced. When the book was finally published by Secker & Warburg in August 1945, it became an immediate bestseller and has remained one of the most widely read political texts in the English language.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Why Context Matters for Edexcel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In the Edexcel Component 1 Section B exam, you answer ONE essay question from a choice of two. Each question is preceded by a short quotation from the text as a stimulus. The assessment objectives are AO1 (informed personal response with textual references), AO3 (understanding context) and AO4 (spelling, punctuation and grammar). There are no AO2 marks for this section, which means you do not need to analyse language techniques in detail — instead, focus on developing a clear argument about characters, themes or events and connecting them to context.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Orwell was a democratic socialist who wrote Animal Farm to warn against the corruption of revolutionary ideals under Stalin — not to attack socialism itself.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Animal Farm is simultaneously a fable, an allegory of the Russian Revolution, and a satire of totalitarianism.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">For Edexcel (AO1 + AO3 + AO4), weave contextual knowledge about the Russian Revolution throughout your essay rather than bolting it on as a separate paragraph.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">Edexcel Section B is worth 40 marks (AO1 + AO3 + AO4). There is no AO2 in this section, so you do not need to analyse individual words or language techniques. Instead, focus on building a clear argument that references specific moments from the text and connects them to the Russian Revolution and Orwell’s political purpose. Always address the quotation given in the question — it is your starting point, not something to ignore. Use specialist vocabulary like ‘allegory’, ‘totalitarianism’ and ‘propaganda’ to boost your AO4 marks.</p>
