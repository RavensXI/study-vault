--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Old Major’s Speech: The Spark of Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novella opens with <dfn class="term" data-def="The elderly prize-winning boar whose speech inspires the Rebellion, representing Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin.">Old Major</dfn>, a prize-winning boar, calling the animals together for a secret meeting in the barn. His speech is the catalyst for everything that follows. He tells the animals that “our lives are miserable, laborious, and short” and that Man is the source of all their suffering. His argument is simple and powerful: remove the human oppressor, and the animals will be free and equal.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and answer: who does Old Major represent (name both figures), and what famous document does his speech mirror?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Old Major represents both Karl Marx (who developed the theory of communism) and Vladimir Lenin (who led the October Revolution). His speech mirrors <em>The Communist Manifesto</em>’s call for workers to overthrow their oppressors. He dies before the revolution happens, just as Marx died before the Russian Revolution.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Old Major teaches the animals the song ‘Beasts of England’, which becomes a revolutionary anthem. The song paints a <dfn class="term" data-def="An imagined place or state of things in which everything is perfect.">utopian</dfn> vision of a future without humans: “rings shall vanish from our noses, and the harness from our back.” This vision of freedom and equality is genuinely moving — Orwell wants the reader to understand why revolution seems so appealing before showing how it can go wrong.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Rebellion and the Seven Commandments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">After Old Major dies, the animals — led by the pigs Napoleon and Snowball — drive Mr Jones from the farm. The speed and ease of the Rebellion mirrors the October Revolution, where the Bolsheviks seized power with relatively little resistance. The animals rename Manor Farm as ‘Animal Farm’ and establish <dfn class="term" data-def="The system of beliefs developed by the pigs, based on Old Major's teachings, that all animals are equal and humans are the enemy.">Animalism</dfn> as their governing philosophy — a direct parallel to Marxism-Leninism.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The pigs condense Old Major’s teachings into the Seven Commandments, painted on the barn wall. These commandments represent the founding principles of the revolution, just as the Soviet constitution promised equality and workers’ rights. The most important is the seventh: “All animals are equal.”</p>

These commandments function as both the constitution of Animal Farm and a structural device — Orwell uses their gradual alteration throughout the novella to track the corruption of the revolution.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Early Optimism: The Harvest and the Cowshed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The early chapters of Animal Farm are deliberately hopeful. The animals work harder than they ever did for Jones, but they do so willingly because they believe they are working for themselves. The harvest is “the biggest harvest that the farm had ever seen” — a detail that mirrors the genuine early achievements of Soviet collective farming before Stalin’s disastrous policies took hold.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is the Battle of the Cowshed, who leads the defence, and what real event does it parallel?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="The battle where the animals defend the farm against Jones and his men, paralleling the Russian Civil War.">Battle of the Cowshed</dfn> occurs when Jones and neighbouring farmers try to retake the farm. Snowball leads the defence brilliantly, planning military tactics inspired by Julius Caesar. This parallels the Russian Civil War (1918–1921), where Trotsky organised the Red Army to defeat the counter-revolutionary White forces.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Seeds of Corruption: The Milk and Apples</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Even in these optimistic early chapters, Orwell plants seeds of future corruption. On the very first day after the Rebellion, the cows’ milk mysteriously disappears. The animals later discover that the pigs have been taking the milk and apples for themselves. Squealer justifies this by claiming the pigs need them because they do “brainwork” — and warns that if the pigs fail, “Jones would come back!” This is the first use of propaganda to justify privilege, and it establishes the pattern that will define the rest of the novella: the pigs claim special treatment is for the common good, when it is really for their own benefit.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Pigs Take Charge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">From the very beginning, the pigs position themselves as the natural leaders. They do not do any physical labour during the harvest — instead, they “directed and supervised the others.” The word ‘naturally’ recurs when describing the pigs’ assumption of authority, as though their leadership is inevitable rather than chosen. This mirrors how the Bolshevik intellectuals positioned themselves as the ‘vanguard of the proletariat’ — the educated elite who would lead the revolution on behalf of the workers. Orwell subtly questions this assumption: if the revolution is about equality, why should one group automatically lead? The pigs’ early claim to intellectual superiority is the first crack in Animalism’s promise of equality, and it widens throughout the novella until the hierarchy is as rigid as it was under Jones.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Orwell’s Structural Choices</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Orwell structures the opening chapters to create a deliberate arc from hope to warning. The reader shares the animals’ excitement about the Rebellion, making the later betrayal more devastating. The simplicity of the fable form means that every detail carries allegorical weight. The milk and apples are not just food — they represent how revolutionary leaders immediately begin appropriating resources for themselves. For the Edexcel exam, connecting these narrative choices to context will demonstrate the sophisticated understanding that earns higher marks.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Old Major’s speech represents Marx’s and Lenin’s revolutionary ideas; ‘Beasts of England’ is the utopian vision that inspires rebellion.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The Seven Commandments are a structural device — their gradual alteration tracks the corruption of the revolution throughout the novella.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Even in the hopeful early chapters, the disappearance of the milk and apples foreshadows how the pigs will betray the revolution for personal gain.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When writing about the early chapters, avoid simply retelling the plot. Instead, focus on Orwell’s purpose: why does he make the Rebellion seem so positive? The answer is that he wants the reader to share the animals’ hope so that the subsequent corruption feels more shocking and devastating. This structural choice mirrors how the early Soviet period generated genuine optimism before Stalin’s betrayal. Always connect narrative events to Orwell’s message and the historical context.</p>
