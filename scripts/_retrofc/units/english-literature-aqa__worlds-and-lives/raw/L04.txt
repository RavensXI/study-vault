<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Memory & Time — Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">These three poems explore how memory shapes identity and how the passage of time transforms our relationship with the past. Patience Agbabi, Caleb Femi, and Raymond Antrobus each use different techniques to capture how moments, people, and places persist in memory — altered but still powerful.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Patience Agbabi — “The Jewellery Maker”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Agbabi, a British-Nigerian poet known for her virtuosic formal skills, uses the figure of a jewellery maker as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A longer metaphor that extends across several lines or an entire poem, developing a comparison in detail.">extended metaphor</dfn> for the craft of memory and storytelling. The jewellery maker selects, shapes, and connects fragments — just as we select and arrange memories to create a coherent narrative of our lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The poem explores the process of crafting something beautiful and lasting from raw materials — a metaphor for how we transform lived experience into art, story, or identity. Each piece of jewellery, like each memory, is both personal and potentially universal: made from individual experience but recognisable to others.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What is the extended metaphor in Agbabi's poem, and what does it represent?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The jewellery maker represents the artist or storyteller who crafts meaning from fragments of experience. Agbabi draws a parallel between the careful, skilled process of making jewellery (selecting, shaping, connecting) and the process of constructing identity from memory.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Key techniques:</strong> Agbabi uses extended metaphor, precise <dfn class="term" data-def="A word or phrase that refers to a specific craft, profession, or field of knowledge, used to create authenticity and precision.">technical vocabulary</dfn> from jewellery-making (soldering, setting, polishing), and a controlled structure that mirrors the careful craftsmanship described. The poem’s form enacts its content — it is itself a carefully made object.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Context:</strong> Agbabi is known for working within strict poetic forms (sonnets, villanelles) while bringing contemporary, multicultural content. Her interest in craft and skill connects to the poem’s celebration of making — both the jeweller’s craft and the poet’s.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Caleb Femi — “Thirteen”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Femi, a British-Nigerian poet and the first Young People’s Laureate for London, captures the specific intensity of being thirteen — the age when childhood gives way to adolescence, when the world expands and becomes both thrilling and threatening. The poem is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem that pays tribute to or commemorates a person, place, time, or experience, often mixing celebration with a sense of loss.">elegy</dfn> for a particular moment of youth, captured with vivid sensory detail.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The title’s simplicity is deceptive: “Thirteen” is not just a number but a whole world — a specific age with its own culture, fears, freedoms, and landscapes. Femi writes about growing up in a London estate, and the poem captures both the joy and the danger of that environment for a young Black boy.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Femi’s poetry is rooted in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The physical and social environment of a city, including its architecture, communities, rhythms, and culture.">urban landscape</dfn> of North London estates. He presents these spaces not as stereotypical deprivations but as complex, alive environments full of community, creativity, and love alongside genuine risk. “Thirteen” captures a threshold moment when the speaker begins to understand both the beauty and the threat of their surroundings.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Context:</strong> Femi uses photography alongside poetry, and his work has a cinematic quality — each image is carefully composed. His poem captures thirteen as both universal (everyone passes through it) and specific (shaped by race, class, and geography).</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Raymond Antrobus — “With Birds You’re Never Lonely”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Antrobus, a British-Jamaican poet who is deaf, explores memory, connection, and communication through the image of birds. The title quotes something said to the speaker — perhaps by a parent or grandparent — and the poem explores how this simple statement carries layers of meaning about companionship, nature, and the ways we find connection despite barriers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For Antrobus, whose deafness shapes his relationship with the world, birds represent a form of communication that transcends spoken language. They are visible, present, always moving — a <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that exists on a boundary between two states or worlds, neither fully one thing nor the other.">liminal</dfn> presence between the human and natural worlds. The poem explores how memory preserves the voice of a loved one through a single, resonant phrase.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how each poet presents the relationship between memory and craft/making.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Antrobus’s poem shows how a single remembered phrase can contain an entire relationship. The birds become symbols of ongoing connection — the loved one’s voice persists in the natural world, offering companionship beyond death or distance. Memory is not a complete record but a fragment that contains the whole.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Key techniques:</strong> Antrobus uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A concise, memorable statement expressing a general truth or piece of advice, often attributed to a specific speaker.">aphorism</dfn> (the title as a piece of inherited wisdom), natural imagery, and the interplay between sound and silence that characterises much of his work. His poetry often explores what can and cannot be heard, making birds — which communicate visually as well as aurally — a perfect symbol.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Connections Between the Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">All three poems explore how memory selects and preserves particular moments or images. Agbabi presents memory as a craft — deliberate, skilled, transformative. Femi captures a whole era of life in a single age. Antrobus shows how a single phrase can carry the weight of an entire relationship.</p>

<div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: what survives in each poem? Agbabi (the crafted object), Femi (sensory experience of youth), Antrobus (a loved one’s phrase).">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Each poet has a different relationship with time. Agbabi treats time as raw material — something to be worked and shaped into lasting form. Femi freezes a specific age, making thirteen eternal through the poem. Antrobus suggests that time is defeated by memory — a loved one’s words persist beyond the moment they were spoken.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">A useful comparison: all three poets are concerned with what survives. For Agbabi, craft survives — the made thing outlasts the moment. For Femi, sensory experience survives — the sounds, textures, and feelings of thirteen. For Antrobus, love survives — carried in a simple phrase that echoes through a lifetime.</p>
