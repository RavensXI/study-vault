--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ethics and environment shape business decisions — and the trade-offs with profit.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Doing the Right Thing — at a Price</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Primark found in 2013 that a factory in Bangladesh it bought clothes from had collapsed, killing more than a thousand workers, it faced a choice: keep paying the lowest price for garments or spend more to audit suppliers and improve conditions. When BrewDog set a 2030 carbon-negative target, it agreed to invest heavily in land restoration and renewable energy. These are classic examples of <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that businesses should act in ways that their stakeholders consider to be fair and honest, even when it costs more.">ethical behaviour</dfn> — doing what stakeholders consider fair and honest, even when it costs more.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name two benefits of ethical behaviour and two costs that come with it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Acting ethically usually raises costs — higher wages, better-sourced materials, environmental clean-up — but can also raise revenue and loyalty by winning customers who care. An OCR answer will always weigh both sides.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Treatment of Workers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Ethical treatment of workers covers pay, safety, hours, and dignity at work. Paying above the legal minimum wage raises staff costs but often cuts turnover and training bills. John Lewis pays a share of profits to all staff (called ‘partners’) — the scheme has run since 1929 and is one of the reasons staff retention is so strong. In 2020 Boohoo was accused of buying from Leicester suppliers paying far below the minimum wage; sales fell, the share price dropped, and the company had to commission an independent review.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Treatment of Suppliers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Businesses choose how hard to push the firms that supply them. Ethical treatment means paying a fair price, paying on time, and checking that suppliers themselves are not using unsafe conditions or modern slavery. Tony’s Chocolonely, the Dutch chocolate brand sold widely in UK supermarkets, was founded in 2005 after journalist Teun van de Keuken discovered child and forced labour in West African cocoa supply. Tony’s buys directly from farmer cooperatives at a premium above market price and traces every bean — a genuinely slavery-free supply chain. Big supermarkets that delay supplier payments by 60 or 90 days often face legal and reputational challenge for squeezing small farmers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Treatment of Customers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Ethical treatment of customers means honest advertising, safe products, clear terms, and genuine after-sales support. In 2015 Volkswagen was caught fitting ‘defeat devices’ to hide the true emissions of its diesel cars — the dieselgate scandal cost the firm over thirty billion dollars in fines and lost sales, and damaged its brand for years. Consumers punish firms that mislead them, and regulators increasingly back them up.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Sourcing of Materials and Marketing Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Ethical <dfn class="term" data-def="Choosing raw materials or ingredients from suppliers who meet ethical standards on pay, safety and environmental impact.">sourcing</dfn> means choosing raw materials from suppliers who meet ethical and environmental standards — Fairtrade coffee and chocolate, certified sustainable timber, cotton that is not picked by forced labour. It also means saying no to cheap options that fail those tests. Costa Coffee and Greggs both sell only Fairtrade coffee in their UK cafes, accepting the higher cost in exchange for credibility with customers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Ethical marketing means not targeting vulnerable groups (gambling adverts around children’s TV), not exaggerating product claims, and being honest about ingredients and origins. The UK’s Advertising Standards Authority fines firms that get this wrong.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Ethics-Versus-Profit Trade-off</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where a business must accept a downside in one area to gain a benefit in another.">trade-off</dfn> is when a business must give up something in one area to gain in another. Paying a UK living wage raises staff costs — but can cut staff turnover and build brand reputation. Switching to recyclable packaging raises material costs — but can attract new customers and avoid future regulation. Sometimes the ethical choice is profitable long-term: Patagonia, the outdoor brand, built its entire identity on environmental responsibility (it famously ran a ‘Don’t Buy This Jacket’ ad) and grew faster than rivals. But in the short run, ethical choices can hit profits and test shareholders’ patience.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Pressure groups and consumers amplify unethical behaviour fast. Greenpeace campaigns have forced tuna brands to switch to pole-and-line fishing to protect dolphins; PETA campaigns have pushed cosmetics firms to stop testing on animals. Social media means a single factory-scandal video can reach millions before a PR team drafts a response.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The message is clear: unethical shortcuts can generate short-term profit, but when media, regulators or customers find out, the long-term cost usually outweighs the gain.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Environmental Considerations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Every business decision has an environmental footprint. Operations generate traffic congestion (think of Amazon vans on every street), noise and air pollution, and waste that ends up in landfill. Consumers, councils and governments increasingly expect businesses to act. Supermarkets charge for carrier bags; Starbucks and Pret offer discounts for reusable cups; Deliveroo is testing electric bikes and cargo cycles for last-mile deliveries.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Meeting the needs of today without using up resources that future generations will need.">Sustainability</dfn> means meeting today’s needs without using up resources future generations will need. The UK’s net-zero target has pushed firms into electric vans, energy-efficient buildings and carbon offsets.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Waste disposal:</strong> recycling is now a standard cost. Costa Coffee runs cup-recycling schemes; Tesco takes back soft plastic at its stores. <strong>Pollution:</strong> vehicles in UK clean-air zones like London’s ULEZ pay daily charges. <strong>Climate change:</strong> sourcing, transport and packaging all generate carbon — McDonald’s has committed to sourcing fish only from certified sustainable fisheries and beef from farms with reduced methane emissions, protecting its future supply chain while cutting the impact today.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Costs and Benefits of Ethical and Green Behaviour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Costs include higher-priced raw materials, staff training on new procedures, investment in cleaner technology, and the risk of <dfn class="term" data-def="Claiming environmental credentials in marketing without genuine action to back them up.">greenwashing</dfn> — claiming green credentials without genuine action. Customers and regulators are quick to spot and punish this.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Benefits include stronger brand reputation, the ability to charge premium prices, better staff retention, and early compliance with future laws. When governments later tighten rules, ethical firms do not need to scramble. Ethics and sustainability are no longer optional — they shape what materials a firm uses, how it hires, what it claims in adverts, and how quickly it can react when a new law lands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and test yourself: write down three environmental impacts of business activity and one example of a UK business reducing each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Environmental impacts of business activity include traffic congestion, noise and air pollution, waste sent to landfill, and the use of scarce resources. Sustainable practices aim to reduce each of these without shutting the business down.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Ethical behaviour covers treatment of workers, suppliers, customers, sourcing of materials and marketing decisions — every area carries a cost and a reputational benefit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Environmental considerations include sustainability, waste disposal, pollution and climate change — firms that act early avoid later fines and protect supply chains.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Every ethical choice is a trade-off between short-term cost and long-term reputation; a balanced OCR answer names both and applies them to the business in the case study.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Ethics questions almost always reward a balanced answer — do not just write ‘being ethical is good’. For ‘Analyse’ questions, build a chain of reasoning: ‘recyclable packaging raises material costs, which squeezes margin, but attracts eco-conscious customers, which lifts revenue.’ For 12-mark ‘Evaluate’ or ‘Recommend’ questions, the judgement must introduce a weighting — for example, ‘it depends on the customer base’ — not just repeat the points above. Always name a real example where you can, and apply every point to the business in the stem.</p>
