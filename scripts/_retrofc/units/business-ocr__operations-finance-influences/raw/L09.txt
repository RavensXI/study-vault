--- DESCRIPTION ---
Fixed, variable and total costs, revenue, and how to work out gross profit and net profit.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Two Streams of Money in Every Business</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Two streams of numbers run through every business: money going out (costs) and money coming in (revenue). What’s left after costs have been taken off revenue is <dfn class="term" data-def="What is left of revenue after all costs have been deducted.">profit</dfn>. If costs are greater than revenue, the business makes a <dfn class="term" data-def="What results when costs are greater than revenue in a given period.">loss</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding the different kinds of cost is the key to nearly every Finance question in the exam. A business that cannot tell which costs are fixed and which are variable cannot set prices sensibly, cannot work out break-even, and cannot forecast what happens to profit if sales rise or fall.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Fixed, Variable and Total Costs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short term.">Fixed costs</dfn> stay the same whether the business makes 10 units or 10,000. Rent, salaries of managers, insurance and business rates are classic examples. A coffee shop pays the same rent whether it sells one latte a day or a thousand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that change directly with the level of output.">Variable costs</dfn> rise and fall with output. Every latte needs coffee beans, milk and a cup — sell twice as many, and those costs double.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fixed costs plus variable costs for a given level of output.">Total costs</dfn> are simply fixed costs plus variable costs for a given level of output:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">$$\text{Total costs} = \text{Fixed costs} + \text{Variable costs}$$</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down, from memory, one example each of a fixed cost and a variable cost for a UK coffee shop.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Fixed costs stay the same when output changes (rent, salaries, insurance). Variable costs change directly with output (raw materials, packaging, hourly wages). Total costs = fixed + variable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Revenue</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total income from selling goods or services: price per unit multiplied by quantity sold.">Revenue</dfn> (sometimes called turnover or sales) is the money coming in from selling products or services. The formula is straightforward:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$\text{Revenue} = \text{Price per unit} \times \text{Quantity sold}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Revenue is not profit. It is the gross figure <em>before</em> any costs are deducted. A business with £1 million in revenue may be making a loss if its costs are £1.2 million.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Worked Example — Oakleaf Coffee</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Imagine a fictional UK coffee shop, Oakleaf Coffee. It sells a latte for £3.50. In one week it sells 800 lattes. Its fixed costs (rent, manager’s salary, insurance) are £1,200 per week, and variable costs (beans, milk, cups, hourly staff) are £1.10 per latte.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Step 1 — revenue:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">$$\text{Revenue} = \pounds3.50 \times 800 = \pounds2{,}800$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Step 2 — total variable costs:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">$$\text{Variable costs} = \pounds1.10 \times 800 = \pounds880$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Step 3 — total costs:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$\text{Total costs} = \pounds1{,}200 + \pounds880 = \pounds2{,}080$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Step 4 — profit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">$$\text{Profit} = \pounds2{,}800 - \pounds2{,}080 = \pounds720$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Oakleaf makes £720 profit that week. If sales dropped to 400 lattes, variable costs would fall to £440 but fixed costs stay at £1,200. Revenue would be only £1,400, and the shop would lose £240. This is why the split between fixed and variable costs matters so much — it tells you how sensitive profit is to a change in sales.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Gross Profit and Net Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">OCR distinguishes between two kinds of profit. <dfn class="term" data-def="Revenue minus the direct cost of producing the goods sold (cost of sales).">Gross profit</dfn> measures how much a business makes after paying for the goods it sold. <dfn class="term" data-def="Gross profit minus other expenses such as rent, salaries, utilities and interest.">Net profit</dfn> goes further and takes off every other cost too — rent, utilities, salaries, interest.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">$$\text{Gross profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost of sales}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">$$\text{Net profit} = \text{Gross profit} - \text{Other expenses}$$</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example — Gross vs Net Profit</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29">A small clothing shop has annual revenue of £200,000. The cost of the clothes it bought from suppliers (cost of sales) was £120,000. Its other expenses — rent, utilities, shop assistant wages, insurance — totalled £50,000.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">Gross profit = £200,000 − £120,000 = £80,000.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31">Net profit = £80,000 − £50,000 = £30,000.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">The business is profitable both gross and net. But if rent rose by £20,000 and other expenses jumped to £70,000, net profit would fall to £10,000 even though gross profit stayed at £80,000. Net profit is always the more cautious figure — and the one investors care about most.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the gross profit and net profit formulae without peeking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales. Net profit = Gross profit − Other expenses. Gross profit shows how much the products themselves earn; net profit shows what’s left after every cost of running the business.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why the Fixed/Variable Split Matters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>High fixed costs, low variable costs</strong> — think of a cinema. The building, projector and staff salaries are expensive, but each extra viewer costs almost nothing. Once fixed costs are covered, every extra ticket sold is nearly pure profit. But if attendance drops, the huge fixed costs still have to be paid.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Low fixed costs, high variable costs</strong> — think of a market-stall sandwich seller. Stall rent is low, but bread, fillings and packaging cost real money per sandwich. Profit per unit is smaller but the business is far less risky in a quiet week.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n36">Why These Numbers Drive Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n37">Understanding revenue, costs and profit is not just an accounting exercise. Managers use these figures every single day: to set prices sensibly (variable cost per unit plus a margin that covers fixed costs), to spot problems early (rising costs with flat revenue means trouble ahead), to talk credibly to lenders and investors (banks want to see strong, steady profit before agreeing to a loan), and to decide which product lines to keep and which to drop. A business that cannot explain its own numbers in a meeting is unlikely to walk out of a bank with the loan it needs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n38">The same logic applies to small businesses too. A newsagent tracking daily sales against weekly rent, wages and stock costs knows within minutes whether Tuesday was profitable. Scale the principle up and you have the dashboard every UK PLC relies on: Tesco, Greggs and Next all publish revenue, cost of sales, gross profit and net profit figures to their shareholders every six months, using the same basic formulas taught here.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n41">Fixed costs stay the same when output changes; variable costs change directly with output; total costs are fixed + variable.</li><li data-narration-id="n42">Revenue = Price × Quantity. Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales. Net profit = Gross profit − Other expenses.</li><li data-narration-id="n43">The split between fixed and variable costs shapes how risky a business is. High fixed costs mean big gains at high volume but big losses in a quiet month.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n39">Calculation questions are where students most often lose easy marks. OCR does not give you the formulae in the exam — memorise them. Always show your working: method marks are awarded even if the final number is wrong. Always add the correct unit (£ or %) to the final answer — a bare number loses a mark. For ‘Explain’ questions on costs, a definition alone is not enough: you need a ‘because’ clause showing <em>why</em> the cost behaves the way it does, tied back to the business in the stem.</p>
