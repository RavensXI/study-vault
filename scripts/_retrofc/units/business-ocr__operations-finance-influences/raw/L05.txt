--- DESCRIPTION ---
Costs, market, labour and materials — the four factors behind where a business chooses to locate.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Location as an Operations Decision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Where a business is based shapes its costs, its customers and the way its operations actually run. A Pret a Manger on Oxford Street lives or dies on foot traffic. A JCB factory near Uttoxeter needs space, steel suppliers and skilled engineers. An Amazon fulfilment centre in Rugby is there because it can reach most of the UK population within a day’s delivery. Pick the wrong spot and no amount of marketing can rescue you — the shop with no passing trade, the factory miles from its suppliers, the office where no-one wants to work. That is why the <dfn class="term" data-def="The choice of where a business physically operates, based on factors such as cost, proximity to market, labour and materials.">location decision</dfn> is an operations decision first and foremost: it affects how the business produces, supplies and serves every single day.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Factor 1: Costs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The total expense of operating in a location — rent, business rates, wages, utilities and transport.">Costs</dfn> are often the first test. Rent, business rates, wages, transport and utilities differ enormously between locations. A retail unit on London’s Oxford Street can cost thousands of pounds per square metre per year; a unit in Doncaster a fraction of that. Warehousing, office space and factory floor space are all cheaper in the north and midlands than in London and the south-east. For many businesses — especially warehouses and back-office operations — lower costs are the single biggest reason they pick a particular town.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Government support can shift the balance. Some UK regions offer tax breaks, cash grants or cheap land to attract new investment. Nissan chose Sunderland in the 1980s partly because of government support, and the plant still employs thousands today.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Factor 2: Proximity to Market</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="How close a business is to its customers — critical for service businesses that rely on foot traffic.">Proximity to the market</dfn> — being close to customers — matters most for service businesses. A hairdresser, a takeaway and a Costa Coffee all need foot traffic to survive. The closer you are to a busy town centre, a train station or a large residential area, the more potential customers walk past. That is why high-street rents are eye-watering: the footfall justifies the cost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">For online-only businesses such as ASOS or Gousto, this factor falls away — customers can be anywhere in the country, so what matters instead is warehouse location close to fast delivery networks rather than close to the customers themselves.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Factor 3: Availability of Labour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A business needs people with the right skills at an affordable wage. <dfn class="term" data-def="The people available to work for a business in a given area — quality, quantity and wage levels all matter.">Availability of labour</dfn> has two dimensions: whether there are enough workers, and whether they have the right skills. A tech firm sets up in Cambridge or Manchester because that is where the software engineers cluster. A fulfilment centre locates in Rugby or Wakefield because those areas have plentiful lower-skilled labour near motorway links. Wage levels vary too: a call centre set up in Derby costs far less to staff than one in central London.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four location factors named on the specification, with one real UK business example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four named location factors: <strong>costs</strong>, <strong>proximity to market</strong>, <strong>labour</strong>, and <strong>materials</strong>. Every location decision weighs these against each other, and no business wins on all four at once.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Factor 4: Proximity to Raw Materials</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Businesses that transform bulky <strong>raw materials</strong> want to be close to those materials to cut transport costs. A cheese maker in the Lake District sits near dairy farms. A stone quarry’s cutting shed sits next to the quarry. A fish and chip supplier locates near fishing ports. If transporting raw materials costs more than transporting the finished product, locate near the materials. If the finished product is the bulky one (bottled drinks, for example), locate near the market.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Location does not sit in isolation — it shapes how every other part of operations works. A factory close to its suppliers has shorter supply chains, which means faster restocking and more reliable delivery to the business’s own customers. A shop close to its market can offer same-day delivery that a warehouse 200 miles away cannot. A call centre in an area of high unemployment finds it easier to recruit and retain staff at lower wages. This is why Paper 2 synoptic questions often link location to supplier choice, customer service and costs: they are part of the same decision.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Trade-Offs: You Cannot Win on Every Factor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">A central London coffee shop wins on footfall but loses on rent. A business park unit in Hull wins on costs but loses on customer access. Most location decisions involve balancing two or three strong factors against one weaker one. The skill in exam answers is spotting which factor matters most for <em>this</em> business. A fine-dining restaurant lives on the quality of its customer experience — a prestigious address justifies the rent. A warehouse operator lives on unit cost per square metre — the cheap site wins.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Service businesses (cafes, hairdressers, high-street retailers) usually weigh proximity to market most heavily — they cannot exist without footfall. Manufacturers that transform heavy raw materials (quarries, sawmills, fish processors) put availability of materials first. Warehouses and online fulfilment centres (Amazon in Rugby, Ocado in Hatfield) weigh costs and motorway access above all else. Knowledge-based businesses (software, design, pharmaceutical research) often pick labour first because they need a cluster of skilled workers in one place.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why an online-only retailer weighs location factors differently from a high-street shop.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Digital and online-only businesses weigh location factors differently — proximity to the customer matters less, but warehouse rent, motorway access and a reliable lower-wage workforce all matter more. ASOS, Boohoo and Gousto all chose their locations around fulfilment, not footfall.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">How Businesses Actually Decide</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Real location decisions combine the four specification factors with commercial research: customer demographics, planning permission, transport access and rival activity. A chain like Greggs uses data analytics to pick sites where their typical customer walks past in the greatest numbers. A family business making handmade furniture often starts near home and stays there because moving would mean losing craftspeople who cannot commute further. Whichever approach, location is not a one-off choice — Tesco, Boots and John Lewis have all closed underperforming sites and opened new ones as consumer habits change.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The four location factors on the specification are costs, proximity to market, availability of labour, and proximity to raw materials.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The right mix depends on the business — service firms prioritise footfall, manufacturers prioritise raw materials, online firms prioritise warehouse cost and transport access.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Location decisions are trade-offs: the strongest answers identify which factor matters most for the business in the scenario and justify why.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Students lose marks by listing all four factors at a surface level. Pick the two that matter most for the named business in the scenario and develop each with a ‘because’ chain. For a small town bakery, proximity to market and cost dominate; for an online clothing retailer, warehouse costs and transport links dominate. In 12-mark <em>Recommend</em> questions, always name the single factor you are weighting most heavily — this is what turns a description into a supported judgement.</p>
