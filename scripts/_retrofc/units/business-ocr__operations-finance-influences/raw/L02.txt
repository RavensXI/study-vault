--- DESCRIPTION ---
Quality control versus assurance, and why reputation, returns and recalls depend on it.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Do Customers Mean by ‘Quality’?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The standard to which a product or service meets customer expectations — covering performance, reliability, appearance and the experience of using it.">Quality</dfn> is not the same as ‘expensive’. A Greggs sausage roll and a John Lewis television both have a claim to be quality products, but they deliver very different customer expectations. The sausage roll has to be hot, fresh and consistent every time. The television has to be reliable, cleanly designed and backed by proper after-sales support.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Customer expectations cover both the <strong>product itself</strong> (does it work, does it last, does it match the description?) and the <strong>service around it</strong> (was staff knowledge good, was delivery on time, if it broke was a refund easy?). Businesses lose customers when the reality falls below what was promised.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list: three ways customers notice quality in a product, and three ways they notice it in a service.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Quality has two sides: <strong>production of goods</strong> (durability, accuracy to specification, zero defects) and <strong>provision of services</strong> (staff expertise, punctuality, how complaints are handled). The specification asks about both.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Two Ways to Build Quality: Control vs Assurance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The specification names two methods a business uses to make sure its goods and services are consistently good enough: <strong>quality control</strong> and <strong>quality assurance</strong>. Students regularly mix these up, so be precise.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Quality Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Checking finished or nearly-finished products against a standard, and rejecting anything that fails — quality is inspected in at the end of the process.">Quality control</dfn> inspects products at the <em>end</em> of the process. A trained inspector, a sensor, or an automated camera checks the finished item against an agreed standard. Anything that fails is rejected, reworked or scrapped. In a brewery, a technician samples one bottle in every hundred for colour and taste. At an airport, security staff re-scan any suitcase the machine flags. On a clothing line, a final checker looks for loose threads and missed stitches.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The strength of quality control is simplicity: one team or one machine is responsible, and the rules for passing or failing are clear. The weakness is waste. By the time you reject a faulty loaf of bread or a defective car door, you have already paid for the flour, labour and machine time to make it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Quality Assurance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Building quality into every step of the process so defects do not happen in the first place — quality is assured rather than inspected in.">Quality assurance</dfn> builds quality into the process itself, at <em>every</em> stage. Rather than a final inspection, each worker (or each machine station) follows agreed procedures and checks their own output before passing it on. If anything looks wrong, the line is stopped there and then. John Lewis trains every partner to treat quality as part of their job. Toyota’s production system famously gives any worker the authority to stop the line by pulling a cord when they spot a problem, so faults are fixed before more work is added on top.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The strength of quality assurance is prevention. Defects are caught early — before expensive later work has been added on top — so waste falls and customer complaints drop. The weakness is the up-front effort: more training, clearer procedures, and a workplace culture where staff feel able to raise problems.</p>

    <span>Worked example: the same bakery, two approaches</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A bakery running <strong>quality control</strong> places an inspector at the end of the line. Burnt or misshapen loaves are pulled out and binned. The team still have to pay for the flour, the energy and the baker’s time.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The same bakery running <strong>quality assurance</strong> trains every baker to check the oven temperature, the rising time and the shape at each stage. The problem is spotted and fixed before a whole batch goes wrong — and in many cases before any ingredients are even wasted.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Neither method is automatically better. Quality control is cheaper to set up but wastes more. Quality assurance is more expensive to introduce but pays back in less scrap and fewer complaints.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence the difference between quality control and quality assurance, using the word 'stage'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Quality control</strong> checks the product at the <em>end</em> of the process; <strong>quality assurance</strong> builds quality in at <em>every</em> stage so problems do not reach the end in the first place.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Why Quality Matters: Reputation, Returns and Recalls</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The specification is explicit about why a business invests in quality: to protect the <strong>reputation of the business</strong>, to <strong>gain and retain customers</strong>, and to reduce product <strong>returns</strong> and <strong>recalls</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Reputation</strong> takes years to build and minutes to damage. A single bad review on Trustpilot or a viral social media clip of a faulty product can undo months of careful marketing. <strong>Gaining and retaining customers</strong> depends on it: customers trust brands with a strong quality reputation enough to come back — and trust saves enormous marketing spend compared with constantly chasing new buyers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Returns</strong> cost money directly: the refund, the handling cost, the damaged item that cannot be resold. <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business asks customers to return an already-sold product because of a safety or quality defect.">Product recalls</dfn> are worse still. Volkswagen’s 2015 emissions scandal forced recalls of millions of vehicles worldwide, cost the company billions in fines, and took years to rebuild trust. Toyota’s Prius recalls in 2009–2010, covering around 8 million cars, cost an estimated $2 billion.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The cost of getting it wrong</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">The financial hit of a quality failure is rarely just the refund. Tesco’s 2013 horsemeat crisis — beef burgers found to contain horse DNA — cost the supermarket a reported £300 million in lost sales as shoppers switched to rivals. Samsung’s Galaxy Note 7 recall in 2016, caused by exploding batteries, cost the firm around $5 billion and forced it to withdraw the phone entirely. In both cases the long-term reputational damage outweighed the direct cost of replacing the product.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Bringing It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Quality is not a bonus extra — it sits at the heart of how a business competes. Whether the firm chooses to <em>inspect quality in</em> through control or <em>build quality in</em> through assurance, the goal is the same: a product or service the customer trusts, bought again, recommended to others, and safe from the worst-case costs of returns and recalls.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Quality is the standard to which a product or service meets customer expectations, covering both goods (durability, specification) and services (staff, delivery, complaints).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Quality control inspects at the end and rejects failures; quality assurance builds checks in at every stage so defects do not happen in the first place.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Quality protects reputation, wins and keeps customers, and reduces the direct and reputational costs of returns and recalls.</li>
</ul>

On 12-mark <em>Evaluate / Recommend</em>, remember the OCR phrasing: make a supported judgement, not a summary. Stick to the two named methods in the specification — quality control and quality assurance — and do not invent other labels.</p>
