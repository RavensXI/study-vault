--- DESCRIPTION ---
Gross and net profit margins as efficiency measures, plus ARR for judging investments.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Absolute Profit Is Not Enough</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Two UK coffee chains both report £500,000 of net profit this year. On the face of it they look equally successful. But one made its £500,000 from £2 million of revenue; the other made its £500,000 from £10 million of revenue. The first business is keeping 25p of every pound as net profit. The second is keeping just 5p. Who is really the better performer?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Absolute profit figures hide the real story. <dfn class="term" data-def="A ratio that expresses profit as a percentage of revenue, allowing businesses of different sizes to be compared.">Profit margins</dfn> turn profit into a percentage so businesses can be compared fairly and so managers can see whether costs are creeping up even when revenue is growing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Gross Profit Margin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Gross profit as a percentage of revenue; shows the profit left after paying for the goods sold, before other expenses.">Gross profit margin</dfn> shows the percentage of revenue left after paying the direct cost of the goods sold. The formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A supermarket might have a gross profit margin of about 25% — of every pound spent, 25p is left after paying for the stock. A luxury fashion brand like Burberry often posts gross margins above 70% because its products carry a large price markup over the cost of materials.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Worked Example — Gross Profit Margin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A clothing shop has revenue of £200,000 and gross profit of £80,000.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">$$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\pounds80{,}000}{\pounds200{,}000} \times 100 = 40\%$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Interpretation:</strong> for every £1 of sales, the business keeps 40p after paying for the clothes it sold. That 40p still has to cover rent, wages and every other overhead — but it is a healthy starting point. If the figure falls to 35% next year, either suppliers are charging more or the shop is discounting too heavily.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Net Profit Margin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Net profit as a percentage of revenue; shows the profit left after every cost of running the business has been paid.">Net profit margin</dfn> takes every cost into account — not just the cost of sales, but rent, utilities, wages, interest and any other expense. The formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">$$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100$$</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Worked Example — Net Profit Margin</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The same clothing shop has net profit of £30,000 on its £200,000 revenue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">$$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\pounds30{,}000}{\pounds200{,}000} \times 100 = 15\%$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Interpretation:</strong> after every single cost is paid, the shop keeps 15p of each pound of sales as profit. Comparing this against last year, or against another shop on the same high street, shows whether the business is getting more efficient.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write both profit-margin formulae from memory, including the ×100 at the end.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gross Profit Margin = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100. Net Profit Margin = (Net profit ÷ Revenue) × 100. Always multiply by 100 to get a percentage — forget that step and you lose a mark.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Small gap</strong> between gross and net margins — the business has low overheads relative to its core activity. An online retailer with a warehouse and no shops will often have a small gap.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Large gap</strong> between gross and net margins — overheads are eating a lot of the gross profit. A high-street retailer with expensive shop rents and many staff will usually have a large gap. If gross margin is healthy but net margin is thin, the problem is not the products — it’s the running costs.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Average Rate of Return</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Businesses don’t only think about everyday profit. When weighing up a one-off investment — a new delivery van, a factory extension, a coffee roaster — managers calculate the <dfn class="term" data-def="The average annual profit from an investment expressed as a percentage of the initial cost, used to compare investment options.">Average Rate of Return</dfn> (ARR). ARR turns the total profit from a project into an annual percentage so it can be compared directly to savings rates, loan costs or rival investments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The formula is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average Annual Profit}}{\text{Initial Investment}} \times 100$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Where average annual profit = total profit from the project ÷ number of years it runs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Worked Example — Buying a Coffee Roaster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Oakleaf Coffee spends £20,000 on a new roaster. Over five years it generates £30,000 of total profit.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Step 1 — average annual profit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">$$\text{Average annual profit} = \frac{\pounds30{,}000}{5} = \pounds6{,}000$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Step 2 — ARR:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\pounds6{,}000}{\pounds20{,}000} \times 100 = 30\%$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Interpretation:</strong> every year the roaster returns on average 30% of its initial cost. That comfortably beats a UK bank savings rate (around 4–5%) and suggests the investment is worthwhile. If Oakleaf had a rival project offering 45% ARR at similar risk, she would pick that instead.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the ARR formula from memory. Then list two things ARR does NOT take into account.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>ARR = (Average Annual Profit ÷ Initial Investment) × 100. It does NOT take timing of returns or risk of the project into account — managers have to weigh those separately before making a final call.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Strengths and Weaknesses of ARR</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Strengths:</strong> ARR is simple to calculate and easy to explain to non-finance staff. It produces a percentage, which can be compared directly with interest rates on savings, loans or alternative investments. It uses the full life of a project, not just the first year or two.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Weaknesses:</strong> ARR averages profits over the whole project, ignoring <em>when</em> the returns arrive — a project paying big profits in year 1 is treated the same as one paying them in year 5, even though money now is worth more than money later. ARR is also based on forecasts that may be wrong, and it ignores the level of risk attached to a project. A 30% ARR on a reckless venture is worse than a 15% ARR on a safe one.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n37">Putting the Numbers Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n38">Margins and ARR do different jobs. Margins judge how efficient a business is at turning revenue into profit day after day. ARR judges whether a one-off investment is worth making. A strong business uses both: watching margins each month to catch cost drift, and running ARR checks before committing to a new machine, store or factory.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n41">Gross Profit Margin = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100. Net Profit Margin = (Net profit ÷ Revenue) × 100.</li><li data-narration-id="n42">ARR = (Average Annual Profit ÷ Initial Investment) × 100. It turns a project’s total profit into an annual percentage for comparison.</li><li data-narration-id="n43">Every figure must be interpreted — what does the % mean for the business? OCR marks interpretation, not just calculation.</li></ul>
