--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses sell, why e-commerce reshapes the high street, and how service wins repeat customers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From First Contact to Repeat Customer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every sale a business makes is the end of one process and the start of another. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The method and the sequence a business uses to move a potential customer from awareness of a product to the point of purchase.">sales process</dfn> covers how a business reaches customers and closes the deal; <dfn class="term" data-def="The support and interaction a business provides to customers before, during and after a purchase, shaping how satisfied they feel with the whole experience.">customer service</dfn> covers everything that happens around and after the sale. Together they decide whether the buyer comes back — and whether they recommend you to anyone else.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Methods of Selling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The specification lists three main methods of selling: <strong>e-commerce</strong>, <strong>face to face</strong> and <strong>telesales</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Selling goods or services over the internet, usually through a website or app.">E-commerce</dfn> means selling online. ASOS, Amazon, Ocado and boohoo take their orders through websites and apps, and most high-street retailers (Tesco, John Lewis, Next) now combine a physical store with a strong online channel. Customers can shop 24 hours a day, compare prices in seconds and read reviews before they buy. For the business, the big attraction is reach: one warehouse can serve the whole country without the rent of a shop on every high street.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Face-to-face selling</strong> happens in a shop, showroom or at a trade stand. The Apple Store, John Lewis, a Mercedes-Benz showroom and a local bakery all rely on this method. Customers can touch, try and ask questions; staff can read body language and adjust their pitch. The cost is higher — rent, store staff, fit-out — but for complicated or expensive purchases the personal connection can close a sale that a website could not.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Telesales</strong> means selling over the phone, either outbound (the business rings the customer) or inbound (the customer rings the business). Insurance renewals, energy contracts and some business-to-business services still use this method. It is cheaper than face-to-face but has a mixed reputation because of unwanted cold calls, so many firms are moving to online chat instead.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Rise of E-commerce</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">E-commerce has reshaped how almost every UK business sells. Retailers that adapted quickly — ASOS, Next, JD Sports — kept growing when high-street rivals closed branches. Businesses that missed the shift — Debenhams, Wilko — lost customers to rivals who made online ordering easier. Even industries that used to be pure face-to-face, such as vehicle purchases (Cinch, Cazoo) or prescription glasses (Warby Parker), now have serious online challengers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">For the business, e-commerce brings four main effects: a wider <strong>customer reach</strong> (anyone with an internet connection), richer <strong>data</strong> (every click shows what customers look at and what they abandon), lower <strong>fixed costs</strong> per sale (one warehouse replaces many shops), and pressure on <strong>prices</strong> because customers can easily compare with rivals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three methods of selling and one UK business example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three methods of selling on the specification: <strong>e-commerce</strong> (ASOS, Amazon), <strong>face to face</strong> (John Lewis, Apple Store) and <strong>telesales</strong> (insurance renewals, some B2B services). E-commerce has widened reach but increased price pressure.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">What Makes Customer Service ‘Good’?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The specification names two named contributors to good customer service: <strong>product knowledge</strong> and <strong>customer engagement</strong>. It also highlights <strong>after-sales service</strong> as an important part of customer service overall.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Product Knowledge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Staff who really understand what they sell save the customer time and build trust. An Apple Store ‘Genius’ guiding a first-time buyer through an iPad, a John Lewis partner comparing two washing machines, or a bike-shop mechanic explaining gear ratios — all these turn an uncertain shopper into a confident one. Strong product knowledge is also what often justifies a higher price than a discount retailer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Poor product knowledge leaves customers feeling dismissed or misled. Worse, it produces the wrong sale — the buyer returns the item, loses trust, and tells other people.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Customer Engagement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Everything a business does to create a positive experience for the customer — from greeting and store layout to personalised follow-ups.">Customer engagement</dfn> is about the whole experience. The welcome when you walk into a Pret coffee shop, the personalised ‘thanks for your order’ email, the store layout that makes finding items easy, the quick hello on social media when someone tags you in a post. Strong engagement makes customers feel valued and remembered.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">ASOS built a reputation on engagement through free returns and chatty order confirmations. John Lewis partners are part-owners of the business, and the firm invests heavily in their training — the pay-off is a service reputation that attracts customers prepared to pay more than they would at a discount retailer.</p>

Currys and John Lewis compete partly on the strength of their after-sales cover — a five-year guarantee at no extra cost is a real decision influence for a big-ticket purchase. For ASOS, free and simple returns are themselves the after-sales service — a confident customer is more likely to order in the first place if they know sending items back will not be a hassle.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Why Good Service Pays Back</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Good customer service feeds profit through a chain of benefits. Customer <strong>satisfaction</strong> rises — the buyer feels listened to and valued. This breeds <strong>customer loyalty</strong>, which means repeat business that costs far less to win than a new customer. Loyal customers also buy more often and try new products. The end result is that the business is able to <strong>gain and retain customers</strong> — the specific phrase the specification uses — which directly supports higher sales and profit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: the two named contributors to good customer service, and why they help a business 'gain and retain customers'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Product knowledge + customer engagement + strong after-sales service = a better experience. That helps the business <em>gain and retain customers</em>, the specification’s exact phrasing for why service matters.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n24">Poor service now travels fast. A single bad review on Trustpilot or a complaint tweeted at a major airline is seen by thousands within minutes — that is why companies like easyJet and John Lewis monitor social media around the clock. One clip of a rude till interaction can undo a week of positive marketing. The cost of fixing a complaint in public is far larger than the cost of training staff to handle it politely in the first place.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Bringing It Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The sales process decides how a business <em>reaches</em> customers. Customer service decides whether those customers stay. E-commerce has widened reach but raised customer expectations — today’s shopper can compare ten rivals before lunch. The businesses that win are those that match their selling method to the product and deliver product knowledge, engagement and after-sales care that earn the customer’s trust again.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Three methods of selling: e-commerce, face to face and telesales. E-commerce has widened reach but increased price competition.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Good customer service depends on product knowledge, customer engagement and strong after-sales support.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">The business gain is the specification’s named outcome: to ‘gain and retain customers’, which converts to repeat sales and higher long-run profit.</li>
</ul>
