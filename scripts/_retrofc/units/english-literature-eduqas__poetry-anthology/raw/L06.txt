--- DESCRIPTION ---
Owen's limbless veteran recalls the vanity behind enlisting and the indifference that follows.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Disabled — Wilfred Owen</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">&ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; gives us a single evening in the life of a young soldier who has lost his legs and an arm in the First World War. He sits in a wheelchair, watching boys play in a nearby park and remembering the vitality he once had. Owen moves between this bleak present and vivid flashbacks of the soldier&rsquo;s pre-war life, his hasty decision to enlist, and the indifference that meets him on his return.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">Poet and Context</h2><p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Wilfred Owen (1893&ndash;1918), a First World War poet who wrote unflinching, realistic poetry about the physical and psychological damage of war, and was killed in action a week before the Armistice.">Wilfred Owen</dfn> wrote most of his war poetry in 1917 and 1918, drawing on his own experience of trench warfare and hospital treatment for shell shock. Unlike the patriotic verse many readers expected during the war, Owen&rsquo;s poems expose the gap between the glory promised to young recruits and the grim reality of injury, disability, and social neglect.</p><p data-narration-id="n5">&ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; is written from the perspective of a survivor rather than a casualty &mdash; the war has not killed this soldier, but it has taken away his future. The poem&rsquo;s power comes from its ordinary setting: a park, a wheelchair, an evening. Owen does not need a battlefield to show war&rsquo;s damage, only its long, quiet aftermath.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall three reasons the soldier gives for joining up &mdash; none of them are really about patriotism."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The poem reveals that the soldier enlisted for shallow, almost accidental reasons. He had &ldquo;drunk a peg&rdquo; after a football match, someone told him he&rsquo;d &ldquo;look a god in kilts,&rdquo; and he wanted &ldquo;to please his Meg.&rdquo; Recruiters did not check his age either &mdash; &ldquo;Smiling they wrote his lie&rdquo; when he claimed to be older. The soldier&rsquo;s decision to fight was driven by vanity and peer pressure, not by patriotism or understanding of war.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Key quotations:</strong> The poem opens with the soldier &ldquo;waiting for dark&rdquo; in his &ldquo;ghastly suit of grey,&rdquo; colourless, institutional clothing that replaces his uniform. He is described bluntly as &ldquo;Legless, sewn short at elbow,&rdquo; language that treats his body almost like damaged fabric. Before he &ldquo;threw away his knees,&rdquo; women&rsquo;s touch delighted him; now it feels &ldquo;like some queer disease.&rdquo; Owen measures the soldier&rsquo;s decline through blood and colour: he has &ldquo;lost his colour very far from here,&rdquo; and a &ldquo;leap of purple spurted&rdquo; from his wounded thigh.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n8">Themes</h2><p data-narration-id="n9">&ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; explores the waste of youth, the gap between wartime propaganda and reality, and society&rsquo;s discomfort with the disabled bodies war produces. The soldier is not honoured as a hero; he is pitied and avoided. Owen contrasts the soldier&rsquo;s past vitality &mdash; football, girls, admiration &mdash; with his present isolation, sharpened by the sound of &ldquo;voices of boys&rdquo; playing nearby, a life he can no longer join.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain what the repeated question &lsquo;Why don&rsquo;t they come?&rsquo; reveals about the soldier&rsquo;s situation."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The poem ends on quiet abandonment rather than dramatic tragedy. The soldier notices women&rsquo;s eyes pass &ldquo;to the strong men that were whole,&rdquo; and his welcome home is bitterly undercut: he was cheered, but &ldquo;not as crowds cheer Goal.&rdquo; A visiting stranger&rsquo;s only real interest is macabre &mdash; he &ldquo;inquired about his soul.&rdquo; The poem&rsquo;s final repeated question, &ldquo;Why don&rsquo;t they come?&rdquo; leaves the soldier waiting, helpless and forgotten, for someone simply to put him to bed.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n11">Form and Structure</h2><p data-narration-id="n12">Owen structures &ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; around flashbacks that interrupt the grim present. The poem moves between the soldier sitting &ldquo;waiting for dark&rdquo; and memories of dances, football, and the day he enlisted, before snapping back to the institution. This structure mirrors the way trauma intrudes on memory &mdash; the soldier cannot enjoy the past without being pulled back to his ruined present.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Language and Techniques in Disabled</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n13">The stanzas vary irregularly in length, and Owen uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of rhyme in which final consonant sounds match but the vowel sounds differ, creating a jarring, incomplete effect.">para-rhyme</dfn> &mdash; near-rhymes like &ldquo;hymn&rdquo; and &ldquo;him&rdquo; &mdash; instead of full rhyme. This creates a sense of things not quite fitting together, echoing the soldier&rsquo;s broken body. The repeated question &ldquo;Why don&rsquo;t they come?&rdquo; creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A pause or break within a line of poetry, often created by punctuation.">caesura</dfn>-heavy, halting close to the poem, slowing the final lines almost to a stop. Elsewhere, <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuation of a sentence or clause across a line break without a pause.">enjambment</dfn> lets sentences spill across lines, mimicking the soldier&rsquo;s drifting, uncontrolled memories.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n14">Comparison Pairings</h2><p data-narration-id="n15">&ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; pairs well with several other anthology poems on the theme of what war does to those who survive it. <em>Kamikaze</em> by Beatrice Garland also shows a soldier &mdash; a pilot who abandons his fatal mission &mdash; treated as an outcast by his own family; both poems ask whether survival can feel worse than death once society withdraws its affection. <em>Remains</em> by Simon Armitage shares Owen&rsquo;s interest in trauma that returns uninvited: where the soldier in &ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; is haunted by memories of the vitality he has lost, the soldier in &ldquo;Remains&rdquo; is haunted by a killing he cannot forget. <em>War Photographer</em> by Carol Ann Duffy connects through its focus on a comfortable society that would rather not look closely at suffering &mdash; the indifferent civilians who watch from a safe distance echo the crowd who once cheered this soldier off to war but no longer notice him.</p><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two anthology poems that pair with &lsquo;Disabled&rsquo; and explain the shared theme for each."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Using These Pairings in the Exam</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n16">In the exam&rsquo;s comparison question, you choose one other anthology poem to compare with the specified poem. If &ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; is set, &ldquo;Kamikaze&rdquo; lets you compare how both poems present survivors rejected by people who once loved them. &ldquo;Remains&rdquo; lets you compare the psychological aftermath of violence and the intrusion of memory. &ldquo;War Photographer&rdquo; lets you compare how each poet critiques a society that looks away from suffering it caused or ignores. Choose the pairing that lets you say the most about language, form, and structure, not just shared subject matter.</p></div></div></div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n19">The soldier in &ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; enlisted for vanity and peer pressure &mdash; &ldquo;to please his Meg&rdquo; and to &ldquo;look a god in kilts&rdquo; &mdash; not patriotism.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Owen structures the poem through flashbacks that contrast the soldier&rsquo;s past vitality with his present isolation and social neglect.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">&ldquo;Disabled&rdquo; pairs well with &ldquo;Kamikaze,&rdquo; &ldquo;Remains,&rdquo; and &ldquo;War Photographer&rdquo; for the comparison question, all exploring war&rsquo;s aftermath.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When you write about &ldquo;Disabled,&rdquo; keep quotations short and exact &mdash; phrases like &ldquo;threw away his knees&rdquo; or &ldquo;not as crowds cheer Goal&rdquo; are easier to embed accurately than long lines, and this is a closed-book exam, so precision matters more than length. For the comparison question, decide your linking idea (rejection, trauma, or indifference) before you start writing, and make sure you analyse language, form, and structure in both poems rather than simply describing each one in turn.</p>
