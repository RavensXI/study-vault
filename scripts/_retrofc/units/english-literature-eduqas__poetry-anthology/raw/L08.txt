--- DESCRIPTION ---
A photograph of a Bombay street sleeper re-examined — the ethics of turning poverty into art.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Decomposition &mdash; Ghose&rsquo;s Photograph of a Bombay Pavement</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Zulfikar Ghose&rsquo;s poem <em>Decomposition</em> describes a photograph the speaker once took in Bombay. It shows an old, grey-haired beggar asleep on a pavement. Years later, looking back at the photograph, the speaker starts to question his own motives for taking it &mdash; and for once calling it art. The poem moves from confident description to uneasy confession, and that movement is the key to understanding it.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Story of the Poem</h2><p data-narration-id="n4">The speaker describes the beggar in unsettling detail: &ldquo;grey-haired, wearing shorts and a dirty shirt,&rdquo; his &ldquo;shadow thrown aside like a blanket.&rdquo; A crowd passes behind him, &ldquo;bemused by a pavement trickster&rdquo; but &ldquo;indifferent to this very common sight&rdquo; of an old man asleep on the street. Poverty here is so ordinary that nobody but the photographer stops to look twice &mdash; and even he, at first, looks only as an artist searching for a striking image.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the two phrases that turn the beggar into stone or fossil."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Ghose uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Language that strips a person of human qualities by comparing them to objects, animals or landscape features.">dehumanising imagery</dfn> to describe the beggar: his limbs are &ldquo;cracks in the stone,&rdquo; and he &ldquo;lies veined into stone, a fossil man.&rdquo; These images turn a living person into landscape and geology &mdash; something ancient, fixed and no longer quite human.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n6">Poet and Context</h2><p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A poet, novelist and critic who grew up in colonial India before settling in Britain and later the United States; his poetry often examines how observers represent other people&rsquo;s suffering.">Zulfikar Ghose</dfn> writes about the uneasy relationship between an artist and the people that artist represents &mdash; especially when the artist comes from comfort and the subject does not. <em>Decomposition</em> puts this question at its centre: is photographing poverty a form of witness, or a form of theft? The poem never fully answers this, which is part of its honesty.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n8">Themes: Looking, Guilt and the Presumption of Art</h2><p data-narration-id="n9">For most of the poem, the speaker sounds pleased with his work. He calls the photograph &ldquo;a good composition&rdquo; and gives it a title, &ldquo;The Man in the Street,&rdquo; treating the beggar as a typical example of India rather than as an individual. But in the final stanza, the speaker&rsquo;s tone changes completely. He now notices the beggar&rsquo;s face &mdash; &ldquo;his head in the posture of one weeping into a pillow&rdquo; &mdash; and admits his own &ldquo;presumption at attempting to compose art of his hunger and solitude.&rdquo; This shift, from self-satisfaction to guilt, is the poem&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="A turn in a poem&rsquo;s argument, tone or perspective, often marking a shift in the speaker&rsquo;s understanding.">volta</dfn>.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the pun in the title 'Decomposition' in your own words."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The title <em>Decomposition</em> puns on two meanings at once: the artistic &lsquo;composition&rsquo; of a photograph, and the physical decomposition &mdash; decay &mdash; of a body left to suffer on the street. By choosing this title, Ghose suggests that turning a person&rsquo;s suffering into an artistic composition is itself a kind of violence done to them.</p></div><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="After reading, list three images that dehumanise the beggar and explain what each one takes away from him."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Language and Imagery in Decomposition</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n11">The poem&rsquo;s imagery works to strip the beggar of individuality before restoring it. Early on, he is compared to landscape and fossil &mdash; &ldquo;cracks in the stone,&rdquo; a &ldquo;fossil man&rdquo; &mdash; and described as &ldquo;Brain-washed by the sun into exhaustion.&rdquo; The insects that might crawl over him are given &ldquo;routes for the ants&rsquo; journeys, the flies&rsquo; descents,&rdquo; as though his body were terrain rather than a person. Only in the final four lines does this <dfn class="term" data-def="Treating a person as an object or a typical example rather than as an individual with feelings.">objectification</dfn> reverse, as the speaker finally pictures the man&rsquo;s grief.</p><p data-narration-id="n12">Ghose writes in a plain, documentary voice, close to the tone of someone captioning a photograph. This flat, matter-of-fact style makes the final confession &mdash; that his &ldquo;good composition&rdquo; was really a &ldquo;presumption&rdquo; &mdash; land with more force, because it breaks so sharply from the detached tone that came before.</p></div></div></div><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain in one sentence how the structure of the poem mirrors the way memory changes over time."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Form and Structure</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n13">The poem is built from five four-line stanzas (quatrains), and the argument develops steadily across them: the description of the photograph, the dehumanising stone and fossil imagery, the indifferent crowd, the speaker&rsquo;s original pride in his &lsquo;good composition&rsquo;, and finally his present-day reconsideration. This structure mirrors how memory works &mdash; the speaker is not simply describing an old photograph, he is re-examining it, and his understanding changes stanza by stanza. The final image, of the beggar&rsquo;s head &ldquo;in the posture of one weeping into a pillow,&rdquo; only becomes visible to the speaker, and to the reader, once his guilt has surfaced in that last quatrain.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n14">Comparing Decomposition with Other Anthology Poems</h2><p data-narration-id="n15"><em>Decomposition</em> pairs naturally with Carol Ann Duffy&rsquo;s <em>War Photographer</em>. Both poems are told from the point of view of someone who turns other people&rsquo;s suffering into an image, and both end with guilt breaking through a professional, detached tone. Duffy&rsquo;s photographer compares his darkroom to a church, himself to &ldquo;a priest preparing to intone a Mass&rdquo;; Ghose&rsquo;s speaker admits his &ldquo;presumption&rdquo; at composing &ldquo;art&rdquo; from a stranger&rsquo;s &ldquo;hunger.&rdquo; Both poets ask whether recording suffering can ever be separated from exploiting it.</p><p data-narration-id="n16">The poem also connects to Wilfred Owen&rsquo;s <em>Disabled</em>, which shows a wounded soldier reduced, by other people&rsquo;s eyes, from a person to an object &mdash; girls&rsquo; hands &ldquo;touch him like some queer disease.&rdquo; Like Ghose&rsquo;s beggar, Owen&rsquo;s soldier is looked at rather than truly seen, until each poem forces the reader to notice what that looking costs him.</p><p data-narration-id="n17">A third pairing works with Simon Armitage&rsquo;s <em>Remains</em>, where a soldier is haunted by an image he cannot forget &mdash; the looter he shot, who &ldquo;bursts again through the doors of the bank&rdquo; in his mind. Both <em>Remains</em> and <em>Decomposition</em> show a single image returning to disturb its creator or witness long after the original moment has passed.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n18">Why This Poem Matters for the Exam</h2><p data-narration-id="n19">If <em>Decomposition</em> is the printed poem on your paper, focus on the shift in the speaker&rsquo;s attitude &mdash; from pride in his &ldquo;good composition&rdquo; to guilt at his &ldquo;presumption.&rdquo; Track how the imagery of stone, fossil and weeping supports that change. If you are choosing a comparison poem, <em>War Photographer</em> is the strongest match, because both poems interrogate the ethics of representing suffering as art; <em>Disabled</em> and <em>Remains</em> also work well if the question focuses on being watched, or on an image that will not leave the mind.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Ghose&rsquo;s speaker moves from pride in his &ldquo;good composition&rdquo; to guilt at his &ldquo;presumption,&rdquo; and this shift is the poem&rsquo;s volta.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Dehumanising imagery &mdash; &ldquo;cracks in the stone,&rdquo; a &ldquo;fossil man&rdquo; &mdash; turns the beggar into landscape before the ending restores his humanity.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">The title puns on artistic composition and physical decomposition, and the poem pairs best with &lsquo;War Photographer,&rsquo; &lsquo;Disabled&rsquo; and &lsquo;Remains.&rsquo;</li></ul>

For the comparison question, spend equal time on both poems and compare methods directly &mdash; for example, explain how Ghose&rsquo;s dehumanising stone imagery and Duffy&rsquo;s priest simile in &lsquo;War Photographer&rsquo; both expose an artist&rsquo;s discomfort, rather than describing each poem separately. Always quote the exact short phrase, in quotation marks, and explain the effect of specific word choices such as &lsquo;fossil&rsquo; or &lsquo;presumption.&rsquo;</p>
