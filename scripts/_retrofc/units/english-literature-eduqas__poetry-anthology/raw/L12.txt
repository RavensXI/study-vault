--- DESCRIPTION ---
Duffy's photographer relives war's horrors in a darkroom, exposing guilt and public indifference.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">War Photographer &mdash; Story and Context</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Carol Ann Duffy&rsquo;s <em>War Photographer</em> is set entirely in one place: a photographer&rsquo;s darkroom in England, days after he returns from a warzone. As he develops his photographs, the images force him to relive what he witnessed. The poem moves between the safety of &ldquo;Rural England&rdquo; and the horror of the conflict zones he has just left, exposing the gap between comfortable domestic life and the suffering he has recorded on film.</p><p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Scottish poet who became the first female Poet Laureate in 2009 and served until 2019, known for dramatic monologues that give voice to characters outside her own experience.">Carol Ann Duffy</dfn> published <em>War Photographer</em> in her 1985 collection <em>Standing Female Nude</em>. She was inspired by real photojournalists who covered conflicts such as the Troubles in Belfast, the Lebanese Civil War in Beirut, and the aftermath in Phnom Penh &mdash; all three named directly in the poem&rsquo;s sixth line.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why Duffy compares the darkroom to a church and the photographer to a priest."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Duffy structures the darkroom as a kind of church. The photographer works &ldquo;as though this were a church and he,&rdquo; like &ldquo;a priest preparing to intone a Mass.&rdquo; This <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended comparison between two unlike things developed over several lines or an entire poem.">extended metaphor</dfn> of religious ritual suggests he is performing a solemn duty &mdash; but it also hints that photography has replaced faith as the only order he can impose on a violent world.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n5">Themes: Suffering, Duty and Indifference</h2><p data-narration-id="n6">The poem&rsquo;s central tension is between the photographer&rsquo;s memory of real horror and the public&rsquo;s brief, shallow reaction to it. Duffy uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The placing of two contrasting ideas or images close together to highlight the difference between them.">juxtaposition</dfn> throughout: the calm of &ldquo;Rural England&rdquo; against &ldquo;fields which don&rsquo;t explode beneath the feet,&rdquo; and the photographer&rsquo;s trauma against an editor who will &ldquo;pick out five or six&rdquo; images &ldquo;for Sunday&rsquo;s supplement.&rdquo; War is reduced to a commercial product, packaged for a Sunday newspaper.</p><p data-narration-id="n7">Duffy is also interested in guilt and detachment. The photographer&rsquo;s hands &ldquo;did not tremble then&rdquo; but &ldquo;seem to now&rdquo; &mdash; his trauma surfaces only in the safety of the darkroom, delayed and displaced. The closing image shows readers&rsquo; &ldquo;eyeballs prick with tears&rdquo; &ldquo;between the bath and pre-lunch beers,&rdquo; a moment of fleeting, comfortable sympathy that vanishes as quickly as it appears. Duffy criticises this casual consumption of other people&rsquo;s suffering.</p><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="After reading, explain how the poem's four six-line stanzas mirror the photographer's need for order."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Form and Structure of War Photographer</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n8">Duffy writes the poem in four six-line stanzas of similar length, giving it a controlled, orderly shape. This regularity mirrors the photographer&rsquo;s attempt to impose order on chaotic, traumatic memories through his methodical darkroom process. Occasional rhymes break through the loose structure &mdash; &ldquo;rows&rdquo; and &ldquo;glows&rdquo; in the first stanza, &ldquo;then&rdquo; and &ldquo;again&rdquo; in the second &mdash; creating brief moments of formal control that echo his need for ritual.</p><p data-narration-id="n9">Duffy also uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Continuing a sentence or clause without a pause beyond the end of a line, so meaning flows into the next line.">enjambment</dfn> to disrupt this order, especially where the photographer&rsquo;s composure breaks down: &ldquo;did not tremble then&rdquo; spills across the line break into &ldquo;seem to now,&rdquo; mimicking the delayed shock he is trying to contain. Short, fragmented sentences &mdash; &ldquo;Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh.&rdquo; &mdash; read like a news report, stripping the place names of context, just as the media strips suffering of its human meaning.</p></div></div></div><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall how many images get published, and the ambiguous word that ends the poem."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The photographer has captured &ldquo;a hundred agonies in black and white,&rdquo; but only &ldquo;five or six&rdquo; images will ever be published. Duffy exposes how the media filters mass suffering into a handful of digestible pictures. The final line, where he &ldquo;stares impassively&rdquo; at a place where &ldquo;they do not care,&rdquo; leaves it unclear whether &ldquo;they&rdquo; means the newspaper&rsquo;s readers or the suffering people themselves.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n11">Key Language and Imagery</h2><p data-narration-id="n12">The line &ldquo;All flesh is grass&rdquo; echoes a well-known biblical phrase about mortality, placed directly after the list of place names. It universalises the individual horrors of Belfast, Beirut and Phnom Penh into one statement about how fragile all human life is. Duffy also plays on a dark pun: &ldquo;Solutions slop in trays&rdquo; refers literally to photographic developing chemicals, but the word &ldquo;solutions&rdquo; also gestures ironically toward political or military solutions that never actually stop the suffering.</p><p data-narration-id="n13">Duffy&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="Repetition of soft consonant sounds, especially the 's' sound, to create a hushed or hissing effect.">sibilance</dfn> in phrases like &ldquo;Solutions slop&rdquo; and &ldquo;spools of suffering&rdquo; creates a hushed, secretive tone, appropriate for the quiet, controlled space of the darkroom. This softness contrasts sharply with the violence the photographs actually show, reinforcing the gap between the calm surface of the photographer&rsquo;s work and the horror underneath it.</p><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name one anthology poem that pairs with 'War Photographer' and explain the shared idea."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Comparison Pairings for the Exam</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Decomposition (Zulfikar Ghose):</strong> This is the strongest pairing. Both poems feature a man who photographs another person&rsquo;s suffering and later feels guilty about turning it into art. Ghose&rsquo;s speaker admits his &ldquo;presumption at attempting to compose&rdquo; art from a stranger&rsquo;s hunger, directly matching Duffy&rsquo;s photographer&rsquo;s uneasy relationship with his own profession.</p><p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Remains (Simon Armitage):</strong> Both poems explore how a man carries the trauma of violence home with him and cannot leave it behind. Armitage&rsquo;s soldier is haunted by a killing that &ldquo;bursts again through the doors&rdquo; of his memory, just as Duffy&rsquo;s photographer&rsquo;s hands &ldquo;seem to&rdquo; tremble only once he is safely back in England.</p><p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Disabled (Wilfred Owen):</strong> Both poems contrast a comfortable home setting with the horror of war. Owen&rsquo;s soldier can only listen to &ldquo;voices of play and pleasure&rdquo; he can no longer join; Duffy&rsquo;s photographer watches readers turn from &ldquo;a hundred agonies&rdquo; back to their ordinary lives without a second thought.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n17">Using This Poem in the Exam</h2><p data-narration-id="n18">If <em>War Photographer</em> is the printed poem, focus on the darkroom setting, the religious extended metaphor, and the criticism of media indifference. If you are comparing it to another poem, choose one of the pairings above and focus your comparison on a shared idea &mdash; guilt, the gap between home and conflict, or the difficulty of representing suffering &mdash; rather than just listing similar techniques.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Duffy sets the whole poem in a darkroom, using the extended metaphor of a church and Mass to show the photographer treating his work as a solemn ritual.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">The poem criticises how the media reduces &ldquo;a hundred agonies&rdquo; into a handful of published images, and how readers&rsquo; sympathy fades quickly &mdash; &ldquo;between the bath and pre-lunch beers.&rdquo;</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Pair this poem with <em>Decomposition</em>, <em>Remains</em>, or <em>Disabled</em> for the comparison question, focusing on guilt, trauma, or the contrast between home and conflict.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When the exam gives you <em>War Photographer</em> as the printed poem, spend the first few minutes annotating the religious imagery and the shift between England and the warzones &mdash; this material supports both close language analysis and a personal response to the poem&rsquo;s ideas. For the comparison question, always compare methods as well as ideas: don&rsquo;t just say both poems are &ldquo;about guilt&rdquo; &mdash; explain how each poet&rsquo;s form, structure or imagery creates that guilt differently.</p>
