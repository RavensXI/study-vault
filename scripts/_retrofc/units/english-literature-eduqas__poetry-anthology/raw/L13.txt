<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dusting the Phone &mdash; Jackie Kay</h2><p data-narration-id="n2">Jackie Kay&rsquo;s &ldquo;Dusting the Phone&rdquo; captures the agony of waiting for a lover to call. Written as a single, spiralling monologue, the poem takes place entirely inside the speaker&rsquo;s head as she swings between hope and dread. The named poem is printed on the exam paper, so for the specified-poem question you can quote directly from it; for the comparison question no text is given beyond a list of titles, so you must learn short quotations from your chosen second poem and know which poems pair well with this one.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Poem&rsquo;s Situation</h2><p data-narration-id="n4">The poem opens with brutal honesty. The speaker admits she is &ldquo;imagining the worst that could happen&rdquo;, even though she knows this is &ldquo;not a good idea&rdquo;. She recognises that being in love should mean dwelling on &ldquo;the best that has been happening&rdquo; instead, but her anxious mind will not cooperate. This gap between what she should feel and what she actually feels sets up the poem&rsquo;s central tension.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why both a ringing phone and a silent phone mean &lsquo;disaster&rsquo; for the speaker."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Kay builds anxiety through repetition and contradiction. Whether the phone rings or stays silent, the outcome feels equally threatening: the phone rings &ldquo;heralding some disaster&rdquo;, but &ldquo;it doesn&rsquo;t ring which also means disaster&rdquo; too &mdash; both possibilities end in the single word &ldquo;Sirens&rdquo;, repeated twice. The speaker cannot escape her fear whatever happens.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n6">The uncertainty spreads beyond the phone itself. The speaker imagines a chaotic set of possible futures, described through a comma-less list: &ldquo;A marriage. A full house.&rdquo; and time spent &ldquo;in stranger&rsquo;s white sheets&rdquo;. She pictures &ldquo;the future&rdquo; as &ldquo;a long gloved hand&rdquo; and &ldquo;an empty cup&rdquo; &mdash; strange, ominous images with no clear meaning, mirroring racing thoughts that jump from one possibility to the next without pause.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n7">Jackie Kay &mdash; Poet and Context</h2><p data-narration-id="n8">Jackie Kay (born 1961) is a Scottish poet and novelist. She served as the National Poet for Scotland, known as the Makar, between 2016 and 2021. Kay&rsquo;s poetry is known for its warm, conversational voice and its honest exploration of relationships, identity and belonging. &ldquo;Dusting the Phone&rdquo; uses this same direct, everyday language to explore an experience many readers recognise: the powerlessness of waiting for someone to make contact.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n9">Themes: Obsession and the Fragility of Modern Love</h2><p data-narration-id="n10">The poem is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem spoken entirely in the voice of one character, revealing their private thoughts and feelings as if overheard.">dramatic monologue</dfn>, spoken by the anxious lover from start to finish. Kay explores how love can tip into obsession: the speaker admits &ldquo;I am trapped in it. I can&rsquo;t move.&rdquo; and wants her lover &ldquo;all the time&rdquo;. These short, blunt sentences contrast with the poem&rsquo;s earlier long, breathless lines, capturing how obsessive thoughts can suddenly stop the speaker in her tracks.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the two things the speaker does to the phone that make it seem like a religious object."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The telephone becomes an object of near-religious devotion. The speaker calls her care of it &ldquo;silver service&rdquo;, then says &ldquo;I polish it. I dress for it.&rdquo; This <dfn class="term" data-def="A sustained comparison developed across several lines, using one image or object to stand for a larger idea.">extended metaphor</dfn> of ceremonial preparation shows how far normal behaviour has been distorted by longing &mdash; an ordinary household object has become an altar.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n12">Elsewhere the speaker&rsquo;s desperation becomes almost comic and unsettling: she &ldquo;assault[s] the postman for a letter&rdquo; and &ldquo;look[s] for flowers&rdquo;, desperate for any sign of affection. Even unwanted contact is agony &mdash; &ldquo;infuriatingly, it sends me hoaxes, wrong numbers&rdquo; &mdash; because every call that is not from her lover is a fresh disappointment. When her lover&rsquo;s voice does come, it &ldquo;disappears into my lonely cotton sheets&rdquo;, suggesting that even contact cannot fully satisfy her loneliness.</p><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Form and Structure of Dusting the Phone</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n13">Kay writes in <dfn class="term" data-def="Poetry without a regular rhyme scheme or metre, allowing rhythms to follow natural speech patterns.">free verse</dfn>, avoiding rhyme or a fixed rhythm so the poem sounds like a spontaneous, spiralling stream of thought. Frequent <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuation of a sentence or clause across a line break without punctuation, creating flow or urgency.">enjambment</dfn> &mdash; for example &ldquo;Your voice / disappears into my lonely cotton sheets&rdquo; and &ldquo;One night per week / in stranger&rsquo;s white sheets&rdquo; &mdash; mimics thoughts tumbling over one another without control, while the clipped, end-stopped fragments on a single line (&ldquo;The future is a long gloved hand. An empty cup.&rdquo;) jolt the reader from one image to the next. Kay also uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A list of words or phrases with the connecting words, such as &lsquo;and&rsquo;, deliberately left out.">asyndeton</dfn> in short, unlinked statements like &ldquo;A marriage. A full house.&rdquo;, speeding up the pace and suggesting panic. The poem is structurally circular: it opens with the speaker imagining disaster and ends with an unanswered demand &mdash; &ldquo;Come on, damn you, ring me&rdquo; &mdash; followed by &ldquo;I don&rsquo;t know what&rdquo;, showing that her anxious cycle has no resolution.</p></div></div></div><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Comparison Pairings for Dusting the Phone</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n14">&ldquo;Dusting the Phone&rdquo; pairs well with Elizabeth Barrett Browning&rsquo;s &ldquo;Sonnet 29&rdquo;, because both poems show a speaker consumed by obsessive thoughts about a lover. Browning&rsquo;s speaker admits her thoughts &ldquo;twine and bud&rdquo; around her lover like wild vines, wanting his real presence rather than her imagination of him. Kay&rsquo;s speaker is similarly trapped by her own thoughts, though her tone is far more anxious than Browning&rsquo;s calm confidence &mdash; a useful contrast for the comparison question.</p><p data-narration-id="n15">The poem also compares well with Christina Rossetti&rsquo;s &ldquo;Cousin Kate&rdquo;, since both present women left powerless by love. Rossetti&rsquo;s speaker has been abandoned by &ldquo;a great lord&rdquo; and can only &ldquo;howl in dust&rdquo; over her situation, while Kay&rsquo;s speaker waits helplessly for a phone call she cannot control. Both poems capture how love can strip a woman of agency, though Rossetti&rsquo;s speaker suffers a public betrayal while Kay&rsquo;s speaker&rsquo;s suffering is private and psychological.</p></div></div></div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Kay uses free verse, enjambment and asyndeton to mimic anxious, racing thoughts about an absent lover.</li><li data-narration-id="n19">The telephone becomes an extended metaphor for obsession &mdash; the speaker &ldquo;polishes&rdquo; and &ldquo;dresses&rdquo; for it as if it were sacred.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The poem pairs well with &ldquo;Sonnet 29&rdquo; (obsessive love) and &ldquo;Cousin Kate&rdquo; (powerlessness in love) for the comparison question.</li></ul>
