--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Eve L. Ewing turns her parents' first meeting into a comic-book myth about love, identity and memory.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Origin Story &mdash; Love as a Myth We Tell Ourselves</h2><p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Origin Story</em> tells the tale of how the speaker&rsquo;s parents met &mdash; not as a plain memory, but as a myth shaped like a comic book. The poem opens with the words &ldquo;This is true&rdquo;, immediately raising the question of what &ldquo;true&rdquo; means when a family story has been retold so many times it starts to feel invented. Eve L. Ewing borrows the structure of a comic-book origin story to explore how families turn ordinary beginnings into legend.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">Poet and Context</h2><p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="Eve L. Ewing is a contemporary American poet, sociologist and writer based in Chicago, known for exploring identity, family and community history in her work.">Eve L. Ewing</dfn> sets the poem in Chicago in the mid-1980s, where her parents &ldquo;met at the Greyhound bus station&rdquo;. The poem borrows the language of comic books &mdash; an <dfn class="term" data-def="A comic-book genre convention explaining how a hero first gained their powers or purpose, often treated as a foundational legend.">origin story</dfn> in comics explains how a hero began. By titling the poem &ldquo;Origin Story&rdquo;, Ewing treats her parents&rsquo; meeting with the same reverence a comic gives a superhero&rsquo;s first transformation, elevating an everyday event into something mythic.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three details does Ewing give about her mother, and what three does she give about her father?"><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Ewing individualises her parents through vivid, contrasting details. Her mother is &ldquo;thick glass and afro puff&rdquo;, came west by train at nineteen, and &ldquo;played tambourine in a Chaka Khan cover band&rdquo;. Her father is &ldquo;sleeveless and soft eye&rdquo;, &ldquo;ran away from home&rdquo; at seventeen, and &ldquo;mimeographed communist newspapers&rdquo;. These specific, unglamorous facts root the myth in real working lives before the poem turns to comic-book imagery.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n6">The turning point of the story is small and ordinary: her father sold comic books he had drawn himself for &ldquo;one dollar&rdquo;, and &ldquo;my mother bought one&rdquo;. This single transaction becomes the seed of the whole family. Ewing lets the everyday detail carry enormous weight &mdash; love begins not with fireworks but with a dollar and a drawing.</p><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="After reading, explain in your own words why Ewing shifts from narrating a story to arguing a metaphor halfway through the poem."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Form and Structure of Origin Story</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n7">Ewing writes in <dfn class="term" data-def="Poetry without a regular rhyme scheme or metre, using natural speech rhythms instead.">free verse</dfn>, with no rhyme and irregular line lengths, giving the poem a conversational, storytelling voice. Frequent <dfn class="term" data-def="The running of a sentence or idea from one line of poetry to the next without a pause.">enjambment</dfn> lets sentences spill across lines &mdash; the phrase &ldquo;never meant to last&rdquo; flows straight out of the line before it, mirroring how a story keeps unfolding rather than stopping neatly. The poem has two movements: first, the narrative of the parents&rsquo; meeting; second, an extended reflection on what that story means for love itself. The shift is marked by the line &ldquo;love is like a comic book&rdquo;, where narrative turns into argument.</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n8">Themes: Fragility, Memory and Identity</h2><p data-narration-id="n9">The opening claim &ldquo;This is true&rdquo; sets up a tension the whole poem explores: is this history, or is it myth? Ewing does not resolve the question. Instead, she shows that families need myths &mdash; polished, retold versions of ordinary events &mdash; to hold their history together. The comic-book framing suggests that identity itself can be an <dfn class="term" data-def="An extended comparison between two unlike things developed over several lines or an entire poem.">extended metaphor</dfn>: something drawn, collected and passed down rather than simply lived.</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence how the comic book metaphor captures both love's fragility and its endurance."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>Ewing extends the comic-book metaphor across several lines: &ldquo;love is like a comic book. it&rsquo;s fragile&rdquo;, and later, more starkly, &ldquo;love is paper&rdquo;. A cherished comic gets &ldquo;curled into a back pocket&rdquo; and &ldquo;read under covers&rdquo; until it is &ldquo;worn thin, staples rusted&rdquo;. Love, like the comic, survives only through careful, imperfect handling &mdash; and even when it wears out, the poem insists it &ldquo;has a good ending&rdquo;.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n11">Cultural identity runs quietly through the poem&rsquo;s details. The mother&rsquo;s &ldquo;afro puff&rdquo; and her place in a &ldquo;Chaka Khan cover band&rdquo; root the story in Black American culture and music, while the father&rsquo;s &ldquo;mimeographed communist newspapers&rdquo; hint at a countercultural, politically engaged background. These small, specific facts matter because they resist the glossy simplicity of an actual comic-book hero&rsquo;s backstory &mdash; Ewing&rsquo;s parents are real, complicated people before they become characters in her origin myth.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n12">Comparisons with Other Anthology Poems</h2><div class="collapsible" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name one anthology poem that shares Ewing's use of extended metaphor and explain the shared technique."><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Which Poems Pair Well with Origin Story?</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Catrin</strong> by Gillian Clarke also uses an extended metaphor for a family bond &mdash; the &ldquo;red rope of love&rdquo; connecting mother and daughter, echoing their &ldquo;fierce confrontation&rdquo;. Both poems show love as something physical and imperfect rather than simply sentimental, though Clarke focuses on ongoing conflict while Ewing looks back at how love began.</p><p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Sonnet 29</strong> by Elizabeth Barrett Browning uses a different extended metaphor for love &mdash; the speaker&rsquo;s thoughts as &ldquo;wild vines, about a tree&rdquo;, with the beloved as the tree (&ldquo;O my palm-tree&rdquo;) &mdash; to explore presence and absence. Where Browning wants her lover physically present rather than just imagined, Ewing is happy for her parents&rsquo; story to exist partly as imagined myth, showing two different attitudes to how love should be represented in words.</p><p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Kamikaze</strong> by Beatrice Garland also retells a parent&rsquo;s history to the next generation, &ldquo;recounting it later to her children&rdquo;. Both poems explore how family stories are passed down and reshaped by the people who tell them, though Garland&rsquo;s story carries the weight of shame and silence, while Ewing&rsquo;s carries warmth and humour.</p></div></div></div><p data-narration-id="n16">For the comparison question, <em>Origin Story</em> works well whenever the specified poem also uses an extended metaphor to explore love or family, or whenever a poem explores how personal or family history gets retold across generations. Examiners reward candidates who can identify the shared method &mdash; metaphor, narrative retelling, or structural shift from story to reflection &mdash; rather than just noting a shared topic.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Ewing frames her parents&rsquo; meeting as a comic-book &ldquo;origin story&rdquo;, blurring the line between true memory and family myth.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">The extended metaphor of love as a fragile, well-worn comic book runs through the poem&rsquo;s second half, from &ldquo;it&rsquo;s fragile&rdquo; to &ldquo;a good ending&rdquo;.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Free verse and enjambment give the poem a conversational, storytelling voice that suits its theme of retelling family history.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">When the comparison question pairs &ldquo;Origin Story&rdquo; with another poem, do not just say both poems are &ldquo;about love&rdquo; &mdash; compare the actual methods. Ewing builds her argument through an extended metaphor (comic book as love) layered onto a narrative structure, and short quotations like &ldquo;love is paper&rdquo; or &ldquo;worn thin, staples rusted&rdquo; are easy to memorise and analyse precisely. Remember that one anthology poem is printed on the paper, but the poem you choose to compare it with is not &mdash; so practise recalling two or three short, exact quotations for each poem you might choose, rather than vague paraphrases &mdash; examiners reward accurate embedded quotation far more than a general summary of the poem&rsquo;s content.</p>
