--- DESCRIPTION ---
Armitage's soldier haunted by a looter he shot in 'Remains' — guilt, trauma and memory that will not stay behind.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Remains &mdash; Context and the Soldier&rsquo;s Voice</h2><p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Simon Armitage (born 1963), the current Poet Laureate, known for accessible, colloquial poetry that explores ordinary lives and extraordinary circumstances.">Simon Armitage</dfn> based &lsquo;Remains&rsquo; on real testimony from a soldier interviewed for <em>The Not Dead</em>, a documentary poetry project exploring the psychological damage that war leaves on veterans of conflicts including Bosnia, the Gulf War and Iraq. Rather than imagining war from a distance, Armitage worked directly from what the soldier told him, keeping close to the rhythms of everyday speech. The poem is written as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A poem in which a single speaker addresses a listener, revealing their character and state of mind through what they say.">dramatic monologue</dfn>, giving voice to a soldier who cannot escape one particular killing.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Poem&rsquo;s Story</h2><p data-narration-id="n4">The soldier describes being sent to stop looters raiding a bank. One man runs, &ldquo;probably armed, possibly not,&rdquo; and the soldier and two colleagues open fire: &ldquo;Well myself and somebody else and somebody else&rdquo; all shoot together. The looter is left &ldquo;sort of inside out,&rdquo; and a colleague &ldquo;tosses his guts back into his body&rdquo; before he is &ldquo;carted off in the back of a lorry.&rdquo; Armitage signals immediately that this will not be a simple war story: &ldquo;End of story, except not really.&rdquo;</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the phrase describing the looter's uncertain threat, and explain why it returns later in an altered form."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The phrase &ldquo;probably armed, possibly not&rdquo; appears early in the poem and returns later in a slightly altered form during the soldier&rsquo;s sleepless nights: &ldquo;he&rsquo;s probably armed, and possibly not.&rdquo; The comma splitting the phrase is a stark <dfn class="term" data-def="A grammatical pause or break within a line of poetry, often marked by punctuation.">caesura</dfn> that mirrors the soldier&rsquo;s own uncertainty &mdash; he never knows for sure that the looter was a genuine threat, and that doubt is what haunts him.</p></div><p data-narration-id="n6">The looter&rsquo;s death refuses to stay in the past. His &ldquo;blood-shadow stays on the street,&rdquo; and the soldier walks over it &ldquo;week after week&rdquo; while on patrol. Even at home, memory intrudes: he blinks and the man &ldquo;bursts again through the doors of the bank.&rdquo; Armitage uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The continuation of a sentence beyond the end of a line of poetry without a pause, keeping the sense flowing forward.">enjambment</dfn> to spill one memory into the next, showing how the soldier&rsquo;s mind cannot contain or control the trauma.</p><h2 data-narration-id="n7">Themes: Guilt and a Trauma That Will Not Leave</h2><p data-narration-id="n8">The soldier cannot separate what he did on duty from what he carries home. Sleep brings no rest: &ldquo;Sleep, and he&rsquo;s probably armed, and possibly not. / Dream, and he&rsquo;s torn apart by a dozen rounds.&rdquo; Even &ldquo;the drink and the drugs&rdquo; fail to remove the memory. The repeated present participles and unfinished thoughts show a mind stuck in replay, unable to reach any conclusion or resolution. The poem&rsquo;s final line collapses past and present, killer and victim, into one image: &ldquo;his bloody life in my bloody hands.&rdquo;</p><div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the two meanings of 'bloody' in the poem's final line."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div><p>The poem ends &ldquo;his bloody life in my bloody hands,&rdquo; repeating the word &ldquo;bloody&rdquo; as both a swear word of frustration and a literal description of blood on his hands. This <dfn class="term" data-def="The repetition of a word or short phrase at intervals throughout a poem for emphasis.">refrain</dfn>-like repetition captures how ordinary language cannot hold the weight of what he has done.</p></div><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Form and Structure of Remains</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n10">Armitage writes in loose, unrhymed stanzas that mimic natural speech, reflecting the poem&rsquo;s roots in a real soldier&rsquo;s spoken testimony. The tone is <dfn class="term" data-def="Everyday, informal language typical of ordinary speech.">colloquial</dfn> &mdash; phrases like &ldquo;Well myself and somebody else and somebody else&rdquo; and &ldquo;Three of a kind all letting fly&rdquo; sound like an unscripted, halting account rather than crafted poetry. This makes the horror feel closer to a confession than to literature.</p><p data-narration-id="n11">The structure moves through time &mdash; from the shooting, to daily patrols, to being &ldquo;home on leave,&rdquo; to sleepless nights &mdash; showing that trauma does not stay contained to the battlefield. The final stanza drops all distance and addresses the moment directly: &ldquo;near to the knuckle, here and now.&rdquo;</p></div></div></div><div class="collapsible"><button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false"><span>Comparison Pairings for Remains</span><svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg></button><div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p data-narration-id="n12">&lsquo;Remains&rsquo; pairs naturally with <strong>Kamikaze</strong> by Beatrice Garland. Both poems explore how a single act connected to war leaves lasting damage &mdash; the kamikaze pilot lives with the shame of returning alive, while Armitage&rsquo;s soldier lives with the guilt of a killing he cannot forget. 'Kamikaze' ends by suggesting death might have been easier than the living death of rejection &mdash; 'he must have wondered / which had been the better way to die' &mdash; while 'Remains' ends instead on inescapable personal guilt: 'his bloody life in my bloody hands.'</p><p data-narration-id="n13">It also compares well with <strong>War Photographer</strong> by Carol Ann Duffy. Both speakers are trained to face violence &mdash; a soldier, a photographer &mdash; yet both are shown struggling to process what they have witnessed once they return to ordinary life. Duffy&rsquo;s photographer is left staring back at his own memories; Armitage&rsquo;s soldier cannot stop the looter bursting back into his.</p><p data-narration-id="n14">A third pairing is <strong>Disabled</strong> by Wilfred Owen. Both poems show men permanently changed by conflict, but they handle time differently: Owen contrasts his soldier's present with an ordinary past of football and dances before his injury, while Armitage's soldier has no such refuge &mdash; the killing follows him from patrol ('out on patrol / I walk right over it week after week') into life at home ('Then I'm home on leave. But I blink').</p></div></div></div><h2 data-narration-id="n15">Why This Matters for the Exam</h2><p data-narration-id="n16">For the specified-poem question, focus on how the caesura in &ldquo;probably armed, possibly not,&rdquo; the enjambment carrying memories forward, and the final repeated &ldquo;bloody&rdquo; work together to show guilt intruding into everyday life. For the comparison question, use <em>Kamikaze</em>, <em>War Photographer</em> or <em>Disabled</em> to discuss how different poets explore the long-term psychological cost of conflict, and note that Armitage&rsquo;s colloquial, dramatic-monologue form is distinctive &mdash; it makes trauma sound like unfiltered testimony rather than crafted verse.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n19">&lsquo;Remains&rsquo; is a dramatic monologue based on real soldier testimony, using colloquial language and caesura to show doubt and guilt.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Enjambment and repeated phrases (&ldquo;probably armed, possibly not&rdquo;) show memory intruding uncontrollably into the present.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">The final line, &ldquo;his bloody life in my bloody hands,&rdquo; fuses guilt and literal violence, and pairs well with Kamikaze, War Photographer or Disabled in the comparison question.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">This is a closed-book exam, so memorise short, exact quotations rather than whole lines &mdash; &ldquo;probably armed, possibly not,&rdquo; &ldquo;sort of inside out&rdquo; and &ldquo;bloody life in my bloody hands&rdquo; are all compact enough to recall precisely under pressure. If &lsquo;Remains&rsquo; is the specified poem, spend real time on form and structure, not just content &mdash; examiners reward comment on caesura, enjambment and the colloquial voice. If it appears in the comparison question, choose a partner poem you know well (Kamikaze, War Photographer or Disabled) and plan your comparison around shared methods, not just shared subject matter.</p>
