Brownfield and greenfield development, and Egan's wheel for sustainable communities.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Makes a City Sustainable?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A place where people want to live now and in the future, meeting present needs without harming opportunities for future residents.">sustainable community</dfn> is a place that meets the needs of today’s residents without making life harder for the people who will live there in fifty years. UK towns and cities face three pressures at once — population growth, climate change and the need to cut carbon emissions. Building sustainably means tackling all three together rather than one at a time.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Egan’s Wheel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In 2004 Sir John Egan led a government review into how planners could build neighbourhoods that last. The result was <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram by Sir John Egan setting out eight components of a sustainable community, used in UK planning since 2004.">Egan’s wheel</dfn> — a diagram with eight spokes, each one a component a sustainable community must include.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Eight Spokes of Egan’s Wheel</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Governance</strong> — effective local representation, with residents able to influence decisions. <strong>Transport</strong> — good public transport, walking and cycling routes that cut car use. <strong>Services</strong> — nearby schools, GPs, shops and leisure within walking distance. <strong>Environment</strong> — clean air, green space and low carbon emissions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Economy</strong> — a mix of local jobs that match the skills of residents. <strong>Housing</strong> — a range of tenures and sizes (private, social, shared ownership) so people of all incomes can live there. <strong>Social and cultural life</strong> — community spaces, places of worship, sports clubs. <strong>Equity</strong> — fair access to opportunity for all groups in the community.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list at least five of the eight spokes of Egan's wheel.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Egan’s wheel sets out eight components of a sustainable community: governance, transport, services, environment, economy, housing, social and cultural, and equity. UK planners use the wheel to test new developments.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Brownfield Development — Cardiff Bay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Land that has been previously built on, often industrial or derelict, now reused for new development.">Brownfield</dfn> sites are previously developed pieces of land — old docks, gasworks, factories or rail yards. Reusing them protects the countryside and uses existing roads, sewers and public transport. The classic UK example is Cardiff Bay. From the 1860s the Bay was a working dock exporting Welsh coal; by the 1980s most docks had closed and the area was derelict.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Cardiff Bay Development Corporation began regeneration in 1987. A 1.1 km barrage built across the river mouths in 1999 created a 200-hectare freshwater lake, ending the tidal mudflats. The Wales Millennium Centre opened in 2004; the Senedd (Welsh Parliament building) followed in 2006. Today the Bay has 6,000 new homes, 25,000 jobs and tourism brings around 5 million visitors a year. The site scores well on Egan’s wheel for transport, services and economy.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Brownfield Trade-offs at Cardiff Bay</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Successes: rising house prices have brought professional residents into a previously deprived area; landmark buildings have boosted Welsh national identity; the freshwater lake supports water sports and a permanent population of birds.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Concerns: contamination clean-up costs were significant; the barrage destroyed an internationally important mudflat habitat for wading birds; new flats are too expensive for many original residents of nearby Butetown, raising fears of <strong>gentrification</strong>; equity within Egan’s wheel remains the weakest spoke.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Greenfield Development — Northstowe</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Land that has not been built on before, often farmland or open countryside, used for new development.">Greenfield</dfn> sites are previously undeveloped land — usually farmland on the edge of a town. They are quicker and cheaper to build on, but consume countryside, lengthen commutes and lock in car dependency unless planned carefully. The contrasting example is Northstowe, eight miles north-west of Cambridge. Built on a former RAF airfield (a brownfield core) and surrounding farmland, Northstowe is the largest new town in the UK since Milton Keynes (1967).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">When complete around 2030, Northstowe will have 10,000 homes for 24,000 people. The masterplan includes a bus rapid-transit corridor into Cambridge (cutting commuter traffic), three primary schools, a secondary school, and a town centre within a 10-minute walk of every home. 30% of the homes are affordable. Power comes partly from a community-scale solar farm. Northstowe scores well on transport, services and environment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two strengths of Cardiff Bay (brownfield) and two strengths of Northstowe (greenfield).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cardiff Bay is a brownfield regeneration of derelict docks — 6,000 new homes, 25,000 jobs, but contested on equity. Northstowe is a greenfield new town near Cambridge — 10,000 homes, bus rapid transit and 30% affordable housing, but consumes farmland.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Choosing Between Brownfield and Greenfield</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">UK national planning policy presumes brownfield first — reusing existing land cuts greenhouse-gas emissions from new road and sewer construction, protects the green belt and revives struggling neighbourhoods. But there is a limit. Brownfield sites are often expensive to clean up and rarely come in big enough blocks to deliver thousands of homes at once. Greenfield development can deliver complete neighbourhoods (schools, shops, transport links) in a single masterplan.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A balanced strategy uses both. Egan’s wheel asks the harder question: whichever land is chosen, will the eight spokes still spin? A brownfield flat block with no green space, no shops and poor transport is no more sustainable than a greenfield estate of car-dependent detached homes.</p>

  <li data-narration-id="n22">A sustainable community meets present needs without harming the future; Egan’s wheel sets out eight components UK planners use as a checklist.</li>

  <li data-narration-id="n24">Northstowe shows planned greenfield expansion near Cambridge — 10,000 homes with bus rapid transit, but builds on farmland.</li>
</ul>
