--- DESCRIPTION ---
Acts 4–5 of Much Ado: truth revealed, Claudio's penance, Hero restored, and the double wedding.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Truth Emerges (Act 5, Scenes 1–2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Dogberry finally brings Borachio and Conrade before Leonato. Borachio confesses: 'What your wisdoms could not discover, these shallow fools have brought to light.' The irony is pointed — the 'shallow fools' of the Watch have uncovered what the wise and powerful missed. Don John has fled Messina. Claudio and Don Pedro are devastated to learn Hero was innocent.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Claudio's remorse is genuine but raises questions about <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of making amends for wrongdoing. Claudio's penance (mourning at Hero's tomb, agreeing to marry a stranger) may not adequately compensate for his public destruction of Hero.">penance</dfn>. Leonato tells him that Hero has died (maintaining the Friar's deception) and commands him to mourn at her tomb and then marry Leonato's 'niece' — actually Hero herself, veiled. Claudio agrees to everything, but the audience may wonder: is his grief proportionate to his crime? He destroyed a woman's life based on a lie. A night at her tomb and a song seem inadequate.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and argue whether Claudio's penance is adequate for his crime, with specific evidence.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Claudio's penance — mourning at Hero's tomb and agreeing to marry a stranger — is the play's most debated moment. Some see genuine remorse; others see a man following orders with the same passivity that led him to shame Hero. Shakespeare leaves it for the audience to judge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Tomb Scene (Act 5, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Claudio visits Hero's tomb at night and hangs an <dfn class="term" data-def="A short poem or piece of writing honouring a dead person. Claudio's epitaph for Hero is formal and poetic but may feel impersonal.">epitaph</dfn>: 'Done to death by slanderous tongues / Was the Hero that here lies.' He then sings a song of mourning. The scene is brief and ritualised. Some productions play it as deeply felt; others as performative and hollow. The epitaph acknowledges the slander but does not name Claudio as its perpetrator — he distances himself from responsibility even in mourning.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Claudio arrives to marry Leonato's 'niece.' The veiled woman is revealed to be Hero. She says: 'And when I lived, I was your other wife; / And when you loved, you were my other husband.' This <dfn class="term" data-def="Something that can be interpreted in more than one way. Hero's words may suggest forgiveness, or they may suggest that both she and Claudio have been fundamentally changed by the experience.">ambiguous</dfn> line can be played as warm reconciliation or cool recognition that their relationship has been irreparably altered. Hero's 'death' has changed her from a passive figure to someone who has experienced the worst her society can do — and survived.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Shakespeare gives Hero remarkably few lines in this scene. She is unveiled, she speaks briefly, and the play moves on to the Beatrice/Benedick resolution. This silence may reflect the inadequacy of the comic form to address what happened to her. The play resolves formally (wedding, dancing) but the emotional resolution is questionable.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Benedick and Beatrice: The Final Exchange</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Benedick and Beatrice's resolution is far more satisfying. They initially try to deny their love again ('I love you with so much of my heart that none is left to protest'), but when their love poems to each other are produced as evidence, they give in. Benedick says: 'Peace! I will stop your mouth' — and kisses her. Their love is sealed not with formal ceremony but with characteristic wit and warmth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Benedick's final word is a call to dance and a dismissal of anyone who takes things too seriously: 'Prince, thou art sad; get thee a wife.' The play ends with music and dancing — the conventional comic ending. But like <em>The Merchant of Venice</em>, the happiness is complicated. Hero's near-destruction lingers. Don John is captured but not yet punished. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An unresolved question that complicates the play's happy ending. Hero's silence, Claudio's inadequate penance, and Don John's delayed punishment all create lingering unease.">loose ends</dfn> prevent the comedy from feeling entirely comfortable.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">The play ends with two weddings and dancing — formal comedy requirements. But the audience has witnessed Hero's destruction, Leonato's betrayal, and the fragility of Claudio's love. Hero marries the man who publicly shamed her. Beatrice marries happily, but her rage at the injustice ('O God, that I were a man!') still echoes. Like <em>The Merchant of Venice</em>, <em>Much Ado</em> is a comedy that leaves uncomfortable questions unanswered.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: argue both sides of whether the ending is truly happy, with evidence for and against from across the play.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The ending is formally comic (weddings, dancing) but emotionally complex. Benedick and Beatrice's resolution is genuinely satisfying. Hero and Claudio's is troubled: she marries the man who destroyed her, his penance may be inadequate, and her silence speaks volumes. Shakespeare creates a 'happy' ending with an uneasy undertone.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Claudio's penance (tomb, song, blind marriage) may be inadequate for his crime — Shakespeare leaves this judgement to the audience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Hero's restoration is formally satisfying but emotionally complex: she has few lines and marries the man who publicly destroyed her.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Benedick and Beatrice's resolution is genuinely earned through tested commitment, contrasting with the troubled Claudio/Hero reconciliation.</li></ul>
