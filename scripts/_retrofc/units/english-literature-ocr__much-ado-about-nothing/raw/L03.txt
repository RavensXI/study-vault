Acts 2–3 of Much Ado: Don John's plot, the window deception, and the growing threat to Hero.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Don John's Plot (Act 2, Scene 2 – Act 3, Scene 2)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">While the benevolent gulling of Benedick and Beatrice brings lovers together, Don John plots to tear Claudio and Hero apart. His scheme is simple but devastating: he will convince Claudio and Don Pedro that Hero is unfaithful by staging a scene at Hero's bedroom window. His associate Borachio will woo Margaret (Hero's gentlewoman) at the window, while Don John ensures Claudio watches from below, believing the woman to be Hero.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Don John tells Claudio and Don Pedro: 'the lady is disloyal.' He invites them to witness 'proof' on the night before the wedding. Claudio's response is instant and extreme: 'If I see any thing tonight why I should not marry her, tomorrow in the congregation, where I should wed, there will I shame her.' He decides to publicly humiliate Hero <em>before</em> seeing any evidence. His readiness to believe and to punish reveals a <dfn class="term" data-def="A tendency to assume the worst about others' behaviour, especially regarding female chastity. Claudio's instant belief in Hero's infidelity reflects the Elizabethan obsession with female honour.">deep-rooted suspicion</dfn> about women that goes beyond Don John's manipulation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain why Claudio's readiness to shame Hero publicly — before seeing proof — reveals more about his character than about Don John's villainy.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Claudio agrees to publicly shame Hero before seeing any evidence. His extreme response reveals that his love was always fragile and appearance-based. Don John provides the trigger, but Claudio's suspicion of women and obsession with honour were already present.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Window Deception (Act 3, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Shakespeare does not show the window scene directly. Instead, we learn about it through Borachio's drunken confession to Conrade, overheard by the Watch. Borachio boasts: 'I have tonight wooed Margaret, the Lady Hero's gentlewoman, by the name of Hero.' He calls Claudio and Don Pedro 'two of Don John's most villainous enemies' who were 'close by' and 'saw afar off in the orchard this amiable encounter.'</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Watch — led by the incompetent <dfn class="term" data-def="The bumbling constable of Messina whose malapropisms (misused words) provide comic relief but who accidentally discovers Don John's plot and becomes the instrument of truth.">Dogberry</dfn> — overhears and arrests Borachio and Conrade. This is a turning point: the truth is now known, but Dogberry's incompetence delays its delivery to Leonato. Shakespeare creates agonising dramatic irony: the audience knows Hero is innocent, and the means of proving it exist, but the information arrives too late to prevent her public shaming.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Dogberry is one of Shakespeare's great comic creations. He speaks in <dfn class="term" data-def="The mistaken use of a word that sounds similar to the intended word, creating comic effect. Dogberry says 'desartless' for 'deserving' and 'aspicious' for 'suspicious.'">malapropisms</dfn>: he is 'the most desartless man' (meaning 'deserving'), suspects 'aspicious persons' (meaning 'suspicious'), and instructs the Watch to 'comprehend all vagrom men' (meaning 'apprehend vagrant'). His mangled language is hilarious, but it also carries thematic weight: in a play about miscommunication and deception, the character who speaks most inaccurately is the one who accidentally discovers the truth.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Shakespeare uses Dogberry to comment on the relationship between language and truth. The educated, eloquent characters (Claudio, Don Pedro, Don John) are the ones who create and believe lies. The uneducated, inarticulate Watch stumbles upon the truth. This irony reinforces the play's argument that appearances are deceptive and that wisdom does not always belong to the powerful.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Building Tension Towards the Wedding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Act 3 builds tension through parallel plots moving towards the wedding. The Beatrice/Benedick gulling has succeeded — both are now awkwardly trying to be nice to each other, creating comedy. Don John's plot has also succeeded — Claudio and Don Pedro believe Hero is unfaithful. And Dogberry has the truth but cannot communicate it clearly. Shakespeare weaves these three strands together, creating a dramatic structure where the audience watches comedy, tragedy, and incompetence converge on a single event.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Hero's preparation for her wedding (Act 3, Scene 4) is poignant because the audience knows what is coming. She is excited and hopeful; Claudio is preparing to destroy her. The <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something the characters do not, creating tension or pathos. The audience knows Hero is innocent and that the truth has been discovered, but no one tells her.">dramatic irony</dfn> is devastating. Shakespeare positions the audience as helpless witnesses — we know the truth but cannot intervene.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Margaret is a morally ambiguous figure. She was at the window with Borachio, called 'Hero' — but did she know the purpose of the deception? Shakespeare never clarifies. She appears at the wedding and says nothing. She is never punished. Some productions present her as an innocent dupe; others as knowingly complicit. This ambiguity is typical of the play's refusal to offer simple moral answers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: explain how Shakespeare creates dramatic irony in Act 3 by weaving together three separate plot strands that all converge on the wedding.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Act 3 weaves three plots towards the wedding: the successful gulling (comedy), Don John's plot (tragedy), and Dogberry's discovery of the truth (incompetence). The audience watches all three converge with agonising dramatic irony — we know the truth that could save Hero, but it arrives too late.</p>
</div>
