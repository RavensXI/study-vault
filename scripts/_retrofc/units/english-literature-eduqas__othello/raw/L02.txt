<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Act 1, Scene 1 — Iago’s Opening Assault</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The play opens not with Othello but with Iago and Roderigo outside Brabantio’s house at night. Iago’s language is immediately racist and sexually degrading: “Even now, now, very now, an old black ram / Is tupping your white ewe” (Act 1, Scene 1). The <dfn class="term" data-def="Language that compares humans to animals, stripping them of dignity and humanity. Iago consistently uses bestial imagery to describe Othello, reducing the general to a creature driven by lust.">bestial imagery</dfn> reduces Othello to a sexually predatory animal, and the colour contrast (“black ram” / “white ewe”) weaponises racial difference. Shakespeare introduces Othello through others’ prejudice before we ever see him — a structural choice that forces the audience to confront racist language before forming their own impression.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Iago also tells Brabantio: “You’ll have your daughter covered with a Barbary horse” and “your daughter and the Moor are now making the beast with two backs.” Every description of the marriage is animalistic and sexual. Shakespeare shows that racist language does not describe reality — it distorts it. The audience will soon see Othello speak with dignity and eloquence that completely contradicts Iago’s portrait.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three pieces of animal imagery does Iago use about Othello in Act 1, Scene 1?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Iago introduces Othello through racist, bestial imagery: “old black ram,” “Barbary horse,” “beast with two backs.” Shakespeare presents Othello through others’ prejudice before the audience meets him — forcing us to confront how racist language distorts reality.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">“I Am Not What I Am” — Iago’s Philosophy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Iago’s most revealing line is his declaration: “I am not what I am” (Act 1, Scene 1). This is an inversion of God’s self-definition in Exodus: “I am that I am.” Where God is pure truth and essence, Iago is pure deception — a walking negation. The line establishes him as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A dramatic figure who embodies evil, often deceiving other characters for personal gain. Iago echoes the medieval Vice figure and the Devil in morality plays — a manipulator who disguises evil as honesty.">Vice figure</dfn>, a dramatic descendant of the Devil in medieval morality plays. He will present himself as “honest Iago” throughout the play while systematically destroying everyone around him.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">His motives are famously ambiguous. He claims multiple reasons: Othello passed him over for promotion in favour of Cassio (“Mere prattle, without practice”, Act 1, Scene 1), he suspects Othello has slept with his wife Emilia (“it is thought abroad that ’twixt my sheets / He has done my office”, Act 1, Scene 3), and he covets Desdemona himself. But no single motive adequately explains the scale of his destruction. Coleridge famously described Iago’s self-justification as “the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity” — evil for its own sake.</p>

Roderigo’s willingness to believe Iago’s promises demonstrates a key theme: people believe what they want to believe. Iago tells each character what they want to hear — and each character’s desire becomes the tool of their destruction.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Senate Scene — Othello’s Eloquence (Act 1, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">When Brabantio brings his accusations before the Venetian Senate, Othello defends himself with extraordinary <dfn class="term" data-def="The art of persuasive speaking. Othello’s speech in the Senate scene demonstrates his eloquence and self-possession — qualities that will be systematically destroyed by Iago.">rhetoric</dfn>. His speech is measured, dignified, and compelling: “Rude am I in my speech, / And little blessed with the soft phrase of peace.” This claim of linguistic limitation is itself eloquent — a rhetorical device called <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which the speaker claims to be unskilled or unworthy while actually demonstrating the opposite. Othello’s claim to be ‘rude in speech’ is itself brilliantly eloquent.">litotes</dfn>. He narrates how he won Desdemona through storytelling: “She loved me for the dangers I had passed, / And I loved her that she did pity them.” Their love is built on mutual respect and shared vulnerability.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Desdemona’s testimony is equally significant. She tells the Senate: “I saw Othello’s visage in his mind” — she fell in love with his character, not his appearance. She asserts her own agency: “I do perceive here a divided duty” between father and husband, but chooses Othello. This independence marks her as a transgressive figure in Jacobean terms — a woman who defies patriarchal authority to follow her own heart.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n13">At the end of Act 1, Iago reveals his plan in soliloquy. He will use his contemptuous view of Cassio (“A fellow almost damned in a fair wife”, Act 1 Scene 1 — spoken to Roderigo; meaning Cassio is compromised by a woman’s influence, not a comment about his attractiveness) to make Othello believe Desdemona is unfaithful. His reasoning is chillingly logical: “The Moor is of a free and open nature, / That thinks men honest that but seem to be so.” Othello’s greatest virtue — his trusting nature — becomes his greatest vulnerability. Iago identifies this and plans to exploit it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">He also reveals his method: “I have’t. It is engendered. Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world’s light.” The birth metaphor is grotesque — his plan is a “monstrous birth” emerging from “Hell and night.” This links Iago to diabolical forces and establishes his manipulation as something actively evil, not merely clever.</p>
