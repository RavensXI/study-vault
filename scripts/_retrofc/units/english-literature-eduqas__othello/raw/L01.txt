<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Race and Otherness in Jacobean England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare wrote <em>Othello</em> around 1603–1604, at the start of James I’s reign. Attitudes toward race in Jacobean England were deeply prejudiced. <dfn class="term" data-def="A term used in Shakespeare’s time for people of North African or Middle Eastern origin. Moors were often associated with Islam, exoticism, and fear of the ‘other’ in European culture.">Moors</dfn> — people of North African descent — were viewed with a mixture of fascination and hostility. In 1601, Queen Elizabeth I issued a proclamation ordering the deportation of “blackamoors” from England, reflecting widespread racial anxiety. The word “black” in Jacobean culture carried associations of evil, sin, and the Devil — associations that Shakespeare’s characters exploit throughout the play.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Othello is a Moor who has risen to the rank of general in the Venetian army through exceptional military skill. His position is extraordinary: a Black man commanding the forces of a white European state. Shakespeare places him in a society that simultaneously values his abilities and despises his race. This tension drives the play. Every character who attacks Othello — Iago, Brabantio, Roderigo — uses racist language, suggesting that his race makes him perpetually vulnerable regardless of his status.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What was the Jacobean attitude to Moors, and how does Othello’s position reflect this tension?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Jacobean England was deeply prejudiced against Moors. Elizabeth I ordered their deportation in 1601. Shakespeare places Othello in an impossible position: valued for his military skill but despised for his race. This racial vulnerability is exploited by Iago, Brabantio, and Roderigo throughout the play.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Venice vs Cyprus — Two Worlds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Shakespeare sets the play across two contrasting locations. <dfn class="term" data-def="A wealthy, sophisticated European city-state. In Shakespeare’s plays, Venice represents civilisation, law, order, and commerce — but also hypocrisy and prejudice beneath the surface.">Venice</dfn> represents civilisation, law, and social order. The Senate scene (Act 1, Scene 3) shows a functioning justice system where Othello can defend himself eloquently against Brabantio’s accusations. Venice’s laws protect Othello’s marriage — his eloquence wins the Duke’s approval.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">But the action moves to <dfn class="term" data-def="A Mediterranean island outpost, far from Venice’s civilising influence. Cyprus represents isolation, vulnerability, and the breakdown of social order — the perfect environment for Iago’s manipulation.">Cyprus</dfn> — a military outpost far from Venice’s civilising structures. Isolated on this island, Othello is removed from the legal and social systems that protect him. Cyprus becomes a space where Iago can operate freely, where rumour replaces evidence, and where Othello’s self-doubt can flourish without the anchor of Venetian society. The geographical shift mirrors the psychological shift: as they move further from civilisation, characters descend into chaos.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">In Jacobean society, marriage was a patriarchal transaction. A daughter was her father’s property until she became her husband’s. Desdemona’s elopement with Othello violates this system twice: she marries without her father’s consent, and she marries a Black man. Brabantio’s outrage — “She is abused, stol’n from me, and corrupted / By spells and medicines” (Act 1, Scene 3) — reflects the <dfn class="term" data-def="A social system in which men hold primary authority. In Jacobean England, fathers controlled their daughters’ marriages, and wives were expected to be obedient and chaste.">patriarchal</dfn> belief that a woman who chooses her own husband must have been bewitched. He cannot accept that Desdemona freely chose Othello.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Brabantio’s parting warning — “Look to her, Moor, if thou hast eyes to see: / She has deceived her father, and may thee” — plants the seed of doubt that Iago later exploits. If Desdemona deceived one man (her father), she might deceive another (her husband). This line echoes through the play like a curse.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Honour Killing and Jacobean Values</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The play’s climax — Othello’s murder of Desdemona — must be understood within the context of <dfn class="term" data-def="The killing of a family member (usually a woman) who is perceived to have brought shame through sexual misconduct. While not legally sanctioned in Jacobean England, the concept of male honour tied to female chastity was deeply embedded in the culture.">honour killing</dfn>. Othello does not kill Desdemona out of simple jealousy — he believes he is performing an act of justice: “It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul” (Act 5, Scene 2). He sees himself as purging corruption, not committing murder. For a Jacobean audience, male honour was inextricably linked to female chastity: a wife’s infidelity did not just hurt her husband — it destroyed his social standing, his identity, and his reputation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Shakespeare adapted the play from an Italian story by Giraldi Cinthio (1565), but made significant changes. In Cinthio’s version, the Moor is a straightforward villain; Shakespeare transforms him into a complex, sympathetic figure destroyed by external forces and internal vulnerability. This choice is crucial: Shakespeare does not endorse this worldview — the play exposes its catastrophic consequences. But understanding it is essential for the exam. Othello’s tragedy is not simply that he is jealous, but that a system of patriarchal honour, combined with racial insecurity and Iago’s manipulation, drives him to destroy the thing he loves most.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how three contextual factors (race, patriarchy, honour) combine to make Othello vulnerable to Iago.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three contextual forces drive the tragedy: racial prejudice (Othello is perpetually ‘othered’), patriarchal honour (male identity depends on female chastity), and Jacobean attitudes to marriage (wives as property, elopement as theft). Iago weaponises all three to destroy Othello.</p>
</div>
