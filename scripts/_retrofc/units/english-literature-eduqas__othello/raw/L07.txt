<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Othello’s Verse vs Iago’s Prose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Shakespeare uses the contrast between <dfn class="term" data-def="Structured, rhythmic speech (iambic pentameter) used by noble characters to show control and dignity.">verse</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Unstructured speech without regular rhythm, used to show lower status, informality, or psychological breakdown.">prose</dfn> as a barometer of Othello’s psychological state. In Acts 1–2, Othello speaks in controlled iambic pentameter: “Most potent, grave, and reverend signiors, / My very noble and approved good masters.” The rhythm is measured, the vocabulary elevated. By Act 4, Scene 1, he has collapsed into fragmented prose: “Lie with her? Lie on her?” — a jumble of repetitions, exclamations, and non-sequiturs that mirrors his mental disintegration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Iago, by contrast, moves fluidly between verse and prose depending on his audience. He speaks prose with Roderigo (lowering himself to his dupe’s level), verse with Othello (matching the general’s register), and prose in soliloquy (revealing his true calculating nature). This <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to adapt one’s language and behaviour to different social situations. Iago is a master of code-switching, adjusting his speech to manipulate each character.">code-switching</dfn> demonstrates Iago’s fundamental characteristic: he is all performance, no substance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and describe how Othello’s language changes across the play, with one quotation from Acts 1 and 4.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Othello’s verse arc: controlled iambic pentameter (Acts 1–2) → increasingly disturbed verse (Act 3) → fragmented prose and epileptic collapse (Act 4) → restored verse in the murder scene and final speech (Act 5). Iago code-switches between verse and prose, adapting his register to manipulate each character.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Animal Imagery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Animal imagery dominates the play’s language, almost exclusively through Iago. He calls Othello an “old black ram”, a “Barbary horse”, and refers to the couple as making “the beast with two backs”. He describes jealousy as a “green-eyed monster” and his plan as a “monstrous birth”. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Consistent patterns of imagery in a literary text. In Othello, animal imagery is concentrated in Iago’s speech, reducing human beings to creatures driven by instinct.">image cluster</dfn> serves a clear purpose: Iago reduces human beings to animals, stripping away dignity, reason, and love. By the time Othello internalises this language (“goats and monkeys!”, Act 4, Scene 1), he has begun to see the world through Iago’s debasing lens.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Iago has seven soliloquies — more than any other Shakespeare villain. These serve multiple purposes. First, they reveal his plan, creating <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience knows something characters do not. Iago’s soliloquies create dramatic irony: the audience watches helplessly as his plans unfold.">dramatic irony</dfn> as the audience watches helplessly. Second, they establish a relationship between Iago and the audience: he confides in us, making us unwilling accomplices. Third, they reveal the emptiness behind his masks — even in soliloquy, Iago shifts motives and contradicts himself, suggesting there is no “real” Iago beneath the performance.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">His most revealing soliloquy ends Act 2, Scene 3: “Divinity of hell! / When devils will the blackest sins put on, / They do suggest at first with heavenly shows.” He openly acknowledges being diabolical — and takes pleasure in it. The phrase “Divinity of hell” is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech combining contradictory terms. ‘Divinity of hell’ juxtaposes the sacred with the damned, reflecting Iago’s inversion of moral values.">oxymoron</dfn> that captures Iago’s essence: he makes evil look holy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Handkerchief as Dramatic Device</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The handkerchief functions as the play’s central <dfn class="term" data-def="A theatrical device that drives the plot. The handkerchief is a prop whose movement through different characters’ hands determines the plot’s direction.">prop</dfn>, its movement through different hands driving the plot. Desdemona drops it → Emilia finds it → Emilia gives it to Iago → Iago plants it on Cassio → Othello sees Cassio with it → Othello accepts it as “ocular proof.” Structurally, the handkerchief is the mechanism that converts Iago’s lies into what Othello believes is evidence. Its smallness is the point: the entire tragedy pivots on a domestic object, showing how easily trust can be destroyed by manufactured evidence.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and trace the handkerchief’s journey through six different hands, explaining each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The handkerchief’s journey: Desdemona (drops it) → Emilia (finds it) → Iago (takes it) → Cassio (planted) → Bianca (copies it) → Othello (sees it as proof). Each transfer is a stage in the tragedy. The domestic scale of this object contrasts with the cosmic consequences it triggers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Dramatic Irony and Staging</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Shakespeare creates sustained dramatic irony through the word “honest.” Iago is called “honest Iago” over fifty times in the play. Every use is ironic: the audience knows he is the most dishonest character on stage. This repetition creates a grinding tension — the audience watches characters trust the one person who will destroy them, powerless to intervene.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The eavesdropping scene (Act 4, Scene 1) uses staging to devastating effect. Iago positions Othello where he can see but not hear Cassio’s conversation. Cassio laughs about his relationship with Bianca (a courtesan), but Othello believes he is laughing about Desdemona. The gap between what the audience sees (the truth) and what Othello sees (manufactured evidence) creates agonising tension. Shakespeare turns the theatre’s physical space into a tool of Iago’s manipulation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Shakespeare uses colour symbolism throughout: black = evil/otherness (exploited by racists), white = purity/innocence (Desdemona), green = jealousy (“green-eyed monster”). These associations are both used and subverted: Othello, the “black” character, begins as the most noble; Iago, presumably white, is the most evil. Shakespeare interrogates the colour associations of his own culture.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Hell imagery clusters around Iago: “Divinity of hell”, “Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth”, “I am not what I am” (inverting God). Othello absorbs this imagery as his mind is corrupted: “Arise, black vengeance, from the hollow hell!”. By the end, Othello describes himself as damned: “Whip me, ye devils!”. The hell imagery tracks the spiritual corruption from Iago to Othello.</p>

</div>
