--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Theme 1: Jealousy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An intense feeling of suspicion or resentment. In Othello, jealousy is presented as a destructive force that consumes reason, dignity, and love.">Jealousy</dfn> is the play’s most visible theme. Iago describes it as “the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on” (Act 3, Scene 3) — a creature that torments its victim while feeding on them. Othello’s jealousy is not innate but manufactured by Iago, exploiting racial insecurity and patriarchal honour codes. Shakespeare shows that jealousy is not a private emotion but a social disease, created by the systems of prejudice and ownership that govern Jacobean relationships.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Theme 2: Race and Otherness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Race is not a subplot but the foundation of the tragedy. Othello is perpetually <dfn class="term" data-def="The state of being perceived as fundamentally different from the dominant group. Othello’s race makes him a permanent outsider in Venetian society, no matter how much he achieves.">othered</dfn>: valued for his military skill but never fully accepted. Iago’s racist language (“old black ram,” “sooty bosom”) reflects Jacobean attitudes. Brabantio’s “spells and medicines” links blackness to witchcraft. Most devastatingly, Othello internalises this racism: “Haply, for I am black / And have not those soft parts of conversation.” The tragedy is that the racist assumptions of Venetian society become the weapon that destroys Othello from within.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain how racial prejudice operates from three different sources in the play.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Racial prejudice operates on three levels: external (Iago, Brabantio, Roderigo use racist language), structural (Othello is valued for military skill but socially marginalised), and internalised (“Haply, for I am black”). Iago does not need to be racist to Othello’s face — he activates insecurities already planted by the society around him.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Theme 3: Appearance vs Reality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The gap between appearance and reality drives every relationship in the play. Iago appears “honest” but is the play’s greatest deceiver (“I am not what I am”). Desdemona appears guilty (through the handkerchief) but is innocent. Othello appears noble but becomes a murderer. Cassio appears to discuss Desdemona but talks about Bianca. Shakespeare shows that in a world governed by <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to deceive without technically lying. Throughout Othello, characters and situations are not what they seem — the entire tragedy is built on misperception.">equivocation</dfn> and prejudice, seeing is not believing — because what people see is filtered through their fears, desires, and assumptions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Honour drives destruction across the play. Cassio’s loss of reputation (“the immortal part of myself”) devastates him. Othello’s honour is tied to Desdemona’s chastity — her supposed infidelity destroys not just his love but his identity (“Othello’s occupation’s gone”). He kills her as an “honour killing” (“It is the cause”), believing he performs justice. Iago exploits the honour code relentlessly: every character’s sense of reputation becomes a lever for manipulation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Theme 5: Manipulation and Trust</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Iago’s manipulation weaponises people’s virtues. Othello’s trust (“free and open nature”), Desdemona’s generosity, Cassio’s shame, Roderigo’s love — each virtue becomes the tool of its owner’s destruction. Shakespeare presents a deeply pessimistic view: in a world where appearances deceive, the most trusting people are the most vulnerable. Iago’s genius is not inventing lies but identifying what each person wants to believe and feeding it back to them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Theme 6: Love</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Othello and Desdemona’s love is genuine but exists within systems that make it fragile: racial prejudice, patriarchal ownership, and social isolation. “She loved me for the dangers I had passed, / And I loved her that she did pity them” (Act 1, Scene 3) describes a love built on mutual respect and shared vulnerability. But this love is destroyed not by an external enemy but by the systems surrounding it. Shakespeare suggests that love in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A society that judges, excludes, and controls individuals based on prejudice. In Othello, the prejudiced Venetian world makes Othello’s love for Desdemona fatally vulnerable.">prejudiced society</dfn> is always at risk.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the six key themes, with one quotation for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six key themes: jealousy (“green-eyed monster”), race and otherness (“Haply, for I am black”), appearance vs reality (“I am not what I am”), honour (“occupation’s gone”), manipulation (“free and open nature”), and love (“She loved me for the dangers”). For the exam, you need quotations and context for each.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Exam Technique for Othello</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Your Shakespeare paper will typically be <dfn class="term" data-def="An exam where you do not have access to the text. You must memorise quotations and refer to specific scenes from memory.">closed book</dfn>, giving you a printed extract and asking two linked tasks: (i) close analysis of the extract, focusing on language and structure, and (ii) a response on theme or character elsewhere in the play, embedding context. Split your time evenly between the two. Use PEAD structure: Point, Evidence, Analysis, Development (embed context in the whole-text task). Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact wording, timings and mark weightings.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">For Othello specifically: always engage with the race debate (it is central, not optional), track language change (verse/prose as AO2), and embed the honour code context. The strongest responses will reference critical views (Bradley vs Leavis for Othello, Coleridge for Iago) and argue both sides of a debate.</p>

In part (ii), range across the play (3-4 scenes minimum) and embed AO4 in every paragraph. Memorise the quotation bank above — short, versatile quotations that work across multiple questions.</p>
