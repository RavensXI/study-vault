<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Storm and Arrival in Cyprus (Act 2, Scene 1)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A violent storm destroys the Turkish fleet and separates the characters as they travel to Cyprus. The storm functions as <dfn class="term" data-def="When natural elements mirror human emotions or events. The storm in Act 2 symbolises the approaching chaos that Iago will unleash on Cyprus.">pathetic fallacy</dfn>: nature itself signals the approaching destruction. Cassio arrives first and greets Desdemona with courtly manners: “Hail to thee, lady! And the grace of heaven.” Iago watches this innocent greeting and twists it into evidence of an affair: “With as little a web as this will I ensnare as great a fly as Cassio.” The word “ensnare” presents Iago as a spider and his victims as flies — trapped before they realise the web exists.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Cassio’s Disgrace (Act 2, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Iago’s first move is to destroy Cassio’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A person’s social standing and honour. Cassio’s loss of reputation after the brawl is devastating because in Jacobean society, a man’s honour was his most valuable possession.">reputation</dfn>. He gets Cassio drunk during the victory celebrations, knowing Cassio has “very poor and unhappy brains for drinking.” When Cassio fights Montano, Othello strips him of his rank: “Cassio, I love thee, / But never more be officer of mine.” Cassio is devastated: “Reputation, reputation, reputation! O, I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself.” For Cassio, reputation is the “immortal part” — more important than life itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Iago then advises Cassio to appeal to Desdemona to plead his case with Othello. This is the trap: by having Cassio and Desdemona meet privately, Iago creates the appearance of an affair. Desdemona’s generosity and Cassio’s desperation become the weapons of their destruction. Iago’s genius is using people’s virtues against them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: How does Iago engineer Cassio’s disgrace, and how does he use it to create suspicion?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Iago’s method: (1) get Cassio drunk and disgraced, (2) advise Cassio to seek Desdemona’s help, (3) present their innocent meetings as evidence of an affair. He weaponises virtues: Desdemona’s generosity, Cassio’s desperation, and Othello’s trust all become tools of destruction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Temptation Scene (Act 3, Scene 3)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Act 3, Scene 3 is the play’s pivotal scene — the longest scene in Othello and one of the longest in Shakespeare's tragedies. Iago plants suspicion through calculated <dfn class="term" data-def="Hints, suggestions, and half-statements designed to create suspicion without making a direct accusation. Iago uses insinuation to make Othello reach devastating conclusions on his own.">insinuation</dfn> rather than direct accusation. His technique is to say just enough to provoke Othello’s imagination: “Ha! I like not that” (seeing Cassio leave Desdemona), then feigning reluctance: “I am not bound to that all slaves are free to. / Utter my thoughts.” By pretending not to want to speak, he makes Othello desperate to hear.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Iago echoes Othello’s words back to him (“Indeed?” “Honest, my lord?” “Think, my lord?”), turning innocent statements into questions loaded with suspicion. He warns: “O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on.” The famous “<dfn class="term" data-def="A personification of jealousy as a creature that torments its victim while simultaneously feeding on them. Iago warns Othello about jealousy while actually planting it.">green-eyed monster</dfn>” metaphor is itself an act of manipulation: by naming the danger, Iago appears to be protecting Othello while actually planting the very jealousy he describes.</p>

Give me the ocular proof.” “<dfn class="term" data-def="Visual evidence — proof seen with one’s own eyes. Othello demands visible proof of Desdemona’s infidelity, but Iago provides manufactured evidence (the handkerchief) that Othello accepts without question.">Ocular proof</dfn>” means evidence he can see with his own eyes. But Iago provides manufactured evidence — Cassio’s supposed dream, the handkerchief — and Othello accepts it because his trust in Iago is absolute. The tragedy is that Othello applies rigorous evidentiary standards (“ocular proof”) but accepts fabricated evidence from the one person he should not trust.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Handkerchief (Act 3, Scene 4)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The handkerchief is the play’s most important <dfn class="term" data-def="A physical object that represents something beyond its literal meaning. The handkerchief represents Othello and Desdemona’s love, Othello’s heritage, and — once lost — the supposed proof of infidelity.">symbol</dfn>. Othello tells Desdemona it was given to his mother by an Egyptian: “There’s magic in the web of it” and “To lose’t or give’t away were such perdition / As nothing else could match.” The handkerchief connects Othello to his African heritage, his mother, and the magical/exotic world that Venetian society both admires and fears.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">When Desdemona accidentally drops it, Emilia picks it up and gives it to Iago (who has asked for it). Iago plants it in Cassio’s lodgings. When Othello sees Cassio with the handkerchief, he accepts it as the “ocular proof” of Desdemona’s infidelity. The entire tragedy pivots on this small piece of cloth — a domestic object given cosmic significance by Othello’s emotional investment and Iago’s manipulation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does the handkerchief symbolise, how does Iago get it, and why does Othello accept it as proof?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The handkerchief symbolises Othello’s love, heritage, and identity. Emilia finds it after Desdemona drops it; she gives it to Iago, who plants it on Cassio. Othello accepts it as “ocular proof” because (1) he trusts Iago absolutely, (2) it connects to his deepest insecurities about being an outsider, and (3) jealousy has already corrupted his judgment.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">In Act 3, Scene 3, Othello mourns the loss of his peace: “Farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell content! / Farewell the plumed troops and the big wars / That makes ambition virtue! O, farewell! / Farewell! Othello’s occupation’s gone!” The repeated “farewell” (five times) creates a cumulative sense of loss. He is not just losing Desdemona — he is losing his identity as a soldier, a leader, and a man. His “occupation” (his role, his purpose) is gone. From this point, Othello is defined by jealousy rather than honour.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Iago’s method: disgrace Cassio, use Desdemona’s generosity as evidence, weaponise people’s virtues against them.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The temptation scene uses insinuation, echoing, and the “green-eyed monster” metaphor to plant jealousy without direct accusation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The handkerchief symbolises love, heritage, and identity. Its manipulation by Iago becomes the “ocular proof” that seals Desdemona’s fate.</li></ul>
