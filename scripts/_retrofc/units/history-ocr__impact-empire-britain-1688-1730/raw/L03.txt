<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Scotland After 1688</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Scotland&rsquo;s response to the Glorious Revolution was divided. Most Scots accepted the replacement of James VII (James II of England) with William and Mary, but Jacobite Highlanders loyal to the Stuarts mounted an immediate uprising. At the Battle of Killiecrankie (July 1689), Jacobite forces under &ldquo;Bonnie Dundee&rdquo; (John Graham of Claverhouse) routed a Williamite army, though Dundee was killed in the moment of victory. Without his leadership the rising collapsed, and Scotland settled &mdash; uneasily &mdash; into the new Protestant constitutional order.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Massacre of Glencoe, 1692</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The most infamous event of William&rsquo;s reign in Scotland was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The killing of between 38 and 78 members of the MacDonald clan at Glencoe on 13 February 1692, carried out by Campbell troops acting on government orders.">Massacre of Glencoe</dfn> on 13 February 1692. William III had ordered all Highland clan chiefs to swear an oath of loyalty to him by 1 January 1692. Alasdair Maclain, chief of the MacDonalds of Glencoe, arrived a few days late to swear the oath. Government officials used this technicality as a pretext. A company of Campbell soldiers were billeted with the MacDonalds for two weeks, enjoying traditional Highland hospitality, before receiving orders to kill all MacDonalds under 70. Between 38 and 78 people were killed; many more died of exposure fleeing into winter snowfields.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what made Glencoe politically significant beyond the death toll? Think about how the soldiers were given hospitality first.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Glencoe was significant not for its death toll alone, but for the violation of Highland laws of hospitality &mdash; soldiers killed their hosts. The orders were traced back to the Master of Stair (Secretary for Scotland) and arguably to William III himself. A parliamentary inquiry condemned it as &ldquo;murder under trust&rdquo;, though no one faced prosecution.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Darien Scheme, 1698&ndash;1700</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Scotland&rsquo;s most catastrophic independent venture was the <dfn class="term" data-def="Scotland's failed attempt 1698–1700 to establish a trading colony on the Darien peninsula (modern Panama), which resulted in the loss of around a quarter of Scotland's liquid capital.">Darien Scheme</dfn>, devised by William Paterson, founder of the Bank of England. A Scottish joint-stock company raised around &pound;400,000 &mdash; estimated at a quarter of Scotland&rsquo;s liquid capital &mdash; to establish a colony at Darien on the Isthmus of Panama. It aimed to control trade between the Atlantic and Pacific. William III, under pressure from English merchants who feared Scottish competition, refused to provide English navy support and warned English and Dutch colonies not to aid the Scots. The venture failed catastrophically: disease, Spanish attack and lack of supplies killed thousands of settlers. By 1700 the colony was abandoned. The financial devastation left Scotland economically desperate and its Parliament deeply resentful of England.</p>

It demonstrated that Scotland could not pursue independent trade or colonial expansion while England blocked it. At the same time, England needed Scotland&rsquo;s border security, especially with France as a constant enemy. These pressures &mdash; Scottish economic need and English strategic interest &mdash; drove both parliaments toward union. Commissioners negotiated a treaty in 1706; after fierce debate and alleged bribery of Scottish MPs, it was ratified as the Act of Union in 1707.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Act of Union, 1707</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1707 treaty that merged the Kingdom of England and the Kingdom of Scotland into a single Kingdom of Great Britain, with one Parliament at Westminster.">Act of Union</dfn> created the Kingdom of Great Britain on 1 May 1707. Scotland&rsquo;s Parliament was abolished; Scottish MPs and peers joined Westminster. Scotland retained its own legal system, Church of Scotland, and educational institutions. Scotland gained access to English colonial trade &mdash; a crucial economic benefit after Darien. The Union was deeply unpopular with ordinary Scots: the writer Daniel Defoe, working as an English spy in Edinburgh before the vote, reported widespread hostility on the streets. Robert Burns later wrote of Scotland being &ldquo;bought and sold for English gold.&rdquo; Whether the Union was freely negotiated or imposed through bribery and financial necessity is still debated by Scottish historians.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write: three Scottish institutions that survived the Act of Union, and three things Scotland gained by joining.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Under the Act of Union (1707): Scotland kept its Kirk (Church of Scotland), separate legal system, and universities. It gained access to English colonial trade, financial stability, and representation at Westminster. Scotland lost its independent Parliament.</p>
</div>

In 1715, the Earl of Mar raised a Jacobite army in Scotland, nominally supporting James Francis Edward Stuart (&ldquo;the Old Pretender&rdquo;). &ldquo;The Fifteen&rdquo; was poorly led: the indecisive Battle of Sheriffmuir (November 1715) left both sides claiming partial victory. James arrived late and the rising fizzled out. Its failure demonstrated that Hanoverian rule was established enough to withstand an armed challenge, but Jacobite loyalty persisted in the Highlands until the final defeat at Culloden in 1746.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n15">The Massacre of Glencoe (13 February 1692) killed between 38 and 78 MacDonalds; its political significance lay in the violation of Highland hospitality and the &ldquo;murder under trust&rdquo; verdict by a parliamentary inquiry.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n16">The Darien Scheme (1698&ndash;1700) lost around a quarter of Scotland&rsquo;s liquid capital and demonstrated Scotland could not pursue independent colonial trade while England blocked it &mdash; a direct push factor toward the Act of Union.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n17">The Act of Union (1707) merged the English and Scottish Parliaments into a single Parliament of Great Britain; Scotland retained its Kirk, legal system and universities but gained access to English colonial trade.</li>
</ul>
