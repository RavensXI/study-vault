--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Basis of Anglo-Saxon Kingship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By around 1000 AD, England had developed into a single, unified kingdom under the <dfn class="term" data-def="The ruling dynasty of Anglo-Saxon England, descended from the legendary Cerdic of Wessex.">House of Wessex</dfn>. Kings claimed authority through a mixture of hereditary descent, military prowess and — crucially — the blessing of the Church. Coronation ceremonies, introduced in the tenth century, placed the king in a sacred role as God's anointed ruler.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">However, Anglo-Saxon kingship was never simply absolute. Kings governed through custom and consensus. They relied on great nobles, the law, and the support of the Church to make their rule effective. Power was distributed, not concentrated in one pair of hands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what were the three main pillars that upheld an Anglo-Saxon king's authority? Write them down from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Anglo-Saxon kingship rested on three pillars: hereditary descent, military leadership, and the Church's sanction through coronation. No king could rule effectively without all three.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Witan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The most important institution constraining royal power was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The council of senior nobles, churchmen and royal advisers who advised the Anglo-Saxon king and could, in theory, choose a new king.">Witan</dfn> (short for Witenagemot, or &ldquo;meeting of wise men&rdquo;). The Witan was not a parliament in the modern sense &mdash; it had no fixed membership, no regular meetings, and no power to pass laws independently. Yet its importance was real. Kings consulted the Witan before making major decisions: going to war, granting land, issuing law codes, and deciding succession.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Witan&rsquo;s power was most visible at moments of royal succession. When Edward the Confessor died in January 1066, it was the Witan that recognised Harold Godwinson as king &mdash; not simply the closest blood relative. This shows that legitimacy in Anglo-Saxon England required the consent of the great men, not descent alone.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Below the great earls, royal power reached the localities through <dfn class="term" data-def="The Anglo-Saxon landowning nobility, who owed military service to the king and administered royal justice in the shires.">thegns</dfn>. A thegn was a landowner who held his estate by royal grant in return for military service &mdash; typically providing one fully equipped warrior for every five hides of land. Thegns also sat on local courts, collected royal taxes, and enforced the king&rsquo;s peace in their shires.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The thegn class was not closed. A prosperous merchant who owned sufficient land and had served the king faithfully could be recognised as a thegn. This social mobility was unusual for medieval Europe and gave the Anglo-Saxon state a relatively effective administrative reach into the countryside.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The King&rsquo;s Peace and Law Codes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">One of the key responsibilities of an Anglo-Saxon king was to maintain the <dfn class="term" data-def="The royal guarantee of safety and order, under which crimes committed against travellers or in certain places were treated as offences against the king personally.">king&rsquo;s peace</dfn>. This was not merely a metaphor. Crimes that broke the king&rsquo;s peace &mdash; robbery on major roads, attacks on guests, disturbances at court &mdash; were treated as personal affronts to the monarch and carried heavier penalties.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Anglo-Saxon kings issued written law codes as a way of asserting authority. &AElig;thelred II (reigned 978&ndash;1013 and 1014&ndash;1016) is often remembered for paying tribute to Viking invaders &mdash; the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="Tribute paid by Anglo-Saxon kings, especially &AElig;thelred II, to Viking raiders to buy temporary peace; raised by a nationwide land tax.">Danegeld</dfn> &mdash; but he also issued extensive law codes that governed everything from trade to the treatment of slaves. These codes show a state capable of sophisticated governance even under pressure.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what was the Danegeld and which king is most associated with it? Include two details.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Danegeld was a nationwide tax first levied by &AElig;thelred II to pay Viking raiders. It shows Anglo-Saxon kings could raise money across the whole kingdom &mdash; a sign of administrative strength despite military weakness.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Three Kings: Cnut, Edward and Harold</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In the years before the Norman Conquest, England experienced rapid royal change. <strong>Cnut</strong> (king 1016&ndash;1035) was a Danish warrior king who conquered England but then governed as an English monarch &mdash; issuing law codes, building churches, and ruling through the Witan. His reign shows how kingship was defined by behaviour, not just blood.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">After Cnut&rsquo;s sons died without heirs, the Witan turned to <strong>Edward the Confessor</strong> (king 1042&ndash;1066), a deeply pious king whose lack of a direct heir created the succession crisis of 1066. Edward&rsquo;s death on 5 January 1066 left at least three serious claimants: Harold Godwinson (endorsed by the Witan), Harald Hardrada of Norway (a dynastic claim), and William of Normandy (who alleged a deathbed promise). The collision of these claims destroyed the Anglo-Saxon kingdom within a year.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Historians have debated how effective Anglo-Saxon government really was. The Domesday Book of 1086 &mdash; compiled just twenty years after the Conquest &mdash; relied heavily on Anglo-Saxon administrative units (hundreds and shires), suggesting the pre-Conquest state was impressively organised. The ability to levy the Danegeld nationwide also points to real administrative capacity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Yet military weakness was real. &AElig;thelred II earned the nickname &ldquo;the Unready&rdquo; (a mistranslation of &ldquo;ill-advised&rdquo;) for his inability to halt Viking raids. By 1013, Danish king Sweyn Forkbeard had driven &AElig;thelred into exile in Normandy. The contrast between sophisticated law codes and military vulnerability is a defining tension of the period.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Anglo-Saxon kingship rested on hereditary right, Church blessing and the consent of the Witan &mdash; power was distributed, not absolute.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Thegns connected royal authority to the localities through military service, tax collection and local courts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The succession crisis of 1066, triggered by Edward the Confessor&rsquo;s lack of an heir, exposed both the strength and the fragility of the Anglo-Saxon system.</li>
</ul>
