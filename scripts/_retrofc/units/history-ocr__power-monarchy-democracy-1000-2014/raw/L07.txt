--- DESCRIPTION ---
From rotten boroughs to equal franchise — how Britain's electorate expanded between 1832 and 1928.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Unreformed Electoral System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the early nineteenth century, Britain&rsquo;s system of parliamentary representation had not kept pace with population change. Many <dfn class="term" data-def="Constituencies with very few voters, often controlled by a single landowner — able to return an MP despite having almost no resident electorate.">rotten boroughs</dfn> returned MPs despite having almost no voters: Old Sarum in Wiltshire had seven voters and two MPs; Manchester, a growing industrial city with over 100,000 people, had none. The right to vote varied chaotically between constituencies &mdash; in some a man needed a forty-shilling freehold, in others any male householder could vote. Most working-class men and all women were excluded entirely.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The economic and social pressures of industrialisation made reform increasingly urgent. A rapidly growing urban working class had no representation. The fear of popular unrest &mdash; the French Revolution was a recent memory &mdash; made many MPs nervous about ignoring pressure for change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down: what was a 'rotten borough' and name one specific example? Why did the existence of rotten boroughs damage the credibility of Parliament before 1832?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A rotten borough was a constituency with almost no voters, often controlled by a single landlord. Old Sarum (Wiltshire) had seven voters and returned two MPs. Meanwhile Manchester, with over 100,000 inhabitants, had no MP at all. This made the pre-1832 Parliament deeply unrepresentative.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Great Reform Act 1832</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1832 Act of Parliament that abolished rotten boroughs, redistributed seats to the new industrial towns, and extended the vote to middle-class property owners.">Great Reform Act</dfn> of 1832 was passed by Lord Grey&rsquo;s Whig government after sustained popular pressure. It abolished the worst rotten boroughs, gave seats to the new industrial towns (Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Sheffield), and extended the vote in boroughs to men who occupied property worth at least &pound;10 a year.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Act nearly doubled the electorate from around 400,000 to around 650,000. But it remained a very partial reform: it said nothing about the secret ballot (votes were still cast publicly, leaving tenants vulnerable to landlord pressure), and it did not enfranchise working-class men or women. It was a reform that preserved property ownership as the qualification for political participation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1867 Act, passed by Benjamin Disraeli's Conservative government, which extended the vote in towns to male householders and lodgers, roughly doubling the urban electorate.">Second Reform Act</dfn> of 1867 was a much bolder step. Passed by Benjamin Disraeli&rsquo;s Conservative government, it extended the vote in boroughs to male householders and some lodgers, adding around a million new voters and giving the vote to many urban working-class men for the first time. Disraeli calculated that the newly enfranchised working class would be grateful and vote Conservative &mdash; a calculation that proved partly wrong.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">William Gladstone&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1884 Act, passed by Gladstone's Liberal government, which extended the same voting rights given to urban workers in 1867 to rural labourers.">Third Reform Act</dfn> of 1884 extended the same franchise to rural areas, adding another 1.5 million voters. The Redistribution Act of 1885 followed, redrawing constituency boundaries to reflect population. By 1885, roughly two-thirds of adult men could vote &mdash; but no women, and not the poorest urban men.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Suffragists and Suffragettes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The campaign for women&rsquo;s suffrage developed two distinct wings, which students must not confuse. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS), founded 1897, led by Millicent Fawcett, which pursued women's votes through constitutional lobbying and non-violent campaigning.">suffragists</dfn> &mdash; the NUWSS led by Millicent Fawcett &mdash; pursued women&rsquo;s votes through constitutional means: petitions, meetings, lobbying MPs, and peaceful demonstration. Fawcett had been campaigning since the 1860s and believed patient constitutional pressure would succeed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU), founded 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst, which used militant tactics including arson, window-smashing and hunger strikes to demand votes for women.">suffragettes</dfn> &mdash; the WSPU founded in 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst &mdash; took direct action: smashing windows, chaining themselves to railings, arson attacks on post boxes and empty buildings, and hunger strikes in prison. Emily Davison was killed at Epsom on 4 June 1913 when she ran onto the racecourse during the Derby. Emmeline&rsquo;s daughters Christabel and Sylvia both played prominent roles, though they later disagreed: Christabel continued the militant campaign, while Sylvia worked with the labour movement and opposed the First World War.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what is the difference between suffragists and suffragettes? Name the leader and organisation of each, and one tactic each used.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Suffragists (NUWSS, led by Millicent Fawcett) used constitutional means &mdash; petitions and lobbying. Suffragettes (WSPU, led by Emmeline Pankhurst) used militant direct action &mdash; arson, window-smashing and hunger strikes. Examiners penalise using these terms interchangeably.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1918 Act that gave the vote to all men over 21 and to women over 30 who met a property qualification — the largest single extension of the franchise in British history.">Representation of the People Act 1918</dfn> gave the vote to all men over 21 and to women over 30 who met a property qualification. Around 8.4 million women gained the vote. The age and property bar on women was clearly discriminatory, but it prevented women &mdash; who outnumbered men after the war&rsquo;s casualties &mdash; from forming a majority of the electorate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Why 1918? Historians point to several factors: women&rsquo;s vital contributions to the war effort (munitions, nursing, transport) made it impossible to deny their civic participation; the WSPU had suspended militancy during the war and thrown itself into patriotic service; the Representation of the People Act was also driven by the need to give the vote to millions of servicemen who had lost it by moving away from their homes. Full equal franchise came in 1928, when the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1928 Act that gave the vote to all women over 21, equalising the franchise between men and women.">Equal Franchise Act</dfn> lowered the voting age for women to 21, equalising the franchise.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">The Great Reform Act (1832) abolished rotten boroughs and extended the vote to the middle class, but left working-class men and all women unrepresented.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The Second (1867) and Third (1884) Reform Acts progressively extended male suffrage to urban and then rural workers &mdash; by 1885 roughly two-thirds of adult men could vote.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Women over 30 with a property qualification won the vote in 1918; equal franchise for all women over 21 came in 1928, driven by both the constitutional NUWSS campaign and the militant WSPU, alongside the impact of the First World War.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">Examiner reports for this unit specifically flag the error of using &ldquo;suffragette&rdquo; as a generic term for all women who campaigned for the vote. The WSPU (Emmeline Pankhurst) and the NUWSS (Millicent Fawcett) were distinct organisations with different tactics and different views on the effectiveness of militancy. In an &ldquo;Explain Why&rdquo; question on why women won the vote in 1918, a top-band answer will weigh both organisations&rsquo; contributions alongside the impact of the First World War &mdash; and should not present the WSPU as the sole or even primary cause.</p>
