From the Berlin Wall to the White House lawn: the unipolar moment that produced Oslo.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The End of the Cold War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Between November 1989 and December 1991 the geopolitical map of the Middle East was rewritten by events thousands of miles away. The Berlin Wall came down; the Warsaw Pact dissolved; the Soviet Union itself ceased to exist on 26 December 1991. Arab states that had relied on Soviet weapons, advisers and political cover &mdash; Syria most of all, but also Iraq, Egypt before 1972 and South Yemen &mdash; lost their patron. The United States entered what one of its strategists called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The period after the Soviet collapse, c.1991&ndash;2001, when the United States was the only superpower and could impose diplomatic frameworks without a rival to obstruct them.">unipolar moment</dfn>. For the first time since 1945 there was no second great power to fund Arab rejectionism. Cold War alignments that had blocked every previous peace effort lost their force.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: when the Soviet Union dissolved, what the &lsquo;unipolar moment&rsquo; meant, and which Middle Eastern state lost its main superpower patron.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Soviet Union dissolved on 26 December 1991. Syria and Iraq lost their main superpower patron; the United States entered a &lsquo;unipolar moment&rsquo; with no Cold War rival to block diplomatic frameworks. Arab rejectionism lost the financial and political backing it had relied on for forty years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Iraq, Kuwait and the PLO&rsquo;s Mistake</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">On 2 August 1990 Iraq invaded and annexed Kuwait. President George H. W. Bush built a thirty-five-state coalition under UN Security Council Resolution 678 that included not only the NATO allies but Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Syria and Morocco &mdash; an Arab-state line-up that would have been impossible during the Cold War. Yasser Arafat, gambling on his domestic Palestinian audience, publicly supported Saddam Hussein. The Gulf states retaliated by expelling around 350,000 Palestinian guest workers from Kuwait alone after liberation; Saudi, Kuwaiti and Gulf Cooperation Council aid to the PLO was cut, leaving the organisation roughly $300 million a year poorer. Arafat&rsquo;s 1990 alignment cost the PLO the Arab funding it had relied on since the late 1960s &mdash; a financial squeeze that pushed it towards negotiation by 1992.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Gulf War, January&ndash;February 1991</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The air campaign of <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1991 US-led coalition military operation that expelled Iraqi forces from Kuwait following Saddam Hussein&rsquo;s invasion of August 1990; it tested the post-Cold War security order and reshaped Middle Eastern alignments.">the Gulf War</dfn> began on 17 January 1991. Saddam attempted to break the Arab portion of the coalition by firing thirty-nine Scud missiles at Israeli cities &mdash; if Israel retaliated, Egypt, Syria and Saudi Arabia could not stand alongside the Israelis. Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir, after intense US pressure and the deployment of US Patriot missiles in Tel Aviv, held back. The ground war began on 24 February and ended in a ceasefire one hundred hours later. The coalition held; the United States emerged as the only conceivable broker of any future Middle East settlement; and Israeli restraint had been visibly worth something to the United States, providing a debt the next administration could collect.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Students sometimes treat the Rabin-Arafat handshake as a sudden breakthrough. The strongest answers show the chain. The end of the Cold War removed Soviet support for Arab rejectionism. The Gulf War cost the PLO its Gulf state funding. The Madrid Conference of October 1991 brought every regional state to the same table for the first time. Rabin&rsquo;s narrow election win in June 1992 put a Labour government willing to negotiate in office. Arafat, weakened in Tunis, was finally pliable. None of those four conditions had been present a decade earlier; together they made Oslo possible. Nothing about it was inevitable, but everything about it was caused.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Madrid, October 1991</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">From 30 October to 1 November 1991 the United States and the dying Soviet Union co-hosted the <dfn class="term" data-def="The October&ndash;November 1991 international peace conference in the Spanish capital, co-hosted by the US and the USSR; the first occasion on which Israel, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and a Palestinian delegation sat at the same table.">Madrid Conference</dfn>. For the first time Israel, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and a Palestinian delegation (formally part of the Jordanian delegation, with PLO members vetted by Israel) sat at the same negotiating table. Madrid produced no breakthrough but it set up bilateral tracks &mdash; Israeli-Syrian, Israeli-Lebanese, Israeli-Jordanian, Israeli-Palestinian &mdash; that ran in parallel for the next few years. The Madrid track was visible, multilateral, and slow; the deal that broke through came from a track no one in Madrid was watching.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Norwegian Channel, 1992&ndash;93</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In June 1992 Yitzhak Rabin&rsquo;s Labour party won the Israeli election, narrowly displacing Shamir&rsquo;s Likud. From January 1993 the Norwegian government hosted secret meetings outside Oslo between Israeli academics (later Israeli government negotiators) and PLO representatives. The two sides reached a draft Declaration of Principles in August 1993. On 9 September Arafat wrote to Rabin recognising Israel&rsquo;s right to exist; Rabin replied recognising the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people. On 13 September 1993, on the South Lawn of the White House, Rabin and Arafat signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Israeli-PLO Declaration of Principles signed in Washington on 13 September 1993, providing for mutual recognition, a five-year interim period and Palestinian self-government in Gaza and Jericho first.">Declaration of Principles</dfn> in front of US President Bill Clinton, who hosted but did not negotiate. The reluctant handshake between Rabin and Arafat became the defining image of post-Cold War Middle East diplomacy.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: where the Oslo back-channel was hosted, when the Declaration of Principles was signed, and what it established between the two sides.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Oslo back-channel was hosted by Norway from January 1993. On 13 September 1993 Rabin and Arafat signed the Declaration of Principles in Washington with Clinton as host. The two sides exchanged letters of mutual recognition four days earlier; the agreement set out a five-year interim period and self-government for Gaza and Jericho first.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">From Declaration to Authority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Implementation followed quickly. In May 1994 the Cairo Agreement transferred Gaza and Jericho to Palestinian self-government. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Palestinian self-governing body established under the Oslo Accords from May 1994; assumed civil administration in parts of Gaza, Jericho and (after 1995) the major West Bank towns.">Palestinian National Authority</dfn> took over civil administration; Arafat returned to Gaza on 1 July 1994 after twenty-seven years in exile. On 26 October 1994 King Hussein and Rabin signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace treaty signed at Wadi Araba on 26 October 1994 between Israel and Jordan; ended the state of war and remains in force.">Israel-Jordan peace treaty</dfn>, the second Arab-Israeli treaty after the 1979 Treaty of Washington. On 28 September 1995 Rabin and Arafat signed the Interim Agreement, known as Oslo II, in Washington. The agreement divided the West Bank into <dfn class="term" data-def="The three categories of West Bank territory created by Oslo II in 1995: A under full Palestinian Authority control; B under PA civil control and Israeli security; C under full Israeli control.">Areas A, B and C</dfn>: A under full PA control, B under PA civil control and Israeli security, C under full Israeli control.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Rabin&rsquo;s Assassination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Oslo had powerful Israeli opponents. Settler movements and right-wing rallies in 1994&ndash;95 portrayed Rabin as a traitor; some demonstrators waved posters of him in Nazi uniform. On 4 November 1995, after speaking at a peace rally in Tel Aviv&rsquo;s Kings of Israel Square, Rabin was shot twice by Yigal Amir, a 25-year-old Jewish religious nationalist. He died on the operating table. Hamas, which had rejected the Oslo Accords from the start, escalated suicide bombings of Israeli buses and cafes in 1995&ndash;96. Shimon Peres lost the 1996 election by a fraction of a percent to Likud&rsquo;s Benjamin Netanyahu, who had campaigned against the Accords. The Oslo process did not formally end in 1995; the political momentum behind it largely did.</p>

Bilateral peace between Israel and named Arab states held: the 1979 Egypt treaty was still in force, the 1994 Israel-Jordan treaty held, and Gulf states began informal contacts with Israel from the early 1990s. The Palestinian-Israeli track produced mutual recognition, a Palestinian Authority and the Areas A/B/C division of the West Bank &mdash; real institutional change &mdash; but the &lsquo;final-status&rsquo; issues (refugees, Jerusalem, settlements, borders) were deferred indefinitely and never resolved. Strong answers say so plainly rather than judging the whole peace project a success or a failure.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The collapse of the Soviet Union in December 1991 ended Arab rejectionism&rsquo;s superpower patron and produced the &lsquo;unipolar moment&rsquo; in which the United States could broker frameworks without rival.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The 1991 Gulf War cost the PLO its Gulf state funding after Arafat backed Saddam, weakening it economically and politically; the Madrid Conference of October 1991 brought every regional state to the same negotiating table for the first time.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Oslo Accords (Washington, 13 September 1993) produced mutual recognition between Israel and the PLO, the establishment of the Palestinian National Authority in 1994, the Israel-Jordan peace treaty in October 1994 and the Areas A/B/C division of the West Bank in Oslo II (1995); Rabin&rsquo;s assassination in November 1995 stalled the political momentum that had produced them.</li>
</ul>
