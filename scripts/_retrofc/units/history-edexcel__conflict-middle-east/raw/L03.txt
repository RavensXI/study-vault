--- DESCRIPTION ---
Five Arab armies, the new Israeli state and the displacement of around 700,000 Palestinians.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five-Front Invasion, May 1948</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the British Mandate ended at midnight on 14&ndash;15 May 1948, the regular armies of Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon crossed into the territory of the new state of Israel. Egypt advanced from the south through Gaza and the Negev. Jordan&rsquo;s British-trained <dfn class="term" data-def="Jordan&rsquo;s professional army, established by the British during the Mandate; the most effective Arab force in 1948.">Arab Legion</dfn> moved into the West Bank and east Jerusalem. Syria attacked from the north-east, Lebanon from the north and Iraqi units operated alongside the Jordanians. On paper the Arab forces outnumbered Israel&rsquo;s, but they had no unified command, no shared plan and rivalries between the leaders. King Abdullah of Jordan in particular wanted the West Bank for himself rather than for a Palestinian state.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which five Arab states invaded Israel in May 1948 and which directions did each attack from?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On 15 May 1948 the armies of Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon invaded the new state of Israel. They had numerical advantages on paper but no unified command, conflicting war aims, and were facing a Yishuv that had been preparing for this moment since 1945.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Phases of the War, May 1948 &ndash; January 1949</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The war can be divided into clear phases. The first phase from 15 May to 11 June saw heavy fighting and Arab gains, particularly in Jerusalem where the Arab Legion seized the Old City and expelled its Jewish population. A first UN truce ran from 11 June to 8 July, brokered by the UN mediator Count <dfn class="term" data-def="Swedish diplomat appointed by the UN as mediator in Palestine in 1948; assassinated by Lehi in Jerusalem in September 1948.">Folke Bernadotte</dfn>. During the truce Israel re-armed, smuggling weapons in from Czechoslovakia, while the Arab states quarrelled. When fighting resumed in the &lsquo;Ten Days&rsquo; from 8&ndash;18 July, Israel went on the offensive and captured Lod, Ramle and Nazareth. A second truce held from 19 July to 15 October, although Bernadotte was assassinated by Lehi gunmen in September after he proposed a revised plan giving the Negev to the Arab side.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">From October 1948 to January 1949 Israel launched a series of offensives that broke the Arab armies. Operation Yoav drove Egypt out of most of the Negev. Operation Hiram cleared Lebanese and Arab Liberation Army forces from Galilee. Operation Horev pushed Egyptian forces back to the Sinai border. By January 1949 Egypt had agreed to talks. The pattern was clear: the longer the war ran, the more the balance shifted in Israel&rsquo;s favour as it mobilised its full population, while Arab armies struggled to keep their volunteer or expeditionary forces supplied at distance.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Sources written from each side often present the war as a heroic David-and-Goliath struggle &mdash; either Israel against five invading armies, or the Arabs trying to defend Palestine from a well-armed Yishuv. Both pictures contain truth and exaggeration. The Arab armies fielded around 25,000 troops at the outset, while the Yishuv had mobilised the Haganah and was already turning it into the IDF; by the autumn Israel fielded over 100,000 soldiers and was outnumbering its opponents in the field. The new Israeli army also had a unified command, internal lines of communication and a population fighting for its existence. The Arab Legion was professional but small; the other Arab armies were ill-prepared and uncoordinated. Numerical comparisons alone do not explain the outcome.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Battle for Jerusalem</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Jerusalem was the war&rsquo;s most contested ground. The Arab Legion drove Jewish forces out of the Old City in May 1948, expelling its Jewish population from the Jewish Quarter. West Jerusalem remained in Israeli hands but was nearly cut off &mdash; supply convoys had to fight their way along the road from Tel Aviv. The Israelis built the &lsquo;Burma Road&rsquo;, a hastily improvised track through the Judean hills, to bypass Latrun where Jordanian forces blocked the main route. The fighting ended with the city divided: Jordan held the Old City and east Jerusalem, Israel held the west. This division persisted until 1967.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The 1949 Armistice Agreements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">From January to July 1949 Israel signed separate <dfn class="term" data-def="A formal agreement to stop fighting, less binding than a peace treaty.">armistice</dfn> agreements with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria. The Egypt agreement was negotiated on the Greek island of Rhodes under UN mediation. Iraq did not sign &mdash; its forces simply withdrew. The agreements drew the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1949 armistice line between Israel and its neighbours, drawn in green ink on the negotiator&rsquo;s map and treated as the de facto border until 1967.">Green Line</dfn>, which was treated as the de facto Israeli border until 1967. Crucially, none of the agreements were peace treaties. The Arab states refused to recognise Israel as a permanent neighbour and maintained an economic boycott. The way the war ended therefore guaranteed it would not stay ended.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: roughly what percentage of Mandate Palestine did Israel hold by 1949, and how did that compare to the UN partition plan?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By the 1949 armistices Israel held about 78% of former Mandate Palestine &mdash; around 22% more than the UN partition plan had allocated. Egypt held the Gaza Strip; Jordan held the West Bank and east Jerusalem (formally annexed in 1950).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Territorial Outcome and Two Names for the Same War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The war redrew the map of Palestine. Israel&rsquo;s share grew from the 56 per cent allocated by Resolution 181 to about 78 per cent. Egypt held the Gaza Strip, where roughly 200,000 Palestinian refugees crowded into a narrow coastal area. Jordan held the West Bank, including east Jerusalem, and formally annexed it in 1950 &mdash; an act recognised only by Britain and Pakistan. The Palestinian state envisaged by the UN partition plan was never created. How the war is named reflects how it is remembered: in Israel it is called the War of Independence; among Palestinians it is called the <em>Nakba</em>, meaning &lsquo;catastrophe&rsquo;. Both labels can appear in good GCSE answers as long as the student treats them as competing names for the same event.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Refugee Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Around 700,000 Palestinian Arabs &mdash; roughly half the pre-war Arab population &mdash; left or were forced from the territory that became Israel. The causes were varied: direct expulsion in some cases (e.g. Lod and Ramle in July 1948), flight from advancing Israeli forces, instruction by Arab commanders to leave temporarily, and panic after the Deir Yassin attack of April 1948. Israeli &lsquo;New Historians&rsquo; in the 1980s used newly opened archives to argue that displacement was more systematic than the older Israeli account had admitted &mdash; this is named here only as historiographical context, not as a Paper 2 interpretation question. Refugee camps were established in Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, the West Bank and Gaza, run by UNRWA from 1949 (covered in Lesson 4).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n17">Defeat in 1948 destabilised every Arab regime that fought. King <dfn class="term" data-def="King of Egypt to 1952; abdicated after a Free Officers coup in the wake of defeat in the 1948&ndash;49 war.">Farouk</dfn> of Egypt was overthrown by the Free Officers in 1952, opening the way for Nasser. Syria saw three military coups in 1949 alone. King Abdullah of Jordan was assassinated at al-Aqsa in 1951 by a Palestinian who blamed him for partitioning Palestine with Israel. The defeat fed pan-Arab nationalism and a generation of officers who believed only revolution at home would deliver victory abroad &mdash; a thread that runs straight to Suez 1956 and beyond.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Five Arab armies invaded Israel on 15 May 1948 but had no unified command; the war ran in four phases punctuated by UN truces under Folke Bernadotte until his assassination by Lehi.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The 1949 armistices drew the Green Line: Israel held 78% of Mandate Palestine, Egypt held Gaza, Jordan annexed the West Bank and east Jerusalem; no peace treaty was signed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Around 700,000 Palestinian Arabs became refugees &mdash; the event Israelis call the War of Independence and Palestinians call the Nakba &mdash; and the Arab states&rsquo; military defeat destabilised regimes from Cairo to Damascus.</li>
</ul>
