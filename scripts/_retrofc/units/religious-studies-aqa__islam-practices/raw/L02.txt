--- DESCRIPTION ---
How and why Muslims pray, the rituals of Salah, and fasting during Ramadan.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Salah — The Second Pillar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Compulsory prayer performed five times daily by Muslims, involving specific movements, recitations and conditions.">Salah</dfn> is the second of the Five Pillars and the most regular act of worship in a Muslim’s life. It is not simply asking God for things — it is a structured, formal act of submission to Allah that involves the whole body and mind. The Qur’an commands: “Establish prayer for My remembrance” (Qur’an 20:14).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">The Five Daily Prayers</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Sunni Muslims pray at five set times each day, calculated from the position of the sun. The times shift slightly throughout the year. This constant rhythm of prayer is designed to keep Allah at the centre of a Muslim’s day, no matter what else they are doing.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name all five daily prayers and their approximate times.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five daily prayers are: <strong>Fajr</strong> (before sunrise), <strong>Zuhr</strong> (just after midday), <strong>Asr</strong> (afternoon), <strong>Maghrib</strong> (just after sunset), and <strong>Isha</strong> (night). Shi’a Muslims perform the same five prayers but may combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying three times a day.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Preparing for Prayer — Wudu</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Before praying, Muslims must perform <dfn class="term" data-def="Ritual washing (ablution) carried out before prayer, involving washing the hands, face, arms, head and feet in a set order.">wudu</dfn> — a ritual washing that prepares the body and mind for standing in God’s presence. Wudu involves washing the hands, mouth, nose, face, forearms up to the elbows, wiping the head and ears, and washing the feet up to the ankles. The Qur’an instructs: “You who believe, when you are about to pray, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, wipe your heads, wash your feet up to the ankles” (Qur’an 5:6). If water is unavailable, Muslims may use clean earth or sand in a practice called <dfn class="term" data-def="A dry form of ritual purification using clean earth or sand, permitted when water is unavailable.">tayammum</dfn>.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n8">Direction, Movements and Recitations</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n9">All Muslims face the direction of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The cube-shaped building in Makkah that Muslims face during prayer, believed to have been built by Ibrahim and Isma’il.">Ka’aba</dfn> in Makkah when they pray. In a mosque, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A niche in the wall of a mosque indicating the direction of Makkah for prayer.">mihrab</dfn> shows this direction. Each prayer consists of a set number of <dfn class="term" data-def="A unit of prayer consisting of standing, bowing, prostrating and sitting, repeated a set number of times.">rak’ah</dfn> — cycles of standing, bowing, prostrating and sitting, accompanied by recitations from the Qur’an in Arabic. The physical act of prostration — placing the forehead on the ground — symbolises total submission to Allah.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Muslims can pray anywhere that is clean — at home, at work, or outdoors. However, praying in the mosque is considered more meritorious, especially for men. Muhammad said that prayer in congregation is “twenty-seven times more rewarding than prayer performed individually” (Hadith, Sahih al-Bukhari). In the mosque, an <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who leads communal prayers in a mosque and delivers the Friday sermon.">imam</dfn> leads the prayer, and worshippers stand in rows facing the mihrab. Women may pray in a separate area or at home. At home, Muslims use a prayer mat and a compass to find the direction of Makkah.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Jummah — Friday Congregational Prayer</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The compulsory Friday midday prayer performed in congregation at the mosque, including a sermon by the imam.">Jummah</dfn> is the Friday congregational prayer, held at the mosque just after midday. It replaces the normal Zuhr prayer. All adult male Muslims are expected to attend, and women may also attend. The imam delivers a <dfn class="term" data-def="A sermon delivered by the imam during Jummah prayer, offering religious guidance and reminding Muslims of their duties.">khutbah</dfn> (sermon) covering religious guidance, moral teaching and community matters. The Qur’an instructs: “Believers! When the call to prayer is made on the day of congregation, hurry towards the reminder of God and leave off your trading” (Qur’an 62:9). Jummah strengthens the ummah by bringing Muslims together as a community each week.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="From memory, list two differences between Sunni and Shi'a practices in Salah.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sunni and Shi’a Muslims both perform the five daily prayers, but Shi’a Muslims often combine them into three sessions. Shi’a Muslims may also place a small clay tablet called a <strong>turbah</strong> on the ground to prostrate upon, as they believe the forehead should touch natural earth rather than carpet.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Sawm — Fasting During Ramadan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Fasting from dawn to sunset during the month of Ramadan, abstaining from food, drink and sinful behaviour.">Sawm</dfn> is the fourth pillar of Islam. During the holy month of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ninth month of the Islamic calendar, during which the Qur’an was first revealed and Muslims fast from dawn to sunset.">Ramadan</dfn>, Muslims fast from dawn (Fajr) to sunset (Maghrib). They abstain from all food, drink, smoking and sexual relations during daylight hours. The fast is broken each evening with a meal called <dfn class="term" data-def="The meal eaten at sunset to break the daily fast during Ramadan, traditionally beginning with dates and water.">iftar</dfn>, often shared with family and community.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Origins and Significance</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Ramadan is the month in which the Qur’an was first revealed to Muhammad through the angel Jibril. The Qur’an commands: “It was in the month of Ramadan that the Qur’an was revealed as guidance for mankind. So any one of you who is present that month should fast” (Qur’an 2:185). Fasting teaches self-discipline, empathy for the poor, and gratitude to Allah for His provision.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits, Exceptions and the Night of Power</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Benefits of fasting:</strong> Sawm develops taqwa (God-consciousness), strengthens willpower, creates empathy with those who go hungry, and brings the community together through shared iftar meals and increased mosque attendance. Muslims believe fasting purifies the soul and brings them closer to Allah.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Exceptions:</strong> Certain people are exempt from fasting: young children, the elderly, pregnant or breastfeeding women, people who are ill, travellers, and those menstruating. Most of these people are expected to make up the missed days later or feed a poor person for each day missed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power):</strong> This falls within the last ten nights of Ramadan and commemorates the night the Qur’an was first revealed. The Qur’an says: “The Night of Power is better than a thousand months” (Qur’an 97:3). Many Muslims spend the entire night in prayer, believing that worship on this night earns greater reward than a thousand months of ordinary worship.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the two key Qur'anic references for Ramadan and Laylat al-Qadr from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power) falls in the last ten nights of Ramadan. It commemorates the first revelation of the Qur’an and is described as “better than a thousand months” (Qur’an 97:3). Many Muslims spend the night in prayer and reflection.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23">Salah involves praying five times daily after performing wudu, facing Makkah, and completing rak’ah. Jummah on Friday is a compulsory congregational prayer. Shi’a Muslims may combine prayers into three sessions.</li><li data-narration-id="n24">Sawm during Ramadan means fasting from dawn to sunset. It commemorates the revelation of the Qur’an, teaches self-discipline and empathy, and brings the community together.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power) in the last ten nights of Ramadan is considered more valuable than a thousand months of worship.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">When answering questions on Salah, always mention both the physical preparations (wudu, facing Makkah) and the spiritual purpose (submission to God, maintaining God-consciousness). For Sawm questions, link the practice back to the Qur’an (2:185 and 97:3) and explain both the personal benefits (self-discipline, empathy) and the communal benefits (shared iftar, increased worship). In “Explain Similarities & Differences” questions, the Sunni/Shi’a prayer differences (combining prayers, turbah) are excellent points to develop.</p>
