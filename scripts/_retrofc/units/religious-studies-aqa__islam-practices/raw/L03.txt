--- DESCRIPTION ---
The role and significance of Zakah, Khums in Shi'a Islam, and the pilgrimage to Makkah (Hajj).

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Zakah — Almsgiving</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The third pillar of Islam, requiring Muslims to give 2.5% of their annual savings to those in need.">Zakah</dfn> is the third pillar of Islam and means “purification” or “growth.” By giving away a portion of their wealth, Muslims purify what remains and acknowledge that all wealth ultimately belongs to Allah. It is not considered charity in the voluntary sense — it is a compulsory duty for every Muslim whose savings exceed a minimum threshold called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum amount of wealth a Muslim must possess before Zakah becomes obligatory, roughly equivalent to 85g of gold.">nisab</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state the percentage for Zakah and explain the nisab threshold.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Muslims must give <strong>2.5%</strong> of their annual savings above the nisab threshold. The Qur’an specifies eight categories of people who may receive Zakah, including the poor, the needy, those in debt, and travellers in difficulty (Qur’an 9:60).</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Origins and Purpose</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Zakah has its origins in the Qur’an and the practice of the Prophet Muhammad. The Qur’an links prayer and charity repeatedly: “Establish prayer and pay Zakah” (Qur’an 2:43). This shows that worship of God and care for others are inseparable in Islam. The purpose of Zakah is threefold: it purifies the giver from greed and selfishness, it supports those in genuine need, and it reduces inequality within the ummah.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits of Zakah for Individuals and Communities</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>For the giver:</strong> Zakah purifies wealth by removing attachment to material possessions. It reminds Muslims that everything they own is a trust from Allah and develops generosity and compassion. Muslims believe that giving Zakah brings spiritual reward and protects them on the Day of Judgement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>For the recipient:</strong> Zakah provides essential support for the poor, the sick, travellers in need and those in debt. It helps prevent extreme poverty and ensures basic needs are met. The Qur’an identifies eight specific categories of recipient (9:60), ensuring money reaches those who need it most.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>For the community:</strong> Zakah reduces the gap between rich and poor, creating a more just and equal society. It strengthens social bonds within the ummah because the wealthy are directly responsible for supporting the vulnerable. In some Muslim-majority countries, Zakah is collected and distributed by the state.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Khums in Shi’a Islam</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A 20% tax on surplus annual income paid by Shi’a Muslims, half going to descendants of the Prophet and half to religious scholars.">Khums</dfn> means “one fifth” and is an additional financial duty unique to Shi’a Islam. Shi’a Muslims pay 20% of their surplus income (after living expenses) each year. Half of the Khums goes to <dfn class="term" data-def="Descendants of the Prophet Muhammad who are entitled to receive a share of Khums in Shi’a Islam.">Sayyids</dfn> (descendants of the Prophet) and half to religious scholars and institutions. Khums is one of the Ten Obligatory Acts and reflects the Shi’a emphasis on supporting the Prophet’s family and the religious leadership.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="From memory, compare Zakah and Khums: the percentage, who pays each, and how the money is used differently.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Zakah (2.5% of savings) is paid by all Muslims, while Khums (20% of surplus income) is an additional duty for Shi’a Muslims. Both serve the purpose of social justice and redistributing wealth, but Khums also supports the Prophet’s descendants and Shi’a religious institutions.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Hajj — Pilgrimage to Makkah</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The annual pilgrimage to Makkah that every Muslim must undertake at least once in their lifetime if they are physically and financially able.">Hajj</dfn> is the fifth pillar of Islam. Every Muslim who is physically and financially able must make the pilgrimage to Makkah at least once in their lifetime. Hajj takes place during the Islamic month of Dhul Hijjah and attracts over two million Muslims from around the world each year. The Qur’an states: “Pilgrimage to the House is a duty owed to God by people who are able to undertake it” (Qur’an 3:97).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Origins</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Hajj traces its origins to the Prophet <dfn class="term" data-def="A prophet in Islam (also known as Abraham) who, with his son Isma’il, is believed to have built the Ka’aba in Makkah.">Ibrahim</dfn> (Abraham). Muslims believe that Ibrahim and his son Isma’il built the Ka’aba as the first house of worship dedicated to the one true God. Many of the rituals performed during Hajj commemorate events from Ibrahim’s life — his willingness to sacrifice his son, his wife Hajar’s search for water, and his rejection of Satan’s temptations.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">How Hajj is Performed</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Hajj lasts five to six days and involves a series of rituals at sacred sites in and around Makkah. Before beginning, pilgrims enter a state of <dfn class="term" data-def="A sacred state of spiritual purity entered before Hajj, marked by wearing simple white garments and abstaining from certain actions.">ihram</dfn> — they wear simple white garments (two unstitched cloths for men), symbolising equality before God. All distinctions of wealth, status and nationality are stripped away.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Key Rituals of Hajj</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Tawaf:</strong> Pilgrims walk around the <dfn class="term" data-def="The cube-shaped building at the centre of the Grand Mosque in Makkah, the most sacred site in Islam.">Ka’aba</dfn> seven times in an anti-clockwise direction. This represents the unity of Muslims in worshipping one God. The Ka’aba is believed to be directly below God’s throne in heaven.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Sa’i:</strong> Pilgrims walk seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, re-enacting Hajar’s desperate search for water for her son Isma’il. This commemorates God’s provision of the Zamzam well.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Arafat:</strong> Pilgrims travel to the plain of Arafat and stand in prayer and reflection from noon to sunset. This is considered the most important ritual of Hajj — the Prophet Muhammad said: “Hajj is Arafat.” It represents standing before God on the Day of Judgement.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Muzdalifah:</strong> After sunset at Arafat, pilgrims travel to Muzdalifah where they pray, rest under the open sky, and collect pebbles for the next day’s ritual.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Mina — Stoning the Pillars:</strong> At Mina, pilgrims throw pebbles at three stone pillars representing the places where Ibrahim rejected Satan’s temptations. This symbolises the Muslim’s rejection of evil and temptation in their own life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Sacrifice:</strong> An animal (usually a sheep or goat) is sacrificed at Mina, commemorating Ibrahim’s willingness to sacrifice his son in obedience to God. The meat is distributed to the poor. This coincides with the festival of Id-ul-Adha celebrated by Muslims worldwide.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the five Hajj sites in order and the ritual performed at each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The key stages of Hajj include Tawaf (circling the Ka’aba), Sa’i (walking between Safa and Marwa), standing at Arafat, collecting pebbles at Muzdalifah, stoning the pillars at Mina, and animal sacrifice. Each ritual commemorates events from the life of Ibrahim and symbolises key aspects of Muslim faith.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Zakah requires Muslims to give 2.5% of their savings above the nisab threshold to those in need. It purifies wealth, supports the poor, and reduces inequality. Shi’a Muslims also pay Khums (20% of surplus income).</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Hajj is a once-in-a-lifetime pilgrimage to Makkah involving rituals at the Ka’aba, Safa and Marwa, Arafat, Muzdalifah and Mina — each commemorating events from Ibrahim’s life.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">Both Zakah and Hajj demonstrate submission to Allah, strengthen the global ummah, and connect Muslims to the origins of their faith.</li></ul>
