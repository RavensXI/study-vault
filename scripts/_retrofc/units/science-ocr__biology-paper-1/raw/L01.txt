--- DESCRIPTION ---
Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, sub-cellular structures, and how microscopy reveals them.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Eukaryotic and Prokaryotic Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All living organisms are made of <dfn class="term" data-def="The fundamental unit of all living organisms, containing sub-cellular structures that carry out life processes.">cells</dfn>. You need to understand two fundamental cell types: <dfn class="term" data-def="A cell with a true nucleus enclosed by a membrane, plus membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic.">eukaryotic</dfn> cells and <dfn class="term" data-def="A small, simple cell with no true nucleus. The genetic material is a single DNA loop in the cytoplasm. Bacteria are prokaryotic.">prokaryotic</dfn> cells.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Eukaryotic cells include animal and plant cells. They have a <strong>nucleus</strong> that encloses their genetic material within a nuclear membrane. They also contain membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria and ribosomes. Prokaryotic cells — such as bacteria — are much smaller and simpler. They lack a true nucleus; instead, their genetic material is a single loop of DNA floating freely in the cytoplasm. Prokaryotes may also contain small rings of extra DNA called <dfn class="term" data-def="Small circular rings of extra DNA found in prokaryotic cells that can replicate independently of the main chromosomal DNA.">plasmids</dfn>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Draw a comparison table of eukaryotic vs prokaryotic cells. Include: nucleus, DNA form, size, organelles. This is one of the most frequently examined topics in B1.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Eukaryotic cells (animal and plant) have a nucleus that encloses their DNA. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no true nucleus — their DNA is a single loop in the cytoplasm, and they may also contain plasmids.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Sub-cellular Structures and Their Functions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Both animal and plant cells are eukaryotic, but they contain different sub-cellular structures. The exam requires you to explain how each structure's design relates to its function. This means you need to go beyond naming the structure — you must say <em>why</em> its shape, composition or position allows it to carry out its role efficiently.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Animal Cell Structures</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Nucleus</strong> — contains the cell's genetic material (chromosomes made of DNA) and controls the cell's activities by determining which proteins are made. The nuclear membrane keeps the DNA separate from the cytoplasm, protecting the genetic code.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cytoplasm</strong> — a jelly-like substance where most chemical reactions take place. Enzymes in the cytoplasm control these metabolic reactions. It also contains structural proteins that give the cell its shape.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Cell membrane</strong> — a thin, selectively permeable layer that controls which substances enter and leave the cell. It contains <dfn class="term" data-def="Molecules on the surface of a cell membrane that detect specific chemical signals such as hormones.">receptor molecules</dfn> that allow the cell to respond to chemical signals such as hormones and neurotransmitters.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Mitochondria</strong> — the site of aerobic <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which cells break down glucose to release energy, transferring it to ATP. It occurs in the mitochondria (aerobic) or cytoplasm (anaerobic).">respiration</dfn>. They contain the enzymes needed for cellular respiration and transfer energy for the cell's processes. Cells with high energy demands (such as muscle cells) contain more mitochondria.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Ribosomes</strong> — tiny structures in the cytoplasm where <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which ribosomes use the genetic code from DNA (via mRNA) to assemble amino acids into proteins.">protein synthesis</dfn> occurs. They are found in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, though they are smaller in prokaryotes.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Additional Plant Cell Structures</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Plant cells have all the structures listed above, plus three additional ones that reflect their roles in photosynthesis and structural support:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Chloroplasts</strong> — contain the green pigment <dfn class="term" data-def="The green pigment found in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.">chlorophyll</dfn> and are the site of photosynthesis. They absorb light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose. Chloroplasts are found mainly in leaf cells and cells near the surface of stems, where light is available.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Permanent vacuole</strong> — a large, fluid-filled space containing cell sap (a solution of sugars and salts dissolved in water). It helps maintain the cell's rigidity by pressing the cytoplasm against the cell wall, keeping the cell <strong>turgid</strong> and supporting the plant structure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Cell wall</strong> — made of cellulose and surrounds the cell membrane. It strengthens and supports the cell, preventing it from bursting when water enters by osmosis. Unlike the cell membrane, the cell wall is fully permeable and allows all dissolved substances through.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Remember: plant cells have THREE extra structures compared to animal cells — cell wall, chloroplasts and permanent vacuole. Bacterial cells have a cell wall but NO nucleus, NO mitochondria and NO chloroplasts.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Plant cells have a cell wall (cellulose), chloroplasts (photosynthesis) and a permanent vacuole (cell sap) in addition to animal cell organelles. Bacterial cells have a cell wall, but no chloroplasts, no true nucleus and no mitochondria.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Microscopy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Microscopes allow us to see cells and sub-cellular structures invisible to the naked eye. You need to know about <strong>light microscopes</strong> and <strong>electron microscopes</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Light microscopes use glass lenses and visible light. They have a useful magnification of about \(\times 1500\) and a <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to distinguish between two closely spaced points as separate objects. Higher resolution means finer detail is visible.">resolution</dfn> of around 200 nm. To prepare a slide, place a thin sample on a glass slide, add a drop of stain (such as iodine or methylene blue) to make colourless structures visible, then lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Preparing and Using a Light Microscope</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">You should be able to describe how to prepare slides, use staining techniques, and calculate magnification. Key steps:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">1. Place a thin sample (e.g. onion epidermis or cheek cells) on a clean glass slide. 2. Add a drop of stain — <strong>iodine</strong> stains plant cell walls and nuclei yellow-brown, while <strong>methylene blue</strong> stains animal cell nuclei blue. 3. Gently lower a coverslip at 45° to avoid air bubbles. 4. Start on the lowest magnification objective lens, focus, then switch to higher magnifications.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">To calculate the total magnification: \(\text{total magnification} = \text{eyepiece lens} \times \text{objective lens}\). For example, a \(\times 10\) eyepiece with a \(\times 40\) objective gives \(\times 400\) magnification.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n23">Electron microscopes fire a beam of electrons at the specimen. A <strong>transmission electron microscope (TEM)</strong> has much higher resolution — down to about 0.2 nm — and a magnification of up to \(\times 2\,000\,000\). This allowed biologists to see sub-cellular structures like ribosomes and the internal folds of mitochondria (cristae) in fine detail, greatly increasing our understanding of how cells work.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The development of electron microscopy was a turning point in biology. Before electron microscopes, scientists could see cells and nuclei with light microscopes, but they could not resolve the detailed internal structure of organelles. Electron microscopy revealed that mitochondria have folded inner membranes (cristae) that increase the surface area for respiration enzymes, explaining why mitochondria are so efficient at energy transfer.</p>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="The magnification formula is a favourite calculation question. Always convert both measurements to the SAME unit before dividing. Use: magnification = image size ÷ real size.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The magnification formula: \(\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{real size}}\). Always ensure both measurements are in the same units before calculating. Electron microscopes have much higher resolution (~0.2 nm) than light microscopes (~200 nm), allowing sub-cellular detail to be seen.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Eukaryotic cells (animal and plant) have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no true nucleus, a single DNA loop, and may contain plasmids.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a permanent vacuole in addition to animal cell organelles. Each structure is adapted to its function.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Light microscopes use lenses and staining; electron microscopes have much higher resolution and magnification, enabling us to see sub-cellular structures in fine detail and understand their roles.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">When answering questions on cell structure, always link the structure to its <strong>function</strong> — for example, “mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration, transferring energy for the cell.” In magnification calculations, the most common mistake is forgetting to convert units (e.g. mm to μm). Show every step of your working for full marks on calculation questions. When comparing cell types, use a structured approach: state what each type <em>has</em> and what it <em>lacks</em>.</p>
