--- DESCRIPTION ---
How plants use light energy to convert CO2 and water into glucose and oxygen.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Photosynthesis?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which plants and algae use light energy to convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. It occurs in chloroplasts.">Photosynthesis</dfn> is the process by which green plants and algae produce food. They are the main <strong>producers</strong> of biomass for life on Earth — almost all food chains begin with a photosynthetic organism (B1.4a). Photosynthesis takes place in the <strong>chloroplasts</strong>, which contain the green pigment <dfn class="term" data-def="The green pigment found in chloroplasts that absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.">chlorophyll</dfn> that absorbs light energy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Photosynthesis is an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, usually as light, causing the temperature of the surroundings to decrease.">endothermic</dfn> reaction — it takes in energy from the surroundings in the form of light (B1.4c). This is the opposite of respiration, which is exothermic. The word equation:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">$$\text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} \xrightarrow{\text{light}} \text{glucose} + \text{oxygen}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The balanced symbol equation: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\)</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The OCR spec describes photosynthesis as a <strong>two-stage process</strong> (B1.4b). In the first stage, light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll and used to split water molecules, releasing oxygen as a by-product. In the second stage, the hydrogen from water is combined with carbon dioxide to produce glucose. Both stages occur in the chloroplasts.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Learn both word and symbol equations for photosynthesis. Remember it is endothermic (takes in light energy), the opposite of respiration which is exothermic. The examiner expects you to know both equations.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Photosynthesis is an endothermic reaction in chloroplasts: \(6CO_2 + 6H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2\). Light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll. It is a two-stage process: light splits water, then hydrogen combines with CO\(_2\) to make glucose.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What Happens to the Glucose?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Glucose produced by photosynthesis has several important uses in the plant:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Respiration</strong> — glucose is broken down to transfer energy for the plant’s life processes. <strong>Starch storage</strong> — excess glucose is converted to insoluble starch for storage (starch does not affect osmosis because it is insoluble). <strong>Cellulose</strong> — glucose is converted to cellulose for building cell walls. <strong>Amino acids and proteins</strong> — glucose is combined with nitrate ions absorbed from the soil to make amino acids, which are assembled into proteins. <strong>Lipids</strong> — glucose can be converted to lipids (fats and oils) for energy storage in seeds.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Factors Affecting the Rate of Photosynthesis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Three main factors affect the rate: <strong>light intensity</strong>, <strong>carbon dioxide concentration</strong> and <strong>temperature</strong> (B1.4e). Each can become a <dfn class="term" data-def="A factor that prevents the rate of photosynthesis from increasing further, even if other conditions are favourable.">limiting factor</dfn> — the factor in shortest supply that prevents the rate from increasing.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Each Factor Affects the Rate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Light intensity</strong> — as light intensity increases, the rate increases because more energy is available for the light-dependent stage. At high intensity, the rate levels off because CO\(_2\) concentration or temperature becomes the limiting factor. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A mathematical relationship where one variable is proportional to the inverse of the square of another. For photosynthesis: light intensity is proportional to 1/distance squared.">inverse square law</dfn> applies: \(\text{light intensity} \propto \frac{1}{\text{distance}^2}\). Doubling the distance from a light source quarters the intensity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Carbon dioxide concentration</strong> — increasing CO\(_2\) provides more raw material for the carbon fixation stage, so the rate increases. Eventually, the rate plateaus when another factor (light or temperature) becomes limiting. In commercial greenhouses, farmers pump in extra CO\(_2\) to maximise crop yields.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Temperature</strong> — enzymes controlling photosynthesis work faster as temperature increases up to an optimum (typically around 25–30°C for many plants). Above this, enzymes denature and the rate drops sharply. Below the optimum, molecules have less kinetic energy and collide less frequently with enzyme active sites.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n16">The three factors interact (B1.4f, Higher Tier). On a graph of rate vs. light intensity, you might see the rate plateau even though light is still increasing — this means CO\(_2\) or temperature is now the limiting factor. To increase the rate further, you would need to increase whichever factor is limiting. All three must be sufficient for the maximum rate to be achieved.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="When interpreting rate graphs, identify the plateau. If increasing light no longer raises the rate, then CO2 or temperature is now the limiting factor. State which factor is limiting and why. This is a very common 4- or 6-mark question.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A limiting factor is the one in shortest supply preventing the rate from increasing further. On a graph, it shows as a plateau. The three limiting factors interact — all must be sufficient for maximum rate.</p>
</div>
</div>

    <span>Investigating Photosynthesis (PAG B4)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A common practical uses <strong>Cabomba</strong> (pondweed) or <strong>Elodea</strong>. Place the pondweed underwater in a beaker with a lamp nearby and count oxygen bubbles produced per minute at different distances from the lamp. As the lamp moves further away, light intensity decreases (inverse square law) and fewer bubbles are produced, indicating a lower rate of photosynthesis.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">For more accurate results, collect the gas in a <strong>gas syringe</strong> or measuring cylinder over water and measure the volume per unit time, rather than counting individual bubbles. Repeat each distance at least three times and calculate a mean to improve reliability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">You can also test leaves for starch using <strong>iodine solution</strong> (B1.4d): boil the leaf in water to kill the cells, then in ethanol to remove chlorophyll (the green colour), rinse in water, and add iodine. Blue-black = starch present (photosynthesis occurred). Brown-orange = no starch (no photosynthesis). This test can show which parts of a variegated leaf photosynthesise (only the green parts).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n21">Farmers and horticulturalists use their understanding of limiting factors to maximise crop yields in <strong>greenhouses</strong>. They may use artificial lighting to extend the growing day, add CO\(_2\) from gas burners, and control temperature with heaters or ventilation. The cost of these measures must be balanced against the extra income from improved yields — there is no point adding expensive lighting if CO\(_2\) is the limiting factor.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Photosynthesis is an endothermic, two-stage reaction in chloroplasts: CO\(_2\) + water \(\rightarrow\) glucose + oxygen, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Light intensity, CO\(_2\) concentration and temperature are limiting factors that interact to determine the rate. On a graph, a plateau indicates another factor is now limiting.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Glucose is used for respiration, stored as starch, converted to cellulose for cell walls, combined with nitrates for amino acids and proteins, or converted to lipids.</li>
</ul>
