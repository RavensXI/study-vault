--- DESCRIPTION ---
Maintaining internal environments: blood glucose control, diabetes, and temperature regulation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What is Homeostasis?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The regulation of internal conditions to maintain a stable internal environment in response to changes, both internal and external.">Homeostasis</dfn> is the maintenance of a constant internal environment (B3.3a). This is crucial because enzymes and other biological processes work best within narrow ranges of temperature, pH, water balance and blood glucose concentration. If internal conditions change too much, metabolic reactions slow down, become less efficient, or stop entirely — potentially killing the organism.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">All homeostatic control systems have three key components working together: <strong>receptors</strong> (detect changes in the internal or external environment), a <strong>coordination centre</strong> (processes the information and determines the response — e.g. the brain, the pancreas or the thermoregulatory centre) and <strong>effectors</strong> (muscles or glands that bring about the corrective response). This system works through <dfn class="term" data-def="A control mechanism where the output of a system feeds back to inhibit further change, keeping conditions within a narrow range around a set point.">negative feedback</dfn> — when a condition deviates from the normal level, the body acts to bring it back, then the response is switched off.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Homeostasis means maintaining a CONSTANT internal environment. The three components are: receptor (detects change), coordination centre (processes), effector (responds). Learn this framework — it applies to blood glucose, temperature control, and water balance.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment using receptors, coordination centres and effectors working through negative feedback. It is essential for enzymes and metabolic reactions to function at their optimum rates.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Blood Glucose Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Blood glucose concentration is monitored and controlled by the <strong>pancreas</strong> (B3.3b–c). When blood glucose rises (e.g. after eating a carbohydrate-rich meal), the pancreas detects the increase and secretes <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by beta cells in the pancreas that lowers blood glucose levels by stimulating cells to take up glucose and the liver to convert glucose to glycogen for storage.">insulin</dfn>. Insulin has two main effects: it causes body cells (especially muscle and liver cells) to take up glucose from the blood, and it stimulates the liver to convert excess glucose into <strong>glycogen</strong> for storage. These actions lower the blood glucose back to normal.</p>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n7">When blood glucose drops (e.g. during exercise or between meals), the pancreas detects the decrease and secretes <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by alpha cells in the pancreas that raises blood glucose levels by stimulating the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose and release it into the blood.">glucagon</dfn>. Glucagon stimulates the liver to break stored glycogen back into glucose and release it into the blood, raising blood glucose back to normal. This opposing action of insulin and glucagon is a classic example of <strong>negative feedback</strong> — both hormones work together to keep blood glucose within a narrow, safe range.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Type 1 diabetes</strong> (B3.3d): The pancreas produces little or no insulin because the <strong>immune system has destroyed</strong> the insulin-producing beta cells (it is an <strong>autoimmune</strong> condition). Without insulin, blood glucose can rise to dangerously high levels after eating. Treatment requires regular <strong>insulin injections</strong> (or an insulin pump) combined with careful monitoring of blood glucose levels and carbohydrate intake. Type 1 usually develops in childhood or early adulthood and cannot be prevented.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong> (B3.3d): The body’s cells become <strong>resistant</strong> to insulin — they no longer respond properly to it, even though the pancreas may still produce insulin (sometimes in reduced amounts). Blood glucose remains elevated because cells do not take up glucose effectively. Type 2 is strongly linked to <strong>obesity</strong>, a sedentary lifestyle, and genetic factors. Treatment involves a <strong>controlled diet</strong> (reducing sugar and refined carbohydrate intake), regular <strong>exercise</strong>, weight loss, and sometimes medication (such as metformin). In some cases, insulin injections may eventually be needed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Type 2 diabetes is becoming increasingly common worldwide, particularly in countries with high obesity rates. It can often be prevented or managed through lifestyle changes, unlike Type 1 which cannot be prevented.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Type 1 = no insulin produced (autoimmune, treated with insulin injections, usually childhood onset). Type 2 = insulin resistance (lifestyle-linked, treated with diet, exercise and medication, usually adult onset). Do NOT mix these up — it is a very common exam error.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Type 1 diabetes: the pancreas does not produce insulin (autoimmune); treated with insulin injections. Type 2 diabetes: body cells become resistant to insulin (often lifestyle-linked, especially obesity); treated with diet, exercise and medication.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Temperature Regulation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Body temperature in humans is maintained at approximately 37°C by the <strong>thermoregulatory centre</strong> in the brain, which acts as the coordination centre. It receives information from temperature receptors in the skin (detecting external temperature) and in the blood (detecting core body temperature).</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Responses to Being Too Hot</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">When the body is too hot, several effectors are activated: blood vessels near the skin surface <strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The widening of blood vessels near the skin surface, allowing more blood to flow close to the surface and more heat to be lost to the surroundings by radiation.">vasodilate</dfn></strong> (widen), allowing more blood to flow close to the skin surface so more heat is lost to the surroundings by radiation. <strong>Sweat glands</strong> produce sweat that evaporates from the skin surface, transferring heat energy away from the body (evaporative cooling). Body hairs lie flat against the skin (reducing the insulating layer of trapped air).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Responses to Being Too Cold</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">When the body is too cold: blood vessels near the skin surface <strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The narrowing of blood vessels near the skin surface, reducing blood flow close to the surface and reducing heat loss to the surroundings.">vasoconstrict</dfn></strong> (narrow), reducing blood flow near the surface and therefore reducing heat loss by radiation. Muscles <strong>shiver</strong> — rapid, involuntary contractions that generate heat from respiration in muscle cells. Body hairs stand up (erector muscles contract), trapping a thicker layer of insulating air close to the skin.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Temperature regulation is another example of negative feedback. If the body temperature rises above 37°C, cooling mechanisms activate to bring it down. If it drops below, warming mechanisms activate to bring it up. Once the temperature returns to normal, the response is switched off.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">If temperature regulation fails, it can be life-threatening: <strong>hypothermia</strong> occurs when core body temperature drops below about 35°C (organs begin to fail), while <strong>heat stroke</strong> occurs when the body overheats and cannot cool down effectively (can cause organ damage).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Homeostasis maintains a stable internal environment using receptors, coordination centres and effectors, operating through negative feedback.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Insulin (from the pancreas) lowers blood glucose by stimulating cells to absorb glucose and the liver to store glycogen. Glucagon raises blood glucose by stimulating the liver to release glucose. Both work as negative feedback.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Type 1 diabetes = no insulin produced (autoimmune, treated with injections). Type 2 = insulin resistance (often lifestyle-linked, treated with diet, exercise and medication).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Body temperature is regulated by the thermoregulatory centre in the brain: vasodilation and sweating cool the body; vasoconstriction and shivering warm it.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n18">When comparing Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes, organise your answer in a structured comparison: cause, treatment, and link to lifestyle for each. Always specify that insulin <strong>lowers</strong> blood glucose while glucagon <strong>raises</strong> it — state the direction of change. For temperature regulation questions, use the correct terms: <strong>vasodilation</strong> (not “blood vessels get bigger”) and <strong>vasoconstriction</strong> (not “blood vessels get smaller”). Explain the mechanism, not just the name.</p>
