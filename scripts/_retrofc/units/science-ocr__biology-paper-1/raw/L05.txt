--- DESCRIPTION ---
Diffusion, osmosis, active transport, and why multicellular organisms need transport systems.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Moving Substances Into and Out of Cells</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Cells need to transport substances across their membranes to survive. There are three main mechanisms: <dfn class="term" data-def="The net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient.">diffusion</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated solution (low water potential) through a partially permeable membrane.">osmosis</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of substances from a lower concentration to a higher concentration against the concentration gradient, requiring energy from respiration.">active transport</dfn> (B2.1a).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><strong>Diffusion</strong> is the net movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration — it is passive (no energy needed). Oxygen diffuses into cells for respiration; carbon dioxide and urea diffuse out. The steeper the <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference in concentration of a substance between two areas. A steeper gradient means faster diffusion.">concentration gradient</dfn>, the faster the rate of diffusion. Temperature and surface area also affect the rate — higher temperature means faster-moving particles and more frequent collisions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Osmosis</strong> is specifically the movement of water molecules through a <dfn class="term" data-def="A membrane that allows some molecules (like water) to pass through but blocks larger molecules.">partially permeable membrane</dfn> from a dilute solution (high water potential) to a more concentrated one (low water potential). The OCR spec uses the term <strong>water potential</strong> but does not require mathematical calculations with it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Active transport</strong> moves substances <em>against</em> the concentration gradient — from low to high concentration. This requires energy from respiration. Root hair cells use active transport to absorb mineral ions from the soil, where the concentration of minerals is lower than inside the cell. The energy comes from ATP produced by mitochondria in the root hair cells.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Remember: diffusion and osmosis are PASSIVE (no energy). Active transport requires ENERGY from respiration and moves substances AGAINST the concentration gradient. This is a very common exam distinction — draw a comparison table.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Diffusion and osmosis are passive processes (no energy needed). Active transport requires energy from respiration to move substances against the concentration gradient, from low to high concentration.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Cell Cycle and Mitosis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Cells divide to produce new cells for growth, repair and replacement of damaged tissue. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The sequence of events in a cell leading to division: cell growth, DNA replication, further growth, then division by mitosis.">cell cycle</dfn> has several stages (B2.1b): first the cell grows and increases in size, then the DNA replicates so there are two copies of every chromosome, the cell grows a little more, and finally it divides by <strong>mitosis</strong>. Mitosis produces <strong>two genetically identical daughter cells</strong>, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent cell.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Differentiation</strong> is the process by which a cell becomes specialised to perform a particular function (B2.1c). Specialised cells have specific structures suited to their role, making the organism more efficient. For example, red blood cells are biconcave with no nucleus to maximise space for haemoglobin; nerve cells have long axons to transmit impulses over distance; root hair cells have extensions to increase surface area for absorption.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Stem cells</strong> are undifferentiated cells that can divide and produce different cell types (B2.1d–f). <strong>Embryonic stem cells</strong> can differentiate into any cell type in the body, making them potentially useful for treating diseases. <strong>Adult stem cells</strong> (e.g. in bone marrow) can only produce a limited range of cell types, such as blood cells. <strong>Meristems</strong> in plants contain stem cells that allow plants to continue growing and differentiating throughout their lives — unlike most animal cells, which lose the ability to differentiate early in development.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The use of embryonic stem cells raises ethical questions because the embryo is destroyed in the process. Some people argue the medical benefits outweigh this concern; others oppose it on moral or religious grounds.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Challenges of Size</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">As organisms get larger, their <dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of an organism’s surface area to its volume. As organisms get larger, this ratio decreases, making diffusion alone insufficient.">surface area to volume ratio</dfn> (SA:V) decreases (B2.2a). Consider a cube: a 1 cm cube has a SA:V ratio of 6:1, while a 2 cm cube has a ratio of only 3:1. This means simple diffusion alone cannot supply enough substances or remove waste fast enough for large organisms. Multicellular organisms therefore need <strong>specialised exchange surfaces</strong> and <strong>transport systems</strong>.</p>
<!-- DIAGRAM -->
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Human Circulatory System</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Humans have a <strong>double circulatory system</strong> (B2.2c): one circuit pumps blood from the heart to the lungs for gas exchange (pulmonary circuit), the other pumps oxygenated blood from the heart to the body and back (systemic circuit). This maintains high blood pressure for efficient delivery of oxygen and nutrients.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The heart has four chambers — two <strong>atria</strong> (receive blood) and two <strong>ventricles</strong> (pump blood out). The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body. The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall because it pumps blood further (around the whole body). Valves prevent backflow and ensure blood flows in one direction (B2.2d).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Arteries</strong> carry blood away from the heart at high pressure (thick muscular walls, small lumen, elastic fibres). <strong>Veins</strong> carry blood back to the heart at low pressure (thinner walls, larger lumen, valves to prevent backflow). <strong>Capillaries</strong> are tiny vessels with walls just one cell thick, allowing efficient exchange of substances by diffusion between the blood and surrounding tissues (B2.2d).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Red blood cells</strong> are biconcave discs with no nucleus, packed with <strong>haemoglobin</strong> to carry oxygen. Their biconcave shape increases surface area for oxygen absorption. <strong>Plasma</strong> is the liquid component of blood that transports dissolved substances including glucose, amino acids, urea, hormones, carbon dioxide and antibodies (B2.2e).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="For SA:V ratio calculations, use cubes: a 1 cm cube has SA = 6 cm², V = 1 cm³, SA:V = 6:1. A 2 cm cube has SA = 24 cm², V = 8 cm³, SA:V = 3:1. As size increases, the ratio decreases — this is why larger organisms need transport systems.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>As organisms get larger, their surface area to volume ratio decreases. This is why multicellular organisms need specialised exchange surfaces (e.g. alveoli in lungs, villi in the intestine) and transport systems (e.g. the circulatory system) to move substances efficiently.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Plant Transport: Xylem and Phloem</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Root hair cells</strong> have long thin extensions that greatly increase the surface area for absorbing water (by osmosis) and mineral ions (by active transport) from the soil (B2.2f).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Xylem</strong> vessels transport water and dissolved minerals upwards from roots to leaves in a continuous column (B2.2h). Xylem cells are dead, hollow tubes reinforced with <strong>lignin</strong> (a waterproof, rigid material) that provides structural support. The movement of water through the plant is called the <strong>transpiration stream</strong> — water evaporates from the leaves through <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny pores on the underside of leaves that open and close to control gas exchange and water loss. Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells.">stomata</dfn>, creating a “pull” that draws water up through the xylem (B2.2g).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Phloem</strong> tubes transport dissolved sugars (sucrose) from the leaves (where they are made by photosynthesis) to the rest of the plant — this is called <strong>translocation</strong> (B2.2g–h). Phloem cells are living and have sieve plates with tiny pores to allow movement of dissolved substances. Unlike xylem, translocation requires energy and can move substances both up and down the plant.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting Transpiration Rate</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Environmental factors affect how quickly water is lost from leaves (B2.2i–j): <strong>light intensity</strong> (more light causes stomata to open wider), <strong>temperature</strong> (higher temperature increases evaporation rate), <strong>air movement/wind</strong> (blows away humid air from around the leaf, maintaining a steep diffusion gradient). A <strong>potometer</strong> is used to measure the rate of water uptake by a plant cutting, which is a proxy for transpiration rate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Diffusion and osmosis are passive; active transport requires energy from respiration and moves substances against the concentration gradient.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">The cell cycle includes growth, DNA replication and mitosis. Stem cells (embryonic, adult, meristems) differentiate to form specialised cells.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Larger organisms have a smaller SA:V ratio and need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems. Xylem carries water up; phloem carries sugars around the plant.</li>
</ul>
