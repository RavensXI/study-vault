--- DESCRIPTION ---
Key features of pop music: song structures, chord progressions and instrumentation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Pop Music?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of popular music that is commercially produced, usually with catchy melodies, simple structures and broad appeal.">Pop music</dfn> is short for “popular music.” It is music that is commercially produced and designed to appeal to a wide audience. Pop has evolved through different eras — from the rock ’n’ roll-influenced pop of the 1950s and 60s, through the synthesiser-driven sounds of the 1980s, to the digitally produced pop of today. What stays the same across all eras is the focus on catchy melodies, repetitive structures and memorable hooks.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For the Eduqas GCSE, you need to be able to identify the key features of pop music when listening to an unfamiliar extract. This means knowing the typical structures, chord progressions, and instruments used in pop songs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Acoustic and Electronic Instruments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Pop music usually uses a mixture of <dfn class="term" data-def="Instruments that produce sound naturally without electrical amplification, such as piano, acoustic guitar and drum kit.">acoustic</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Instruments that require electricity to produce or amplify sound, such as synthesisers, drum machines and electric guitars.">electronic</dfn> instruments. Being able to sort instruments into these two categories is an important listening skill.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Acoustic instruments</strong> produce sound without electricity. In pop music, common acoustic instruments include: vocals, piano, acoustic guitar, drum kit, strings (such as violins and cellos), horn section and percussion (such as tambourine or shaker).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Electronic instruments</strong> require electricity to produce or modify sound. In pop music, these include: keyboard, electric guitar, bass guitar, synthesiser and drum machine.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Note that vocals are acoustic — the human voice does not need electricity to produce sound, even if a microphone is used to amplify it. Similarly, a drum kit is acoustic because the sound comes from hitting the drum heads and cymbals, whereas a drum machine is electronic because it generates sounds digitally.</p>

  <p>Pop music will usually use a mixture of acoustic and electronic instruments. In the exam, you may be asked to identify specific instruments and classify them as acoustic or electronic.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Pop Song Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">One of the most important features of pop music is its <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a piece of music is organised into sections, such as verse, chorus and bridge.">structure</dfn>. Pop songs tend to follow predictable patterns that make them easy to remember. You need to know three key structures for the exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">1. Verse–Chorus Form (ABAB)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The simplest pop song structure is <dfn class="term" data-def="A song structure that alternates between verses (A sections) and choruses (B sections), following an ABAB pattern.">verse–chorus form</dfn>. It follows an ABAB pattern, where A is the verse and B is the chorus. The verses usually have different words each time, while the chorus stays the same. The simple structure relies on a strong, catchy chorus that listeners remember.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Examples:</strong> “We Are The Champions” by Queen and “HUMBLE.” by Kendrick Lamar both use verse–chorus form.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">2. Verse–Chorus–Bridge Form (ABABCB)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">This is the standard modern pop song structure. It adds a contrasting <dfn class="term" data-def="A contrasting section in a song (sometimes called the middle eight) that provides variety before returning to the chorus.">bridge</dfn> section (C) that provides variety before the final chorus. The bridge often has different harmony or melody to keep the listener’s interest. The full pattern is ABABCB.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Examples:</strong> “Since U Been Gone” by Kelly Clarkson and “Shape of You” by Ed Sheeran both follow verse–chorus–bridge form.</p>

<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/gcse-music/popular-music/diagram_pop_music.jpg" alt="Diagram: Pop Music" loading="lazy"></figure>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">3. 32-Bar Song Form (AABA)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A song structure divided into four 8-bar sections in an AABA pattern, common in earlier pop and Tin Pan Alley songs.">32-bar song form</dfn> follows an A–A–B–A pattern, where each letter represents an 8-bar phrase.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <strong>A section</strong> (verse or strophe) is usually 8 bars long. It contains the main melody and often the song’s title, usually ending on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The first note or chord of a key; the 'home' note that a melody feels drawn back to.">tonic</dfn>. The <strong>B section</strong> (bridge or middle eight) is a contrasting 8-bar section that provides harmonic or melodic variation, often leading back to the A section.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Example:</strong> “I Wanna Hold Your Hand” by The Beatles uses 32-bar song form.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 3 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three pop structures you must know are: Verse–Chorus (ABAB), Verse–Chorus–Bridge (ABABCB), and 32-Bar Song Form (AABA). In the exam, label sections using letters (A, B, C) and name them (verse, chorus, bridge).</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">When listening to an extract, focus on these clues to identify the structure:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Verse:</strong> The lyrics change each time, the melody stays similar, and the texture is often thinner than in the chorus. <strong>Chorus:</strong> The same lyrics and melody return each time, the texture often builds, and the chorus is usually the catchiest part. <strong>Bridge:</strong> New musical material appears — often a change of harmony, a different vocal melody, or a shift in dynamics. It usually only appears once.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">If you hear an ABAB pattern with no contrasting middle section, it is verse–chorus form. If there is a contrasting section before the final chorus, it is verse–chorus–bridge. If you hear four 8-bar sections in an AABA pattern, it is 32-bar song form.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Chord Progressions in Pop Music</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Pop songs tend to focus around the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three most important chords in a key, built on the 1st (I), 4th (IV) and 5th (V) notes of the scale.">primary chords</dfn>: I, IV and V. In the key of C major, the chords built on each degree of the scale are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>I</strong> = C major   |   <strong>ii</strong> = D minor   |   <strong>iii</strong> = E minor   |   <strong>IV</strong> = F major   |   <strong>V</strong> = G major   |   <strong>vi</strong> = A minor   |   <strong>vii°</strong> = B diminished</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Notice that major chords are written with uppercase Roman numerals (I, IV, V) and minor chords with lowercase (ii, iii, vi). The vii° chord is diminished and is rarely used in pop music.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Many pop songs use just three or four chords looped throughout the whole track. A very common <dfn class="term" data-def="A sequence of chords that is repeated in a pattern throughout a song or section.">chord progression</dfn> is I–V–vi–IV (in C major: C–G–Am–F). This four-chord loop has been used in hundreds of hit songs. Other songs stick to just the primary chords I, IV and V.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about pop songs tend to focus around the primary.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Pop songs tend to focus around the primary chords I, IV and V. A very common pop chord progression is I–V–vi–IV. You should be able to identify primary chords aurally and using Roman numerals.</p>
</div>

In the exam, you may be asked to compare two versions of the same song. When doing this, refer to:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Instrumentation and use of technology</strong> — Does one version use more electronic instruments? Are effects like reverb or Auto-Tune used? <strong>Structure</strong> — Do both versions follow the same form, or has the cover changed the order of sections? <strong>Harmony and chord progressions</strong> — Are the same chords used? Has the key changed? <strong>Lead and backing vocals</strong> — How do the vocal styles differ? Is there harmony singing? <strong>Texture</strong> — Is one version thicker or thinner? Does one build layers more gradually?</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Pop music uses a mix of acoustic instruments (vocals, piano, acoustic guitar, drum kit, strings) and electronic instruments (keyboard, synthesiser, electric guitar, bass guitar, drum machine).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">The three main pop structures are Verse–Chorus (ABAB), Verse–Chorus–Bridge (ABABCB) and 32-Bar Song Form (AABA).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Pop songs focus around the primary chords I, IV and V, and the chord progression I–V–vi–IV is extremely common.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n34">In Eduqas listening questions on pop music, always use correct musical vocabulary. Don’t just say “it has a guitar” — say whether it is an acoustic guitar or an electric guitar, and describe its role (e.g. strumming chords, playing a riff). When describing structure, use the letter labels (A, B, C) alongside the section names (verse, chorus, bridge). If you are asked to compare two extracts, organise your answer by musical element (instrumentation, structure, harmony, vocals, texture) rather than writing about each extract separately. This makes it easier for the examiner to award comparison marks.</p>
