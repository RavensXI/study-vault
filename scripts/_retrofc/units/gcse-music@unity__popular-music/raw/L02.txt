<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Makes Rock Music “Rock”?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Rock music is built around a core band of electric instruments playing with energy, volume and attitude. For your Eduqas GCSE (AoS4), you need to identify the typical <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of instruments used in a piece of music.">instrumentation</dfn>, harmony, effects and melodic features that set rock apart from other popular music styles. This lesson covers four key spec areas: rock music features, power chords, riffs, and effects such as distortion and panning.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Instrumentation: The Rock Band Line-Up</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A standard rock band typically includes:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Electric guitar</strong> — provides melodic lines, riffs and chords. The electric guitar often uses effects such as <dfn class="term" data-def="An effect that clips the audio signal to create a harsh, gritty or fuzzy tone on an electric guitar.">distortion</dfn> to give it a harsh, gritty <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality or colour of a sound that distinguishes one instrument or voice from another.">timbre</dfn>.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Bass guitar</strong> — plays lower-pitched lines that underpin the harmony and lock in with the drums.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Drum kit</strong> — keeps the momentum pushing forward with driving rhythms and brings intensity to the music. The drum kit is the rhythmic engine of any rock track.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Vocals</strong> — rock bands will often have a lead singer, with other members of the band providing backing vocals.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about is common for rock singers to use falsetto.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>It is common for rock singers to use <dfn class="term" data-def="A vocal technique where a singer produces notes higher than their normal range, creating a lighter, thinner tone.">falsetto</dfn> — singing higher than their normal range — to bring extra intensity and emotion to the music.</p>
</div>

Without the driving rhythms of the drum kit, rock music would lose much of its power and momentum. In an exam listening question, listen for whether the drums are playing a straight, driving beat or a more complex, syncopated pattern — this helps you identify the style.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Effects: Distortion & Panning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Music technology plays a big role in shaping the rock sound. Two effects you must know for the exam are distortion and panning.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Distortion</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Distortion is the signature effect of rock guitar. It clips the audio signal so the sound becomes harsh, gritty and aggressive. A clean electric guitar tone sounds smooth and bell-like; add distortion and it becomes thick, crunchy and powerful. Distortion can range from mild “overdrive” to extreme, heavily saturated fuzz. In the exam, describe the guitar tone as “distorted” or “overdriven” rather than just saying “loud.”</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Panning</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A studio technique that positions a sound to the left, right or centre of the stereo field.">Panning</dfn> is a studio technique that positions a sound in the left, right or centre of the stereo field. In recorded rock music, panning is most commonly used on the drum kit — for example, the hi-hat might be placed slightly to the left while the floor tom sits to the right, creating a wide, immersive sound. Panning can be used on any instrument though. Other common uses are on electric guitars (particularly if there are multiple guitar parts) and backing vocals.</p>

<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/gcse-music/popular-music/diagram_generated_ee812e57.jpg" alt="Generated diagram for Rock Music"><figcaption>Generated diagram for Rock Music</figcaption></figure>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Panning is not the same as volume. It controls <em>where</em> a sound appears in the stereo field (left to right), not how loud it is. In the exam, if you hear a guitar more in one ear than the other, describe it as “panned to the left/right.”</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Harmony: Power Chords</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Rock music often uses <dfn class="term" data-def="The three most important chords in a key, built on the 1st (I), 4th (IV) and 5th (V) degrees of the scale.">primary chords</dfn> — chords I, IV and V. However, in rock these are frequently played as <dfn class="term" data-def="A chord made up of only the root note and the 5th, with no 3rd, so it is neither major nor minor.">power chords</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A power chord is made up of just two notes: the <strong>root</strong> (1st) and the <strong>5th</strong>. Because it leaves out the 3rd of the chord, a power chord is neither major nor minor. This gives it an ambiguous, open quality that suits the aggressive timbre of a distorted electric guitar.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Power Chords in the Key of G</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">In the key of G major, the primary chords are G (I), C (IV) and D (V). As power chords, these become:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>G5</strong> — notes G and D (root + 5th)</li>
      <li><strong>C5</strong> — notes C and G (root + 5th)</li>
      <li><strong>D5</strong> — notes D and A (root + 5th)</li>
    </ul>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">Notice there is no B in the G chord, no E in the C chord, and no F# in the D chord. The missing 3rd is what makes power chords sound neither major nor minor.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Do Power Chords Work So Well with Distortion?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Distortion amplifies every harmonic in a chord. When you distort a full major or minor chord (with a 3rd), the extra harmonics clash and the sound becomes muddy. Because a power chord contains only two notes — the root and the 5th — it stays clear and punchy even with heavy distortion. This is why rock guitarists favour power chords over full triads.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Melody: Riffs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A short, repeated melodic or rhythmic pattern that forms a memorable hook in a piece of music.">riff</dfn> is a short, repeated melodic or rhythmic pattern. Riffs are one of the most recognisable features of rock music. They are usually played on the electric guitar or bass guitar and often form the main hook of a song.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">When describing a riff in the exam, think about these features:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Instrument</strong> — which instrument is playing the riff? (Electric guitar, bass guitar, or both in unison?)</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Contour / shape</strong> — is the riff <dfn class="term" data-def="Movement by step (conjunct) or by leap (disjunct) in a melody.">conjunct</dfn> (moving by step) or disjunct (moving by leap)? Is it ascending, descending or a mixture?</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Tonality</strong> — does the riff use a major scale, minor scale or <dfn class="term" data-def="A five-note scale often used in rock and blues music, for example C, D, E, G, A.">pentatonic scale</dfn>? Many rock riffs use the minor pentatonic scale, which gives them a bluesy feel.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Rhythm</strong> — is the rhythm driving and regular, or does it use syncopation?</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n31" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A riff is <strong>repeated</strong> — that is what separates it from a one-off melodic phrase. In an exam, if a short pattern comes back several times throughout the track, call it a riff. Describe its contour, tonality and the instrument playing it for full marks.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Putting It All Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">In a typical rock track, you might hear a distorted electric guitar playing a repeating riff built on power chords, with a bass guitar doubling the root notes underneath. The drum kit drives the rhythm with a strong backbeat, and the lead singer uses falsetto in the chorus to add intensity. In the recorded mix, the drums are panned across the stereo field, while two electric guitar parts are panned hard left and hard right to create a wide, full <dfn class="term" data-def="The layers of sound in a piece of music and how they relate to each other.">texture</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">When you hear a rock extract in the listening exam, work through these elements systematically: instrumentation, effects, harmony (power chords or full chords?), melody (riff-based?), and use of technology (panning, distortion). This approach ensures you cover everything the question asks for.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Rock instrumentation centres on electric guitar (with distortion), bass guitar, drum kit and vocals (often using falsetto for intensity).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Power chords contain only the root and 5th — no 3rd — so they are neither major nor minor, and they stay clear even with distortion.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Riffs are short, repeated patterns; describe them by instrument, contour, tonality and rhythm. Panning positions sounds in the stereo field and is most commonly used on the drum kit.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">When describing rock music in a listening question, avoid vague words like “loud” or “heavy.” Instead, use precise musical vocabulary: say the guitar uses <strong>distortion</strong>, the chords are <strong>power chords</strong> (root and 5th only, neither major nor minor), or the opening is based on a <strong>riff</strong>. If you can hear a sound more in one ear, describe it as <strong>panned</strong> to the left or right. Strong answers show correct terminology — the more specific you are, the more marks you earn.</p>
