--- DESCRIPTION ---
Reading and producing bar charts, histograms, line graphs and pie charts in engineering contexts.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Data and Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Engineering decisions rarely rest on a single measurement. A quality control team might test hundreds of components and need to see the spread of results at a glance. A production manager might track output over a six-month period to spot a downward trend. Presenting data as a visual <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that displays numerical data visually, making patterns, trends and comparisons easier to identify.">graph</dfn> makes patterns that are invisible in a column of numbers immediately obvious. Your exam may show you a graph based on an engineering scenario and ask you to read values from it, describe trends, or choose which type of graph is most appropriate for a given set of data.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Bar Charts &mdash; Comparing Discrete Categories</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A chart that uses rectangular bars of equal width to compare separate (discrete) categories, where bar height represents frequency or quantity.">bar chart</dfn> uses separate rectangular bars to compare distinct categories. The bars do not touch each other because the categories are discrete &mdash; they are not part of a continuous scale. Each bar has equal width; only height varies. The vertical axis usually shows frequency, quantity or value; the horizontal axis lists the categories.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Bar charts are the right choice when the data falls into named, unordered groups. For example: the number of defective components produced by five different machines in a factory; the number of each type of engineering join used in a product (welded, brazed, bolted, riveted); or the frequency of failures by fault type recorded in a maintenance log.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Close this and list: what feature of a bar chart shows the categories are discrete? And what must be true of the bars' widths?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In a bar chart the bars do <strong>not touch</strong> because the data is discrete (separate categories). All bars must be the same width. The height of each bar represents the value or frequency for that category.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Histograms &mdash; Showing Continuous Data Distributions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that uses adjacent bars to display the distribution of continuous data grouped into class intervals, where bar area (not just height) represents frequency.">histogram</dfn> looks similar to a bar chart but there are two critical differences. First, the bars <strong>touch</strong> because the data is continuous &mdash; the intervals follow on from each other with no gap. Second, strictly speaking the area of each bar represents frequency (frequency = frequency density &times; class width); however, when all class widths are equal (which is common in GCSE-level questions) bar height and frequency are proportional, so you can read frequency directly from the height.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Histograms are suited to continuous data grouped into intervals. Engineering examples include: the distribution of shaft diameters measured across a production batch (e.g. 19.90&ndash;19.95 mm, 19.95&ndash;20.00 mm, 20.00&ndash;20.05 mm); the distribution of load-bearing test results grouped into force ranges; or the distribution of measured temperatures at different points in a manufacturing process.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Line Graphs &mdash; Showing Trends Over Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph that uses points connected by straight lines to show how a quantity changes continuously over time or another continuous variable.">line graph</dfn> plots a series of data points and connects them with straight lines. It is ideal for showing how a quantity changes over time, or how one continuous variable responds to another. The horizontal axis (x-axis) typically shows the independent variable (for example, time or temperature); the vertical axis (y-axis) shows the dependent variable (for example, output, force or power).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Line graphs allow you to identify <strong>trends</strong> (general directions of change), <strong>peaks</strong> and <strong>troughs</strong>, and periods where values stay constant. A rising line indicates an increase; a falling line indicates a decrease; a flat line means no change. Engineering uses for line graphs include: machine output rate over a production shift; voltage across a capacitor charging over time; or temperature of a component being heat-treated in a furnace.</p>

Describe what the data shows, then explain the likely engineering reason if you can.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Pie Charts &mdash; Showing Proportions of a Whole</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A circular chart divided into sectors, where each sector's angle represents its proportion of the total, used to compare parts of a whole.">pie chart</dfn> divides a circle into sectors. The full circle represents the whole data set (100%). Each sector&rsquo;s angle is proportional to the percentage it represents. To find a sector angle:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">$$\text{Sector angle} = \frac{\text{Percentage}}{100} \times 360°$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Alternatively, if you have frequencies: $$\text{Sector angle} = \frac{\text{Frequency}}{\text{Total frequency}} \times 360°$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Pie charts work well when you want to show the relative proportions of several categories that together make up a whole &mdash; for example, the breakdown of material costs in a product (steel 40%, aluminium 25%, plastics 20%, fixings 15%), or the distribution of defect types found during a quality inspection.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Problem:</strong> A quality control log records 80 defective components. The faults are: surface scratches 40, incorrect dimensions 20, cracks 16, other 4. Calculate the sector angle for each fault type.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Step 1 &mdash; Check total:</strong> 40 + 20 + 16 + 4 = 80 ✓</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Step 2 &mdash; Calculate each angle:</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Surface scratches: \(\frac{40}{80} \times 360 = 180°\)<br>Incorrect dimensions: \(\frac{20}{80} \times 360 = 90°\)<br>Cracks: \(\frac{16}{80} \times 360 = 72°\)<br>Other: \(\frac{4}{80} \times 360 = 18°\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Step 3 &mdash; Check:</strong> 180 + 90 + 72 + 18 = 360° ✓ Always check your sector angles sum to exactly 360°.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Choosing the Right Graph Type</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Selecting the wrong graph type is a straightforward way to lose marks. Use this guide:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Bar chart</strong> &rarr; Discrete (separate) categories you want to compare. E.g. number of components from five different suppliers.<br><strong>Histogram</strong> &rarr; Continuous data grouped into intervals. E.g. distribution of component dimensions across a production run.<br><strong>Line graph</strong> &rarr; Continuous data showing change over time or another variable. E.g. temperature of a furnace over two hours.<br><strong>Pie chart</strong> &rarr; Proportions of a total. E.g. percentage breakdown of production costs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each of the four graph types, write one engineering scenario where it would be the most appropriate choice.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Bar charts compare discrete categories (bars do not touch). Histograms show continuous data distributions (bars touch). Line graphs show trends or change over time. Pie charts show proportions of a whole (sector angle = percentage &times; 3.6).</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Bar charts compare discrete categories (bars do not touch). Histograms show continuous grouped data (bars touch). Line graphs show trends over time. Pie charts show proportions of a whole.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">Pie chart sector angle = (frequency &divide; total) &times; 360°. Always verify that all sector angles sum to 360°.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">When describing a trend from a line graph, use the actual values from the axes and identify specific phases (rise, peak, drop, constant) with an engineering explanation where possible.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">For graph-reading questions, always read axis labels and scales carefully before picking off any value &mdash; a common error is misreading the scale (e.g. confusing 200 with 20 because the axis counts in multiples). When asked to &ldquo;describe the trend&rdquo;, use specific values from the graph rather than vague words like &ldquo;it goes up&rdquo;. For pie chart calculations, always check that your sector angles add to 360° before drawing. If you are asked which type of graph is appropriate, state the graph type and give the reason (discrete vs continuous, or &ldquo;shows proportion of a whole&rdquo;).</p>
