<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is an Engineering Drawing?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A written description of a component leaves too much to interpretation. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A precise, scaled technical drawing that communicates the exact shape, size, and construction details of an engineering component.">engineering drawing</dfn> communicates shape, size, and detail in a form that any trained engineer anywhere can read without ambiguity. The conventions used in engineering drawings are standardised in Britain under <dfn class="term" data-def="The British Standard that defines conventions for engineering drawings, including line types, symbols, and projection method.">BS 8888</dfn> &mdash; the British Standard for technical product documentation. Knowing these conventions is not just useful background; the exam tests your ability to interpret and produce drawings to these standards.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Third-Angle Orthographic Projection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A drawing method that shows a 3D object in three 2D views (front, plan and end) arranged so the top view sits above the front view and the right-side view sits to the right.">Third-angle orthographic projection</dfn> is the standard used in the United Kingdom and the United States. It shows an object as three flat views &mdash; front, plan (top) and end (side) &mdash; laid out on paper according to a fixed rule: the view of the top sits above the front view, and the view of the right-hand side sits to the right of the front view. Think of the object being placed inside a transparent glass box; the views are projected onto the glass walls nearest to each face, then the walls are unfolded flat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Third-angle projection uses a specific symbol: a truncated cone drawn in side view, with the smaller circular end of the cone on the right. You will often see this symbol in the title block of a drawing. Always check for it when reading a drawing &mdash; if it is absent or shows first-angle projection (where the views are arranged on the opposite sides), the layout will be different and you risk misreading dimensions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Sketch from memory a simple box shape and add the three third-angle views in their correct positions — top view above, right-side view to the right.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In <strong>third-angle projection</strong> (the UK standard): the top view (plan) sits <em>above</em> the front view, and the right-side view sits <em>to the right</em> of the front view. The symbol is a truncated cone with the small circle on the right. Do not confuse this with first-angle projection, which places views on the opposite sides.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Three Views in Orthographic Projection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Every orthographic drawing has three standard views:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Front view (elevation):</strong> The most informative face of the object. It is usually chosen to show the greatest number of features clearly. This view is drawn first and the others are projected from it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Plan view (top view):</strong> What the object looks like from directly above. In third-angle projection this view is positioned above the front view.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>End view (side view):</strong> What the object looks like from one side. In third-angle projection the right-side view is placed to the right of the front view.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">All three views share the same scale and are drawn in alignment &mdash; features that appear in one view project directly across to the same position in the adjacent view. This alignment allows you to locate any feature precisely in three dimensions from a flat drawing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Standard Line Types (BS 8888)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Different lines carry different meanings in engineering drawings. Using the wrong line type is read by an examiner as a misunderstanding of the drawing convention &mdash; so accuracy here matters.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Continuous thick line</strong> &mdash; Visible edges and outlines of the object as seen from the viewing direction. These are the most prominent lines on the drawing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Continuous thin line</strong> &mdash; Dimension lines, leader lines, projection lines and hatching lines. Thin because they should not compete visually with the outline.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Thin dashed line</strong> &mdash; Hidden detail: edges or features that exist on the object but are not visible from the current viewing direction (for example, a drilled hole seen from above, or an internal pocket seen from the front).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Thin chain line (long-dash short-dash)</strong> &mdash; Centre lines: the axes of holes, cylinders, arcs and lines of symmetry. These run through the centre of any circular or symmetrical feature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Thick chain line</strong> &mdash; Cutting plane: shows where an imaginary cut has been made through the object to create a sectional view.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down all five line types from BS 8888. For each one, state what it looks like and when it is used.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Five BS 8888 line types: continuous thick (visible edges), continuous thin (dimensions/leaders), thin dashed (hidden edges), thin chain (centre lines), thick chain (cutting plane). Each carries a specific meaning &mdash; using the wrong line for a feature is an error.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Isometric Projection &mdash; A 3D Picture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pictorial drawing method that shows an object in three dimensions using axes set at 30° to the horizontal, with true lengths preserved along all three axes.">Isometric projection</dfn> is used alongside orthographic views to give a clear three-dimensional impression of an object. Three axes are used: one vertical and two running at 30° either side of the horizontal. Lengths are drawn true to scale along these three axes only &mdash; diagonal and curved features must be constructed indirectly (by plotting points along the axes and connecting them). Circles in isometric projection become ellipses.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Isometric projection is not a view from a specific angle in the way orthographic projection is. It is a convention that produces consistent, proportionally correct pictures. It is particularly useful for assembly drawings and instructions because the shape of the part is immediately clear to anyone, including non-specialists.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Sectional Views &mdash; Cutting to See Inside</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Some internal features &mdash; such as bores, internal threads, or recesses &mdash; are difficult to describe with dashed lines alone. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A view created by imagining an object cut along a defined plane, revealing its internal shape; the cut surfaces are shown with hatching.">sectional view</dfn> imagines the object sliced along a flat plane. The cut is shown in the other views using the thick chain cutting plane line, with arrows indicating the direction of view. The cut surfaces exposed by the imaginary saw cut are shown with <dfn class="term" data-def="Diagonal lines drawn at 45° across a cut surface in a sectional view to indicate solid material that has been cut through.">hatching</dfn> &mdash; evenly spaced diagonal lines drawn at 45°. Thin, consistent hatching lines distinguish material from empty space and are drawn across the entire cut face.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The section label is typically written as &ldquo;Section A&ndash;A&rdquo; (or B&ndash;B, C&ndash;C for multiple sections on the same drawing). The letters appear at each end of the cutting plane line to identify which section view corresponds to which cut.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Scenario:</strong> A drawing of a cylindrical housing shows a front orthographic view and a side view. On the front view, a thick chain line (cutting plane) runs horizontally across the centre, labelled &ldquo;A&ndash;A&rdquo; with arrows pointing downward. A separate view labelled &ldquo;Section A&ndash;A&rdquo; shows the inside of the housing with hatching at 45° covering the top and bottom walls, and a clear circular bore in the centre with no hatching (empty space).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Reading the section:</strong> The hatched areas represent the solid metal walls of the housing that have been cut through. The un-hatched circle in the middle is the hollow bore &mdash; empty space through which a shaft or pipe would pass. Without the sectional view, this internal bore would only be visible as a dashed circle in the front view, which is less clear.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">When drawing or interpreting sectional views, remember: hatching covers <em>only</em> the material that the cutting plane has physically sliced through. Features beyond the cut plane (visible in the background) are drawn as normal outlines, not hatched.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Datums and Dimensions on Drawings</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Just as dimensions in a workshop are measured from a datum point or line, engineering drawings record dimensions from a datum. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed reference line or point on an engineering drawing from which dimensions are measured, ensuring consistency across the drawing.">drawing datum</dfn> prevents cumulative measurement error in the workshop. All dimensions are written in millimetres by default on UK engineering drawings unless another unit is stated. Dimension lines are continuous thin lines with arrowheads touching the projection lines that frame the feature being dimensioned. The number sits above or in a gap in the dimension line.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Third-angle projection (the UK standard): top view above the front, right-side view to the right of the front. Symbol is a truncated cone with the small circle on the right.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Five BS 8888 line types: continuous thick (visible edges), continuous thin (dimensions/leaders), thin dashed (hidden edges), thin chain (centre lines), thick chain (cutting plane).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Isometric projection uses 30° axes with true lengths. Sectional views show internal features: thick chain cutting plane, cut surfaces hatched at 45°. All dimensions in mm from a fixed datum.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">For engineering drawing questions, always state the projection system when asked &mdash; &ldquo;third-angle projection&rdquo; not just &ldquo;orthographic&rdquo;. When naming a line type, give both its appearance (e.g. &ldquo;thin dashed&rdquo;) and its purpose (e.g. &ldquo;hidden detail&rdquo;) for full marks. In sketch questions, the three views must be aligned correctly in the third-angle layout or the question cannot be marked &mdash; draw a light construction grid first. For sectional view questions, remember that hatching is drawn at 45° and covers only the cut material, not the empty spaces.</p>
