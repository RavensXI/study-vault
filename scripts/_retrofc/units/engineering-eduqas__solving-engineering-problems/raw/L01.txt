Ohm's law, mechanical advantage and velocity ratio — using and rearranging engineering formulae.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Formulae Matter in Engineering</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Engineers solve practical problems with mathematics. Whether they are designing a circuit to power a security alarm or calculating how much force a lever needs to lift a heavy component, they rely on tried-and-tested <dfn class="term" data-def="A mathematical equation that expresses a fixed relationship between quantities, allowing an unknown value to be calculated.">formulae</dfn> to get reliable answers. Your exam will always ask you to show your working &mdash; so understanding the formula, substituting correctly, and writing the unit are all part of a full-marks response.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down Ohm's law in symbols. Then rearrange it to make I the subject, and then R the subject.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="The law stating that voltage equals current multiplied by resistance: V = IR, provided temperature stays constant.">Ohm&rsquo;s law</dfn> states that \(V = IR\), where V is voltage in volts (V), I is current in amperes (A), and R is resistance in ohms (&Omega;). Rearranging gives \(I = \frac{V}{R}\) and \(R = \frac{V}{I}\).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ohm&rsquo;s Law &mdash; Voltage, Current and Resistance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In any simple electrical circuit, three quantities are linked. <dfn class="term" data-def="The potential difference across a component, measured in volts (V), that drives current through it.">Voltage</dfn> (V) is the &ldquo;push&rdquo; that drives electrons around the circuit. <dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of flow of electric charge around a circuit, measured in amperes (A).">Current</dfn> (I) is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes (A). <dfn class="term" data-def="The opposition to current flow in a circuit, measured in ohms (&Omega;).">Resistance</dfn> (R) is the opposition to that flow, measured in ohms (&Omega;). The relationship is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$V = IR$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">If you know any two of the three quantities you can always find the third by rearranging the formula. Engineers use a triangle to help: write V at the top, I and R side by side at the bottom. Cover the quantity you want and read off the operation that remains.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Electrical Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Power is the rate at which a circuit transfers energy. It is measured in watts (W). Three equivalent formulae link power to voltage, current and resistance:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">$$P = IV \qquad P = I^2 R \qquad P = \frac{V^2}{R}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Use whichever version matches the quantities you already know. For example, if you know current and voltage, use \(P = IV\). If you only know current and resistance, use \(P = I^2 R\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Mechanical Advantage &mdash; Getting More Force Out</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the load moved by a machine to the effort applied to it, showing how many times the machine multiplies force.">Mechanical advantage</dfn> (MA) tells you how much a machine multiplies your applied force. It is a dimensionless ratio &mdash; it has no unit because it compares two forces in the same unit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">$$\text{MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Load is the force the machine must overcome (for example, the weight of an object being lifted), measured in newtons (N). Effort is the force you apply to the machine, also in newtons (N). An MA greater than 1 means the machine makes the task easier by magnifying the force you put in. An MA less than 1 means the machine reduces force but increases speed &mdash; useful in different applications.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: what is the formula for mechanical advantage, and what does an MA of 4 tell you about the machine?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mechanical advantage = Load &divide; Effort. If MA = 4, the machine multiplies your effort four times &mdash; a 50 N push produces 200 N of output force. Mechanical advantage has no unit because it is a pure ratio of two forces.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Velocity Ratio &mdash; How Far the Machine Moves</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load, showing the movement trade-off in a machine.">Velocity ratio</dfn> (VR) is a separate ratio that compares the distances moved, not the forces. It tells you how many metres the effort must travel for every metre the load moves:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{Distance moved by effort}}{\text{Distance moved by load}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">A lever with VR = 3 means the effort end of the lever travels 3 m for every 1 m the load end moves. Like MA, VR is dimensionless. A common misconception is to confuse MA and VR &mdash; remember that MA is about force while VR is about distance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Efficiency &mdash; How Well a Machine Uses Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">In a perfect (ideal) machine there are no energy losses and MA equals VR exactly. Real machines always have friction and other losses, so some input energy is wasted as heat. <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of input energy that a machine converts into useful output, calculated as (MA &divide; VR) &times; 100%.">Efficiency</dfn> expresses how close a machine comes to the ideal:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\%$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">An efficiency of 100% is physically impossible for a real machine. A well-lubricated lever might reach 80&ndash;95%. Efficiency must always be between 0% and 100%; if your calculation gives a value above 100% you have made an error.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Problem:</strong> A lever is used to lift a 600 N crate. The effort applied is 150 N. The effort end of the lever moves 2 m and the load end moves 0.4 m. Calculate (a) the mechanical advantage, (b) the velocity ratio, and (c) the efficiency of the lever.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>(a) Mechanical advantage:</strong><br>\(\text{MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{600\,\text{N}}{150\,\text{N}} = 4\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>(b) Velocity ratio:</strong><br>\(\text{VR} = \frac{\text{Distance (effort)}}{\text{Distance (load)}} = \frac{2\,\text{m}}{0.4\,\text{m}} = 5\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>(c) Efficiency:</strong><br>\(\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\% = \frac{4}{5} \times 100\% = 80\%\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39">The lever is 80% efficient. The 20% &ldquo;lost&rdquo; is dissipated as heat through friction at the pivot. Note how MA and VR are both dimensionless &mdash; no units are written alongside them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n40">Levers and Classes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n41">Levers are one of the simplest machines. All levers have three elements: a <strong>load</strong> (the force being overcome), an <strong>effort</strong> (the force being applied) and a <strong>pivot</strong> (also called the fulcrum). The three classes of lever differ in where the pivot sits relative to the load and effort. A first-class lever has the pivot between load and effort (a see-saw, or a pair of pliers). A second-class lever has the load between the pivot and the effort (a wheelbarrow). A third-class lever has the effort between the pivot and the load (a pair of tweezers or a human forearm). All three classes follow the same MA and VR formulae.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n43">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n44">Ohm&rsquo;s law: \(V = IR\). Rearrange to find I or R. Always show formula, substitution, answer and unit.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n45">Mechanical advantage = Load &divide; Effort (a force ratio, no unit). Velocity ratio = Distance moved by effort &divide; Distance moved by load (a distance ratio, no unit).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n46">Efficiency (%) = (MA &divide; VR) &times; 100. Real machines are always less than 100% efficient because of friction losses.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n42">For any <strong>calculate</strong> question, write the formula first, then substitute values with units, then give the final answer with its unit. Examiners award method marks for each step &mdash; so if your arithmetic is wrong but your method is correct you still pick up marks. For Ohm&rsquo;s law, double-check you have substituted into the right rearrangement: if the question gives V and R and asks for I, you need \(I = V \div R\), not \(V = IR\). For MA and VR, a common mistake is to invert the fraction &mdash; MA is Load &divide; Effort, not Effort &divide; Load. State that MA and VR have no units.</p>
