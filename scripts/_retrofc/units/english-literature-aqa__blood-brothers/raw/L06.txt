--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mickey Johnstone</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Mickey is Russell’s most powerful vehicle for his political argument. He begins as an energetic, funny, streetwise child — full of life despite his poverty. Russell makes him immediately likeable: his language is vivid (“Gis a sweet”), his games are physical and joyful, and his loyalty is fierce. The audience roots for Mickey from the start, which makes his decline all the more devastating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">As an adolescent, Mickey becomes self-conscious and awkward. His <dfn class="term" data-def="A single character's inner thoughts or feelings spoken aloud on stage, allowing the audience direct access to their emotions.">soliloquy</dfn> about wanting to be like “that guy” — confident, smooth, able to talk to Linda — reveals his growing insecurity. Russell uses this to show that class affects not just material wealth but psychological well-being. Mickey compares himself to an idealised version of masculinity he cannot achieve because his circumstances deny him opportunity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: List four stages of Mickey's character arc from childhood to the ending.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mickey’s character arc traces the destruction of working-class potential: energetic child → awkward teenager → factory worker → unemployed → criminal → prisoner → medicated, broken adult. Each stage is caused by class, not personal failure. His final line — “I could have been him” — is Russell’s thesis.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Eddie Lyons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Eddie is well-meaning, generous, and genuinely kind. He shares his sweets with Mickey, is fascinated by working-class life, and never deliberately uses his privilege against anyone. Russell does not present Eddie as a villain — he is a product of his class just as Mickey is a product of his. Eddie’s tragedy is that he benefits from a system he did not choose and cannot control.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">However, Eddie’s kindness has a <dfn class="term" data-def="A condescending attitude from a privileged person towards someone less fortunate, often disguised as kindness. Eddie's help for Mickey and Linda reflects his privilege rather than genuine equality.">patronising</dfn> edge that Russell subtly exposes. When Eddie helps Mickey find a job and later provides Linda with housing, he does so from a position of power. His generosity is possible only because of his class privilege. Russell asks the audience to consider whether kindness from a position of power can ever truly be equal.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Mrs Johnstone is the emotional heart of the play. Despite crippling poverty, abandonment by her husband, and the impossible choice forced on her by Mrs Lyons, she remains warm, loving, and resilient. Russell uses her songs to reveal her inner life: she dreams of Marilyn Monroe glamour, hopes for a better future, and loves her children fiercely. Her <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to recover from difficult circumstances. Mrs Johnstone's resilience despite poverty and loss makes her one of Russell's most sympathetic characters.">resilience</dfn> is presented as a working-class virtue — she endures what would break someone with fewer inner resources.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Crucially, Mrs Johnstone is not idealised. She makes a terrible decision in giving away her child, she is superstitious and easily manipulated, and she keeps the secret for decades despite its damage. Russell presents her as a fully human character whose flaws are products of her circumstances, not her nature.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Mrs Lyons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Mrs Lyons begins as a sympathetic character — a wealthy but lonely woman desperate for a child. However, Russell gradually reveals her as manipulative, possessive, and ultimately violent. She fabricates the superstition, exploits her power over Mrs Johnstone, and eventually tries to stab Mrs Johnstone with a kitchen knife. Russell uses her deterioration to argue that wealth does not bring happiness or stability — it brings a different kind of damage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Mrs Lyons’ <dfn class="term" data-def="An excessive, irrational fear that a secret will be discovered or that something will go wrong. Mrs Lyons' paranoia about the twins discovering the truth drives her to increasingly extreme behaviour.">paranoia</dfn> increases throughout the play. She is terrified that Eddie will discover the truth, that Mrs Johnstone will tell, that the superstition will come true. Russell contrasts her anxiety with Mrs Johnstone’s acceptance and warmth. Money cannot buy peace of mind — in fact, it fuels Mrs Lyons’ obsessive need for control.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list three actions that show Mrs Lyons' deterioration from sympathetic to dangerous across the play.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Russell presents Mrs Lyons as a mirror of Mrs Johnstone: both are mothers, both are flawed, but their class positions produce opposite responses. Mrs Johnstone’s poverty creates resilience and warmth; Mrs Lyons’ wealth creates paranoia and violence. Russell uses this contrast to argue that the class system damages everyone — not just the poor.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Linda</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Linda is introduced as a bold, fearless child who can outshoot both boys and speaks her mind. Russell presents her as the strongest character in childhood — she defends Mickey, challenges authority, and refuses to be limited by gender expectations. However, as an adult, Linda is ground down by the same forces that destroy Mickey: poverty, unemployment, and hopelessness.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Linda’s turn to Eddie is not betrayal — it is survival. Mickey is unemployed, medicated, and emotionally absent. Linda has a child to care for and no resources. Eddie can offer what Mickey cannot. Russell uses Linda to show that class inequality corrupts even the most genuine relationships: love is not enough when poverty makes daily life impossible.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="In Greek tragedy, a group of performers who comment on the action and address the audience directly. The Narrator in Blood Brothers functions as a modern chorus.">chorus</dfn>-like Narrator stands outside the action, observing and commenting. He creates tension with warnings (“the devil’s got your number”), reminds the audience of the tragic outcome, and asks the final question about superstition versus class. His ambiguity is deliberate: he could represent fate, society, guilt, or Russell himself.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The Narrator is the only character who sees the full picture. He knows the twins are brothers, he sees the class divide operating, and he watches the tragedy unfold without intervening. Russell may be suggesting that society itself is like the Narrator — it sees the injustice but does nothing to stop it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Mickey’s arc from joyful child to broken adult is Russell’s central argument: class, not character, determines life outcomes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Mrs Johnstone and Mrs Lyons are mirror images — Russell uses their contrasting responses to motherhood to show that class damages everyone differently.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Every character’s fate is shaped by their class position. Russell does not blame individuals — he blames the system.</li></ul>
