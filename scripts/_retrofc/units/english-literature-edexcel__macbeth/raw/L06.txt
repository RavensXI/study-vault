Character analysis of Macbeth, Lady Macbeth, Banquo, Macduff, and the Witches.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Macbeth — The Tragic Hero</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Macbeth is Shakespeare’s most complex villain-hero. He begins as a loyal, brave soldier — “brave Macbeth — well he deserves that name” — and ends as a nihilistic <dfn class="term" data-def="A ruler who seizes and maintains power through fear, violence, and oppression rather than legitimate authority.">tyrant</dfn> who has destroyed everything he valued. His journey follows the pattern of a <dfn class="term" data-def="A noble character whose downfall is caused by a specific flaw in their character — Macbeth’s flaw is his ‘vaulting ambition.’">tragic hero</dfn>: a figure of high status whose fatal flaw causes their destruction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">What makes Macbeth compelling is his moral awareness. He is not a simple villain — he knows what he is doing is wrong. His soliloquy in Act 1, Scene 7 shows him weighing the arguments against killing Duncan. He sees Duncan’s virtues “pleading like angels.” Yet he chooses ambition over conscience. This conscious choice is what makes his downfall genuinely tragic rather than merely deserved.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what is Macbeth's hamartia, and what complicating question does Shakespeare raise?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Macbeth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A character flaw that leads to a tragic hero’s downfall — from the Greek word ‘hamartia.’">hamartia</dfn> (tragic flaw) is his “vaulting ambition.” But Shakespeare complicates this: is the flaw ambition itself, or the weakness that allows him to act on it?</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Lady Macbeth — From Power to Destruction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Lady Macbeth is introduced as a character of terrifying willpower. Her “unsex me here” speech (Act 1, Scene 5) asks the spirits to strip away her femininity and fill her with “direst cruelty.” She is the driving force behind Duncan’s murder, manipulating Macbeth through attacks on his masculinity and her own iron resolve. She plans the details, frames the guards, and dismisses guilt with “a little water clears us of this deed.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Yet Shakespeare traces her complete psychological collapse. By Act 3, Macbeth excludes her from his plans. By Act 5, she is sleepwalking, washing invisible blood, and speaking in fragmented prose rather than her earlier controlled verse. The character who called on “thick night” to hide the murder now “has light by her continually” — she fears the dark. Her death (reported offstage, possibly suicide) completes the devastating arc of a woman who thought she could suppress her humanity.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Lady Macbeth challenges the <dfn class="term" data-def="The culturally defined roles and behaviours expected of men and women in a given society — Jacobean England expected women to be submissive and nurturing.">gender roles</dfn> of Jacobean England. She equates femininity with weakness and masculinity with the capacity for violence. Her manipulation of Macbeth (“when you durst do it, then you were a man”) weaponises this definition. But Shakespeare ultimately shows that suppressing one’s nature has catastrophic consequences — her sleepwalking represents the return of the emotions she tried to destroy.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A Jacobean audience would have seen Lady Macbeth as a warning about women who exceed their ‘natural’ role. A modern audience may find her a complex figure caught between ambition and the limited options available to women. Both perspectives are valid for AO3.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Banquo — The Moral Contrast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Banquo functions primarily as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A character whose qualities contrast with and highlight those of another character — Banquo’s moral restraint highlights Macbeth’s corruption.">foil</dfn> to Macbeth. Both men receive prophecies, but Banquo does not act on his. He warns that the “instruments of darkness tell us truths, / Win us with honest trifles, to betray’s / In deepest consequence.” His wisdom in recognising the witches’ deception contrasts with Macbeth’s willingness to be seduced by it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">However, Banquo is not entirely innocent. In Act 3, Scene 1, he admits: “Thou hast it now: King, Cawdor, Glamis, all, / As the weird women promised, and I fear / Thou play’dst most foully for’t.” He suspects Macbeth murdered Duncan but says nothing. His silence is morally ambiguous — is he protecting himself, or waiting for his own prophecy to be fulfilled?</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why Banquo is not purely virtuous - what does his silence about Duncan reveal?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Banquo serves as Macbeth’s moral foil, but he is not without fault. His silence about Duncan’s murder suggests moral compromise — perhaps influenced by the prophecy that his descendants will be kings. Shakespeare creates nuanced characters, not simple opposites.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Macduff — The Avenger</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Macduff is the character who ultimately destroys Macbeth. He is the first to suspect foul play after Duncan’s death (“O horror, horror, horror!”), he refuses to attend Macbeth’s coronation, and he flees to England to join Malcolm’s army. His patriotism and moral clarity contrast with Macbeth’s self-serving ambition.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Macduff’s most significant contribution is his redefinition of masculinity. When told to “dispute it like a man” after his family’s murder, he replies: “I shall do so; / But I must also feel it as a man.” This redefines manhood as encompassing both strength and emotional depth, directly challenging the violent definition promoted by Macbeth and Lady Macbeth throughout the play. Macduff is the play’s moral compass — he feels deeply, acts decisively, and restores justice.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Witches — Agents of Chaos</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The witches (also called the “Weïrd Sisters”) are the play’s most debated characters. They open the play, deliver the prophecies that drive the plot, and conjure the apparitions that seal Macbeth’s fate. But do they cause Macbeth’s downfall, or merely reveal desires already within him?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Shakespeare never gives a definitive answer. A Jacobean audience would have viewed the witches as genuinely dangerous — agents of Satan who use <dfn class="term" data-def="The use of deliberately ambiguous or misleading language — the witches’ prophecies are technically true but designed to deceive.">equivocation</dfn> to lead mortals to damnation. A modern audience might see them as symbolic representations of Macbeth’s inner ambition or as forces of political disruption. Their speech patterns (trochaic tetrameter, rhyming couplets) set them apart from the iambic pentameter of the human characters, reinforcing their otherness.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Duncan represents the ideal of legitimate kingship. He is generous (“signs of nobleness, like stars, shall shine / On all deservers”), trusting, and kind. His weakness — excessive trust — is arguably the only flaw that allows his murder. Shakespeare makes him a near-perfect king to maximise the horror of his killing.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Malcolm is the play’s resolution figure. His testing of Macduff and his listing of “king-becoming graces” (justice, verity, temperance, mercy, devotion, patience, courage, fortitude) establish him as the rightful heir who will restore Scotland after Macbeth’s tyranny. He represents the reassertion of the Great Chain of Being.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Macbeth is a tragic hero whose conscious awareness of his own evil makes his downfall genuinely tragic — he chooses ambition over his moral knowledge.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Lady Macbeth’s arc from ruthless planner to psychologically destroyed sleepwalker shows that suppressing one’s humanity has devastating consequences.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Shakespeare creates a spectrum of masculinity: Macbeth and Lady Macbeth define it as violence, while Macduff redefines it to include compassion and grief.</li></ul>
