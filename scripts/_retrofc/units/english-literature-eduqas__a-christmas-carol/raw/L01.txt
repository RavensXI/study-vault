<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Did Dickens Write A Christmas Carol?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charles Dickens published <em>A Christmas Carol</em> in December 1843, during a period of extreme inequality in Victorian Britain. The gap between rich and poor was enormous — while wealthy Victorians lived in luxury with servants and grand houses, millions of working-class families struggled to survive on starvation wages. Dickens had personal experience of poverty: when he was twelve, his father was imprisoned for debt, and young Charles was sent to work in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A factory where workers pasted labels onto pots of boot polish, a type of shoe blacking.">blacking factory</dfn>. This childhood trauma shaped his lifelong commitment to social justice and his determination to give the poor a voice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">In 1843, Dickens read a Parliamentary Commission report on the employment of women and children in mines and factories. He was horrified by the conditions described — children as young as four were working long hours for pitiful pay. He originally planned to write a political pamphlet, but while walking the streets of Manchester, he decided a story would be far more powerful. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A short fictional narrative, longer than a short story but shorter than a novel, typically under 100 pages.">novella</dfn> could reach people’s hearts in a way that statistics never could.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three biographical facts about Dickens link to A Christmas Carol?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dickens’s three key biographical connections: (1) his father was imprisoned for debt, (2) he worked in a factory as a child, and (3) he was driven to change conditions for the poor, especially children. These experiences directly influenced the themes of <em>A Christmas Carol</em>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Victorian Poverty & the Poor Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Victorian society was deeply divided. Those who were poor lived in squalor and misery, barely surviving. Child mortality was extremely high, and there was no free healthcare — if you fell ill and could not work, your family risked starvation. The only safety net was the <dfn class="term" data-def="An institution established under the Poor Law where destitute people were housed and forced to work in harsh conditions in exchange for basic food and shelter.">workhouse</dfn>, created by the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Conditions inside workhouses were deliberately made harsh to discourage people from seeking help. Families were separated, food was minimal, and the work was gruelling. Many wealthy Victorians believed that poverty was caused by laziness or moral failure, not by an unjust system. This attitude is directly reflected in Scrooge’s dismissive references to prisons and workhouses in Stave 1.</p>

In the novella, Scrooge echoes Malthusian thinking when he says the poor should “decrease the surplus population.” The Ghost of Christmas Present throws these words back at him in Stave 3, forcing Scrooge to confront the cruelty of his own views.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Industrial Revolution & Child Labour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">From the mid-1700s onwards, Britain’s economy shifted from agriculture to factory-based industrial production. This required a huge workforce, including children who were forced into horrific working conditions. Children worked in coal mines, textile mills, and factories, often suffering injuries or death. Without education, poor people had almost no chance of escaping the cycle of poverty. Dickens hated this injustice. His own experience in the blacking factory left him with a deep, lifelong empathy for exploited children, and through <em>A Christmas Carol</em>, he wanted wealthy readers to see the human cost of their indifference and change their behaviour.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The publication of <em>A Christmas Carol</em> was itself an act of social purpose. Dickens priced the book at five shillings — affordable for middle-class readers — and insisted on high-quality illustrations. He wanted the novella to be beautiful, accessible, and emotionally overwhelming. It sold out its first edition of 6,000 copies within days. The story was performed, adapted, and discussed across Victorian Britain, and it transformed public attitudes towards Christmas generosity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three features of the Industrial Revolution that Dickens wanted to expose.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dickens set <em>A Christmas Carol</em> at Christmas because people were already thinking about generosity, family, and goodwill. The Victorian era was when Christmas traditions — cards, crackers, Christmas trees — became popular. Dickens used the festive season to amplify his message about social responsibility.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Dickens’s Purpose & the Gothic Tradition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Dickens wrote <em>A Christmas Carol</em> to highlight the shocking contrast between rich and poor and to persuade his readers to change their attitudes. Through his iconic characters and ghostly plot, he manipulated his readers’ emotions to make his argument hit harder than any political essay could. The novella is also a <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of fiction combining horror, the supernatural, and extreme emotion, using dark settings to create atmosphere.">Gothic</dfn> ghost story — featuring Marley’s ghost, dark foggy streets, and supernatural visitations to create tension and fear.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">Dickens divides his novella into five “staves” rather than chapters. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A set of five horizontal lines on which musical notes are written; Dickens uses the term to connect the novella to the idea of a Christmas carol.">stave</dfn> is a musical term, connecting the book’s structure to its title. Just as a carol builds towards a joyful conclusion, the five staves chart Scrooge’s journey from cold-hearted miser to generous, redeemed man. Dickens also makes an <dfn class="term" data-def="A reference within a text to another literary work, drawing parallels or adding meaning.">allusion</dfn> to Shakespeare’s <em>Hamlet</em> at the opening, insisting “Marley was dead: to begin with” so the reader will be shocked when his ghost appears.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Eduqas C720U Exam & Context</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In the Eduqas exam (Component 2, Section B), you will receive a printed extract from <em>A Christmas Carol</em> and must write about both the extract and the wider text. Context (AO3) is worth a significant proportion of marks. Never just list historical facts — always link context directly to Dickens’s methods and purpose. For example, rather than writing “Workhouses existed,” explain how Dickens uses Scrooge’s dismissal of the poor to workhouses to criticise Victorian society’s cruel attitudes.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Dickens published <em>A Christmas Carol</em> in 1843 to criticise the huge gap between rich and poor in Victorian society and to persuade wealthy readers to take social responsibility seriously.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Victorian attitudes blamed poverty on laziness (influenced by Malthus). Workhouses were deliberately harsh. Dickens used Scrooge to represent — and challenge — these cruel attitudes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The novella blends Christmas tale, ghost story, and Gothic fiction. The five staves link to the musical structure of a carol, charting Scrooge’s redemption.</li></ul>
