--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Comparing Youth and Age Across Time</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The Youth and Age collection spans from Blake through early 20th-century modernism (Lawrence, Thomas) to contemporary voices (Walker, Joseph). Comparing across time reveals how attitudes to childhood, ageing, and death have shifted — the key insight for AO3.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: How have attitudes to childhood, ageing, and death changed across the three eras?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Romantic era:</strong> Childhood as <dfn class="term" data-def="An ideal state of purity and natural goodness.">Edenic innocence</dfn> threatened by industrialisation. <strong>Early 20th century:</strong> Memory as a psychological force (Lawrence, Thomas) — influenced by Freud and war trauma. <strong>Contemporary:</strong> Identity as self-constructed (Walker, Joseph) — influenced by feminism and changing social expectations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Key Contextual Shifts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Childhood:</strong> Romantic poets saw childhood as natural goodness corrupted by society. Modern poets see it as a specific, embodied experience preserved through sensory memory.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Death:</strong> Thomas writes in a Welsh tradition of passionate resistance. Joseph writes as a feminist reclaiming old age. Causley blends Christian imagery with personal grief.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Lawrence (1918) presents memory as an “insidious mastery” that overwhelms the adult against his will. His shame at “weeping like a child” reflects early 20th-century masculine norms. Memory is dangerous because it threatens composure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Sheers (2000) presents memory as identity-building: in “Farther,” the act of climbing a hill with his father is both physical effort and emotional inheritance. Sheers transforms a shared walk into a symbol of continuity. Memory is empowering because it connects to heritage.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Embedding Context Effectively</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Three strategies for embedding context:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>1. Within analysis:</strong> “Lawrence’s shame at weeping reflects early 20th-century expectations that men should suppress emotion.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>2. As comparison bridge:</strong> “While Lawrence treats nostalgia as dangerous, Walker treats memory as a source of strength — reflecting the shift from masculine emotional suppression to feminist empowerment.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>3. As genre explanation:</strong> “Thomas uses the villanelle because his grief demands a structure that refuses to let go.”</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write: One embedded context sentence using each of the three strategies.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Context must explain a <strong>difference in attitude</strong>. The exam rewards sentences like: “While [Poet A], writing in [era], presents [topic] as [attitude], [Poet B] presents it as [different attitude], reflecting [contextual shift].”</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Contextual Errors to Avoid</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Avoid vague history (“life was hard”), irrelevant biography (“Thomas was born in 1914”), and generalisations (“all Romantic poets believed…”). Make context <em>specific</em> to the poem and link it to a <em>method</em> or <em>attitude</em>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">The collection spans Romantic, modern, and contemporary eras — each brings different attitudes shaped by gender expectations, psychology, and social change.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Context must be embedded within analysis to explain differences in attitude, not listed as separate facts.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Key shifts: childhood from Edenic innocence to embodied experience; memory from psychological threat to political resource; death from feared to celebrated.</li></ul>
