<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Old Age & Mortality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The final stage in the Youth and Age arc is old age and death. The anthology includes poems that confront mortality with courage, rage, acceptance, or dark humour. These poems ask fundamental questions: What does it mean to grow old? Is death something to fear or accept? Does the self remain the same as the body declines?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What three attitudes to old age and death appear in the collection?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The collection presents three attitudes to mortality: <strong>rage and resistance</strong> (refusing to accept decline), <strong>quiet acceptance</strong> (finding peace with the inevitable), and <strong>continuity through memory</strong> (the self persists in the memories of others even after death).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Do Not Go Gentle into that Good Night — Dylan Thomas (1951)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Thomas’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A 19-line poem with two repeating refrains and a specific rhyme scheme (ABA).">villanelle</dfn> was written as his father lay dying. The form’s two refrains — “Do not go gentle into that good night” and “Rage, rage against the dying of the light” — create an insistent, almost desperate rhythm. Thomas begs his father to fight death rather than accept it peacefully.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The central stanzas catalogue different types of men — wise men, good men, wild men, grave men — all of whom resist death in their own way. “Wise men at their end know dark is right” acknowledges that death is natural, but “Because their words had forked no lightning” shows they rage because their work is unfinished. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An expression that says the opposite of its literal meaning, or a contrast between expectation and reality.">paradox</dfn> is Thomas’s central message: knowing death is inevitable does not mean you should accept it quietly. The final couplet — “And you, my father, there on the sad height, / Curse, bless, me now with your fierce tears, I pray” — is devastatingly personal. “Curse, bless” combines opposing emotions; the son wants his father’s anger as proof that life still matters.</p>

The metaphor of light = life and dark = death runs through the poem. Thomas does not deny death but insists on the value of resistance — dying fighting is better than dying passively.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Warning — Jenny Joseph (1961)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Joseph’s poem takes an entirely different approach. The speaker imagines old age as a time of <strong>liberation</strong>: “When I am an old woman I shall wear purple / With a red hat which doesn’t go, and doesn’t suit me.” Instead of fearing decline, the speaker looks forward to freedom from social expectations. She will “sit down on the pavement when I’m tired,” “press alarm bells,” and “pick the flowers in other people’s gardens.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The poem’s humour serves a serious purpose. Joseph suggests that youth and middle age are constrained by duty, respectability, and self-consciousness. Only old age brings the freedom to be truly yourself. The final stanza undercuts this: “But maybe I ought to practise a little now?” The question implies the speaker does not actually need to wait — the “warning” is to start living freely <em>now</em>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: How does Joseph challenge the negative stereotype of old age?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Joseph challenges the stereotype of old age as decline by presenting it as <strong>liberation</strong>. Wearing purple, pressing alarm bells, and eating three pounds of sausages are acts of joyful rebellion against the restraints of respectable adulthood. The poem suggests the real loss is not youth but freedom — and old age restores it.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Similarity:</strong> Both poems are about confronting old age and death. Both reject passive acceptance — Thomas demands rage, Joseph demands freedom.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Difference:</strong> Thomas is intensely serious, writing from the deathbed of his father. Joseph is playfully humorous, imagining a future self. Thomas fears death; Joseph welcomes the freedom of old age. Thomas uses a strict villanelle form; Joseph uses loose, conversational free verse.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Context:</strong> Thomas writes in a Welsh literary tradition of passionate, musical poetry. Joseph writes as a woman challenging gendered expectations of how older women should behave — quiet, invisible, dignified. Her rebellion is feminist as well as personal.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17">Thomas’s villanelle uses refrains, imperatives, and light/dark imagery to demand that his dying father resist death with passion.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18">Joseph’s “Warning” presents old age as liberation from social constraints, using humour to challenge negative stereotypes of ageing.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Both reject passive acceptance but from opposite positions: Thomas rages against death; Joseph celebrates the freedom it brings.</li></ul>
