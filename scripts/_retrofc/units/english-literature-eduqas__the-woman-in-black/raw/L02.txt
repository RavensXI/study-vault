<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Arthur's Confidence and the Journey North</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The early chapters of <em>The Woman in Black</em> establish Arthur Kipps as the classic rational protagonist — a young London solicitor sent to attend the funeral of Mrs Alice Drablow and sort through her papers at Eel Marsh House. Arthur's employer, Mr Bentley, treats the assignment as routine, and Arthur himself approaches it with breezy confidence. He describes himself as <strong>"a young man, healthy, optimistic, with a future before me"</strong> — language that radiates security and naivety. Hill uses this self-portrait deliberately: the greater Arthur's initial confidence, the more devastating his eventual collapse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A story within a story, where the main narrative is 'framed' by an outer narrative — here, the older Arthur looking back on his younger self's experiences.">frame narrative</dfn> is crucial from the opening pages. We know from Christmas Eve that the older Arthur cannot bring himself to tell his story aloud. When the stepchildren beg him to join their ghost stories, he is physically unable to participate — he leaves the room. This creates immediate <dfn class="term" data-def="When the reader knows something that the character within the story does not — here, we know Arthur's confidence will be destroyed.">dramatic irony</dfn>: as we read the young Arthur's cheerful departure from London, we already know something terrible awaits him.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Track Arthur's emotional arc across the novel: confident in London, uneasy at the funeral, terrified at Eel Marsh House, broken by the ending. Each stage shows Hill dismantling his rationality.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Arthur's early confidence serves a structural purpose: Hill establishes normality so that the supernatural has maximum impact. The frame narrative tells us from the start that this confidence will be destroyed — creating dramatic irony that underpins the entire novel.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Journey: London to Crythin Gifford</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Hill structures Arthur's journey as a gradual transition from safety to danger, civilisation to isolation. London is familiar and comfortable; the train journey takes Arthur through <strong>"flat, open countryside"</strong> that becomes increasingly bleak. When he arrives at Crythin Gifford, the landscape has shifted entirely: the town sits on the edge of vast, treacherous marshes. Hill uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A technique in which weather, landscape, or natural phenomena reflect or intensify the emotional state of characters or the mood of a scene.">pathetic fallacy</dfn> throughout the journey — the weather deteriorates as Arthur moves further from safety, the sky darkening, the fog thickening.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The journey also introduces Arthur's first encounter with the town's collective fear. When he mentions Mrs Drablow or Eel Marsh House, conversations die. Mr Jerome, the Drablow family's local agent, becomes <strong>"pale"</strong> and <strong>"agitated"</strong> — his hands shake, he avoids Arthur's questions. The landlord at the inn falls silent. Hill uses the townspeople's fear as a structural device: their refusal to speak tells us that Eel Marsh House holds a secret too terrible to name, which builds suspense before Arthur ever arrives there.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Mrs Drablow's Funeral</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The funeral is the novel's first major set-piece and the scene where Arthur first sees the woman in black. The church is almost empty — only Arthur and Mr Jerome attend, emphasising Mrs Drablow's isolation and the town's fear of anything connected to Eel Marsh House. Hill uses the sparse attendance to create an atmosphere of abandonment and dread.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">It is during the funeral that Arthur notices a figure standing apart from the other mourners: <strong>"a woman, dressed in deepest black, her face partly hidden by a veil."</strong> When she moves, Arthur sees her face — <strong>"a wasted face"</strong> with skin <strong>"tautly stretched and thin, so that the bones of her face showed through."</strong> The word <strong>"wasted"</strong> carries connotations of disease, starvation, and death; <strong>"tautly stretched"</strong> evokes a skull barely covered by flesh. Hill gives us enough detail to register that something is deeply wrong, but not enough to explain what — following the M.R. James principle that restraint creates more fear than graphic description.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="The funeral sighting is a key extract for the exam. Focus on Hill's word choices: 'wasted' (decay/illness), 'tautly stretched' (skull-like), 'bones showed through' (death). The restraint is the technique — Hill shows just enough.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Arthur's first sighting of the woman in black at the funeral establishes the ghost's physical appearance through restrained, suggestive detail. The description of a <strong>"wasted face"</strong> with <strong>"bones showing through"</strong> creates unease without graphic horror — a technique borrowed directly from M.R. James.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Mr Jerome's Reaction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">After the funeral, Arthur mentions the woman in black to Mr Jerome, expecting a simple explanation. Jerome's reaction is extreme: he goes <strong>"grey"</strong> in the face, his hands tremble, and he becomes <strong>"unable to speak."</strong> Hill uses Jerome's physical response to convey terror without explaining its cause. This is a key structural technique: the reader's fear builds not because we know what the woman is, but because we see the effect she has on people who <em>do</em> know. Jerome's inability to articulate the danger mirrors the town's collective silence — the curse is too terrible to name.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Arthur, characteristically, dismisses Jerome's reaction as <strong>"superstitious nonsense."</strong> This response reveals his Edwardian rationalism — he assumes that educated, modern men have no reason to fear ghosts. Hill uses Arthur's dismissiveness to create irony: the reader senses that Jerome's terror is justified, even as Arthur refuses to take it seriously. This gap between what the reader suspects and what Arthur believes generates the novel's mounting tension.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Structural Patterns in Chapters 1-4</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Hill establishes several structural patterns in these early chapters that recur throughout the novel. The first is the pattern of <strong>warning and dismissal</strong>: a character warns Arthur (Sam Daily, Mr Jerome, the landlord), and Arthur ignores the warning. The second is the pattern of <strong>approach and retreat</strong>: Arthur moves towards danger (Eel Marsh House), then briefly retreats to safety (the inn, Sam Daily's house), before advancing again. The third is the pattern of <strong>sensory intensification</strong>: each chapter escalates the sensory detail — sounds become louder, darkness deeper, the weather more hostile. These patterns create a relentless forward momentum that mirrors Arthur's journey deeper into the supernatural.</p>

<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Arthur's early confidence creates dramatic irony — we know from the frame narrative that it will be destroyed.</li>
