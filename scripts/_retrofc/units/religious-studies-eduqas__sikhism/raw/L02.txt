--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Gurdwara &mdash; The Sikh Place of Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Sikh place of worship &mdash; literally 'gateway to the Guru'. Any building where the Guru Granth Sahib is installed and Sikhs gather to worship.">Gurdwara</dfn> means &lsquo;gateway to the Guru&rsquo;. Any building where the Guru Granth Sahib is permanently installed and Sikhs gather for worship can be a gurdwara. The gurdwara has several key architectural and functional features that reflect Sikh values of equality, community and devotion to God.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The most important feature of any gurdwara is the <strong>Darbar Sahib</strong> (Presence Chamber) &mdash; the main hall of worship where the Guru Granth Sahib is kept on a raised platform (<em>takht</em> or throne) beneath a canopy (<em>palki</em>). The Guru Granth Sahib is treated as the living Guru: it is brought in a ceremonial procession in the morning, read aloud throughout the day, and put to rest at night. Outside the gurdwara flies the <dfn class="term" data-def="The distinctive triangular Sikh flag flown above every gurdwara, bearing the Khanda symbol, indicating that a gurdwara is nearby.">Nishan Sahib</dfn> &mdash; the triangular Sikh flag carrying the Khanda symbol. The flag signals to all that this is a gurdwara and that all are welcome.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list four physical features of a gurdwara. For each, state what it represents or what purpose it serves.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key features of a gurdwara include: the Darbar Sahib (prayer hall with the Guru Granth Sahib on the takht), the Nishan Sahib (flag outside), the langar hall (community kitchen and dining area), and a room for the Guru Granth Sahib to &lsquo;rest&rsquo; at night. All these features embody Sikh values: devotion, equality, welcome and service.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Guru Granth Sahib in Worship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The eternal, living Guru of Sikhism &mdash; the sacred scripture that is treated as the eleventh and final Guru, installed in the gurdwara on a throne and read aloud in worship.">Guru Granth Sahib</dfn> is central to every act of Sikh worship. It is not simply a book: Sikhs regard it as the eleventh and final Guru &mdash; the living Word of God. Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth human Guru, declared before his death in 1708 that there would be no further human Gurus; henceforth the scripture itself would serve as Guru for the Sikh community. This is one of the most distinctive aspects of Sikhism &mdash; a living scripture that functions as a living teacher.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In worship, the Guru Granth Sahib is read aloud and its words are sung in a distinctive style called <em>kirtan</em>. Professional musicians and readers called <em>ragis</em> sing the hymns of the Guru Granth Sahib to classical Indian musical modes (<em>ragas</em>). The congregation sits on the floor in front of the Guru Granth Sahib &mdash; everyone at the same level, reflecting the Sikh commitment to equality. Worshippers bow before the scripture and pass in front of it barefoot and with heads covered as a mark of respect.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The person responsible for the care and reading of the Guru Granth Sahib in a gurdwara &mdash; not a priest but a reader and caretaker.">granthi</dfn> is the person who reads and cares for the Guru Granth Sahib in the gurdwara. The granthi is not a priest in the sense of having special spiritual authority &mdash; any Sikh who can read the Guru Granth Sahib in its original Gurmukhi script can perform this role. This is theologically significant: Sikhism has no clergy as an intermediary between God and the worshipper. Both men and women can serve as granthis, reflecting Sikh teaching on equality.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">A Sikh service typically ends with the <dfn class="term" data-def="The closing prayer of a Sikh service, recited together by the congregation, containing a petition for the welfare of all humanity.">Ardas</dfn> &mdash; a formal communal prayer recited standing, with hands clasped, petitioning God for the welfare of the community and all humanity. After the Ardas, a random passage is opened from the Guru Granth Sahib &mdash; this is called the <em>Hukamnama</em> (divine command) &mdash; and the opening verse is read aloud as God&rsquo;s guidance for the day. The service concludes with the distribution of karah prashad.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Karah Prashad and Langar</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A sweet, sacred food made from equal quantities of flour, sugar and ghee, distributed to all present at the end of a Sikh service as a symbol of equality.">Karah prashad</dfn> is a sacred sweet food made from equal quantities of flour, sugar and ghee (clarified butter), prepared with prayers. It is distributed to everyone present at the end of worship &mdash; regardless of religion, caste or background. The equal distribution of karah prashad symbolises the Sikh belief in the equality of all people before God. No one is turned away and every portion is equal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The community kitchen and free meal served in every gurdwara, open to all people regardless of religion, caste, gender or background, expressing Sikh values of equality and selfless service.">langar</dfn> is the community kitchen and free vegetarian meal served in every gurdwara. It was introduced by Guru Nanak and developed by subsequent Gurus as a practical expression of Sikh values. The langar meal is prepared and served by volunteers as an act of <em>sewa</em> (selfless service). Its significance is profound: by eating together, sitting on the floor in rows, people of all backgrounds, faiths and social positions are made equal. Historically, this was a radical challenge to caste-based dining restrictions. The Guru Granth Sahib (page 349) affirms the value of seva and the community practice of sharing food together.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Explain in two sentences how both karah prashad and langar express the Sikh belief in equality. State one thing they have in common.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Both karah prashad (sacred food shared equally) and langar (free community meal for all) express the Sikh belief that all people are equal before God. Both challenge caste distinctions: sharing food with people of all backgrounds was historically radical. The Guru Granth Sahib (p.349) connects langar to sewa and equality.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Naming a Sikh Child</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">When a Sikh baby is born, the family brings the child to the gurdwara for a naming ceremony. A passage is chosen at random from the Guru Granth Sahib (Hukamnama), and the baby is given a name beginning with the first letter of the first word of the passage. This is called <em>Naam Karan</em>. The baby&rsquo;s name connects them to the Guru Granth Sahib from the very first days of life. All Sikh boys receive the additional name <dfn class="term" data-def="The name given to all male Sikhs, meaning 'lion', originally given at the Khalsa initiation but now given to all Sikh boys at birth.">Singh</dfn> (meaning &lsquo;lion&rsquo;) and all girls receive the name <dfn class="term" data-def="The name given to all female Sikhs, meaning 'princess', expressing the equality of all Sikh women before God.">Kaur</dfn> (meaning &lsquo;princess&rsquo; or &lsquo;princess of the royal court&rsquo;). These names were given by Guru Gobind Singh to abolish caste-based surnames, which would reveal a person&rsquo;s birth status. By giving all Sikhs the same names, the Gurus declared the equality of all.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Amrit Sanskar &mdash; Sikh Initiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sikh initiation ceremony in which a person joins the Khalsa by drinking and receiving amrit (sacred sugar water), committing to follow the Sikh code of conduct.">Amrit Sanskar</dfn> (also called the Khalsa initiation or Amrit Ceremony) is the ceremony in which a Sikh formally joins the <em>Khalsa</em> &mdash; the community of initiated Sikhs. It was established by Guru Gobind Singh at Vaisakhi in 1699. The ceremony involves:</p>

    <span>Features of the Amrit Sanskar Ceremony</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The ceremony takes place in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib and five initiated Sikhs (the Panj Pyare &mdash; &lsquo;Five Beloved Ones&rsquo;, representing the original five Sikhs who volunteered their lives to Guru Gobind Singh in 1699). Amrit &mdash; sacred sugar water stirred with a double-edged sword (khanda) while hymns are recited &mdash; is prepared. The initiate drinks amrit five times, has it sprinkled on their eyes and hair, and repeats the Mool Mantar five times. They commit to observe the Sikh code of conduct (<em>Rehat Maryada</em>) and to wear the <dfn class="term" data-def="The five articles of faith worn by initiated Sikhs (Khalsa), each beginning with the letter K: Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (wooden comb), Kara (steel bracelet), Kachera (cotton undergarment) and Kirpan (steel sword).">Five Ks</dfn> (Panj Kakars).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The Five Ks are: <strong>Kesh</strong> (uncut hair &mdash; symbolising acceptance of God&rsquo;s will and distinctively Sikh identity); <strong>Kangha</strong> (a small wooden comb kept in the hair &mdash; symbolising cleanliness and spiritual discipline); <strong>Kara</strong> (a steel bracelet on the right wrist &mdash; symbolising eternal God, who has no beginning or end, and the Sikh&rsquo;s commitment to righteous action); <strong>Kachera</strong> (cotton shorts &mdash; symbolising chastity and moral restraint); and <strong>Kirpan</strong> (a steel sword &mdash; symbolising the duty to defend the weak and fight injustice). Together the Five Ks make a Khalsa Sikh visually identifiable and connect every initiated Sikh to the same community of commitment.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Not all Sikhs take Amrit. Those who have not yet done so are called <em>Sahajdhari</em> Sikhs &mdash; those on the path but who have not yet undergone initiation. Sikhism recognises both groups as genuine members of the Sikh faith. This reflects an important diversity within the Sikh community that students should acknowledge when answering exam questions about Sikh identity.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The gurdwara ('gateway to the Guru') is the Sikh place of worship. Key features include the Darbar Sahib (hall with the Guru Granth Sahib on the takht), the Nishan Sahib flag, and the langar hall. The granthi reads and cares for the Guru Granth Sahib but is not a priest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Karah prashad (sacred food shared equally) and langar (free community meal for all) both express Sikh belief in the equality of all people before God — rooted in sewa (selfless service) and the Guru Granth Sahib (p.349).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The Amrit Sanskar (Khalsa initiation) was established at Vaisakhi 1699 by Guru Gobind Singh. Initiates drink amrit and commit to wearing the Five Ks: Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (comb), Kara (steel bracelet), Kachera (shorts) and Kirpan (sword). All Sikhs receive the names Singh (boys) or Kaur (girls), affirming equality by abolishing caste surnames.</li>
</ul>
