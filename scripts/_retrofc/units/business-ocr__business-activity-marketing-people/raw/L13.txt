--- DESCRIPTION ---
How the four Ps work as a system and how market data signals when the mix should change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Over the last few lessons we have looked at each of the four Ps — product, price, promotion and place — one at a time. Real businesses do not decide them one at a time. They work as a system. A change to the price affects the right promotion. A change to the place affects the target customer, which feeds back into the product. This lesson shows how the four Ps work together and how businesses read market data to decide when the mix needs to change.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">The Four Ps as a System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The <dfn class="term" data-def="When all four Ps — product, price, promotion and place — work together and send the same consistent message to the target customer.">integrated marketing mix</dfn> is the idea that the four Ps must reinforce each other. If they pull in different directions, customers get a confused message and sales suffer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Apple is the textbook example. The product is premium (clean design, aluminium and glass), the price is premium (£1,000+ for a flagship iPhone), the promotion is sleek (minimal TV adverts, clean website), and the place is premium (Apple Stores on the best streets in London, Manchester and Liverpool plus apple.com). Every P says the same thing: ‘this is high-end’. If Apple suddenly started selling through Poundland, the whole brand image would collapse.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Primark — The Opposite Example</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Primark aims at the opposite end of the UK market, and its mix is just as integrated. The product is cheap, fast-fashion basics. The price is the lowest on the high street. The promotion relies on word-of-mouth and shop-window displays rather than TV adverts. The place is big high-street stores in city centres, with deliberately no online sales — delivery costs would wipe out the margins on a £5 T-shirt. Every P says ‘cheap, cheerful, impulse-buy in store’, and that consistency is what makes the strategy work.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in one sentence what an integrated marketing mix is, with a UK example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An integrated marketing mix is one where all four Ps — product, price, promotion and place — reinforce the same message for the target customer. Apple and Primark are both integrated, even though they target opposite ends of the market.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">ASOS — Digital-First Mix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">ASOS shows how all four Ps can lock together around a digital-first model. The product is on-trend fashion aimed at 18-to-34-year-olds. The price is competitive with Zara and H&M, with frequent student-discount offers. The promotion is heavy on Instagram and TikTok because that is where the target customer is. The place is 100% digital — a website and an app — because the target customer shops on their phone. If ASOS tried to run television adverts aimed at over-60s while keeping everything else the same, the mix would fall apart: the channel would not match the customer the other three Ps were designed for.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Reading the Market — Interpreting Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The mix is never fixed. Customer behaviour, technology and rivals all keep moving, and businesses use <dfn class="term" data-def="Numerical information about customers, sales, demand and market share — used to judge whether the current marketing mix is still working.">market data</dfn> to spot when a change is needed. Three common data signals are changes in demand, changes in market share, and the effect of promotion on sales.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Imagine a UK chocolate bar is selling 2 million units a week in 2023, 1.8 million in 2024 and 1.6 million in 2025. Demand has fallen 20% in two years. The mix needs attention. The business could refresh the product (new flavours, different packaging), cut the price to attract price-sensitive shoppers, promote more heavily (TV or social media relaunch), or change the place (push into smaller convenience stores). Data on its own does not decide — it tells the business that something is wrong and prompts a choice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Example 2 — Rising Rival Market Share Forces a Response</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">For years the UK supermarket <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of total sales in a market that is held by one business — calculated as business sales divided by total market sales and multiplied by 100.">market share</dfn> was dominated by Tesco, Sainsbury’s, Asda and Morrisons. Between 2010 and the early 2020s Aldi and Lidl climbed from under 5% to roughly 18% combined. Tesco saw the data every quarter and had to respond: it launched the Aldi Price Match promotion (price), refreshed Clubcard (a promotion loyalty tool), expanded the Finest range (product) and pushed online delivery (place). All four Ps moved together — the mix had to change as a system, not one P at a time.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Example 3 — Measuring the Effect of a Promotion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">A coffee chain spends £50,000 on a two-week Instagram campaign. Weekly sales before the campaign averaged £200,000. During the campaign they climbed to £240,000 a week — a 20% lift. Afterwards sales settled at £210,000. The data tells the business two things: the campaign worked in the short term, and there is a small lasting lift even after the spend ended. The question for next time is whether £50,000 was the right amount — would £25,000 have achieved most of the effect? Data interpretation turns a marketing decision into a numbers-based judgement.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">When Part of the Mix Falls Behind</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Debenhams is a cautionary case. For years the product (mid-tier fashion) and price (mid-tier) stayed roughly the same while rivals like Next invested in e-commerce and ASOS captured the online segment. Debenhams’ place (mainly high-street stores) fell behind the market — the mix stopped being integrated because only three Ps were moving forward. The business fell into administration in 2020. When data shows one P dragging, the business has to decide quickly whether to invest in bringing it up to date or shrink to match the remaining strengths.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write one sentence for each of the three data signals (demand, share, promotion) and the mix change they might trigger.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Market data signals — changes in demand, changes in market share and the measured effect of promotion — tell a business when the marketing mix needs to change. A good mix moves all four Ps together, not one at a time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Mix Follows the Customer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">All of this comes back to one idea: the marketing mix exists to serve the target customer. Data tells the business what the customer is doing. The four Ps are the levers the business can pull in response. A mix that matches the customer — Apple for premium tech fans, Primark for value-led fashion, ASOS for digital-native Gen Z — keeps the business growing. A mix that drifts out of step with the customer loses market share quickly.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The four Ps must work together as an integrated mix — changing one usually means rethinking the others.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Market data (demand changes, market share changes, promotion results) signals when the mix needs to change.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">A mix that matches the target customer drives growth; a mix that drifts out of step loses share quickly.</li>
</ul>
