--- DESCRIPTION ---
What a business plan does, what goes in it, and how it reduces risk and helps secure finance.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Purpose of Planning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A good idea by itself is not a business — it is a hope. The step that turns the idea into a venture someone will lend money to, and that rivals will have to take seriously, is a written plan. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A written document that sets out a business's idea, market, finances and operations, used to guide decisions and attract investors.">business plan</dfn> forces the entrepreneur to answer the awkward questions early: who will buy this, for how much, against whom, and with what money? The specification says the purpose of planning business activity is to reduce risk and help the business succeed. Both matter, and both flow from the same document.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Planning reduces risk because the process itself flushes out hidden problems. Writing down your expected sales forces you to check your market research. Drafting your forecast costs makes you ring suppliers for real prices. Adding a cash flow forecast reveals the month when money will be tightest. None of these problems go away because they are ignored — but they are cheaper to fix on paper than after launch.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list in your own words the two stated purposes of business planning and why each matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The two purposes of planning business activity are to reduce risk and to help the business succeed. Writing the plan forces entrepreneurs to answer awkward questions early — before the money is at stake.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">What Goes Inside a Business Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A typical business plan runs to 15–30 pages and contains a standard set of sections. The <strong>executive summary</strong> opens with the one-paragraph pitch — what the business is, who it serves, and why it will work. The <strong>market section</strong> lays out target customers, market size and main competitors, with evidence drawn from market research. The <strong>operations section</strong> explains how the product will actually be made or delivered — premises, suppliers, staff. The <strong>finance section</strong> contains the numbers: start-up costs, forecast sales, forecast profit and loss, and a cash flow forecast for the first year or two. The <strong>people section</strong> names the founders and any key hires and explains why this team can deliver. A short <strong>appendix</strong> holds CVs, research data and quotations from suppliers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Each section has a job. The market section proves customers exist. The operations section proves the founders know how the product reaches them. The finance section proves the maths works. Skipping one leaves a question a lender or investor will immediately ask at interview.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Role of the Plan — Identifying Markets and Resources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The specification lists four roles for a business plan once it has been written. It <strong>identifies markets</strong> — who the customers are, where they are and how many there might be. A cafe owner in Leeds who writes ‘everyone’ as her target customer has not identified a market; writing ‘office workers on Greek Street lunching between 12 and 2’ has.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The plan also <strong>identifies the resources the business needs to operate</strong>. Resources here means premises, equipment, stock, staff and start-up cash. A bakery needs ovens, a van, a shop lease, ingredients, two part-time counter staff and insurance — all of which cost real money, and all of which have to be found before the doors open. A plan that lists resources makes it obvious when the founder’s savings will not stretch and finance will be needed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Role of the Plan — Obtaining Finance and Achieving Objectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A written plan helps <dfn class="term" data-def="Money a business raises from banks, investors or its own savings to pay for starting up or running its activities.">finance</dfn> in two ways. First, lenders almost always require one. No high-street bank will lend to a start-up without seeing a business plan with a realistic cash flow forecast. Second, a plan presents the idea credibly — an investor reading a plan with clear numbers, market evidence and supplier quotations is more willing to back the founder than one listening to an excited pitch across a kitchen table.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Finally, a plan helps the business <strong>achieve its aims and objectives</strong> — the SMART targets covered later in this unit. By writing objectives down and sequencing them against a timeline, the founder has a map: month three, break even on weekly sales; month six, hire a second baker; year two, open a second shop. Running without a plan is running without a compass: you may keep moving, but you will not know if you are getting closer to your destination.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write the four roles of a business plan from memory, matching each to a concrete example.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A business plan has four roles: identifying markets, helping to obtain finance, identifying resources the business needs, and achieving aims and objectives. Skipping any one of them leaves a gap a lender or investor will notice.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Limitations of a Business Plan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A plan is useful, but it is not the business. Plans are written with <dfn class="term" data-def="Predictions of future sales, costs or cash flows — always uncertain because they depend on events that have not yet happened.">forecasts</dfn> — and forecasts are not facts. If the economy slows, if a rival opens nearby, or if a pandemic closes the high street, the numbers will be wrong. Plans can quickly go <strong>out of date</strong>: a plan written in January 2020 that assumed busy central London footfall was useless by April. Over-optimistic founders sometimes stretch the forecasts to win the loan, then struggle to hit numbers that were never realistic. A good plan is therefore a <em>living document</em> — reviewed every few months, adjusted when reality diverges, and not used as a straitjacket that forces the business down a road conditions no longer support.</p>

The plan did its job not by being right, but by giving the business a clear baseline it could revise when the world shifted.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Planning reduces risk and helps a business succeed by forcing founders to answer the awkward questions — customers, costs, competition — before launch.</li><li data-narration-id="n22">A business plan has four roles: identifying markets, obtaining finance, identifying resources and achieving aims and objectives.</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Plans have limitations — forecasts are uncertain and documents go out of date — so a good plan is reviewed regularly rather than filed away.</li></ul>
