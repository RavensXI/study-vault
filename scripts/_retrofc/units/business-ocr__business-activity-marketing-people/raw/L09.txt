--- DESCRIPTION ---
Splitting markets by age, gender, income, location and lifestyle to target the right customers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Split the Market?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">No business can please everyone. A supermarket that tries to be the cheapest, the most luxurious, the most ethical and the most convenient at the same time ends up being none of them. That is why businesses use <dfn class="term" data-def="Splitting a market into smaller groups of customers who share similar characteristics so a business can target each group more precisely.">market segmentation</dfn> — they split the whole market into smaller groups of customers who share similar characteristics, pick one or more groups to focus on, and design the product, price, promotion and place to match that group exactly. The OCR specification names five ways to segment: age, gender, income, location and lifestyle.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of choosing which market segment or segments a business will focus its marketing on.">Targeting</dfn> is the next step. Once a business has split the market into segments, it chooses which segment or segments to serve. Aldi targets price-conscious shoppers; Waitrose targets high-income households who want quality and service. Both are supermarkets; both are successful; they simply picked different segments.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the five segmentation bases named in the OCR specification, in any order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five segmentation bases in the OCR specification are age, gender, income, location and lifestyle. Each lets a business design a marketing mix aimed at one specific group rather than wasting money trying to please everyone.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Five Bases in Detail</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Age, Gender, Income, Location, Lifestyle</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Age</strong> — splitting the market into age bands. LEGO targets children aged 4–11, Netflix teen comedies target 13–19, and Saga holidays and insurance target the over-50s. Each age group has different spending power, different interests and different media habits. An advert that works on TikTok for a 17-year-old rarely works for a 70-year-old.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Gender</strong> — splitting by male, female or non-binary customers. Fragrance brands like Lynx design products around young male shoppers, while Dove’s ‘Real Beauty’ campaigns target women. Sports brands often launch separate lines too — Gymshark’s womenswear range now matches its menswear in size.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Income</strong> — splitting by how much customers earn. Aldi and Lidl target shoppers who want the lowest price. Waitrose and Fortnum & Mason target higher-income households who will pay more for quality and service. Income segmentation shapes price, product quality and where the shops open — Aldi moves into areas with lower average incomes, Waitrose into wealthier commuter towns.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Location</strong> — splitting by where customers live. A surf shop in Newquay stocks very different products to a ski shop in Aviemore, even though both sell sports gear. Location affects weather, culture, language and which rivals are nearby. Greggs runs different offers in the north and south of England to reflect regional tastes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Lifestyle</strong> — splitting by how customers live: active or sedentary, health-conscious or indulgent, outdoorsy, vegan, luxury-focused. Gymshark targets young fitness enthusiasts who train regularly and post workout content online; The North Face targets the outdoorsy segment who hike, climb and ski. Lifestyle is the newest of the five bases and overlaps with age and income, but it is powerful because it captures <em>how</em> people spend their time and money, not just their demographics.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Segmentation in Action — Aldi vs Waitrose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Aldi and Waitrose sell the same basic goods — bread, milk, vegetables — but their segmentation is completely different. Aldi targets <strong>income</strong> (low to mid), <strong>location</strong> (everywhere, including smaller towns) and <strong>lifestyle</strong> (value-seeking, practical). Its stores are no-frills, its own-brand products sit on pallets, and its adverts use the slogan ‘Like brands, only cheaper’.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Waitrose targets <strong>income</strong> (high), <strong>location</strong> (commuter towns and wealthier city suburbs) and <strong>lifestyle</strong> (quality-focused, ethically minded, time-rich). Its stores feel premium, its adverts focus on British farming and sourcing, and prices are noticeably higher. Neither supermarket is trying to win the other’s customers — both have designed their whole marketing mix around a different segment and are happy to stay there.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Segmentation in Action — Gymshark and Saga</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Gymshark is built around <strong>age</strong> (16–25), <strong>gender</strong> (male and female, with separate ranges), and <strong>lifestyle</strong> (active, social-media-fluent, gym-focused). The brand rose from a Solihull garage in 2012 to more than a billion pounds in valuation by 2024 — not by trying to sell to every shopper, but by owning one tight segment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Saga sits at the opposite end of the age axis. It targets the over-50s with insurance, holidays, magazines and cruises. Its products are designed around older customers — large print, easy booking, holidays that travel slower — and its adverts run in newspapers and TV channels watched by older audiences, not on TikTok. Again, the segmentation drives every part of the marketing mix.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Combining Bases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Most larger UK businesses use more than one segmentation base at the same time. Boots divides the health and beauty market by gender (men’s Boots range vs No7 for women), by age (Boots Baby vs Boots 50+ vitamins), by income (premium brands vs own-label) and by location (city-centre flagship stores, retail park ‘beauty’ stores, local pharmacies). Combining bases gives a much sharper picture of the customer than using one base alone — a 55-year-old woman on a high income shopping in central London has different needs to a 20-year-old man on a low income in a market town, even though both are Boots customers. This kind of detail feeds straight into the <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of product, price, promotion and place that a business uses to sell something.">marketing mix</dfn> every brand builds.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and name one UK business for each of the five segmentation bases.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Segmenting lets a business focus its marketing on people who are likely to buy, which raises the return on every pound spent. Bigger firms usually combine two or more bases (e.g. Boots uses age, gender, income AND location) for a sharper target.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Benefits and Drawbacks of Segmenting</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Benefits:</strong> marketing spend is focused on people likely to buy, so the return on each pound is higher. Products are designed around real customer needs, raising sales and loyalty. Smaller firms can also compete with giants by owning one segment rather than fighting across the whole market.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Drawbacks:</strong> segmentation costs money — the business needs market research to identify segments and may need different products, packaging and adverts for each. There is also a risk of stereotyping customers and missing buyers who do not fit the expected profile. Brands have to watch for segment drift too — when Topshop stopped appealing to its core 16–25 segment, it collapsed into administration in 2021.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">The five OCR segmentation bases are age, gender, income, location and lifestyle. Each is a different way of splitting customers into groups who share characteristics.</li><li data-narration-id="n25">Aldi, Waitrose, Gymshark and Saga all succeed because every marketing decision — product, price, shop location, advert — flows from the segment they have chosen.</li><li data-narration-id="n26">Larger businesses often combine two or more bases for a sharper target — Boots uses age, gender, income and location together across its health and beauty range.</li></ul>

A common mistake is to list the five bases without applying them.
