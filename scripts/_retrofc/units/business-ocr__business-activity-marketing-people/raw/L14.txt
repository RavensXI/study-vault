--- DESCRIPTION ---
Tall, flat and matrix structures, span of control, delegation and modern UK ways of working.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">Human resources (HR) is the business function that looks after people — recruiting them, organising them, motivating them and developing them. Every business, from a two-person coffee van to a supermarket chain, needs some way of showing <em>who reports to whom</em> and how people are employed. This lesson covers two OCR topics: organisational structures (tall, flat, matrix) and ways of working (full-time, part-time, flexible, temporary, remote and self-employed).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Why Structure Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The way a business arranges its employees into layers of authority, showing who reports to whom.">organisational structure</dfn> sets out job roles, responsibilities and lines of communication. It decides how quickly an instruction travels, who is allowed to make which decision, and how many people each manager supervises. Get it right and the business runs smoothly; get it wrong and instructions get lost, staff feel unclear on their role, and decisions take far too long.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Tall (Hierarchical) Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure with many layers of management and narrow spans of control, also called a hierarchical structure.">tall structure</dfn> has many layers of management. An NHS hospital trust is a clear example: a chief executive sits above directors, who sit above senior managers, ward managers and clinical staff. Each manager oversees only a handful of people, which means tight supervision and clear promotion paths — but decisions are slow because a message has to climb many rungs. Large, safety-critical organisations like the Royal Navy, the NHS and national banks tend to be tall because clear lines of responsibility matter more than speed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Flat Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure with few layers of management and wide spans of control.">flat structure</dfn> has few layers. A tech start-up of twenty people might have a founder, one team of engineers and one team of marketers — that is it. Decisions happen fast, staff feel trusted and costs are lower because there are fewer managers to pay. The trade-off is that each manager supervises a lot of people, so they cannot give much individual attention. Start-ups and creative agencies usually run flat; they add layers as they grow.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Matrix Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure where employees report to two managers at once — one for their function and one for their project or product.">matrix structure</dfn> mixes two reporting lines. An engineer at an engineering firm might report to both the ‘engineering department’ manager AND the manager of the specific project they are working on at the moment. This works well for businesses where project teams form and re-form — construction, consultancy, pharmaceuticals. Staff get the benefit of expert function managers plus hands-on project leadership. The risk is that two managers may pull in different directions, so clear rules on who decides what are essential.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three structure types with one UK example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tall, flat and matrix are the three main structures. Tall suits large, safety-critical organisations; flat suits start-ups and small firms; matrix suits project-based businesses where teams re-form often.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Span of Control and Chain of Command</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of employees a manager is directly responsible for.">span of control</dfn> is the number of people a single manager oversees. A narrow span (three or four staff) allows close mentoring; a wide span (fifteen or more) demands that workers manage themselves. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The line of authority from the top of a business to the bottom, showing who instructs whom.">chain of command</dfn> is the route an instruction travels from the top of the business to the bottom. A long chain means careful control; a short chain means fewer people reword the message, so it arrives clearly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Delegation, Subordinates and Authority</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Passing authority for a task down to an employee at a lower level of the business, while the manager remains accountable.">Delegation</dfn> is when a manager hands a task, and the authority to complete it, to a <dfn class="term" data-def="An employee who reports to, and is responsible to, a manager further up the chain of command.">subordinate</dfn> further down the chain. A supermarket duty manager might delegate a promotional display to a supervisor. Good delegation motivates staff by showing trust, develops skills and frees managers for bigger decisions. Poor delegation — giving someone a task without the <dfn class="term" data-def="The right to make decisions, give instructions and allocate resources within a defined area of work.">authority</dfn> to decide, or without proper training — causes mistakes and resentment. The manager stays accountable even after delegating; handing the task over is not the same as handing over the responsibility.</p>

A national retailer with hundreds of sites needs a taller structure so that all shops follow the same standards. Fast-moving sectors like fashion and tech tend to stay flat so they can respond quickly to customers; regulated sectors like banking and healthcare tend to stay taller so risks are controlled. Matrix fits project-based businesses like consultancies, construction firms and pharmaceutical research teams where the same employee contributes to several projects at once.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Ways of Working</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Once the business has a shape, it also has choices about how people are actually employed. OCR names several <dfn class="term" data-def="The different arrangements under which people work for a business — including contract type, location and hours.">ways of working</dfn> that have become common in UK businesses.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Full-time</strong> — usually 35 to 40 hours a week. Gives the business a committed worker and the employee a stable income and holiday pay. Used for most core roles at M&S and Tesco.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Part-time</strong> — fewer hours, often pro-rata pay and benefits. Helps parents, students and carers stay in work and helps businesses match staffing to demand — a café can run three staff at lunchtime and one mid-afternoon.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Temporary</strong> — a fixed-term or casual arrangement, common at Christmas (Royal Mail hires thousands of seasonal sorters) or during exam marking.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Zero-hour</strong> — no guaranteed hours; workers are called in as needed. Common in hospitality and retail. Gives businesses maximum flexibility but leaves workers with no guaranteed income.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Flexible working</strong> — staggered hours, compressed weeks, or flexi-time. Helps staff balance work and life, which can improve retention.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Working from home / remote working</strong> — the employee does the job from home or another location, using a laptop and internet. Exploded in use after 2020. Cuts commuting and can cut office rent, but makes teamwork and training harder.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Self-employed</strong> — the worker is their own boss (a freelance designer, a plumber, a market trader). Not an employee of the business at all — hired for specific jobs and paid per invoice.</li>
</ul>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">The right mix of ways of working helps a business control costs and attract the right people. A supermarket might use full-time staff for managers, part-time staff for evenings and weekends, and seasonal temporary staff for Christmas. A software firm might use full-time engineers, flexible home-working, and self-employed specialists for one-off design jobs. Ways of working also affect morale — staff who are offered flexibility tend to stay longer, which lowers recruitment costs. Overuse of zero-hour contracts or temporary work can hurt the brand as an employer, so businesses balance flexibility against stability.</p>

  <li data-narration-id="n30">Span of control, chain of command, delegation, subordinates and authority all describe how decisions and instructions move through a business.</li>
