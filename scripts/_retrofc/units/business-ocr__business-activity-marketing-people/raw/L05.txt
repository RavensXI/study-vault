--- DESCRIPTION ---
Who has a stake in a business, how business activity affects them, and how they in turn influence the business.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Who Are Stakeholders?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="Any individual or group that is affected by, or has an interest in, the activities of a business.">stakeholder</dfn> is anyone who is affected by or has an interest in what a business does. When a new Greggs opens in a town, the impact spreads far wider than the bakery itself — owners expect profit, staff gain jobs, customers get sausage rolls, rival coffee shops lose trade, suppliers win a new contract, the local community gets extra footfall, and the government gains tax on every sale. Each of these groups has a different ‘stake’ in the decision.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The specification splits stakeholders into two groups. <dfn class="term" data-def="Stakeholders who are inside the business — owners, managers and employees.">Internal stakeholders</dfn> work inside the business: owners, managers and employees. <dfn class="term" data-def="Stakeholders who are outside the business — customers, suppliers, the local community and the government.">External stakeholders</dfn> are outside but still affected: customers, suppliers, the local community and the government. Every big decision helps some and hurts others — and those who are hurt will push back.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Main Stakeholder Groups and Their Objectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Owners</strong> (sole traders, partners or shareholders) invest their money and time. They want profits, <dfn class="term" data-def="A share of profit paid to shareholders, usually paid twice a year.">dividends</dfn> and a rising share value. <strong>Employees</strong> depend on the business for their pay and security. They want high wages, job security, safe conditions and the chance to develop. <strong>Customers</strong> want quality products at fair prices, honest advertising and reliable after-sales service.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Suppliers</strong> want prompt payment and long-term contracts so they can plan their own business. The <strong>local community</strong> wants jobs and investment, but objects to noise, pollution or traffic — if Amazon opens a new warehouse, locals want the jobs but not the lorries clogging the road at 4am. The <strong>government</strong> is also a stakeholder: it collects corporation tax, VAT, income tax and employer National Insurance from every UK business, it regulates how businesses operate through consumer, employment and environmental law, and it funds public services that a healthy business base makes possible. Every time a business grows, hires or imports, the government has a stake in the outcome.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the internal and external stakeholder groups named in the specification, with one objective each group typically has.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Internal stakeholders are owners, managers and employees. External stakeholders are customers, suppliers, local community and government. Each has different, often competing, objectives — the business must balance them to succeed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Effect of Business Activity on Stakeholders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Every business decision radiates out to different stakeholders. When Morrisons cuts 150 middle-management jobs, shareholders may cheer the cost savings while the affected employees lose their income and morale drops across the remaining workforce. When a new Aldi opens in a town, customers gain lower prices, but independent grocers nearby may lose enough trade to close. When Tesla opens a Gigafactory, the local community gets thousands of jobs, but also extra traffic and pressure on local housing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Even an apparently positive decision — raising staff wages, for example — can be divisive: employees welcome the rise, owners see profits dip, and customers face higher prices as costs are passed on. Suppliers caught in the pay squeeze may lose orders if the business stops buying to protect margins. The government is affected too: a business making larger profits pays more corporation tax; a business closing a factory drops employees onto unemployment benefit, costing the taxpayer. Good managers ask, before every major choice, <em>‘who benefits, who pays, and how loudly will the losers object?’</em> A decision that ignores this question rarely survives contact with reality.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">Imagine a delivery firm decides to switch to 24-hour operations. Owners benefit — higher revenue and better use of vans. Customers benefit — overnight deliveries. Employees split: some welcome the night-shift premium, others hate the hours. Suppliers near the depot must also work later. The local community loses — sleep-disturbing lorry noise at 3am. The local council (government) fields complaints and may tighten planning conditions. This is <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where different stakeholder groups want opposing things from a business decision.">stakeholder conflict</dfn>: one decision, multiple opposing reactions. The business cannot please everyone, so it must judge which group matters most to its success.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Effect of Stakeholders on the Business</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Stakeholders are not passive — they have levers they can pull. <strong>Owners and shareholders</strong> can vote at annual general meetings, replace directors or refuse further investment. <strong>Employees</strong> can work harder, strike, quit, or go slow — poor treatment kills productivity. <strong>Customers</strong> vote with their wallets: when Boohoo was linked to underpaid factory workers in Leicester, sales fell and the share price halved within days. <strong>Suppliers</strong> can demand upfront payment or refuse to extend credit when a firm becomes risky.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The <strong>local community</strong> can block planning applications or run a campaign through the local media. The <strong>government</strong> influences businesses through tax rates (higher corporation tax lowers retained profit), regulation (consumer law, minimum wage, environmental rules), and government spending (decisions to contract NHS services can make or break a supplier). Different stakeholders carry different weight. A Tesco Plc decision is shaped heavily by big shareholders, while a village cafe lives or dies on the goodwill of its local community. The shift from private to public ownership often means managers now spend more time managing investor expectations than they do serving customers.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in one sentence why stakeholder objectives often conflict — using a real decision such as opening a 24-hour supermarket.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Stakeholder objectives often conflict: owners want dividends, employees want higher pay, customers want lower prices, the local community wants minimal disruption and the government wants tax and law-compliance. A business cannot maximise all at once — managing the trade-offs is a core leadership skill.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">Businesses resolve conflicts through trade-offs and communication. A factory can invest in quieter machinery to reduce community complaints while preserving production. A supermarket can introduce self-checkouts (cuts costs, helps shareholders) but redeploy staff into personal shopping roles rather than making them redundant. Clear communication — explaining a decision honestly — often softens the blow even when the decision itself is unpopular. The businesses that survive conflict best are those that treat stakeholders as partners rather than obstacles.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Stakeholders are internal (owners, managers, employees) or external (customers, suppliers, local community, government) — each has different objectives that often conflict.</li><li data-narration-id="n20">Business activity affects stakeholders: a single decision can raise profits for owners while costing jobs or disturbing neighbours.</li><li data-narration-id="n21">Stakeholders also affect the business — through purchases, strikes, planning objections, regulation and tax — so managing stakeholder relationships protects long-term success.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">OCR splits stakeholder questions into two directions — the effect OF business activity on stakeholders AND the effect OF stakeholders on the business. Check which direction the question is asking about before you start writing. For Analyse questions, pick one group and develop the chain of reasoning rather than listing all stakeholders superficially. A good answer reads: ‘Employees depend on the business for pay — a round of redundancies cuts their income, which lowers spending power locally, which damages morale for those who remain and increases staff turnover, raising recruitment costs for the business.’</p>
