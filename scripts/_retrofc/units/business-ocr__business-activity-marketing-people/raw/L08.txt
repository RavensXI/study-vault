--- DESCRIPTION ---
Primary vs secondary research, qualitative vs quantitative data — and picking the right method.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Market Research?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of gathering information about customers, competitors and a market to help make business decisions.">Market research</dfn> is how a business finds out what customers want, what rivals are doing and how big the market really is. Without it, decisions are guesswork. With it, a business can launch products, set prices and design adverts based on evidence from real people and real data. The specification requires you to know how different methods and sources of research suit different business questions — and how to use both qualitative and quantitative data.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Tesco is a useful example. Its Clubcard scheme collects data from millions of shoppers every week, telling Tesco which products are selling, which adverts worked and where to open the next store. A small independent bakery cannot match that scale, but it can still do good research — a ten-minute walk around the high street to count rivals is research; a short chat at the counter about what customers would try next is research. Research does not have to be expensive; it has to answer the business question.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the two broad types of research (primary and secondary) and the two types of data (qualitative and quantitative).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Market research splits two ways: by WHO collects it (primary = the business itself; secondary = someone else already) and by WHAT kind of data it collects (qualitative = opinions and reasons; quantitative = numbers and measurable facts).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Primary Research Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="New information collected first-hand by the business itself, tailored to its specific question.">Primary research</dfn> is brand-new information the business collects for itself. It is gathered for a specific purpose, so it is relevant and up to date — but it takes time and money. The OCR specification names four primary methods: questionnaires, interviews, trialling and focus groups.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Four Primary Methods</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Questionnaires</strong> — a set of written questions sent online, emailed, posted or handed out. They can reach large samples quickly and the answers are easy to count, but written questions cannot probe feelings deeply.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Interviews</strong> — a one-to-one conversation between a researcher and a customer. They produce rich, detailed answers, but are slow and expensive, and the interviewer can unintentionally lead the answers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Trialling</strong> — letting a small group use the actual product before launch. Deliveroo did this in Chelsea for six months in 2013 before rolling the app out nationwide. Trialling is powerful because customers react to the real thing, not a description — but it is expensive because the business has to make the product first.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Focus groups</strong> — a small group of 6–10 target customers discusses a product while a researcher listens. Honest reactions are captured, but one loud voice can dominate and bias the result.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Secondary Research Sources</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Information that already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose.">Secondary research</dfn> uses information that already exists — someone else collected it first. It is fast and cheap, but it may be out of date or not quite fit the question. The OCR spec names four secondary sources: newspapers and magazines, the census, websites, and internal data.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Newspapers and magazines</strong> carry articles on market trends, rival launches and industry reports — useful for spotting the competitive landscape. <strong>The census</strong>, carried out every ten years by the Office for National Statistics, shows the age, income and location of the UK population in detail — ideal for picking a target segment. <strong>Websites</strong> include competitor sites, review platforms like Trustpilot, government data and market reports from firms like Mintel; most are free or cheap.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Internal data</strong> is the goldmine most small businesses forget about — information the business already has from its own systems. <dfn class="term" data-def="Electronic Point of Sale — the till and stock system that records every product sold, when, and for how much.">EPOS</dfn> till data shows which products sell best and at what times. Customer databases record repeat buying, email open rates and average order values. Loyalty card schemes join all this together. Internal data is free, specific to the business and always up to date.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Qualitative vs Quantitative Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Data that gathers opinions, feelings and reasons — usually in words rather than numbers.">Qualitative data</dfn> gathers opinions, feelings and reasons — why customers buy, how they feel about a brand. Interviews and focus groups produce qualitative data. It is rich and detailed but hard to turn into a graph.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Data that records numbers and measurable facts — how many, how much, how often.">Quantitative data</dfn> records numbers — how many people, how much they spend, how often they buy. Large-scale questionnaires, till data and census figures produce quantitative data. It is easy to plot on charts and compare across segments, but it does not explain <em>why</em>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Good businesses use both. Tesco’s Clubcard shows <em>what</em> customers buy (quantitative); a focus group then explains <em>why</em> they switched from Tesco to Aldi (qualitative). Together, the two answer the full question. Netflix does the same thing — its servers log which shows are watched and for how long (quantitative), while surveys explore why people cancel their subscription (qualitative). A decision worth hundreds of millions of pounds needs more than one angle.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Matching the Method to the Question</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Which method a business picks depends on the question, the budget and the time available. A brand-new start-up with £500 cannot afford focus groups run by a research agency; it can afford a free online survey shared on Instagram. A big supermarket trialling a new ready meal can trial it in ten stores first, cheaply gathering real sales data before a national rollout. Picking the wrong method wastes money — running a focus group to find out how many people shop at a supermarket is using an expensive qualitative tool to answer a quantitative question.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Limits of Market Research</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Research is never perfect. <strong>Sample size</strong> matters — a survey of 30 people is too small to trust. <strong>Sampling bias</strong> distorts the result — asking shoppers outside Waitrose will not tell you what Aldi shoppers think. <strong>Leading questions</strong> (‘don’t you agree our product is great?’) push customers to answer the way the business hoped. And customers sometimes say what sounds good rather than what they will actually do, especially on price or ethics. A business that does not check these issues can make a very costly decision based on flawed data.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Market Size and Market Share</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Market research also produces the data behind two key numbers. <dfn class="term" data-def="The total sales (in units or value) of all businesses in a market over a period of time.">Market size</dfn> is the total value or volume of sales in a market — the UK coffee shop market, for instance, is worth around £5 billion per year. <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of total market sales achieved by one business.">Market share</dfn> is the slice of that market one business controls. The formula is: market share (%) = (business sales ÷ total market sales) × 100. If Costa’s UK coffee sales are £1.1 billion and the total coffee shop market is £5 billion, Costa’s market share is (1.1 ÷ 5) × 100 = 22%. Tracking market share over time is more useful than tracking sales alone — if Costa’s sales rise 3% but the whole market grows 10%, Costa is actually losing ground to Pret and Caffè Nero.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the market share formula, then give one primary and one secondary method that a small cafe could use.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Market share (%) = (business sales ÷ total market sales) × 100. A change in market share is more revealing than a change in sales on its own, because it shows how the business is doing compared with rivals.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n27">Primary research (questionnaires, interviews, trialling, focus groups) is new and tailored; secondary research (newspapers, the census, websites, internal data) is fast and cheap but may be out of date.</li><li data-narration-id="n28">Qualitative data explains <em>why</em> through opinions; quantitative data tells you <em>how many</em> through numbers — businesses use both together.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Market size is total sales in a market; market share = (business sales ÷ total market sales) × 100, and tracks how a business is doing compared with rivals.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">When a question asks you to pick the ‘best method’ of research, explain <em>why</em> it suits the specific business in the case study — a small start-up cannot afford nationwide focus groups, while a big brand like Cadbury can. For 6-mark ‘Analyse’ questions, link the research method to a decision the business then makes (e.g. EPOS till data → reveals sales drop among over-50s → launches new advert aimed at that segment). For calculations like market share, always show working and include the % sign — examiners credit method marks even if the final number is wrong.</p>
