--- DESCRIPTION ---
Recruiting, motivating, training and developing staff — plus the key UK employment laws.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n1">People are the most expensive resource most businesses buy — and the most valuable. This lesson pulls together four linked HR topics: how businesses recruit and select the right person, how they motivate staff once they arrive, how they train and develop them, and the employment laws that protect workers. One dense lesson because each topic is short, but the connections between them matter.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n2">Why Businesses Recruit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n3">A business needs to <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of attracting and choosing new employees for a business.">recruit</dfn> for three main reasons: to <strong>replace</strong> an employee who has left, to cope with <strong>business growth</strong> (more customers, new sites, new products), or to fill a <strong>skill gap</strong> the current team cannot cover. A Manchester café that expands from one site to three needs new baristas. A tech firm switching to AI needs engineers it cannot grow internally. A retailer losing staff to a rival needs replacements quickly or service suffers.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Internal vs External Recruitment</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Filling a vacancy from inside the business, such as by promotion or transfer.">Internal recruitment</dfn> fills the vacancy from existing staff. John Lewis, the employee-owned department store, often promotes partners from the shop floor into management roles. Internal recruits know the culture, so induction is shorter and cheaper, and promoting from within motivates the rest of the team. <dfn class="term" data-def="Filling a vacancy by hiring someone from outside the business.">External recruitment</dfn> hires from outside — through adverts, agencies, social media or job sites. External applicants bring fresh skills and ideas but cost more in advertising fees, induction time and the risk of a bad fit.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down two benefits of internal recruitment and two benefits of external recruitment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Internal recruitment is faster, cheaper and motivates existing staff, but creates another vacancy and limits fresh ideas. External recruitment widens the talent pool but costs more and takes longer.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Selection Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Selection starts with a <dfn class="term" data-def="A document setting out the duties, responsibilities and working conditions of a job.">job description</dfn> (what the post-holder will do) and a <dfn class="term" data-def="A document listing the skills, qualifications and personal qualities an ideal candidate should have.">person specification</dfn> (the skills and qualities the ideal candidate needs). The vacancy is advertised, applicants send a CV or fill in an application form, and the best candidates are invited to the next stage.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Common methods include the <strong>CV</strong> (candidate’s written summary of experience), <strong>application form</strong> (business-designed questions), <strong>interview</strong> (face-to-face or online questioning), <strong>tests</strong> (skills tests at Dyson, coding challenges at software firms), <strong>group activities</strong> (graduate schemes at PwC use team problem-solving), and <strong>references</strong> from previous employers as the final check before an offer. Businesses often use several methods together because a single interview can miss bad hires — good talkers are not always good workers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Motivation — Why It Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The reasons or drives that make employees work hard and stay with a business.">Motivation</dfn> is why employees turn up, try hard and stay. A motivated team produces more, makes fewer errors and gives better customer service. Demotivated staff are absent more often, leave more quickly and provide poorer service — which raises recruitment costs and lowers profit.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Financial Motivation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Money matters. <dfn class="term" data-def="Motivation that comes from the money or monetary benefits an employee receives.">Financial motivation</dfn> covers <strong>pay</strong> (the basic wage or salary), <strong>bonuses</strong> (extra pay for hitting a target — quarterly at banks, seasonal at retailers), <strong>profit sharing</strong> (staff receive a share of profits — John Lewis pays a partnership bonus based on annual profit), and <strong>fringe benefits</strong> (non-cash perks such as company cars, gym membership, staff discount). Fringe benefits can be very effective: a Tesco 10% staff discount costs Tesco very little but feels valuable to employees.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Non-Financial Motivation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Motivation that comes from sources other than money, such as interesting work, praise and responsibility.">Non-financial motivation</dfn> covers praise, recognition, varied work and responsibility. OCR names specific methods: <strong>job rotation</strong> (moving an employee between different jobs to reduce boredom and widen skills), <strong>job enrichment</strong> (adding more responsibility and challenge to an existing job), <strong>team-working</strong> (organising work around small teams with shared goals), award schemes (Employee of the Month), a positive <strong>working environment</strong> (good lighting, safe kit, respectful managers), and <strong>praise</strong> from managers and peers. These methods are often cheaper than pay rises and can have a bigger long-term effect on <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a business to keep its employees rather than lose them.">employee retention</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Training vs Staff Development — Common Exam Confusion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">OCR examiners have flagged this as the single most common wrong answer on this topic. Read it twice.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Training</strong> is relatively short-term and narrow — teaching a specific skill for a current role. A new barista learning to use the coffee machine. A shop assistant learning to work the till.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Staff development</strong> is longer-term and wider-ranging — preparing an employee to progress. Vocational qualifications, apprenticeships, leadership courses, or secondments to other departments. Development looks beyond today’s job to tomorrow’s career. If a question asks about staff development, do not write about till training. If it asks about training, do not launch into apprenticeships lasting three years.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Training Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Short-term learning aimed at teaching an employee a specific skill for their current role.">Training</dfn> starts with <strong>induction</strong> — the introduction given when an employee first joins, covering company policies, health and safety, job basics and introductions to colleagues. After that, OCR names two ongoing types: <strong>on-the-job</strong> training (learning while doing the real work, shadowing a colleague, coached by a supervisor) and <strong>off-the-job</strong> training (away from the normal workplace — a classroom course, an external training provider, an online module). On-the-job is cheap and directly relevant; off-the-job is more expensive but can bring in expert knowledge the business does not have internally.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Staff Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="Longer-term learning aimed at preparing an employee to progress — includes qualifications, apprenticeships and career progression.">Staff development</dfn> prepares people for bigger roles. <dfn class="term" data-def="Qualifications focused on practical, work-based skills — for example NVQs, BTECs and City & Guilds.">Vocational qualifications</dfn> (NVQs, BTECs, City & Guilds) build career-long skills. <dfn class="term" data-def="A structured programme combining paid work with study, leading to a recognised qualification.">Apprenticeships</dfn> combine paid work with study — BT, BAE Systems and the NHS all run large apprenticeship programmes. Good staff development raises retention because employees see a future in the business.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Employment Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">UK law sets minimum rules that every business must follow. OCR names four areas you should know.</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Minimum wage</strong> — the lowest hourly pay allowed by law. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum hourly rate a worker aged 21 or over must legally be paid in the UK.">National Minimum Wage / National Living Wage</dfn> is reviewed every April. Employers who underpay face fines and naming-and-shaming by HMRC.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Contracts</strong> — every UK employee has a legal right to a written statement of their main job terms on or before their first day. It covers hours, pay, holiday entitlement and notice periods.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Holidays and hours of work</strong> — full-time workers are entitled to at least 5.6 weeks paid holiday a year. The Working Time Regulations cap the average working week at 48 hours unless the worker chooses to opt out.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Equality and discrimination</strong> — the Equality Act protects workers from being treated unfairly because of age, sex, race, disability, religion, pregnancy or sexual orientation. <dfn class="term" data-def="Treating an employee or job applicant less favourably because of a protected characteristic such as age, sex, race, disability or religion.">Discrimination</dfn> in recruitment, pay, training or promotion is unlawful and can lead to Employment Tribunal claims.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the four areas of employment law, with one sentence on what each protects.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>OCR names four key areas of employment law: minimum wage, the right to a contract, holidays and hours of work, and protection from discrimination. All UK businesses must follow these — getting them wrong risks fines, tribunal claims and reputation damage.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Businesses recruit to replace leavers, grow, or fill skill gaps — through internal or external routes and a selection process using CVs, interviews, tests and references.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Motivation has financial methods (pay, bonus, profit share, fringe benefits) and non-financial methods (praise, job rotation, enrichment, team-working). Both raise retention.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Training builds a specific skill for the current job; staff development prepares employees for future roles. UK employment law sets rules on minimum wage, contracts, holidays, hours and discrimination.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">Read ‘training’ and ‘staff development’ questions very carefully — OCR examiner reports flag this as the most common mistake. Training is narrow and short-term (till skills). Development is wider and longer-term (NVQ, apprenticeship, career progression). For ‘Analyse’ questions on motivation, build a chain: method → effect on effort → effect on productivity/retention → effect on the business. For employment law questions, name the specific protection and link it to the scenario — an ‘Evaluate’ answer needs a supported judgement, not a summary.</p>
