<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dennis Kelly and the World of DNA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>DNA</em> was first performed at the National Theatre in 2008 as part of the NT Connections programme, which commissions plays specifically for young performers. Dennis Kelly wrote it for teenagers to perform — and about teenagers doing terrible things. The play explores what happens when a group of ordinary young people commit a horrific act of <dfn class="term" data-def="Violence or cruelty inflicted on a person by a group acting together, often escalating because individuals feel less personally responsible.">mob violence</dfn> and then try to cover it up. There is no adult authority in the play: no teachers, no parents, no police officers making things right. The teenagers are entirely alone with the consequences of what they have done.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Kelly has said that the play is about “gangs, genetics and the need to belong.” It asks uncomfortable questions about how far people will go to protect themselves and fit in with a group. The title itself is a double meaning — it refers both to the forensic DNA evidence used in the cover-up and to the deeper question of what is in our <dfn class="term" data-def="The fundamental characteristics or qualities that define a person or group, sometimes used to suggest that certain behaviours are innate.">nature</dfn> as human beings. Are we hardwired to follow a leader, even into cruelty?</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: What year was DNA first performed, where, and what programme was it part of?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>DNA</em> premiered at the National Theatre in 2008 as part of NT Connections. Dennis Kelly wrote it for young performers, exploring mob violence, group psychology, and the absence of adult authority. The title refers to both forensic evidence and human nature.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Youth Violence and Gang Culture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The play was written during a period of intense public anxiety about youth violence in Britain. In 2007–2008, a series of teenage murders dominated the headlines, and politicians debated “Broken Britain.” Kelly does not set <em>DNA</em> in a specific time or place — there are no street names, no school, no identifiable city. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Describing a setting that is deliberately vague and unspecific, so the story could happen anywhere.">non-specific setting</dfn> makes the play feel universal: this could happen in any group, anywhere.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The group in <em>DNA</em> is not a traditional street gang. They are ordinary teenagers who are bound together not by loyalty or friendship but by a shared secret. Once they have committed their crime against Adam, it is guilt and fear — not affection — that holds them together. Kelly shows that a “gang” does not require knives or drugs; it only requires a group of people too frightened to break away.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Kelly uses several distinctive techniques throughout <em>DNA</em>. Dialogue is often fragmented and overlapping — characters interrupt each other, repeat themselves, and leave sentences unfinished. This creates a naturalistic sense of panic and confusion. Stage directions are minimal, giving directors freedom but also reflecting the play’s stripped-back, confrontational style.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Violence is kept <dfn class="term" data-def="Events that take place off the visible stage, reported by characters rather than shown directly to the audience.">offstage</dfn> throughout. We never see what happens to Adam in the wood; we hear about it through Mark and Jan’s fragmented retelling. This is a deliberate choice. By making the audience imagine the violence rather than watch it, Kelly forces them to become complicit — filling in the horrific details themselves, just as the characters reconstruct and distort what really happened.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Psychology of Obedience</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The behaviour of the group in <em>DNA</em> mirrors real psychological experiments about obedience and conformity. In 1963, Stanley Milgram conducted his famous <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1963 psychology experiment where participants obeyed an authority figure’s instructions to deliver what they believed were dangerous electric shocks, showing how ordinary people comply with harmful orders.">obedience experiment</dfn> at Yale University, in which participants were told by an authority figure to deliver electric shocks to a stranger. Sixty-five per cent obeyed to the maximum voltage, even when they believed they were causing serious harm. Milgram concluded that ordinary people will do terrible things when an authority figure tells them to.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Phil functions as the authority figure in <em>DNA</em>. He rarely speaks, but when he does, his instructions are followed without question. “What are you going to do?” the group asks him repeatedly, and whatever Phil says becomes the plan. The group does not challenge him because following orders is easier than taking personal responsibility. Cathy, who volunteers to threaten the postman (Act 2) and involves herself in extreme acts throughout — reflecting Dennis Kelly’s point that ordinary people become capable of violence within a group, is the most extreme example of obedient cruelty — she carries out Phil’s orders with enthusiasm.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Milgram’s obedience experiment (1963) showed 65% of people would obey harmful orders from an authority figure. Phil is the authority in <em>DNA</em> — the group follows his instructions without question, even when those instructions become morally monstrous.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Stanford Prison Experiment and the Bystander Effect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In 1971, Philip Zimbardo’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A 1971 psychology experiment where volunteers assigned as guards in a mock prison quickly became abusive, showing how situational power corrupts behaviour.">Stanford Prison Experiment</dfn> assigned volunteers to be either “guards” or “prisoners” in a simulated prison. Within days, the guards became sadistic and the prisoners became passive. The experiment had to be stopped early because of the psychological damage. It demonstrated that people adopt the roles they are given — put someone in a position of power and they may abuse it; put someone in a subordinate role and they accept mistreatment.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">This maps directly onto <em>DNA</em>. John Tate begins as the group’s leader but cannot cope with the pressure. Phil steps into the power role and becomes increasingly controlling. Cathy, given permission to use violence, escalates rapidly. Meanwhile, the rest of the group — Richard, Danny, Lou — become passive bystanders, unwilling to intervene.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The psychological phenomenon where individuals are less likely to help a victim when other people are present, because responsibility feels diffused across the group.">bystander effect</dfn> describes how people are less likely to intervene in an emergency when others are present, because each person assumes someone else will act. In <em>DNA</em>, nobody stops the bullying of Adam, nobody refuses Phil’s plan, and nobody goes to the police. Leah is the character who comes closest to moral action — she questions everything, she talks constantly about right and wrong — but even she ultimately fails to act. Her desperate monologues (“You need me”) are directed at Phil, who ignores her, and at an audience who cannot intervene.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Kelly suggests that moral awareness without moral action is meaningless. Leah knows what is right but does nothing, making her complicit. The audience, too, is implicated — we watch and do nothing, mirroring the bystander effect the play dramatises.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n23"><em>DNA</em> (2008) explores youth violence and group psychology through a non-specific setting that makes its themes universal — this could happen anywhere.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Real psychology experiments — Milgram’s obedience study, the Stanford Prison Experiment, and the bystander effect — directly mirror the group dynamics in the play.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Kelly keeps violence offstage and uses fragmented dialogue, forcing the audience to imagine the horror and become complicit in the retelling.</li></ul>
