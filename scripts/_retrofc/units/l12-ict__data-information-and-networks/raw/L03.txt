--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Networks: LAN and WAN</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A network that connects computers and devices within a limited geographical area such as a single building, school or office site.">Local Area Network (LAN)</dfn> connects computers and devices within a small, defined area — typically a single building, school, hospital or office campus. All the hardware is usually owned and managed by the organisation itself. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A network that connects computers and devices across large geographical areas, potentially spanning cities, countries or continents, often using leased telecommunication lines.">Wide Area Network (WAN)</dfn> connects devices across much larger geographical distances — cities, countries or even continents. The internet is the largest example of a WAN. Unlike a LAN, a WAN typically uses infrastructure owned and managed by third-party telecommunications companies, and the organisation leases access to those connections. The main differences to know are: LANs cover a small area and are owned by the organisation; WANs cover a large area and rely on external infrastructure.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Protocols</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="A set of agreed rules and standards that govern how data is formatted, transmitted and received across a network, ensuring different devices can communicate reliably.">Protocols</dfn> are the agreed rules and standards that control how data is sent, received and formatted across a network. Without protocols, devices from different manufacturers would be unable to communicate. The most important protocol suite is TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol), which governs how data is broken into packets, addressed and reassembled at the destination. HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) governs how web pages are requested and delivered; HTTPS is its secure, encrypted version. FTP (File Transfer Protocol) is used to transfer files between computers. Email relies on protocols such as SMTP (for sending) and POP3 or IMAP (for receiving). Protocols allow a smartphone made in South Korea, a laptop running software from the USA, and a server hosted in the UK to exchange data seamlessly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Internet, Intranet and Extranet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <strong>internet</strong> is a global public network accessible to anyone — the network of all networks. An <strong>intranet</strong> is a private network that looks and works like the internet but is accessible only to people within a specific organisation, such as a company's employees. It might host internal documents, HR policies and staff forums. An <strong>extranet</strong> extends an intranet to give controlled, limited access to selected external users — for example, a supplier might be given access to a retailer's stock management system through an extranet, but cannot access the rest of the intranet. The key distinction is access: internet = public; intranet = private, internal; extranet = private, selectively external.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Network Topologies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">A network topology describes how devices are physically or logically arranged and connected. There are three topologies to know. In a <strong>bus topology</strong>, all devices are connected to a single central cable called the backbone or bus. Data sent by one device travels along the bus in both directions until it reaches the intended recipient. Bus networks are cheap to install but if the backbone cable fails the whole network fails, and performance degrades as more devices are added because all devices share the same cable. In a <strong>star topology</strong>, every device connects to a central hub or switch. All data passes through the central device. Star networks are reliable because a single device failure does not affect others; they are easy to manage and fast because each device has a dedicated connection to the hub. The main disadvantage is that if the central hub or switch fails, the entire network goes down, and more cable is required than in a bus topology. In a <strong>ring topology</strong>, devices are connected in a loop and data travels in one direction around the ring, passing through each device until it reaches the correct destination. Ring networks avoid data collisions (since data travels in one direction), but if any single connection or device in the ring fails, the whole network is affected.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Learn the key advantage and disadvantage for each topology. Bus: cheap but backbone failure kills the network. Star: reliable and fast but hub failure kills the network and it needs more cable. Ring: no collisions but one failure breaks the whole loop.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three network topologies: bus (single shared cable — cheap but fragile), star (all connect to a central hub — reliable per device but hub is a single point of failure), ring (loop of devices — no collisions but one break stops everything).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Devices Within a Network</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Several hardware devices make a network function. A <strong>router</strong> directs (routes) data packets between networks — it is what connects your home LAN to the wider internet. A <strong>switch</strong> connects multiple devices within a LAN and directs data only to the device it is intended for, reducing unnecessary traffic. A <strong>hub</strong> is an older, less intelligent version of a switch that broadcasts data to all connected devices. A <strong>modem</strong> converts digital computer signals into a form that can travel over telephone or cable lines (and converts incoming signals back). A <strong>network interface card (NIC)</strong> is the hardware component inside each device that allows it to connect to a network. Wireless Access Points (WAPs) allow Wi-Fi-enabled devices to connect to a wired network wirelessly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">How Data Is Transferred and Threats to Data Transfer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">When data travels across a network it is divided into small chunks called <strong>packets</strong>. Each packet contains the data payload, the destination address, the source address and a sequence number so that packets can be reassembled in the correct order at the other end, even if they travelled by different routes. Packet switching allows networks to be more efficient because different packets can take different routes depending on congestion.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14">One significant threat to data in transit is <strong>packet sniffing</strong>. A packet sniffer is software (or hardware) that intercepts and reads packets as they travel across a network. On an unencrypted network, a malicious user running a packet sniffer can read usernames, passwords, email contents and financial data in plain text. Encryption (such as HTTPS) protects against packet sniffing by scrambling the data so that even if packets are intercepted, they cannot be read without the decryption key. Other threats include man-in-the-middle attacks and denial-of-service (DoS) attacks that flood a network with traffic to overwhelm it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Cloud Computing versus In-house Servers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Cloud computing</strong> means storing data and running applications on remote servers accessed over the internet, rather than on hardware physically located in an organisation's own building. Benefits include: no need to buy and maintain expensive server hardware; storage can scale up or down as needed; data is accessible from anywhere with an internet connection; the cloud provider handles backups, security updates and maintenance. Drawbacks include: dependence on a reliable internet connection; ongoing subscription costs; data is held by a third party (raising questions about privacy and legal jurisdiction); potential security risks if the cloud provider is breached. <strong>In-house servers</strong> give the organisation full physical control over its hardware and data, compliance with strict data regulations is easier to demonstrate, and there is no reliance on internet connectivity for internal systems. The trade-off is higher upfront cost, the need for specialist IT staff to maintain the hardware, and limited scalability.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Exam questions on cloud vs in-house often ask you to weigh them for a specific scenario. A small charity with no IT staff benefits most from cloud. A hospital handling sensitive patient data may prefer in-house for control and legal reasons.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cloud computing: scalable, accessible anywhere, no maintenance burden, but requires internet access and data is held by a third party. In-house servers: full control, no internet dependency, easier regulatory compliance, but expensive to buy and maintain.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Connectivity Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Devices connect to networks through a wide range of methods. <strong>Short-range wireless</strong> connections include: 802.11 (Wi-Fi) — the standard for wireless local networking, covering a building or campus; <dfn class="term" data-def="A short-range wireless standard for connecting devices over distances up to about 10 metres, commonly used for headphones, keyboards and file transfer between phones.">Bluetooth</dfn> — connects devices like headphones and keyboards up to around 10 metres; <strong>NFC (Near-Field Communication)</strong> — works within a few centimetres for contactless payments; <strong>RFID</strong> — uses radio waves to identify tagged objects, used in library tracking and stock management. <strong>Medium-range wireless</strong> connections include mobile data networks: <strong>3G</strong> (basic browsing), <strong>4G</strong> (video streaming on smartphones) and <strong>5G</strong> (very high speeds and low latency). <strong>Long-range wireless</strong> methods include: <strong>microwave</strong> transmission — line-of-sight radio signals between fixed tower antennas; <strong>satellite</strong> — data relayed via an orbiting satellite, enabling remote-area connectivity though with higher latency. <strong>Wired</strong> connections include: <strong>Ethernet</strong> — the standard for wired LANs using twisted-pair copper cable; <strong>USB-C</strong> — a reversible connector with fast speeds and higher power delivery, standard on modern laptops and smartphones; older <strong>USB-A</strong> and <strong>Micro USB</strong> connectors are still widely found on legacy devices.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20">Both NFC and Bluetooth are short-range wireless technologies, but they work at very different distances and serve different purposes. NFC works only within a few centimetres — you must almost touch two NFC-enabled devices together. It is designed for quick, spontaneous interactions: tapping your phone to pay at a checkout, or touching your bus pass to a reader. The connection happens in milliseconds and requires no pairing or setup. Bluetooth works at distances up to around 10 metres and requires a one-time pairing between devices. It is designed for continuous, sustained connections: wireless headphones, a keyboard connected to a laptop, or a smartwatch syncing with a phone. Because NFC is so close-range, it is inherently more secure for payment — it is very difficult for an attacker to intercept a signal that only works at a distance of a few centimetres.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n21">When data travels across a network it is broken into packets. On a shared or poorly secured network — such as an open public Wi-Fi hotspot — any device on the same network can potentially intercept those packets using freely available packet-sniffer software. If the data is unencrypted, the attacker reads the contents directly: login credentials, credit card numbers, private messages. Encryption scrambles the data before transmission using a mathematical algorithm and a key. Even if the attacker captures the packets, all they see is meaningless ciphertext. Only the intended recipient, with the correct decryption key, can read the original data. HTTPS (which you see in your browser's address bar on secure websites) uses TLS encryption to protect web traffic from packet sniffing. Using a VPN (Virtual Private Network) also encrypts all outgoing traffic, providing protection even on unsecured public networks.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">A LAN covers a small area owned by the organisation; a WAN covers a large geographical area using third-party infrastructure. The internet is the largest WAN.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Protocols are agreed rules that allow different devices to communicate — TCP/IP, HTTP/HTTPS, FTP and SMTP/POP3 are examples.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Internet = public; intranet = private internal network; extranet = private network with selective external access.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Three topologies: bus (single cable — cheap but fragile), star (hub at centre — reliable per device), ring (loop — no collisions but one failure breaks everything).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Packet sniffing intercepts unencrypted data in transit; HTTPS encryption defends against it.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Cloud computing offers scalability and remote access; in-house servers offer control and independence from internet connectivity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Connectivity methods: short-range (802.11 Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC, RFID), medium-range (3G/4G/5G), long-range (microwave, satellite), wired (Ethernet, USB, Micro USB, USB-C).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">Network questions frequently ask you to compare topologies or connectivity methods for a specific scenario — always relate your answer to the context given. If asked about threats to data transfer, name packet sniffing specifically and explain how encryption mitigates it — vague answers like "hackers could steal the data" will not reach the higher mark bands. For connectivity methods, the exam may give you a scenario (e.g. a nurse using a tablet on a hospital ward, or a delivery driver in a rural area) and ask which method is most appropriate — think through range, speed, whether infrastructure exists, and cost. Know that NFC is contact-range, Bluetooth is room-range, Wi-Fi is building-range, 4G/5G is city/country-range and satellite works globally.</p>
