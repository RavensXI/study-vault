--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Data, Information and Knowledge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When we talk about what computers work with, three terms come up again and again: data, information and knowledge. It is easy to muddle them, but they mean distinct things. <dfn class="term" data-def="Raw facts and figures that have not yet been given context or meaning — for example, the number 42 or the letters &quot;Y&quot; and &quot;N&quot;.">Data</dfn> consists of raw facts and figures. On their own they carry no meaning: the number 37 could be a temperature, a bus route or someone's age. <dfn class="term" data-def="Data that has been processed by a computer system to give it context and meaning, making it useful to a person or organisation.">Information</dfn> is what we get when data has been processed by a computer to give it context — 37°C tells us it is a dangerously high body temperature. <dfn class="term" data-def="Understanding derived from information by applying rules, experience or expertise to draw conclusions or make decisions.">Knowledge</dfn> takes things one step further: it is derived from information by applying rules. A doctor who knows that a temperature above 38°C in a newborn requires emergency hospital admission is applying knowledge — rules built on clinical information.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">This hierarchy matters in ICT because computers process data to produce information, and well-designed systems encode the rules that turn information into actionable knowledge. When you type your date of birth into a form, you are providing raw data. The system processes it to tell you that you are 16 years old (information), and then applies a rule to decide whether you are old enough to create an account (knowledge in action).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Why Data Must Be Good Quality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">For information to be useful, the data that feeds it must be fit for purpose. Good-quality data has several key characteristics. It must be <strong>accurate</strong> — if a patient's medication dosage is recorded incorrectly, the information produced from it could be dangerous. It must be <strong>complete</strong> — missing fields can make records unusable. It must be <strong>up to date</strong> — a customer address that was correct three years ago may have changed. It must also be <strong>relevant</strong> — collecting data that is never used wastes storage and processing resources. Poor-quality data leads to poor-quality information, which in turn leads to bad decisions. This is sometimes summed up as "garbage in, garbage out": if the raw data going into a system is wrong, no amount of processing will fix the output.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Remember the phrase: garbage in, garbage out. Data quality problems — inaccuracy, incompleteness, being out of date, or irrelevance — all degrade the information produced.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>"Garbage in, garbage out" describes the reality that poor-quality data produces poor-quality information regardless of how powerful the computer is. The four key quality characteristics are: accuracy, completeness, currency (up to date) and relevance.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Encoding Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Converting data into a shorter or standardised representation, such as using M/F for Male/Female or 1/0 for Yes/No, to improve storage efficiency and processing speed.">Encoding</dfn> is the process of converting data into a shorter or standardised form. For example, instead of storing the full word "Female", a database might store the single character "F". Instead of "Yes" or "No", a system might use 1 and 0. Encoding has clear benefits: it reduces the amount of storage space needed, speeds up sorting and searching because shorter codes are faster to compare, and reduces the chance of input errors because staff choose from a fixed set of codes rather than typing free text. However, encoding can also have disadvantages — codes may be misunderstood or misremembered, and they can make data harder for a non-expert to read without a reference key. There is also a risk that a code designed for one purpose becomes ambiguous over time as organisations grow and change.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Advances in ICT hardware have improved both the <strong>speed of access to data</strong> and the <strong>amount of storage available</strong>. Solid-state drives (SSDs) retrieve data far faster than older spinning hard drives. Cloud storage means an organisation is no longer limited by the physical capacity of the hardware on site. These improvements mean that organisations can store larger volumes of data and retrieve it more quickly, which directly improves the quality and timeliness of the information they produce.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Advantages and Disadvantages of Using ICT to Store Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Storing data using ICT systems offers significant advantages. Large volumes of data can be searched in seconds — a task that would take days in a paper-based system. Data can be backed up automatically to prevent loss. Multiple authorised users can access the same records simultaneously from different locations. Data can be sorted, filtered and processed to create reports almost instantly. Despite these advantages, there are also real risks and drawbacks. Digital data is vulnerable to hacking, malware and unauthorised access. Hardware failure or software corruption can destroy records if backups are not in place. There are ongoing costs for hardware, software licences and security measures. Organisations also have legal obligations under data protection law when handling personal information digitally, and a breach can have serious financial and reputational consequences.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="In exam questions about advantages and disadvantages of ICT data storage, aim to balance technical points (speed, capacity, access) against risk points (security, cost, legal obligations). Always develop your answer beyond a bare list.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Key advantages of ICT data storage: fast search and retrieval, simultaneous multi-user access, automated backups, easy sorting and reporting. Key disadvantages: security vulnerabilities, hardware failure risk, ongoing costs, and legal compliance obligations.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">File Types and File Properties</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Not all files are the same. Different types of data are stored in different file formats. Common file types include: text files (.txt, .docx) for written documents; image files (.jpg, .png, .bmp) for photographs and graphics; audio files (.mp3, .wav) for sound; video files (.mp4, .avi) for moving images; and spreadsheet files (.xlsx, .csv) for numerical data. Each file type has different properties and is suited to different purposes. Choosing the wrong file type can reduce quality or cause compatibility problems.
</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Every file also has <strong>properties</strong> that describe it: its name, file type (extension), size (measured in bytes, kilobytes, megabytes or gigabytes), date created, date modified, and the author or owner. File size is particularly important in ICT because large files take longer to transfer over a network, require more storage space, and can slow down a system. Understanding file properties helps ICT users manage storage efficiently and choose the right format for their needs.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Data Compression</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of reducing the size of a file so that it takes up less storage space and can be transferred over a network more quickly.">Data compression</dfn> is the process of reducing a file's size. There are two main types. <strong>Lossless compression</strong> reduces file size without losing any data — when the file is decompressed it is identical to the original. ZIP files and PNG images use lossless compression. <strong>Lossy compression</strong> permanently removes data that is considered less important — the file is smaller but cannot be perfectly restored to its original state. JPEG images and MP3 audio files use lossy compression. The benefit of compression is faster file transfer over networks and less storage space used. The trade-off with lossy compression is reduced quality — though for many uses (streaming music, sharing photos online) the quality loss is not noticeable to most people.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">With <strong>lossless compression</strong> (e.g. PNG, ZIP), no data is permanently removed — when you decompress the file it is bit-for-bit identical to the original. With <strong>lossy compression</strong> (e.g. JPEG, MP3), some data is permanently discarded. The algorithm decides which data is least perceptible to humans and throws it away. A JPEG photograph looks very similar to the original but is not identical — repeated saving of a JPEG degrades quality further each time. For professional photography or archiving legal documents, lossless formats are preferred. For web images and streaming audio where small quality reductions are acceptable, lossy formats save significant bandwidth and storage.</p>

    <span>How does encoding improve storage and speed in real systems?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Consider a supermarket loyalty card database with 10 million customer records. Each record stores a "gender" field. If the system stores the full word "Male" or "Female" that is up to 6 characters per record, or 60 million characters across the database. Encoding it as "M" or "F" (1 character each) reduces this to 10 million characters — a 6× reduction for that field alone. When the database searches or sorts by gender, comparing a single character is faster than comparing six. Scale this across dozens of fields and millions of records and the efficiency gains are enormous. This is why encoding is standard practice in all large database-driven systems.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Data is raw facts and figures; information is data that has been processed by a computer to give it context; knowledge is derived from information by applying rules.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Good-quality data must be accurate, complete, up to date and relevant — poor data produces poor information ("garbage in, garbage out").</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Encoding data (using short codes instead of full text) saves storage space, speeds up processing and reduces input errors.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">ICT data storage offers advantages (speed, capacity, multi-user access) but also risks (security, cost, legal obligations).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Lossless compression reduces file size without losing data; lossy compression removes some data permanently to achieve greater size reduction.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Questions often ask you to distinguish data from information — always use the processing step in your answer ("data is processed by the computer to produce information"). For questions on advantages and disadvantages of ICT data storage, do not just list points: explain them with a brief "because" or "which means" to reach the higher mark bands. If asked to compare lossless and lossy compression, name an example file type for each (PNG / JPEG, or ZIP / MP3) — examiners reward specific examples and penalise vague answers that just say "one keeps quality and one doesn't".</p>
