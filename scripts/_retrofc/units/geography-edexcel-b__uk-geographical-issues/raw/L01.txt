--- DESCRIPTION ---
How geology, glaciation and human activity have shaped upland and lowland UK landscapes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Country Built from Rock and Ice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Stand on Snowdon and look north-west: hard, pointed mountains and deep glacial lakes. Stand on the South Downs and look south: gentle, chalk-and-clay hills with rolling farmland. The two views are nothing alike, yet they are part of the same small country. The reason is that the UK’s landscape has been built up over hundreds of millions of years by three forces: <dfn class="term" data-def="The study of rocks, including how they form and how they have been changed over time.">geology</dfn>, past climate processes (especially glaciation), and human activity. Together these explain why the north and west of the UK are mostly upland, while the south and east are mostly lowland.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Three Rock Families</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Geographers divide rocks into three groups based on how they form. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rocks formed when molten rock from below the Earth's surface cools and hardens, such as granite.">Igneous rocks</dfn> like granite formed when molten rock cooled inside or above the Earth’s crust. They are hard, resistant and tend to make uplands — granite forms the moorlands of Dartmoor and the mountains of the Cairngorms. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rocks formed from layers of sediment compressed over time, such as chalk, clay or limestone.">Sedimentary rocks</dfn> like chalk, clay and limestone formed from layers of compressed sediment, often laid down in ancient seas. They are softer and more easily worn down, so they tend to make lowlands. <dfn class="term" data-def="Rocks that have been changed by heat or pressure, such as slate or schist.">Metamorphic rocks</dfn> like slate and schist were created when other rocks were heated and squeezed underground; they form parts of the Welsh and Scottish highlands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the three rock families with one named UK example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Igneous (e.g. Cairngorms granite) and metamorphic (e.g. Welsh slate) rocks are hard and form uplands. Sedimentary rocks (chalk South Downs, London clay) are softer and form lowlands. The boundary creates the Tees-Exe Line.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Tees-Exe Line</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A useful piece of mental geography is the <strong>Tees-Exe Line</strong> — an imaginary diagonal from the mouth of the Tees in north-east England to the mouth of the Exe in Devon. Roughly speaking, the older, harder rocks lie north and west of the line, forming uplands like the Lake District, Pennines, Snowdonia and the Scottish Highlands. The younger, softer rocks lie south and east, forming lowlands such as the London Basin, the Fens and the Wessex chalk downs. Of course there are exceptions — Dartmoor is an upland granite outcrop south of the line, and parts of the Cumbrian coast are low-lying — but the line works as a quick rule of thumb.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Last Ice Age</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The UK’s landscape has been finished off by ice. During the most recent glacial period (the Devensian, ending around 11,500 years ago) much of the UK was buried under ice up to a kilometre thick. Glaciers carved deep U-shaped valleys, cirques (the basins where glaciers formed), and lakes such as Llyn Idwal in Snowdonia and Wast Water in the Lake District. The ice carried huge amounts of rock, scouring some areas down to bare bedrock and dumping the debris elsewhere. <dfn class="term" data-def="Material such as clay, sand and stones deposited by a glacier, often forming gentle hills called drumlins.">Glacial till</dfn> covers much of East Anglia and the Vale of York, creating fertile farmland on flat ground.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Post-Glacial Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Once the ice had melted, other processes took over. <dfn class="term" data-def="The breakdown of rocks in place by physical, chemical or biological processes, such as freeze-thaw or acid rain.">Weathering</dfn> — especially freeze-thaw on exposed mountain tops — loosens rock fragments. <strong>Mass movement</strong> such as landslides, mudflows and rockfalls slowly drags loose material downhill. Rivers re-carved valleys that had been widened by ice, leaving small, fast streams sitting in oversized U-shaped valleys (a feature called a <strong>misfit stream</strong>). On exposed coasts, the post-glacial sea has steadily eaten cliffs back. The UK’s landscape is still being shaped today — just much more slowly than during the ice age.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Human Landscape</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">People have reshaped the UK landscape too. Forests once covered most of lowland Britain; today only about 13% of the UK is forested, and most of that has been planted. Lowland landscapes have been ploughed, drained and hedged into farmland. Uplands have been cleared for sheep grazing — the ‘wild’ moors of the Yorkshire Dales and Lake District are largely the result of livestock keeping the trees down. Cities like London, Birmingham and Manchester have buried natural landscapes under brick, tarmac and concrete. Even apparently natural features — the Norfolk Broads — turn out to be flooded medieval peat diggings.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The UK has 15 national parks (10 in England, 3 in Wales, 2 in Scotland), covering around 10% of the land. They protect upland landscapes, wildlife and traditional farming. The Lake District — the UK’s most visited park — attracts about 18 million visitors a year, supporting tourism but also creating footpath erosion and traffic problems. National parks are not wildernesses; they are working landscapes shaped by farming, forestry and mining over centuries.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list four ways humans have shaped UK landscapes, with a named example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Human activity has reshaped the UK landscape: deforestation has reduced forest cover to about 13%; farming created lowland fields and hedges; sheep grazing keeps uplands open; cities cover natural surfaces. Even the “natural” Norfolk Broads were dug as medieval peat pits.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Igneous and metamorphic rocks (north and west of the Tees-Exe Line) are hard and form uplands; sedimentary rocks (south and east) are softer and form lowlands.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The most recent ice age carved U-shaped valleys, cirques and lakes in the uplands and dumped fertile till across lowland England.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Human activity — deforestation, farming, sheep grazing and urban growth — has further shaped almost every part of the UK landscape.</li>
</ul>
