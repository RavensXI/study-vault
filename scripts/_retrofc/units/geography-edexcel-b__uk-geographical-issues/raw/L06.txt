--- DESCRIPTION ---
How population, migration and economic change have reshaped UK regions and sectors.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Changing Country</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK’s human landscape — where people live, what they do for work and how communities feel — is constantly being reshaped. Edexcel asks the enquiry question: <em>Why are places and people changing in the UK?</em> The answers lie in three interconnected processes: shifting population structure, decades of migration, and a profound shift from making things to selling services and ideas.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Urban Core and Rural Periphery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The UK is not one uniform space. Geographers describe it as having an <dfn class="term" data-def="The most economically active region of a country, with high population density and concentration of jobs and services.">urban core</dfn> — the densely populated, job-rich London-Birmingham-Manchester triangle — surrounded by a <dfn class="term" data-def="Areas more distant from the urban core, with lower population density and fewer services.">rural periphery</dfn> in the South West, mid-Wales, the Scottish Highlands and parts of Northern Ireland. Population density tells the story: London exceeds 5,500 people per km<sup>2</sup>; the Highlands are below 10. Age structure differs too — the cores attract younger working-age adults, while peripheral areas have above-average proportions of retirees who have moved out for quality of life.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain in your own words why the urban core has higher densities and a younger age structure than the rural periphery.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The UK’s urban core (London, Midlands, North West) holds most jobs, services and migrants. The rural periphery has lower density, an older age structure, and fewer well-paid jobs — so internal migration tends to flow towards the core for work and away from it for retirement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Government Attempts to Reduce Differences</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">UK and EU policies have tried to even things out. <strong>Enterprise Zones</strong> (such as Liverpool, Tees Valley and Humber) offer tax breaks and simplified planning to attract firms to areas of high unemployment. <strong>Transport investment</strong> — HS2 (London to the Midlands), Crossrail/Elizabeth Line, Northern Powerhouse Rail — aims to connect peripheral regions to the core. <strong>Regional development funding</strong>, especially the EU Structural Funds before Brexit, channelled billions into post-industrial cities and the most deprived rural areas. After leaving the EU, the UK Shared Prosperity Fund and the Levelling Up Fund have continued similar work but with a smaller national budget.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">National and International Migration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Migration over the past 50 years has reshaped the UK’s population geography in three ways. <strong>Internal migration</strong>: young adults move from rural and northern areas to London and the South East for work; older people often move out, sometimes called <em>counter-urbanisation</em>. <strong>International migration</strong>: post-war migration from Caribbean and South Asian Commonwealth countries (1948 onwards), EU migration after 2004 (Polish, Romanian, Bulgarian workers), and recent arrivals via humanitarian schemes (Hong Kong British nationals, Ukrainian refugees). Today around 14% of the UK population is foreign-born, with strong concentrations in London (around 40% in some boroughs).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">The net effect: London and other big cities have become more ethnically and culturally diverse; rural and peripheral areas have aged. <dfn class="term" data-def="The UK government policy from the 1970s onwards regulating who can enter and settle in the UK.">UK immigration policy</dfn> has shifted from open Commonwealth movement, through EU free movement (1973–2020), to a points-based system after Brexit that favours skilled workers regardless of nationality. EU migration also shaped agricultural and care sectors, with significant labour shortages emerging since 2021.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three causes of migration into the UK and one impact on the population structure of cities.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Net international migration to the UK has averaged around 250,000 a year for two decades. Migration has concentrated in cities, raised the working-age population, and added cultural and culinary diversity to high streets and schools.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">From Mining and Manufacturing to Services</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The UK economy used to be dominated by primary (mining, fishing, farming) and secondary (manufacturing) sectors. Today these together employ under 20% of the workforce. The <strong>tertiary</strong> sector (services — retail, finance, education, healthcare) employs around 75% of UK workers, and the <strong>quaternary</strong> sector (research, IT, biotech, finance R&D) is the fastest-growing area. This shift is uneven across regions: the old coalfields of South Yorkshire, South Wales and the North East lost hundreds of thousands of mining and steel jobs in the 1980s; growth has come back, but in services rather than the heavy industries that used to define those communities.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">The South East has high concentrations of finance, tech and quaternary jobs, GDP per capita well above the UK average, and house prices three to five times national earnings. The North East has lost hundreds of thousands of secondary-sector jobs (Consett steelworks closed 1980, Wearside shipyards by 1988, Cleveland coalfields by the 1990s) but has more recently grown new sectors — the Nissan plant in Sunderland (opened 1986) is a major TNC investor, and Newcastle has built a thriving digital cluster. GDP per capita and wages remain below the UK average, however, illustrating how stubborn regional inequality can be.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Globalisation, FDI and TNCs in the UK</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">UK economic policy from the 1980s onwards favoured free trade, privatisation of nationalised industries, and openness to <dfn class="term" data-def="Investment by a foreign company in another country, often by setting up factories, offices or buying existing firms.">foreign direct investment</dfn> (FDI). The UK is one of the world’s largest FDI destinations, attracting over £100 billion per year in good years. Major TNCs — Nissan and Toyota in car manufacturing, BMW in Cowley (Mini), Tata Steel in Port Talbot, Indian-owned Jaguar Land Rover — have all reshaped UK regional economies. FDI brings jobs, technology transfer and tax revenue; risks include profits being repatriated overseas and vulnerability when TNCs decide to relocate (as Honda did in 2019, closing the Swindon plant).</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The UK has a population-dense urban core (London, Midlands) and an older, sparser rural periphery; UK and EU policy try to reduce these differences via Enterprise Zones, transport and regional funding.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Migration over 50 years has reshaped the UK — international migrants have concentrated in cities, internal migrants flow young to cities and old to rural areas.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Manufacturing has declined; the UK economy is dominated by services and quaternary jobs. Globalisation, FDI and TNCs (Nissan, Tata, JLR) play a central role.</li>
</ul>
