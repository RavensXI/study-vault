How Birmingham's site, structure and population have changed through migration and growth.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Birmingham?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Edexcel asks students to study one major UK city in depth. <em>Birmingham</em> is a strong choice because almost every theme in the unit — site, situation, migration, structure, decline and renewal — can be seen there in action. Britain’s ‘second city’ has a population of around 1.15 million; the wider West Midlands metropolitan area holds about 2.9 million people. The enquiry question is: <em>How is Birmingham changing?</em></p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Site, Situation and Connectivity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The actual land on which a city is built and its physical characteristics, such as relief, water and resources.">site</dfn> of Birmingham is the Birmingham Plateau in the West Midlands — gently rolling land 150–180 m above sea level, with several small rivers (the Tame, Rea and Cole). It was not the rivers that made Birmingham, but the location at the centre of the country: the <dfn class="term" data-def="A city's location in relation to other places, including transport links and proximity to markets.">situation</dfn>. Birmingham sits at the meeting point of several Roman roads and later the Grand Union and Birmingham canals. By the 19th century it had become the inland hub of Britain’s rail network. Today it is connected by the M6, M5, M40 and M42 motorways; HS2’s first phase will arrive at Curzon Street; and Birmingham Airport handles 12 million passengers per year. Its connectivity nationally and globally is a key reason TNCs locate there.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down three reasons why Birmingham’s national situation matters more than its physical site.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birmingham’s power has always come from its central location and connections, not from any unique physical site. Roman roads, canals, railways and now motorways and HS2 all converge there.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The City’s Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Birmingham follows a fairly classic UK city structure. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Central Business District: the commercial and retail heart of a city, with the highest land values and tallest buildings.">CBD</dfn> covers New Street, the Bullring, Centenary Square, Brindleyplace and Snow Hill — flagship shopping (Selfridges, Bullring), corporate offices (HSBC UK headquarters, Deutsche Bank), the Library of Birmingham and Symphony Hall. Surrounding the CBD is the <strong>inner city</strong>: Victorian terraced housing in Aston, Sparkbrook, Handsworth and Small Heath, originally built for factory workers, with mixed land uses, lower environmental quality and the legacy of the city’s manufacturing past. Beyond that the <strong>suburbs</strong> — semi-detached interwar housing in Sutton Coldfield, Edgbaston and Kings Heath — and the <strong>urban-rural fringe</strong> in places like Sutton Park and Solihull, with retail parks, the NEC and large modern estates.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">In the Bullring CBD, retail rents reach over £200 per square foot per year — among the highest in the UK outside London. Land values fall sharply with distance from the centre: a typical inner-city Victorian terrace in Sparkbrook is worth roughly £200,000, while a similar-sized property in Sutton Coldfield can exceed £500,000. Building density and height drop accordingly: high-rise glass and steel in the centre, three-storey terraces inner city, two-storey semis in suburbs, and detached homes with gardens in the urban-rural fringe.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Migration and Population Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Birmingham was one of the first British cities to be transformed by Commonwealth immigration. The <em>SS Empire Windrush</em> arrived in 1948 and Caribbean families settled in Handsworth and Aston in the 1950s. South Asian migration grew strongly from the 1960s onwards, particularly Pakistani families in Sparkbrook and Bangladeshi families in Aston. EU migration after 2004 added Polish, Romanian and Bulgarian residents in inner-city neighbourhoods. By 2021 the city had become ‘majority-minority’, meaning more than half the population identify as ethnic minorities. Birmingham is also one of the youngest cities in Europe: nearly 40% of residents are under 25.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three migrant groups that have shaped Birmingham’s character and the area each is associated with.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Birmingham’s population is around 1.15 million; nearly 40% are under 25; over 50% are from ethnic-minority backgrounds. Migration has driven both the city’s youthful age structure and its cultural diversity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Inequality Within the City</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Birmingham is also one of the most unequal cities in the UK. Indices of Multiple Deprivation rank inner-city wards like Sparkbrook, Lozells and Bordesley among the most deprived 10% in England, with high unemployment, low wages, poor housing quality, lower life expectancy (men in Sparkbrook live around 7 years less than men in Sutton Coldfield) and crowded schools. Suburban Sutton Coldfield, Edgbaston and Harborne, by contrast, contain some of the most affluent neighbourhoods in the West Midlands. <strong>Inequality</strong> shows in employment (graduate jobs cluster around the CBD), education (secondary-school results vary sharply by ward), and health (childhood obesity and diabetes are concentrated in inner-city areas).</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Inequality has historical roots: the inner ring of housing built for factory workers became the cheapest entry-point for migrants, then for newer arrivals. When the factories closed (Longbridge Rover plant closed 2005, with around 6,000 job losses), the inner ring lost most of its jobs. Suburbs gained from car-friendly retail and tech parks. Differences compound across generations, as poorer schools and limited family savings make it harder for inner-city young people to access higher-paid CBD jobs.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Birmingham Today</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Birmingham continues to change. The 2022 Commonwealth Games regenerated areas including Perry Barr and the Alexander Stadium. HS2’s first phase will cut London-to-Birmingham journey times to 50 minutes by the early 2030s. Major investment from HSBC UK (relocated its retail bank headquarters to the city in 2018), Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank has created thousands of finance and professional services jobs in the centre. The city remains young, diverse and ambitious — but inner-city deprivation persists, making Birmingham’s story a useful case study in how a UK city is shaped by site, situation, migration and inequality.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Birmingham’s power lies in its central UK situation and its connections (canals, railways, motorways, soon HS2), not in any unique physical site.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The city has a classic UK structure (CBD, inner city, suburbs, urban-rural fringe) and is reshaped by Commonwealth, EU and recent international migration.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Inequality is sharp within Birmingham: inner-city areas like Sparkbrook are among the most deprived in England, while suburbs like Sutton Coldfield are highly affluent.</li>
</ul>
