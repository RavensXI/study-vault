--- DESCRIPTION ---
How rivers shape upper, middle and lower courses through erosion, transport and deposition.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">From Source to Sea</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every river in the UK starts as rain falling on high ground, gathers into a channel, and flows downhill to the sea. Along the way it changes shape and behaviour. The <strong>upper course</strong> in the hills is steep and narrow; the <strong>middle course</strong> winds through gentler country; the <strong>lower course</strong> nears the sea is broad and flat. The journey of the River Severn or the Thames captures these changes in miniature — and the same processes shape every river worldwide.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Four Erosion Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A river wears away its bed and banks through four <dfn class="term" data-def="The wearing away of the river's bed and banks by the moving water and the rocks it carries.">erosion</dfn> processes. <strong>Hydraulic action</strong>: the sheer force of moving water pushes air into cracks, which eventually break apart. <strong>Abrasion</strong> (or corrasion): the river drags rocks and pebbles along, and these scour the bed and banks. <strong>Attrition</strong>: rocks crash into one another and break into smaller, rounder fragments. <strong>Solution</strong> (or corrosion): rivers flowing through limestone or chalk dissolve the rock chemically.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the four river erosion processes with one example of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four river erosion processes are hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition and solution. Vertical erosion dominates the upper course; lateral erosion dominates the middle and lower courses.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Transport and Deposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A river carries its <strong>load</strong> in four ways. <strong>Traction</strong>: large rocks roll along the bed. <strong>Saltation</strong>: smaller pebbles bounce. <strong>Suspension</strong>: fine clay and silt are carried in the water column, giving rivers their muddy colour. <strong>Solution</strong>: dissolved minerals travel invisibly. <dfn class="term" data-def="The dropping of material a river was carrying when it loses energy and can no longer transport it.">Deposition</dfn> happens when the river loses energy — on the inside of meander bends, on flat floodplains, or where the river enters a lake or the sea.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Upper Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In the steep upper course, vertical erosion dominates. Rivers cut downwards into the bedrock, creating <strong>V-shaped valleys</strong> with <strong>interlocking spurs</strong> (the river weaves between projecting hills). Where a band of hard rock sits above softer rock, a <strong>waterfall</strong> forms: water drops over the resistant lip, eroding a plunge pool below; the soft rock beneath undercuts, and the lip eventually collapses, retreating upstream. High Force on the River Tees in Co. Durham is a classic example. As waterfalls retreat, they leave a steep-sided <strong>gorge</strong>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Middle Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the middle course, gradient eases and lateral erosion takes over. The river starts to swing from side to side, forming <dfn class="term" data-def="A bend or curve in the middle or lower course of a river, where lateral erosion shapes the channel sideways.">meanders</dfn>. On the outside of a bend, fast-flowing water erodes a steep <strong>river cliff</strong>. On the inside, slower water deposits sand and gravel as a <strong>slip-off slope</strong>. Over time the loops widen until adjacent meanders meet during a flood; the river takes the shorter route and abandons the loop. The cut-off, banana-shaped lake left behind is an <strong>ox-bow lake</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, sketch a meander showing river cliff, slip-off slope and the formation of an ox-bow lake.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meanders form because flow is faster on the outside of bends (eroding) and slower on the inside (depositing). When two meanders meet, the river takes the shorter path and leaves an ox-bow lake.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Lower Course Landforms</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Near the sea, the river is wide, deep and slow. <strong>Floodplains</strong> form on either side: when the river floods, it spreads out, slows down and deposits silt, building up flat fertile land. <strong>Levees</strong> are natural or built-up banks of coarser sediment that the river drops at its edges during floods, slightly raising the channel above the surrounding plain. At the sea, where the river drops the rest of its load, a <strong>delta</strong> can form — a fan of sediment criss-crossed with channels (the Nile and the Mississippi are classic deltas).</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n15">A <strong>storm hydrograph</strong> shows river discharge against time during and after a rain event. Bars show rainfall; a curve shows discharge. <strong>Lag time</strong> is the gap between peak rainfall and peak discharge. Short lag times (after heavy rain on impermeable ground) mean rapid runoff and risk of flash floods. Long lag times (after rain on porous chalk soils, or in heavily wooded catchments) are safer because water is delayed by infiltration. Urbanisation cuts lag time sharply because tarmac and concrete prevent infiltration.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Rivers erode by four processes (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution) and transport their load by traction, saltation, suspension and solution.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Upper-course landforms (V-shaped valleys, waterfalls, gorges) form by vertical erosion; middle/lower-course landforms (meanders, ox-bow lakes, floodplains, levees, deltas) form through lateral erosion and deposition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Storm hydrographs reveal how a river responds to rainfall — short lag times warn of flood risk, especially in urbanised catchments.</li>
</ul>
