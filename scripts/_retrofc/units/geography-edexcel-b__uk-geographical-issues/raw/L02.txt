--- DESCRIPTION ---
How geology, waves and weather create cliffs, headlands, bays, spits and beaches.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Coast Made by Waves and Rock</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The UK has roughly 31,000 km of coastline. Stand at Lulworth Cove on a winter morning and you can hear waves smashing against the chalk cliffs, sending vibrations through your boots. The sea is a slow but relentless engineer. Combined with the rocks it works on and the weather above, it carves cliffs, hollows out caves, and builds new beaches and spits from the broken material it carries away. To explain any UK coastal landscape you need three ingredients: <dfn class="term" data-def="The arrangement and type of rock at the coast, including how hard it is and how the layers are tilted.">geology</dfn>, marine processes (waves and tides), and <dfn class="term" data-def="Processes that act on rocks above the water line, such as weathering and mass movement.">sub-aerial processes</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Two Types of Wave</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Waves are the engine of the coast. <dfn class="term" data-def="High, steep waves with a strong backwash that erode the coast.">Destructive waves</dfn> are tall, steep and frequent (10–14 per minute). Their swash is weak compared with their backwash, so they pull material off the beach and into the sea. They erode cliffs. <dfn class="term" data-def="Long, low waves with a strong swash that build up beaches.">Constructive waves</dfn> are long, low and less frequent (6–9 per minute). Their swash is strong, their backwash weak, so they push material up the beach. They build beaches and spits. The same coastline can shift between the two depending on storms and seasons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the difference between destructive and constructive waves, including frequency and the strength of swash and backwash.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Destructive waves: high, steep, frequent (10–14/min), strong backwash — erode coasts. Constructive waves: long, low, less frequent (6–9/min), strong swash — build beaches.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Coastal Erosion: Four Processes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Destructive waves erode rocks in four ways. <strong>Hydraulic action</strong> — air trapped in cracks is compressed by waves and explodes outwards as the wave retreats, breaking the rock. <strong>Abrasion</strong> — waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff like sandpaper. <strong>Attrition</strong> — rocks already in the water are knocked against each other, becoming rounder and smaller. <strong>Solution</strong> (or corrosion) — sea water dissolves soluble rock such as chalk and limestone. These four processes work together over thousands of years to grind cliffs back.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Geology Shapes the Coast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Where alternating bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles — a <strong>discordant coastline</strong> — the soft rock is eroded faster, leaving the hard rock as <strong>headlands</strong> sticking out into the sea, with <strong>bays</strong> in between. The Dorset coast at Studland Bay (chalk headlands, sandy clay bay) is the classic UK example. Where the rock bands run parallel to the coast — a <strong>concordant coastline</strong> — the front layer protects what is behind it, creating long, straight cliffs broken only where waves punch through. Lulworth Cove formed when the sea breached a hard limestone band and then carved a circular bay out of the soft sands behind.</p>

If two caves meet through a headland, an <strong>arch</strong> forms. Continued erosion undercuts the arch until the roof collapses, leaving an isolated tower of rock called a <strong>stack</strong>. Old Harry Rocks in Dorset and the stacks at Flamborough Head in Yorkshire are textbook UK examples. Eventually a stack collapses to a <strong>stump</strong>, often only visible at low tide.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Sub-Aerial Processes and Cliff Retreat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Cliffs are not only attacked from the sea. From above, <strong>weathering</strong> — especially <dfn class="term" data-def="Repeated freezing and thawing of water in cracks that splits rock apart.">freeze-thaw</dfn> in winter — opens cracks. After heavy rain, <strong>mass movement</strong> drags loose material down the cliff face. The Holderness coast in East Yorkshire, made of soft glacial till, retreats by about 2 metres a year on average — one of the fastest erosion rates in Europe. Whole villages such as Mappleton and Aldbrough are losing houses to the sea each decade.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Transport and Deposition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Material eroded from one part of the coast is moved along the shore by <dfn class="term" data-def="The zigzag movement of beach material along a coast caused by waves arriving at an angle.">longshore drift</dfn>. Waves usually approach the shore at an angle (set by the prevailing wind), so the swash carries pebbles diagonally up the beach. Gravity pulls the backwash straight down the slope, leaving the pebble slightly to the side. Repeated, this zigzags material along the coast, sometimes for tens of kilometres.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15">Where the coast changes direction, longshore drift continues out to sea, dropping its load and building a <strong>spit</strong> — a long ridge of sand and shingle. <strong>Spurn Head</strong>, the 5 km curved spit at the mouth of the Humber, is built from material eroded from the Holderness cliffs further north. Behind a spit, sheltered water silts up into a salt marsh. <strong>Beaches</strong> form wherever constructive waves dominate; <strong>bars</strong> form when a spit grows right across a bay.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n16">The Holderness coast is a <strong>sediment cell</strong> — a stretch of coast where erosion, transport and deposition all link up. Soft glacial till at Bridlington is eroded; longshore drift moves the sediment south past Hornsea and Withernsea; deposition at the Humber mouth builds Spurn Head. Damaging the cell at one point — for example, by adding sea defences upstream — can starve the spit of sediment. This is why coastal management decisions cannot be taken one village at a time.</p>

  <p>Erosion sequence on a headland: weakness → cave → arch → stack → stump. Old Harry Rocks (Dorset) and Flamborough Head (Yorkshire) are classic UK examples. Material eroded is moved on by longshore drift.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Destructive waves erode coasts through hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition and solution; constructive waves build beaches, spits and bars.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Geology controls landform: discordant coasts produce headlands and bays; concordant coasts give straight cliffs and coves like Lulworth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Longshore drift links erosion (Holderness) to deposition (Spurn Head), forming a sediment cell that managers must treat as one system.</li>
</ul>
