<div class="lesson-notice" data-notice-title="Your school&rsquo;s fieldwork" hidden><p>This lesson covers the general fieldwork skills, terminology, and exam-paper expectations that apply to every student. The specific sites, data, and methods your class actually used will be different &mdash; for the case study you&rsquo;ll write about in the exam, refer to your teacher&rsquo;s notes and the data you collected on the day.</p></div><h2 data-narration-id="n1">Geography You Can Touch</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Fieldwork is what makes geography real. Reading about coastal erosion is one thing; actually measuring beach pebble shape, plotting wave fetch and explaining why one bay is wider than another is something else. The Edexcel B course requires every student to carry out two pieces of fieldwork — one in a physical environment (river or coast) and one in a human environment (urban or rural). The exam doesn’t test the result; it tests whether you understand the <dfn class="term" data-def="The structured process geographers use to investigate a question, from forming a hypothesis to drawing conclusions and reflecting on the work.">enquiry process</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Six-Stage Enquiry Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Edexcel sets out six stages and you need to know them in order. <strong>Stage 1: Formulating questions and hypotheses.</strong> A good enquiry question is specific and measurable: not ‘Is the river polluted?’ but ‘Does dissolved oxygen decline downstream of the weir?’. <strong>Stage 2: Selecting techniques and methods.</strong> Decide what to measure, how to measure it, and how often. <strong>Stage 3: Processing and presenting data.</strong> Choose appropriate graphs, maps and statistics. <strong>Stage 4: Analysis and explanation.</strong> Apply geographical theory to your findings. <strong>Stage 5: Drawing conclusions.</strong> State whether the hypothesis was supported by the evidence. <strong>Stage 6: Critical reflection.</strong> Evaluate accuracy, sample size, and what you would do differently.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the six stages of the enquiry process in order, with one example of what you would do at each stage.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six enquiry stages run in order: questions, methods, presenting, analysis, conclusions, reflection. Examiners can ask about ANY two stages on any paper, so all six need to be solid.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Primary and Secondary Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Data collected first-hand by the researcher in the field, such as your own measurements or interviews.">Primary data</dfn> is what you collect yourself: pebble measurements at a beach, traffic counts in a town centre, questionnaires of residents. It’s tailored to your question. <strong>Secondary data</strong> is collected by someone else: census figures, Environment Agency flood maps, historical photographs, BGS geology maps. It’s already there but may not match your question exactly. Most enquiries combine both — primary data answers your specific question; secondary data sets the wider context.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Sampling: Why and How</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">You can’t measure every pebble on a beach or interview every resident. <dfn class="term" data-def="The technique of selecting a smaller representative subset of a population for study, instead of measuring everything.">Sampling</dfn> picks a manageable subset that represents the whole. Three main techniques: <strong>random sampling</strong> uses random numbers to choose locations — statistically unbiased but may miss important areas. <strong>Systematic sampling</strong> measures at fixed intervals (e.g. every 10 metres along a transect) — easy to plan, gives even coverage. <strong>Stratified sampling</strong> deliberately samples each subgroup (e.g. equal numbers from each ethnic group, age bracket or land-use type) — ensures every part of the population is represented.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Presenting Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Match the presentation method to the data type. Continuous data over time (river discharge, temperature) suit <strong>line graphs</strong>. Comparing categories (different beaches’ pebble sizes) suit <strong>bar charts</strong>. Showing patterns over space suit <strong>thematic maps</strong> (choropleth, isoline, proportional symbols, flow lines). Paired data suit <strong>scatter graphs</strong> with a line of best fit. Combine maps with photographs and field sketches for a richer presentation. Examiners often give students a graph and ask them to suggest an alternative method — have a clear answer ready.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three sampling techniques and one strength of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Random sampling is unbiased; systematic sampling gives even coverage; stratified sampling ensures every subgroup is represented. Most fieldwork uses a mix.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Analysis, Conclusions and Reflection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Analysis links your findings to wider geographical theory. If pebbles are smaller and rounder downstream, you can refer to attrition. If a town centre has lower environmental quality scores than the suburbs, you can refer to housing markets and accessibility. Conclusions answer the original question with a clear ‘yes/no/partly’ based on the evidence. Reflection — the most-overlooked stage — is where you evaluate accuracy, sample size, possible bias and what you’d change. Strong reflections suggest specific improvements: ‘more sampling sites between sites 3 and 4 would have captured the change in gradient’ or ‘questionnaires only at 11 a.m. missed working residents’.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n19">The six-stage enquiry process runs from questions to reflection — know all six in order and what each demands.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Sampling strategies (random, systematic, stratified) each have strengths; presentation methods should match the data type.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Critical reflection — on accuracy, sample size, bias and improvements — is the stage students most often weaken; specific examples score top marks.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">For the UK fieldwork section of the exam, the questions will pick at least two of the six enquiry stages. Use the language of the stages directly — ‘at the methods stage’, ‘at the analysis stage’ — rather than describing the work in your own words. Always include a critical reflection point: examiners reward students who name specific limitations and propose specific improvements, not vague phrases like ‘more data would be better’.</p>
