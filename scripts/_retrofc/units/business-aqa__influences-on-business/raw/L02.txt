--- DESCRIPTION ---
Trade-offs between doing the right thing and making a profit, and why sustainability is now a business issue.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Doing the Right Thing — at a Price</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Primark found in 2013 that a factory in Bangladesh it bought clothes from had collapsed, killing more than a thousand workers, it faced a choice: keep paying the lowest price for garments or spend more to audit suppliers and improve conditions. When BrewDog set a 2030 carbon-negative target, it agreed to invest heavily in land restoration and renewable energy. These are classic examples of <dfn class="term" data-def="The idea that businesses should act in ways that their stakeholders consider to be fair and honest, even when it costs more.">ethical behaviour</dfn> — doing what stakeholders consider fair and honest, even when it costs more.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: give two benefits of ethical behaviour and two costs that come with it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Acting ethically usually raises costs — higher wages, better-sourced materials, environmental clean-up — but can also raise revenue and loyalty by winning customers who care. The exam will always ask you to weigh both sides.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Ethics-Versus-Profit Trade-off</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where a business must accept a downside in one area to gain a benefit in another.">trade-off</dfn> is where a business must give up something in one area to gain in another. Paying a UK living wage above the legal minimum raises staff costs — but can cut staff turnover and training costs, and build brand reputation. Switching to recyclable packaging raises material costs — but can attract new customers and avoid future regulation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Sometimes the ethical choice is profitable in the long run. The outdoor brand Patagonia built its entire identity on environmental responsibility — even running a ‘Don’t Buy This Jacket’ ad — and grew faster than rivals. But in the short run, ethical choices can hit profits and test shareholders’ patience.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">In 2015 Volkswagen was caught fitting ‘defeat devices’ to hide the true emissions of its diesel cars. The scandal, known as ‘dieselgate’, cost the firm over thirty billion dollars in fines and lost sales, and damaged its brand for years. In 2020 the UK fashion retailer Boohoo faced accusations that its Leicester suppliers paid staff far below the minimum wage — sales fell, share price dropped sharply, and the company commissioned an independent review.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The message is clear: unethical shortcuts can generate short-term profit, but when the media, regulators or customers find out, the long-term cost usually outweighs the gain.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Environmental Considerations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Every business decision has an environmental footprint. Operations generate traffic congestion (think of Amazon vans on every street), noise and air pollution, and waste that ends up in landfill. Consumers, councils and governments increasingly expect businesses to act on this. Supermarkets charge 5p for carrier bags; Starbucks and Pret offer discounts for reusable cups; Deliveroo is testing electric bikes and cargo cycles for last-mile deliveries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Recycling is now a standard cost of doing business. Costa Coffee runs cup-recycling schemes; Tesco takes back soft plastic at its stores. Pollution is regulated too — firms that breach emissions rules face fines, and vehicles in UK clean-air zones pay daily charges.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sustainability — Thinking Long-Term</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Meeting the needs of today without using up resources that future generations will need.">Sustainability</dfn> means meeting today’s needs without using up resources future generations will need. Global warming has pushed governments to set net-zero targets, which ripple down into business decisions: electric vans, energy-efficient buildings, carbon-offset schemes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Scarce resources are a further concern. Fish stocks, rare earth metals for electronics, and fresh water are all finite. Businesses that rely on them need long-term plans — otherwise their own inputs run out. McDonald’s, for example, has committed to sourcing fish only from certified sustainable fisheries, protecting its future supply chain.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Costs and Benefits of Ethical and Green Behaviour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Costs include higher-priced raw materials, staff training on new procedures, investment in cleaner technology, and the risk that ethical decisions are used as marketing without real change — known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Claiming environmental credentials in marketing without genuine action to back them up.">greenwashing</dfn>. Customers and regulators are quick to spot and punish this. In 2022 the UK’s Competition and Markets Authority opened investigations into whether major fashion retailers’ ‘sustainable’ labels were accurate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Benefits include stronger brand reputation, the ability to charge premium prices, better staff retention (employees want to work for responsible firms), and early compliance with future laws. When governments later tighten rules, ethical firms do not need to scramble.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and test yourself: write down three environmental impacts of business activity and one example of a UK business reducing each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Environmental impacts of business activity include traffic congestion, noise and air pollution, waste sent to landfill, and the use of scarce resources. Sustainable practices aim to reduce each of these without shutting the business down.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Ethics and sustainability are no longer optional extras. They shape what materials a firm uses, how it hires, what it dares to claim in adverts, and how quickly it can react when a new law lands. Businesses that plan for them early pay less and earn more loyalty than those forced to react under pressure.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Ethical behaviour means acting in ways stakeholders see as fair and honest, even when it costs more — the trade-off between ethics and short-term profit is at the heart of every decision.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Businesses affect the environment through traffic, pollution, waste and use of scarce resources; recycling, cleaner logistics and green sourcing can cut these impacts.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Sustainability is long-term planning — firms that invest early avoid later fines, protect their supply chains, and earn reputation that keeps customers loyal.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Ethics questions almost always reward a balanced answer — do not just say ‘being ethical is good’. For Analyse questions, build a chain of reasoning: ‘recyclable packaging raises material costs, which squeezes margin, but attracts eco-conscious customers, which lifts revenue.’ For 12-mark Evaluate questions, the conclusion must introduce a new weighting — for example, ‘it depends on the type of customer’ — not just repeat the points you have already made. Always name a real example where you can.</p>
