--- DESCRIPTION ---
How vaccines and antibiotics work, antibiotic resistance, and the stages of drug testing.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Vaccination</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A preparation containing dead or weakened pathogens (or parts of them) that is introduced into the body to stimulate an immune response without causing disease.">vaccination</dfn> introduces a small quantity of dead or inactive forms of a pathogen into the body. This stimulates the white blood cells (lymphocytes) to produce <dfn class="term" data-def="Proteins produced by lymphocytes that are specific to a particular pathogen. They bind to antigens on the pathogen’s surface to destroy it.">antibodies</dfn>. If the same pathogen enters the body again in the future, the white blood cells can respond rapidly and produce the correct antibodies much faster, preventing infection.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what vaccines contain and which specific cells remain to provide rapid secondary immune responses.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies by introducing dead or weakened pathogens. Memory lymphocytes remain, allowing a rapid secondary immune response if the pathogen is encountered again.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">If a large proportion of the population is vaccinated, the spread of the pathogen is greatly reduced because there are fewer people who can become infected and pass it on. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="When a large proportion of a population is immune to a pathogen (through vaccination), reducing the spread and protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.">herd immunity</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Antibiotics and Painkillers</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Medicines that kill or prevent the growth of bacteria. They do not work on viruses because viruses reproduce inside host cells.">Antibiotics</dfn> are medicines that kill bacteria or prevent them from reproducing inside the body. They are used to treat bacterial infections. <strong>Antibiotics do not work on viruses</strong> because viruses reproduce inside the host’s own cells, and it is very difficult to damage the virus without also damaging the host cell.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">Painkillers treat the symptoms of disease (e.g. pain, inflammation) but do not kill pathogens. They make the patient feel better but do not cure the infection itself.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Antibiotic Resistance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Some bacteria have developed <dfn class="term" data-def="When bacteria evolve so that they are no longer killed by an antibiotic. This happens through natural selection when antibiotics are overused or not completed.">antibiotic resistance</dfn> through natural selection. Mutations in bacterial DNA can produce strains that are not killed by an antibiotic. These resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, passing on the resistance gene. Overuse of antibiotics and failure to complete courses accelerates this process.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">MRSA (methicillin-resistant <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em>) is an example of a “superbug” that is resistant to several antibiotics. To reduce the development of resistant strains, doctors should only prescribe antibiotics when necessary, patients should always complete the full course, and the agricultural use of antibiotics should be restricted.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Drug Development and Testing</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n11">New drugs must go through rigorous testing before they can be used. The stages are:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Stages of Drug Development</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Stage 1 — Preclinical testing:</strong> The drug is tested on cells, tissues and live animals. This checks for toxicity (is it safe?), efficacy (does it work?) and dose (how much is needed?).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Stage 2 — Clinical trials:</strong> If preclinical tests are successful, the drug is tested on healthy volunteers and then on patients. A very low dose is given first to check for side effects. <dfn class="term" data-def="A clinical trial where some patients receive the drug and others receive a placebo (inactive substance) to test whether the drug truly works.">Double-blind trials</dfn> are used where neither the patient nor the doctor knows who receives the real drug and who receives a <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that looks like a drug but has no therapeutic effect, used in clinical trials to provide a comparison.">placebo</dfn> (an inactive substance). This eliminates bias.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Stage 3 — Peer review and approval:</strong> Results are reviewed by independent scientists and regulatory bodies before the drug is approved for widespread use.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the three stages of drug testing in order, including what double-blind placebo trials involve.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Drug testing stages: preclinical (cells, tissues, animals) → clinical trials (healthy volunteers, then patients, using double-blind placebo trials) → peer review and approval. This process typically takes over 10 years.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n16">Many drugs were originally discovered in plants or microorganisms. For example, the painkiller aspirin was originally derived from willow bark, the heart drug digitalis comes from foxgloves, and the antibiotic penicillin was discovered from a mould by Alexander Fleming in 1928.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Vaccines introduce dead/weakened pathogens to stimulate antibody production and create immune memory.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses; overuse leads to antibiotic resistance through natural selection.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">New drugs go through preclinical testing (animals/cells), clinical trials (double-blind, placebo-controlled) and peer review.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n17">A very common exam question asks why antibiotics cannot be used to treat viral infections. The key point is that viruses reproduce inside host cells, so any drug that kills the virus would also damage the host cell. Make sure you can also explain the stages of clinical trials and the purpose of placebos and double-blind testing.</p>
