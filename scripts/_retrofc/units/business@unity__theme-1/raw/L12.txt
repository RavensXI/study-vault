Short-term and long-term finance options including loans, overdrafts, and crowdfunding.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Businesses Need Finance?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business needs money to operate. A start-up might need cash to buy equipment, rent premises, or pay staff before any sales come in. An established business might need finance to expand, launch a new product, or cover a temporary <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where a business has more money going out than coming in over a period of time.">cash-flow problem</dfn>. The type of finance a business chooses depends on how much money it needs, how quickly it needs it, and how long it will take to pay it back.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 examples each of short-term and long-term finance.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Sources of finance are split into two categories: <strong>short-term finance</strong> (repaid within one year) for day-to-day costs, and <strong>long-term finance</strong> (repaid over more than one year) for major investments like equipment, premises, or expansion.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Short-Term Sources of Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money a business borrows or uses for a short period, usually less than one year, to cover day-to-day costs.">Short-term finance</dfn> helps a business pay its immediate bills — things like wages, stock, or rent — until more cash comes in from customers. It is not meant for large investments.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Overdrafts</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n7">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A facility that allows a business to spend more money than it has in its bank account, up to an agreed limit.">overdraft</dfn> is an arrangement with a bank that lets a business spend more than it has in its account, up to an agreed limit. For example, if a business has £500 in its account and an overdraft limit of £2,000, it can spend up to £2,500. Overdrafts are flexible — the business only pays interest on the amount it actually uses — but interest rates are usually higher than on a loan. The bank can also withdraw the overdraft at any time, which makes it risky to rely on.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n8">Trade Credit</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement where a supplier allows a business to receive goods now and pay for them at a later date, usually 30 to 60 days.">Trade credit</dfn> means a supplier delivers goods to a business now, but the business does not have to pay for them straight away — typically for 30 to 60 days. This is useful because the business can sell the products and receive cash from customers before it needs to pay the supplier. However, suppliers may charge higher prices for goods bought on credit, and if the business fails to pay on time it could damage the relationship and the supplier may refuse credit in the future.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Overdraft benefits:</strong> Quick and flexible — the business only borrows what it needs. No need to apply for a separate loan. Good for covering short gaps in cash flow, such as a seasonal business that earns most of its revenue in summer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Overdraft drawbacks:</strong> High interest rates compared to loans. The bank can cancel the overdraft at short notice. Not suitable for large or long-term spending.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Trade credit benefits:</strong> The business never runs out of stock. It can sell products and collect cash before paying the supplier, easing cash flow.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Trade credit drawbacks:</strong> Suppliers may charge more for credit arrangements. New businesses may be refused trade credit because they have no track record. Late payment can damage the business’s reputation.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Long-Term Sources of Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money raised by a business that is repaid over more than one year, used for major investments like premises or equipment.">Long-term finance</dfn> is used when a business needs a large sum of money for a major purchase or investment, such as buying new premises, expensive machinery, or funding a major expansion. These sources are repaid over several years.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Loans</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A fixed sum of money borrowed from a bank or lender, repaid over an agreed period with interest.">loan</dfn> is a fixed amount of money borrowed from a bank, repaid in regular instalments over an agreed period (often 1–10 years) with <dfn class="term" data-def="The extra amount charged by a lender for borrowing money, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed.">interest</dfn>. Loans provide a lump sum that can cover set-up costs, equipment, or expansion. The longer the loan term, the lower each monthly payment — but the more interest is paid overall. Banks may require security (such as property) and may refuse to lend to brand-new businesses with no trading history.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Personal Savings</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money saved by the business owner or borrowed from friends and family to fund the business.">Personal savings</dfn> are often the first source of finance for a start-up. The owner uses their own money (or borrows from friends and family) to get the business going. This avoids paying interest, but the owner risks losing their savings if the business fails.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Retained Profit</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="Profit that a business keeps and reinvests rather than distributing to shareholders or the owner.">Retained profit</dfn> is money left over after a business has paid all its costs and taxes. Instead of distributing this profit to the owners, the business keeps it to invest in future projects. This is free (no interest to pay) and the business does not give up any control. However, only established businesses that have already made a profit can use this method — a start-up has no retained profit.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what is the main source of finance a start-up CANNOT use, and why?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A start-up business cannot use <strong>retained profit</strong> because it has not yet traded or made any profit. Start-ups typically rely on personal savings, loans, or external investors.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Share Capital</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money raised by a private limited company by selling shares to investors, who become part-owners of the business.">Share capital</dfn> is money raised by selling shares in the business to investors. In a private limited company (Ltd), shares can be sold to family, friends, or approved investors. The business receives a large sum of money without having to repay it, but the original owner gives up a share of ownership and future profits. Shareholders may also want a say in how the business is run.</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<span>Venture Capital and Crowdfunding</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="Investment from a wealthy individual or specialist firm in exchange for a share of the business or an agreed return.">Venture capital</dfn> is investment from a wealthy individual or specialist firm who provides a large sum of money in exchange for a share of the company or an agreed return on investment. Venture capitalists often invest in businesses with high growth potential and may also offer expertise and business contacts. However, the business owner loses some control and must share profits.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="Raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, usually through an online platform.">Crowdfunding</dfn> involves raising money from a large number of people, each contributing a small amount, usually through an online platform such as Kickstarter or GoFundMe. In return, investors may receive a reward (such as early access to a product), a share of the business, or simply the satisfaction of supporting an idea. Crowdfunding also acts as free marketing — if many people invest, it shows there is demand for the product. However, the business must share its idea publicly (risking imitation), and there is no guarantee the funding target will be reached.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Choosing the Right Source of Finance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The right source of finance depends on several factors. A start-up with no trading history may have to rely on personal savings, crowdfunding, or venture capital because banks may refuse to lend. An established business with a strong track record can access loans, retained profit, or share capital more easily. Short-term needs (like covering a quiet month) suit overdrafts or trade credit, while long-term needs (like buying new premises) require loans, share capital, or retained profit.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Close this and list 4 key factors to justify the suitability of a finance source in an exam.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In the exam, you may be asked to <strong>justify</strong> which source of finance is most suitable for a given business. Always consider: Is the business a start-up or established? Is the need short-term or long-term? Does the owner want to keep full control? Can the business afford interest repayments?</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">

<span>Case Study: Choosing Finance for a Seasonal Business</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n30">Imagine an ice-cream van business that earns most of its revenue during summer. In winter, the owner still has to pay insurance, storage, and vehicle maintenance, but sales are very low. A bank overdraft would be a suitable short-term solution — the owner borrows small amounts during winter and repays them when summer sales pick up. A long-term loan would be less appropriate because the owner does not need a large lump sum and would be paying interest all year round on money that is only needed for a few months.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">Short-term finance (overdrafts, trade credit) covers day-to-day costs for up to one year, while long-term finance (loans, share capital, retained profit, venture capital, crowdfunding) funds major investments over several years.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Each source has trade-offs — loans charge interest, share capital means giving up ownership, retained profit is only available to established businesses, and overdrafts carry high interest rates.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">The best source depends on the business’s situation: whether it is a start-up or established, the size and duration of the need, and whether the owner wants to retain full control.</li></ul>

State the source, explain why it suits that business (e.g. start-up vs established, short-term vs long-term need), and then consider a drawback or alternative. Use connectives like “This means…”, “As a result…”, and “However…” to build chains of reasoning.</p>
