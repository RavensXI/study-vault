--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses use primary and secondary research to reduce risk and inform decisions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Businesses Carry Out Market Research?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of gathering information about customer needs, preferences, and competitor activity to inform business decisions.">Market research</dfn> is the process of finding out what customers want and need, and what competitors are doing. Without it, a business is guessing — and guessing costs money. The Edexcel spec identifies four key purposes of market research that you need to know.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the FOUR purposes of market research.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four purposes of market research are: <strong>to identify and understand customer needs</strong>, <strong>to identify gaps in the market</strong>, <strong>to reduce risk</strong>, and <strong>to inform business decisions</strong>.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A business uses market research to understand its potential and existing customers. For example, a clothing brand might discover through research that its target audience prefers sustainable fabrics. This is known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Information a business gathers about its customers’ behaviour, preferences, and motivations that helps guide business decisions.">customer insight</dfn> — smart clues about what people like, need, and why they buy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Market research also helps businesses spot gaps in the market where no existing product meets customer needs. It reduces risk by testing ideas before a full launch. Greggs, for example, used social listening and in-store trials before rolling out its vegan sausage roll nationally — they tested small, measured actual buying behaviour, and only scaled up once demand was proven.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Primary Research Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The collection of new, first-hand data that is specific to the business, also called field research.">Primary research</dfn> (also called field research) involves collecting brand-new data directly from customers or potential customers. It is tailored to the business but tends to be more expensive and time-consuming than secondary research. Edexcel requires you to know four primary methods.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Surveys and Questionnaires</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">A <strong>survey</strong> is the whole process of planning, collecting, and analysing responses. A <strong>questionnaire</strong> is simply the set of questions used within a survey. Surveys can collect large amounts of data quickly — for example, satisfaction buttons in shops or pop-up forms on websites. Questionnaires are cost-efficient, especially online, and can be targeted at past customers. However, surveys may include poor-quality data (people pressing random buttons), and questionnaires often suffer from low return rates because customers dislike giving out personal details.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Focus Groups and Observation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A small group of selected people brought together by researchers to discuss and give opinions on a product or service.">focus group</dfn> brings together a small group of potential customers to discuss a product or idea. It generates rich qualitative data about how customers feel. Disney, for example, uses kid-centric focus groups to test reactions to new characters and merchandise. The downside is that focus groups are expensive to organise, and dominant personalities can influence others’ responses.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Observation</strong> means watching how customers behave without them knowing — for example, tracking which route shoppers take around a store or whether they notice promotions. This gives accurate, natural data with no recall error, but it is very time-consuming and relies on the observer’s interpretation, which may be biased.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Secondary Research Methods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Research using data that already exists, collected by someone else for another purpose, also called desk research.">Secondary research</dfn> (also called desk research) uses information that already exists. It is usually cheaper and faster than primary research, but may not be specific to the business. Edexcel requires you to know three secondary methods.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the THREE secondary research methods and give an example cost.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The three secondary research methods on the spec are: <strong>internet research</strong> (competitor websites, search trends, reviews), <strong>market reports</strong> (e.g. Mintel reports costing £1,000–£4,000), and <strong>government reports</strong> (e.g. the Office for National Statistics). All are cheaper than primary research but may be out of date or not specific to the business.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Internet research is free and available 24/7. A business can check competitor prices, read customer reviews, track search trends, and run A/B tests on its own website. Market reports from companies like Mintel compile detailed industry data — for example, showing that demand for high-protein snacks is rising, which might prompt a supermarket to launch a new product line. Government reports from the ONS provide demographic and industry data that helps businesses decide whether to expand into new areas.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Qualitative vs Quantitative Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Numerical data that can be measured, counted, and displayed in charts and graphs, such as sales figures and percentages.">Quantitative data</dfn> is numerical — statistics, percentages, and figures that can be displayed in charts. For example, “62% of respondents preferred delivery over click-and-collect.” It is useful for spotting patterns and making comparisons, but it does not explain <em>why</em> customers feel a certain way.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Descriptive data based on opinions, feelings, and experiences that explores the reasons behind customer behaviour.">Qualitative data</dfn> explores feelings and opinions — the “why” behind the numbers. Focus groups, interviews, and observation all generate qualitative data. Comments like “the burger was too salty but arrived fast” give a business specific, actionable feedback that numbers alone cannot provide.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Role of Social Media in Market Research</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">Social media has transformed market research. It is cheaper than organising a focus group, provides real-time data, and reaches a much larger audience. Businesses can track trending hashtags, run Instagram polls, and monitor customer comments to spot emerging trends. However, not all social media data is reliable — responses may come from people outside the target market, and opinions can be influenced by viral trends rather than genuine preference.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Reliability of Market Research Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Whether research results are consistent and could be replicated if the study were repeated under the same conditions.">Reliability</dfn> means the research produces consistent results that could be repeated. If a business surveys only 40 people but has thousands of customers, the sample may be too small to be reliable. <dfn class="term" data-def="Whether research measures what it is supposed to measure and the results are representative of the wider population.">Validity</dfn> means the research actually measures what it set out to measure — asking clear, unbiased questions to a representative sample.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how UNRELIABLE research could lead to business failure.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Reliable research leads to informed decisions, reduced risk, and higher chances of success. Unreliable research can lead to launching products that do not sell, wasting money, and ultimately business failure.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Market research helps businesses identify customer needs, spot gaps, reduce risk, and make informed decisions — the four purposes you must know for the exam.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Primary research (surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, observation) collects new, specific data but is expensive. Secondary research (internet, market reports, government reports) is cheaper but may be outdated or generic.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Quantitative data gives the numbers; qualitative data explains the “why.” Businesses need both, and must check that their data is reliable and valid before making decisions.</li></ul>
