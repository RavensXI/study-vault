--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses divide customers into groups using demographics, location, income, and lifestyle.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Market Segmentation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not every customer is the same. A teenager buying trainers has different needs to a retired couple looking for walking shoes. <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of dividing a market into distinct sub-groups of consumers who share similar characteristics or needs.">Market segmentation</dfn> is the process of breaking down a whole market into smaller sub-groups so that a business can target the right customers with the right products.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Market research will show that a particular product appeals to a specific group of people. A business needs this information to identify groups with similar wants and needs. Once a business knows who its customers are, it can tailor its marketing, pricing, and product design to match.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the 5 market segments Edexcel wants you to know, plus market mapping's purpose.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Edexcel spec requires you to know five ways of segmenting a market: <strong>location</strong>, <strong>demographics</strong>, <strong>lifestyle</strong>, <strong>income</strong>, and <strong>age</strong>. You also need to understand <strong>market mapping</strong> to identify gaps in the market and the competition.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Segmenting by Location</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A business may decide to sell its product in just one country, one region, or in an even smaller area. <dfn class="term" data-def="Dividing the market based on where customers live or where a product will be sold, such as urban vs rural or hot vs cold climates.">Location segmentation</dfn> means targeting customers based on where they live or where the product will be used. For example, a business selling raincoats would focus on rainy coastal areas like Cornwall or the Lake District, while a company selling beach towels would target seaside resort towns.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Businesses can also target urban versus rural areas. A food delivery app like Deliveroo focuses on cities where there are enough customers in a small area to make the service profitable. A farm shop, by contrast, targets rural customers who want locally sourced produce.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Segmenting by Demographics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Statistical data about a population, including age, gender, ethnicity, family size, and occupation, used to identify customer groups.">Demographics</dfn> means “statistical data relating to the population and particular groups within it.” Businesses can segment their market by age, gender, family size, ethnicity, religion, or occupation. For example, a publisher knows that not everyone wants to read the same book — a children’s fiction range targets a completely different demographic to a business management title.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Family setup:</strong> Single adults buy meal kits for one and studio-sized furniture. Large families (4+ children) need 12-pack cereals and 7-seater car deals.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Religion & ethnicity:</strong> Observant Muslim customers may look for halal meal boxes and Ramadan evening delivery slots. The Black haircare market targets protective-style products and satin pillowcases.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Segmenting by Lifestyle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Customers can be grouped according to the way they lead their lives and the attitudes they share. A young professional may drive a sports car because of the image they want to project, while parents with children need a larger family car to suit their lifestyle. <dfn class="term" data-def="Dividing the market based on how customers live, including their hobbies, values, and spending habits.">Lifestyle segmentation</dfn> helps businesses design products that fit the way people actually live.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Holiday companies use lifestyle segmentation extensively. Multi-generational families need villas with pools and kids’ clubs. Pet owners want pet-friendly cottages with nearby walks. Adventure backpackers aged 18–25 look for hostels, group treks, and budget city-to-city passes.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Segmenting by Income</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The population can be segmented according to annual salary or type of job. Establishing a group’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The money a person has left to spend after all essential bills and taxes have been paid, also known as discretionary income.">disposable income</dfn> is important so that products can be targeted to the relevant income group. For example, Toyota sells the Aygo at £15,000 for budget-conscious buyers, the RAV4 at £44,000 for middle-income families, and the Supra at £57,000 for high-income enthusiasts.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Social Class Categories (A–E)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The UK uses a social class classification system based on occupation. <strong>Group A</strong> includes upper-class professionals such as lawyers and accountants. <strong>Group B</strong> covers middle-class professionals like teachers and nurses. <strong>Group C1</strong> is lower middle-class skilled non-manual workers such as police officers and clerical staff. <strong>Group C2</strong> covers skilled working-class workers like plumbers and electricians. <strong>Group D</strong> includes unskilled manual workers such as labourers. <strong>Group E</strong> covers those on state benefits, including pensioners and the unemployed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">A business may decide to target ABC1 consumers with premium products or DE consumers with budget alternatives, depending on the product.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define 'disposable income' and explain how it affects a gym targeting students.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Disposable income</strong> is what a person has left after all bills and taxes have been paid. Businesses must identify their target group’s disposable income level to price products correctly — a premium gym membership will not sell to students with very little disposable income.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Market Mapping</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that plots existing brands or products on two axes (such as price and quality) to identify gaps in the market and understand the competition.">Market mapping</dfn> is the process of finding the variables that differentiate brands in a market and then plotting them on a diagram. The two axes could be price versus quality, functional versus luxury, or modern versus traditional — the choice depends on the industry.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">For example, plotting shoe brands on a market map with price on one axis and quality on the other might show Jimmy Choo in the premium/luxury corner, Nike in the mid-price/high-quality area, Primark in the budget/functional area, and a possible gap in the mid-price/luxury space where no brand currently operates.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Uses of Market Mapping</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Identify gaps in the market:</strong> If no brand occupies a particular position on the map, that space could represent an opportunity. A start-up can use this to decide what products to launch.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Understand the competition:</strong> Plotting competitors helps a business see where the market is crowded and where it might face less direct competition.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Reposition a brand:</strong> An established business can use market mapping to see whether it needs to move its brand. The North Face started as professional climbing gear but repositioned itself as a fashion brand through collaborations with Supreme and Gucci, shifting its position on the market map from functional/premium to fashionable/premium.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain how many axes a market map has and why a gap on it isn't always a winner.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A <strong>market map</strong> always has two axes. Exam questions may ask you to draw one, label the axes, or explain what a gap on the map means. Remember: a gap does not guarantee success — there might be no demand in that space.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Benefits & Risks of Segmentation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Segmenting a market allows a business to focus its marketing spend on the customers most likely to buy. It reduces waste because advertising reaches the right people rather than everyone. A segmented approach also helps businesses tailor products to specific needs, which can increase customer satisfaction and loyalty.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">However, there are risks. Segmenting too narrowly can exclude potential customers. A personal trainer who targets only high-income clients in a small area might not find enough customers to fill her diary. There is also a cost to researching and understanding each segment — smaller businesses may not have the resources to do this effectively.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">Market segmentation divides a market into sub-groups by location, demographics, lifestyle, income, or age so businesses can target the right customers with the right products.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Market mapping plots brands on two axes to identify gaps in the market and understand the competitive landscape.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">Segmentation reduces marketing waste and increases customer satisfaction, but segmenting too narrowly risks excluding profitable customers.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">When answering questions on market segmentation, always name the specific segment type (location, demographics, lifestyle, income, or age) rather than just saying “they target a specific group.” For <em>Explain</em> questions, use connective chains: “This means… Therefore… Leading to…” to build your analysis. For <em>Justify</em> questions, remember the P-A-BLT structure: Point, Application (use case study facts), Because, Leading to, Therefore.</p>
