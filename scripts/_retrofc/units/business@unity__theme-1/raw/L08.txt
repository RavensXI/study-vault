Financial and non-financial aims, from survival and profit to independence and control.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Business Aims and Objectives?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business needs a clear sense of direction. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A broad, long-term goal that a business wants to achieve, such as growth or survival.">aim</dfn> is a broad, long-term goal that a business wants to achieve. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific, measurable target set by a business to help it achieve its aims.">objective</dfn> is a specific, shorter-term target that helps a business reach its aim. For example, a business might have the aim of “becoming profitable” and set the objective of “increasing sales by 10% in the next year.”</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and state the difference between an aim (like 'grow the business') and an objective (like 10% sales increase).">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>An <strong>aim</strong> is a big, long-term goal (e.g. “to grow the business”). An <strong>objective</strong> is a specific, short- to medium-term target that helps a business reach its aim (e.g. “increase sales by 10% this year”). Objectives give aims a measurable focus.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Businesses set aims and objectives for three important reasons. First, they provide <strong>specific targets</strong> against which performance can be measured. Second, they help <strong>motivate workers</strong> by giving them clear goals to work towards. Third, they <strong>clarify direction</strong> and help managers make better decisions about where to invest time and money.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Financial Aims and Objectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Financial aims and objectives focus on the money side of a business. The Edexcel specification identifies five key financial aims for businesses starting up: <strong>survival</strong>, <strong>profit</strong>, <strong>sales</strong>, <strong>market share</strong>, and <strong>financial security</strong>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Survival</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">In its first year of trading, many businesses simply aim to <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business generates enough revenue to cover its costs and continue trading.">survive</dfn>. This means the owner wants the business to keep trading long enough to become established. To survive, the business needs to reach a sustainable level of sales that covers its costs — in other words, it needs to reach its <strong>break-even point</strong>. This often means keeping prices low at first. Profit may be small initially, but once the business is established it can raise prices with more confidence.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Profit</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Total revenue minus total costs. The money a business keeps after paying all its expenses.">Profit</dfn> is total revenue minus total costs (P = TR − TC). A business aiming to maximise profit must either increase the revenue coming in or reduce the costs going out — or ideally both. Profit is essential because it rewards the owner for the risk they have taken and provides funds for reinvestment and growth.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Sales:</strong> A new business may set an objective to grow its sales volume. For example, an online shop might keep prices low to attract customers and encourage repeat purchases. Growing sales helps build the customer base that the business needs to survive long-term.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Market share:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of total sales in a market that is held by one business.">Market share</dfn> is the percentage of total sales in a market held by one business. To increase market share, a business must take sales away from competitors. This could be achieved through heavy promotion, discounts, or making products more appealing. In 2019, ASDA attempted to merge with Sainsbury’s to overtake Tesco as the UK’s largest supermarket group, but the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) blocked the deal because it would reduce competition and could lead to higher prices for shoppers.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-1/diagram_generated_3f99a05f.png" alt="Generated diagram for Business Aims and Objectives"><figcaption>Generated diagram for Business Aims and Objectives</figcaption></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Financial security:</strong> If an entrepreneur has left a regular paid job to start a business, they need the business to generate enough income to cover personal bills such as mortgage payments, loans, and household costs. <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business owner generates enough income from their business to meet their personal financial needs.">Financial security</dfn> means the business provides a reliable, stable income for its owner.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Non-Financial Aims and Objectives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Not every business goal is about money. Non-financial aims focus on personal fulfilment and making a positive difference. The specification identifies five non-financial aims: <strong>social objectives</strong>, <strong>personal satisfaction</strong>, <strong>challenge</strong>, <strong>independence</strong>, and <strong>control</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list 3 financial aims and 3 non-financial aims that a business could have.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Financial aims include survival, profit, sales, market share, and financial security. Non-financial aims include social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence, and control. You must be able to classify each aim correctly in the exam.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Social objectives:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="A business set up to trade for a social or environmental purpose, reinvesting most profits back into the community.">Social enterprises</dfn> are businesses that trade for social or environmental purposes. Their social mission is central to everything they do. For example, Mecoco is a social enterprise that works with disabled adults and young people to create scented home fragrance products, providing meaningful employment opportunities regardless of support needs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Personal satisfaction:</strong> Many entrepreneurs start a business because they want to do something they are passionate about. Working for yourself can provide a deep sense of achievement and fulfilment that a regular job might not offer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Challenge:</strong> Some people are motivated by the challenge of building something from scratch. The idea of a predictable 9-to-5 job does not suit their personality, and they thrive on the unpredictability and problem-solving that comes with running a business.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Independence:</strong> Entrepreneurs may want the freedom to make their own decisions rather than working for someone else. Being their own boss means they can shape the business exactly as they want.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Control:</strong> Owning a business gives entrepreneurs more control over their working lives. They can choose their own working hours, decide what products or services to sell, and determine how the business is promoted.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Why Aims and Objectives Differ Between Businesses</h2>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Private Sector vs Public Sector vs Voluntary Sector</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Private sector</strong> businesses (manufacturers, retailers, banks) are owned by private investors and typically aim to maximise profits, increase market share, and expand. <strong>Public sector</strong> organisations (the BBC, NHS, state schools) are owned by the government and aim to meet customer targets (e.g. reducing patient waiting times) while keeping costs low. <strong>Voluntary sector</strong> organisations (charities, community groups) aim to increase donations, reduce costs, and provide for those in need. Social enterprises like the Big Issue fall into this category too.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How Business Size Affects Aims</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n22">A small sole trader — such as a local plumber — is likely to prioritise survival, personal satisfaction, and financial security. A large PLC like Tesco focuses on increasing market share, maximising profit, and growing sales revenue. The scale and ownership structure of a business directly shapes which aims matter most.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n23" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how a start-up sole trader's aims differ from those of a multinational PLC.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The <strong>sector</strong> a business operates in (private, public, or voluntary) and its <strong>size</strong> both influence which aims and objectives it prioritises. A start-up sole trader has very different goals from a multinational PLC.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Aims are broad long-term goals; objectives are specific short-term targets that help achieve those aims. Businesses set them to measure performance, motivate workers, and guide decisions.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Financial aims (survival, profit, sales, market share, financial security) focus on money, while non-financial aims (social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence, control) focus on personal fulfilment and social impact.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">The sector (private, public, voluntary) and size of a business determine which aims it prioritises.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">When answering questions on aims and objectives, always <strong>classify</strong> each aim as financial or non-financial — this shows the examiner you understand the distinction. For “Discuss” and “Evaluate” questions, argue both sides: explain why a particular aim matters, then consider why a different aim might be more important. Always use <strong>application</strong> — link your answer to the specific business in the question, not just generic theory.</p>
