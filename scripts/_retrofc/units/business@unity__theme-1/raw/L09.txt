--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses calculate revenue, costs and profit using key formulas and worked examples.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Revenue?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The income a business receives from selling its products or services, calculated as price multiplied by quantity sold.">Revenue</dfn> is the money coming into a business through the sale of its products or services. You might also hear it called <dfn class="term" data-def="Another name for revenue — the total income a business earns from selling goods or services.">turnover</dfn> or sales revenue. Think of it as the total amount of cash flowing into the business from customers — whether they pay by card, cash or Apple Pay.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and state the revenue formula using R, P and Q.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The formula for revenue is: <strong>Revenue = Price × Quantity Sold</strong> (or R = P × Q). You must learn this formula for the exam.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Revenue does not tell you how much money the business actually keeps — it is simply the total income before any costs are taken away. A business could have very high revenue but still make a loss if its costs are even higher.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Calculating Revenue</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">A car dealership sells 25 cars at £7,000 each.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Revenue = Price × Quantity = £7,000 × 25 = <strong>£175,000</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A sweet shop sells 1,000 sweets at £0.36 each.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Revenue = £0.36 × 1,000 = <strong>£360</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A clothing store sells 56 t-shirts at £7.99 each.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Revenue = £7.99 × 56 = <strong>£447.44</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Fixed Costs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that do not change with the level of output — they must be paid regardless of how many products a business sells.">Fixed costs</dfn> are costs that do not vary with the level of output. The business has to pay these whether it sells one product or one million products. They are sometimes called overheads.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Common examples of fixed costs include rent, insurance, loan repayments, lease of machinery, and salaries of managers. Notice that wages are not included here — wages often change depending on how many hours staff work, which links to output levels.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two fixed costs a shop might have even when closed.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Fixed costs must be paid even if the business sells nothing. A shop that closes for a week still has to pay its rent and insurance.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Variable Costs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that change in direct proportion to the level of output — as production increases, variable costs rise.">Variable costs</dfn> are costs that do change with the level of output. If a business makes more products, its variable costs go up. If it makes fewer, they go down.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Examples include raw materials, production supplies, delivery costs, packaging, and temporary staff hired for busy periods. To find the total variable costs, use the formula: <strong>Total Variable Costs = Variable Cost per Unit × Quantity</strong>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Classifying Costs: Fixed or Variable?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the exam, you may need to classify costs. Here is a quick guide:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Fixed:</strong> Business insurance, rent, salaries of managers, advertising, utility bills.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Variable:</strong> Raw materials, distribution costs, packaging, wages (linked to output).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The key question to ask is: “Does this cost change when the business produces more or fewer items?” If yes, it is variable. If no, it is fixed.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Total Costs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="The sum of all fixed costs and variable costs in a business.">Total costs</dfn> are the sum of all the costs in a business — fixed and variable added together. As output rises, total costs rise because variable costs increase.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the Total Costs formula, using abbreviations for each term.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The formula for total costs is: <strong>Total Costs = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs</strong> (TC = FC + VC). This is one of the most important formulas in the whole course.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Calculating Total Costs</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25">A business has fixed costs of £2,000 and variable costs of £9 per unit. If it produces 1 unit:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Total Costs = £2,000 + £9 = <strong>£2,009</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Another business has fixed costs of £2 million and variable costs of £600,000.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Total Costs = £2,000,000 + £600,000 = <strong>£2,600,000</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Profit and Loss</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><dfn class="term" data-def="The money left over after all costs have been deducted from total revenue.">Profit</dfn> is the main objective for most businesses. It is the amount of money left over once all the bills have been paid. The owner can keep this money or invest it back into the business to help it grow.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">The formula is straightforward: <strong>Profit (or Loss) = Total Revenue − Total Costs</strong>. If the answer is positive, the business has made a profit. If the answer is negative, the business has made a <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business’s total costs are greater than its total revenue, meaning it spends more than it earns.">loss</dfn>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Profit and Loss Calculation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Profit example:</strong> A glassware business has revenue of £35,000, variable costs of £20,000 and fixed costs of £3,000.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Total Costs = £20,000 + £3,000 = £23,000</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Profit = £35,000 − £23,000 = <strong>£12,000 profit</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Loss example:</strong> A business has fixed costs of £26,000. Variable costs are £10 per unit (wages), £12 per unit (raw materials) and £3 per unit (delivery). It sells 750 units at £26 each.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n36">Variable Costs = 750 × (£10 + £12 + £3) = 750 × £25 = £18,750</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37">Total Costs = £26,000 + £18,750 = £44,750</p>
<p data-narration-id="n38">Revenue = 750 × £26 = £19,500</p>
<p data-narration-id="n39">Profit = £19,500 − £44,750 = <strong>−£25,250 (a loss)</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n40" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain how a business making a loss might continue to trade.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Making a loss does not automatically mean a business will close down. Many businesses make a loss in their early years and continue to trade. They may cut costs or increase revenue the following year to turn the loss into a profit.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n41">How Businesses Use Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n42">Once a business has calculated its profit, the owner must decide how to spend it. A new business like Ezra’s barber shop might reinvest profit into redecorating the premises to attract more customers. After the second year, with the threat of new competitors, Ezra might spend profits on marketing or a loyalty scheme to keep existing customers coming back.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n43">Public limited companies (plcs) and limited companies (ltds) must publish their accounts every year — this is UK law. The accounts show how much profit or loss has been made, giving investors and stakeholders a clear picture of the business’s financial health.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n45">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n46">Revenue is the total income from sales (Price × Quantity), while profit is what remains after all costs are deducted (Total Revenue − Total Costs).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n47">Fixed costs stay the same regardless of output (rent, insurance), while variable costs change with the level of production (raw materials, packaging).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n48">A loss occurs when total costs exceed total revenue, but this does not always mean the business will close — it may recover by cutting costs or increasing sales.</li></ul>

Write out the formula first, then substitute in the numbers, then calculate. For “Discuss” questions about profit and loss, always consider both sides: what happens if revenue increases versus what happens if costs rise. Use real examples from the case study to support your points.</p>
