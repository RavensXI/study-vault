<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Limited and Unlimited Liability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before choosing a type of business ownership, every entrepreneur needs to understand <dfn class="term" data-def="The legal responsibility for paying the debts of a business.">liability</dfn>. Liability means who is responsible for paying debts if a business owes money. There are two types: limited and unlimited.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: what assets are at risk under unlimited liability?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Unlimited liability</strong> means the business and the owner are one legal entity. If the business cannot pay its debts, the owner must pay from their own personal money, savings, and possessions — including their house and car. <strong>Limited liability</strong> means the owner can only lose the money they invested in the business. Their personal belongings are protected.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">For example, imagine a business owes £10,000 but only has £2,000 in the bank. With <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where the business owner is personally responsible for all business debts, meaning personal assets such as a house or car could be seized.">unlimited liability</dfn>, the owner could be chased for the remaining £8,000 from their personal funds. With <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where business owners can only lose the amount they invested in the business, protecting their personal assets.">limited liability</dfn>, the owner usually only loses what they put into the business.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Businesses are classified as either <strong>unincorporated</strong> (sole traders and partnerships — unlimited liability) or <strong>incorporated</strong> (private limited companies — limited liability). An incorporated business is a separate legal entity from its owners.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Sole Traders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A business owned and run by one person who has full control over all decisions and keeps all the profits.">sole trader</dfn> (also called a sole proprietor) is a person who starts, owns, and runs a business on their own. They are the only owner, although they can hire staff to work for them. These staff have no ownership or control over the business. A sole trader does not issue shares.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Common examples of sole trader businesses include plumbers, electricians, hairdressers, cleaners, and website designers. Many small, local businesses operate as sole traders.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Advantages:</strong> The owner can make decisions quickly without needing agreement from others. All profits are kept by the owner and do not need to be shared. The owner can offer personal attention to customers. There is no requirement to make company information public. The owner is their own boss.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> It is difficult to raise money — banks may see a sole trader as a higher risk. There are no economies of scale, for example buying in bulk. There is no one to take over during ill health or holidays. The owner has no one to share ideas or problems with. They rely on only one set of skills. Most importantly, sole traders have <strong>unlimited liability</strong>.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Partnerships</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of business owned by two or more people who share responsibility, decision-making, and profits.">partnership</dfn> is a business with two or more owners who are joint owners. Partners share decision-making, although they may also employ a manager to help run the business. Like sole traders, partnerships have <strong>unlimited liability</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Common partnership businesses include dentists, accountants, solicitors, and vets — professions where partners need to trust each other and bring specialist skills. Profits are distributed based on the percentage each partner has invested. For example, if one partner invested 30% of the total capital, they receive 30% of the profits.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

<span>Advantages and Disadvantages of Partnerships</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Advantages:</strong> Easier to raise capital than a sole trader because more partners can invest. Partners share problems and decisions, reducing the burden on any one person. Partners bring a range of skills — for example, a dental practice might have a dentist, a hygienist, and an orthodontist. Banks may be more willing to lend to a partnership because there are more people to repay the debt. No need to make financial information public.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Partnerships have unlimited liability, meaning personal assets are at risk. Trust and reliability between partners can be an issue. Disagreements between partners can make business decisions difficult. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A legal document that sets out the rules of a partnership, including how profits are shared, roles, and what happens if a partner leaves.">deed of partnership</dfn> must be produced, which is expensive and adds to start-up costs. If one partner wants to leave, it can force changes or even end the partnership.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Private Limited Companies (Ltd)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A small to medium-sized business that has limited liability and can raise finance by selling shares privately to friends and family. Known as a Ltd.">private limited company</dfn> (Ltd) is a business that can raise finance by selling shares, but only privately to friends and family — not to the general public. The business has <strong>limited liability</strong>, which means owners’ personal assets are protected if the business gets into debt.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Private limited companies are often family businesses that started as sole traders and grew. Once they become a Ltd, they can sell shares to raise further capital for expansion. Examples include many small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) such as builders, roofers, electricians, and garden services.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what's one advantage and one disadvantage of a 'Ltd' regarding finances?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Private limited companies benefit from <strong>limited liability</strong> — investors can only lose the amount they invested, not their personal possessions. Banks are also more willing to lend to a Ltd than to a sole trader or partnership. However, the business must prepare annual accounts and make them available to the public, which is expensive and lets competitors see financial information.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Advantages and Disadvantages of Private Limited Companies</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Advantages:</strong> Protection of limited liability for investors. Easier to raise capital by issuing more shares to friends and family. Banks are more willing to lend money. Easier to grow and expand because there is more capital available.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> The business must issue more information about itself, which can be expensive to produce. Annual accounts must be prepared, printed, and sent to all shareholders. These accounts must also be made available for the general public and competitors to see. Selling shares means control can be diluted — majority shareholders may have a say in major decisions.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Franchises</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A business model where a franchisee buys the right to trade under an established brand name, using the franchisor’s products, systems, and logo in return for fees.">franchise</dfn> is when an established business (the <dfn class="term" data-def="The person or business that owns the brand and sells the right to trade under its name to franchisees.">franchisor</dfn>) sells the right to another person (the <dfn class="term" data-def="A person or business that buys a franchise and operates under the franchisor’s brand name and systems.">franchisee</dfn>) to trade using its brand name, logo, products, and business systems. The franchisee pays an initial fee plus ongoing <dfn class="term" data-def="A regular payment made by the franchisee to the franchisor, usually calculated as a percentage of sales revenue.">royalty</dfn> payments based on their sales revenue.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">For example, a Subway franchise in the UK costs around £13,000 as an initial franchise fee, with a total investment starting at approximately £150,000. On top of this, Subway charges around 8% royalty fees plus 4.5% advertising fees — totalling 12.5% of gross sales.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Close this and name 3 things a franchisee receives from the 'business in a box'.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A franchise is often described as a “business in a box” because the franchisee gets an established brand, training, equipment, marketing support, and proven systems. However, they must follow the franchisor’s rules on products, branding, layout, and suppliers.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Advantages and Disadvantages of Franchising</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Advantages for the franchisee:</strong> The business benefits from national or worldwide advertising paid for by the franchisor. Market research is done at head office level. There is less competition because territories are divided up between franchisees. The brand name and reputation are already well known, making it easier to attract customers. Training and support from the franchisor reduces risk compared to starting from scratch.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Disadvantages for the franchisee:</strong> The owner has less control because they must follow the franchisor’s rules on promotions, products, and operations. Ongoing royalty fees reduce profit. The initial franchise fee and set-up costs can be very high. The franchisee cannot easily differentiate their business from others in the franchise.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n28">Choosing the Right Type of Ownership</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The best type of business ownership depends on several factors: how much risk the entrepreneur is willing to take, how much capital they need, whether they want full control or are willing to share decision-making, and how much they want to protect their personal assets. A sole trader keeps all profits but faces unlimited liability. A partnership allows shared skills and finance but can lead to disagreements. A private limited company offers limited liability but requires more paperwork and public disclosure. A franchise provides an established brand but limits independence and requires ongoing fees.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n32">Unlimited liability (sole traders and partnerships) means personal assets are at risk if the business cannot pay its debts, while limited liability (private limited companies) protects owners’ personal belongings.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Each type of ownership has trade-offs — sole traders have full control but limited finance, partnerships share skills but risk disagreements, and Ltds raise more capital but face more paperwork and diluted control.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Franchises offer a proven brand and support system but reduce the owner’s independence and require ongoing royalty payments to the franchisor.</li></ul>
