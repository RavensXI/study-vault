--- DESCRIPTION ---
How interest is calculated on loans and how businesses use cash flow forecasts to stay solvent.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Interest?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a business does not have enough money to fund its plans, it <dfn class="term" data-def="Money provided to a business from a bank or lender that must be repaid over time, usually with interest.">borrows</dfn> money — usually from a bank. The business must pay back the original amount it borrowed plus an extra charge called <dfn class="term" data-def="The charge for borrowing money, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed.">interest</dfn>. Interest is written as a percentage (%) of the amount borrowed, and it is how banks and lenders make their money.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this box and name FIVE types of borrowing where interest is typically charged.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Interest can be applied to many types of borrowing: bank loans, <dfn class="term" data-def="A loan used specifically to buy property, repaid over a long period (often 25 years) with interest.">mortgages</dfn>, credit cards, store cards, car leasing, and buy-now-pay-later schemes. Any time money is borrowed, the lender charges interest as the cost of lending.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Calculating Interest on a Loan</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In the Edexcel exam, you need to be able to calculate the interest rate on a loan. The formula is:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write the EXACT formula for calculating percentage interest.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Interest (%) = (Total Repayment − Borrowed Amount) ÷ Borrowed Amount × 100</strong></p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Here is a worked example. A business borrows £10,000 over 10 years and pays £179.94 per month. First, calculate the total repayment: £179.94 × 120 months = £21,592.80. Then apply the formula: (£21,592.80 − £10,000) ÷ £10,000 × 100 = 115.93%. This means the business pays back more than double what it originally borrowed.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>More Worked Examples</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Example 1:</strong> John borrows £10,000 and pays £300 per month for 3 years. Total repayment = £300 × 36 = £10,800. Interest = (£10,800 − £10,000) ÷ £10,000 × 100 = <strong>8%</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Example 2:</strong> Kim borrows £25,000 and pays £500 per month for 6 years. Total repayment = £500 × 72 = £36,000. Interest = (£36,000 − £25,000) ÷ £25,000 × 100 = <strong>44%</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Notice that longer repayment periods lead to higher total interest, even if the monthly payment seems affordable.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Impact of Rising Interest Rates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Most businesses need to borrow money at some point — to start up, buy equipment, or fund expansion. When interest rates rise, the cost of repaying loans increases. Loan repayments are a <dfn class="term" data-def="Costs that remain the same regardless of how much a business produces, such as rent, salaries, and loan repayments.">fixed cost</dfn>, so higher interest rates push up a business’s total costs and reduce its <dfn class="term" data-def="The money left over after all costs have been deducted from revenue. Profit = Total Revenue minus Total Costs.">profit</dfn>. Businesses can try to plan for this, but unexpected rate rises can cause serious problems.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why Is Cash Important to a Business?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Money available in the business to pay its short-term running costs and bills on a day-to-day basis.">Cash</dfn> is the money a business has available to pay its bills right now. The exam board highlights four reasons cash is essential:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="From memory, list FOUR reasons why a business needs cash; detail each one.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Cash is needed to: (1) <strong>pay suppliers</strong> — without payment, suppliers stop delivering raw materials; (2) <strong>pay overheads</strong> — rent, utilities, and insurance must be paid or services get cut off; (3) <strong>pay employees</strong> — unpaid staff will leave, increasing labour turnover; (4) <strong>prevent insolvency</strong> — a business that cannot pay its debts when they are due is <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business is unable to pay its debts as they fall due. An insolvent business may be forced to close.">insolvent</dfn> and may be forced to close.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Cash vs Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Cash and profit are not the same thing. Profit is what remains after total costs are deducted from total revenue (Profit = TR − TC). Cash is the money actually available in the bank right now. A business can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash. This happens because customers do not always pay immediately — they may receive an invoice and pay 30, 60, or even 90 days later. During that wait, the business still has bills to pay.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Cash Flow Forecasts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A table showing the expected cash inflows and outflows of a business over a future period, usually 12 months.">cash flow forecast</dfn> is a table that predicts the movement of cash into and out of a business over a future period, usually a year. It is included in a business plan and helps the business spot months where it might face a shortfall.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A cash flow forecast has five key components:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Five Components of a Cash Flow Forecast</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>1. <dfn class="term" data-def="Money coming into a business, mainly from sales revenue but also from loans, owner's capital, or grants.">Cash inflows</dfn></strong> — money coming in, mainly from sales revenue. Other inflows include bank loans, owner’s capital, and government grants.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>2. <dfn class="term" data-def="Money leaving a business, including payments for stock, wages, rent, utilities, advertising, and loan repayments.">Cash outflows</dfn></strong> — money leaving the business, such as stock purchases, wages, rent, utilities, advertising, and interest payments on loans.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>3. <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between cash inflows and cash outflows in a given period. Net Cash Flow = Cash Inflows minus Cash Outflows.">Net cash flow</dfn></strong> — the difference between inflows and outflows. Net Cash Flow = Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows. A negative figure means more cash went out than came in.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>4. <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of cash a business has at the start of a given period, carried forward from the previous period's closing balance.">Opening balance</dfn></strong> — the cash the business has at the start of the period, carried forward from the previous month’s closing balance.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>5. <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of cash a business has at the end of a given period. Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Net Cash Flow.">Closing balance</dfn></strong> — the cash at the end of the period. Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Net Cash Flow. If this is negative, the business needs additional finance such as an overdraft.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write down the formulas for Net Cash Flow AND Closing Balance.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Key formulas:</strong> Net Cash Flow = Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows. Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Net Cash Flow. In the exam, when calculating net cash flow, ignore opening and closing balances.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Interpreting a Cash Flow Forecast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Interpreting a cash flow forecast means reading the numbers and explaining what they tell you about the business. Look for months with negative net cash flow or a negative closing balance — these are danger points where the business may need to borrow money. A seasonal business like a fancy dress shop will see high inflows around Halloween and New Year, but quiet months in between where outflows exceed inflows.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How Can a Business Improve Its Cash Flow?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n29">If a business has a cash shortfall, it can take action on both sides of the forecast:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Increase inflows:</strong> arrange an <dfn class="term" data-def="A facility from the bank allowing a business to withdraw more money than it has in its account, for a short period and at a cost.">overdraft</dfn> for short-term cover, offer discounts and promotions to boost sales, or reduce the credit period given to customers so they pay faster.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Reduce outflows:</strong> cut unnecessary costs, reduce waste, switch to cheaper suppliers, or negotiate trade credit with suppliers so the business has longer to pay its own bills.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n34">Interest is the charge for borrowing money, calculated as: (Total Repayment − Borrowed Amount) ÷ Borrowed Amount × 100. Rising interest rates increase a business’s fixed costs and reduce profit.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">Cash is money available to pay bills now — it is not the same as profit. A profitable business can still fail if it runs out of cash (insolvency).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">A cash flow forecast predicts inflows and outflows over time. Businesses use it to spot shortfalls and plan solutions such as overdrafts, cost cutting, or faster customer payments.</li></ul>
