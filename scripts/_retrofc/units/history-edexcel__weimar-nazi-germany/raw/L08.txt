--- DESCRIPTION ---
How Hitler dismantled democracy in just 18 months, from the Reichstag Fire to becoming Führer.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Reichstag Fire — 27 February 1933</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">On the night of 27 February 1933, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The building in Berlin that housed the German parliament. It was set on fire on 27 February 1933, giving Hitler a pretext to suspend civil liberties.">Reichstag</dfn> building was set ablaze. A young Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was found inside and arrested. Hitler immediately declared that the fire was evidence of a Communist plot to overthrow the government. He persuaded President Hindenburg to sign the <strong>Decree for the Protection of the People and the State</strong> (the Reichstag Fire Decree), which suspended key civil liberties — freedom of speech, freedom of the press, and the right to hold political meetings.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain what the Reichstag Fire Decree did and why it was crucial for Hitler's consolidation of power.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Reichstag Fire Decree allowed the Nazis to arrest <strong>4,000 Communist leaders</strong> in the days before the March 1933 election. With Communist opponents imprisoned and their meetings banned, the Nazis had a major advantage at the polls.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Historians still debate whether van der Lubbe acted alone or whether the Nazis started the fire themselves to create an excuse. Either way, Hitler exploited the crisis brilliantly. In the <strong>March 1933 election</strong>, the Nazis won 288 seats (43.9%) — still not a majority, but with the support of the nationalist DNVP, Hitler had enough votes to push through his next move.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Enabling Act — 23 March 1933</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A law passed on 23 March 1933 that gave Hitler the power to make laws without the approval of the Reichstag for four years, effectively ending democracy.">Enabling Act</dfn> was the single most important step in Hitler’s destruction of democracy. It gave the Chancellor the power to pass laws <strong>without the Reichstag’s approval</strong> for four years. To pass the Act, Hitler needed a two-thirds majority in the Reichstag. He achieved this by:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n7">Banning the 81 Communist deputies from attending (already arrested under the Reichstag Fire Decree).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n8">Using the SA to intimidate remaining opponents — Brownshirts surrounded the opera house where the vote took place.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n9">Promising the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Catholic Centre Party, a moderate political party that agreed to support the Enabling Act after Hitler promised to protect Catholic rights.">Centre Party</dfn> that Catholic rights would be protected (a promise he later broke).</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Only the <strong>Social Democrats (SPD)</strong> voted against it. Their leader, Otto Wels, made a courageous speech defending democracy, but it was too late. The Act passed 444 votes to 94.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write out the four key events of early 1933 in order, with one sentence on each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Enabling Act effectively made Hitler a <strong>legal dictator</strong>. He could now pass any law he wanted without parliamentary debate, without Hindenburg’s approval, and even laws that contradicted the Weimar Constitution.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Banning Other Parties and Trade Unions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">With the Enabling Act in place, Hitler moved swiftly to eliminate all remaining opposition. In May 1933, <dfn class="term" data-def="Organisations formed by workers to negotiate better pay and conditions. The Nazis banned all trade unions on 2 May 1933 and replaced them with the German Labour Front.">trade unions</dfn> were banned and their leaders arrested. Their offices were raided by the SA, and all workers were forced to join the new <strong>German Labour Front (DAF)</strong>, controlled by the Nazis. This destroyed workers’ ability to strike or negotiate independently.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In July 1933, the <strong>Law Against the Formation of New Parties</strong> was passed, making the Nazi Party the only legal political party in Germany. The SPD was banned, and the remaining parties dissolved themselves under pressure. Germany was now a <strong>one-party state</strong>.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15">By mid-1933, opposition was extremely dangerous. The SA and the new secret police (Gestapo) arrested anyone who spoke out. Communist and SPD leaders were imprisoned in the first <strong>concentration camps</strong> at Dachau and elsewhere. The Centre Party dissolved itself after reaching a deal with Hitler (the Concordat with the Pope). Many Germans were willing to accept the end of democracy in exchange for the promise of stability and economic recovery after the chaos of the Weimar years.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Night of the Long Knives — 30 June 1934</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By 1934, the biggest threat to Hitler came not from the opposition, but from within the Nazi Party itself. <dfn class="term" data-def="Ernst Röhm, the leader of the SA (Brownshirts), who wanted a ‘second revolution’ that would give the SA control of the army. He was murdered on Hitler’s orders during the Night of the Long Knives.">Ernst Röhm</dfn>, the leader of the SA, commanded over 3 million Brownshirts and wanted a “second revolution” — he believed the SA should absorb the regular army and that the Nazi revolution should go further, redistributing wealth from the rich. This alarmed Hitler for several reasons:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n18">The <strong>German army</strong> would never accept being merged with the undisciplined SA. Hitler needed the army’s support.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Industrialists</strong> who funded the Nazis feared Röhm’s socialist ideas.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Himmler and Göring</strong> were jealous of Röhm’s power and fed Hitler intelligence (some possibly fabricated) that Röhm was planning a coup.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n21">On the night of 30 June 1934, the SS arrested and executed Röhm and at least 85 other SA leaders. The purge also targeted non-Nazis whom Hitler wanted removed, including former Chancellor von Schleicher (shot dead along with his wife). The event became known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The purge of 30 June 1934 in which Hitler ordered the SS to murder the SA leadership and other political opponents. At least 85 people were killed.">Night of the Long Knives</dfn>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Consequences of the Night of the Long Knives</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n22">The purge had three major consequences. First, it removed the SA as a rival power base — the SA was reduced to a minor organisation, and the <strong>SS</strong> under Heinrich Himmler became the dominant force. Second, it won over the <strong>army</strong>, which was relieved that the SA had been neutralised. Third, it showed that Hitler was willing to use <strong>murder</strong> to maintain power, sending a terrifying message to anyone who might oppose him. A new law retrospectively legalised the killings as “emergency measures.”</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Death of Hindenburg — Hitler Becomes Führer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">On <strong>2 August 1934</strong>, President Hindenburg died at the age of 86. Within hours, Hitler merged the offices of President and Chancellor, declaring himself <strong>Führer</strong> (leader) of Germany. There would be no new presidential election. The German army immediately swore a personal <dfn class="term" data-def="A solemn promise. On 2 August 1934, every German soldier swore a personal oath of loyalty to Adolf Hitler, not to Germany or the constitution.">oath of allegiance</dfn> — not to Germany, not to the constitution, but to <strong>Adolf Hitler personally</strong>. This bound every soldier to obey Hitler, regardless of orders.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain what the personal oath of allegiance was and why it made the army loyal to Hitler.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>By August 1934, Hitler had complete control of Germany. He was head of state, head of government, head of the armed forces, and leader of the only legal political party. The Weimar Republic was dead.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n29">The Reichstag Fire gave Hitler the excuse to suspend civil liberties and arrest Communist opponents, while the Enabling Act made him a legal dictator who could pass laws without parliament.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">The Night of the Long Knives removed the SA as a rival, won the army’s loyalty, and showed Hitler would use murder to maintain power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">By August 1934 — just 18 months after becoming Chancellor — Hitler had combined the offices of President and Chancellor, banned all other parties, and secured a personal oath of allegiance from the army.</li></ul>
