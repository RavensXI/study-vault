--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Treaty of Versailles (1919)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In June 1919, Germany was forced to sign the <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace treaty imposed on Germany after the First World War, signed on 28 June 1919 at the Palace of Versailles.">Treaty of Versailles</dfn>. The German government had no choice — the Allies threatened to resume the war if Germany refused. The treaty was a <dfn class="term" data-def="A forced agreement imposed on the losing side of a conflict, with no room for negotiation.">diktat</dfn> (dictated peace) because Germany was not allowed to negotiate the terms. Germans across the political spectrum were outraged.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write down the four main terms of the Treaty of Versailles with specific details for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The key terms of the Treaty of Versailles can be remembered as <strong>LAMB</strong>: <strong>L</strong>and — Germany lost 13% of its territory including Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor, and all overseas colonies. <strong>A</strong>rmy — limited to 100,000 men, no air force, only 6 battleships, no submarines, no tanks. <strong>M</strong>oney — reparations set at £6.6 billion in 1921. <strong>B</strong>lame — Article 231 (the War Guilt Clause) forced Germany to accept full responsibility for starting the war.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Territorial Losses in Detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n4">Germany lost approximately 13% of its European territory and 10% of its population. Key losses included: <strong>Alsace-Lorraine</strong> returned to France; the <strong>Polish Corridor</strong> and Upper Silesia given to Poland, splitting East Prussia from the rest of Germany; the <strong>Saar</strong> coalfields placed under League of Nations control for 15 years; <strong>Danzig</strong> made a free city; the <strong>Rhineland</strong> demilitarised (no German troops allowed). All overseas colonies were taken away and given to the Allies as “mandates.” These losses stripped Germany of valuable farmland, industrial resources, and a significant chunk of its population.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Why Was the Treaty So Unpopular?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Germans were furious for several reasons. They felt the treaty was unjust because Germany had not been allowed to negotiate. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Clause 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to accept full responsibility for causing the First World War.">War Guilt Clause</dfn> (Article 231) was particularly hated — most Germans did not believe they alone had started the war. The massive reparations bill threatened to cripple the economy. The military restrictions humiliated a proud nation. And the loss of territory separated millions of German-speaking people from their homeland.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The politicians who signed the treaty became known as the “November Criminals” — they were blamed for surrendering when many Germans believed the army had never actually been defeated on the battlefield.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The “Stab in the Back” Theory</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The myth, promoted by right-wing nationalists, that the German army was not defeated in battle but was 'stabbed in the back' by politicians, socialists, and Jews who signed the armistice.">Dolchstoßlegende</dfn> (“stab in the back” myth) claimed that the German army had not been defeated in the field but had been betrayed by politicians, socialists, and Jews on the home front. This was a lie — Germany’s military position was hopeless by November 1918 — but it was widely believed, especially by right-wing nationalists. The myth was devastating for the Weimar Republic because it linked the new democratic government with betrayal and humiliation. Hitler later used it extensively in Nazi propaganda.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain what the stab in the back myth was, why it was wrong, and how it damaged the Weimar Republic.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The “stab in the back” theory was a myth, but it fatally damaged the Weimar Republic by associating the new democratic government with defeat and betrayal. Right-wing groups used it to attack the Republic throughout the 1920s.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Spartacist Uprising (January 1919)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Not everyone wanted a democratic republic. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A far-left revolutionary group led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht who wanted a communist revolution in Germany, inspired by the Russian Bolsheviks.">Spartacists</dfn> were a communist group led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht. They wanted a workers’ revolution like the one in Russia. In January 1919, they launched an uprising in Berlin, seizing key buildings including newspaper offices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">President Ebert used the <dfn class="term" data-def="Volunteer paramilitary groups made up of ex-soldiers who were used by the Weimar government to crush left-wing uprisings.">Freikorps</dfn> — right-wing ex-soldiers — to crush the revolt. The Freikorps brutally suppressed the Spartacists within days. Luxemburg and Liebknecht were captured and murdered on 15 January 1919. The uprising failed, but Ebert’s reliance on the Freikorps showed the government’s weakness — it could not even use its own army to maintain order.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Who Were the Freikorps?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Freikorps were groups of demobilised soldiers who refused to give up their weapons after the war. They were fiercely nationalist, anti-communist, and often violent. The Weimar government used them because the regular army (limited to 100,000 by the Treaty of Versailles) was too small to deal with uprisings. This was a dangerous bargain — the Freikorps despised the Republic almost as much as they despised communism.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Kapp Putsch (March 1920)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The threat came from the political right too. In March 1920, Dr Wolfgang <dfn class="term" data-def="A right-wing journalist who led a failed attempt to overthrow the Weimar government in March 1920, seizing control of Berlin with Freikorps support.">Kapp</dfn> and a group of Freikorps soldiers marched into Berlin and seized control of the government. The regular army refused to fire on the Freikorps — “troops do not fire on troops,” declared General von Seeckt. The government fled to Stuttgart.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The putsch was defeated not by the army but by the workers of Berlin, who launched a <dfn class="term" data-def="A widespread refusal to work, used as a form of protest or political pressure.">general strike</dfn>. With no electricity, water, gas, or transport, Kapp could not govern. The putsch collapsed after just four days. However, the episode revealed two alarming facts: the army could not be relied upon to defend the Republic, and right-wing extremism was a serious threat.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Without looking, compare the Spartacist uprising and Kapp Putsch: who led each, which side, and the outcome.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Spartacist uprising (left-wing, January 1919) was crushed by the Freikorps. The Kapp Putsch (right-wing, March 1920) was defeated by a general strike. Both showed the Weimar Republic was vulnerable to extremists on both sides of the political spectrum.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">The Treaty of Versailles (LAMB: Land, Army, Money, Blame) was hated by Germans and fatally linked the Weimar Republic with humiliation and defeat.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The “stab in the back” myth falsely claimed the army was undefeated, undermining the Republic’s legitimacy from the start.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Violent challenges from the left (Spartacists) and right (Kapp Putsch) showed the Republic was fragile and could not rely on its own army for protection.</li></ul>
