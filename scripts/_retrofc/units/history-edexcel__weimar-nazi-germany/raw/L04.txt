--- DESCRIPTION ---
Economic recovery, international diplomacy, and cultural change during Weimar's 'Golden Years'.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Stresemann and Economic Recovery</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Gustav <dfn class="term" data-def="A leading German politician who served as Chancellor (1923) and then Foreign Minister (1924–29), credited with stabilising the Weimar Republic during the 1920s.">Stresemann</dfn> became Chancellor in August 1923, at the height of the hyperinflation crisis. Although he was Chancellor for only 100 days, he then served as Foreign Minister until his death in 1929. His actions during these years are often credited with saving the Weimar Republic — at least temporarily.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Stresemann’s first priority was to end hyperinflation. He called off passive resistance in the Ruhr (an unpopular decision that angered nationalists) and introduced a new currency, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A temporary new currency introduced in November 1923 to replace the worthless Papiermark and end hyperinflation. It was backed by German land and industrial assets rather than gold.">Rentenmark</dfn>, in November 1923. The Rentenmark was backed by German land and industrial assets rather than gold, and it was issued in strictly limited quantities. Confidence in the currency was restored remarkably quickly.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list Stresemann's economic policies and international achievements separately from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Stresemann introduced the Rentenmark in November 1923 to end hyperinflation. This was followed by the Dawes Plan (1924) and the Young Plan (1929), which restructured reparations and brought American investment flooding into Germany.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Dawes Plan (1924)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In 1924, the <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement in 1924 that restructured Germany's reparations payments, setting them at an affordable level and providing $800 million in American loans to boost the German economy.">Dawes Plan</dfn>, negotiated with the United States, restructured Germany’s reparations. Annual payments were reduced and linked to Germany’s ability to pay. Crucially, American banks loaned $800 million to Germany to help rebuild its economy. French and Belgian troops withdrew from the Ruhr.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">American money poured into Germany. Factories were modernised, new roads and public buildings were constructed, and employment rose. Between 1924 and 1929, Germany received around $3 billion in loans and investment from the United States. The economy appeared to be booming.</p>

<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">In 1929, the <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement in 1929 that further reduced Germany's total reparations bill from £6.6 billion to £2 billion and extended the payment period to 59 years.">Young Plan</dfn> further reduced the total reparations bill from £6.6 billion to approximately £2 billion and extended the payment period to 59 years (until 1988). This eased the burden on Germany, but right-wing nationalists — including Hitler — attacked it, arguing that Germany should not be paying reparations at all.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">International Acceptance: Locarno and the League</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Stresemann’s greatest achievements were in foreign policy. In 1925, Germany signed the <dfn class="term" data-def="Treaties signed in 1925 where Germany voluntarily accepted its western borders with France and Belgium, easing tensions and improving Germany's international standing.">Locarno Pact</dfn>, voluntarily accepting its western borders with France and Belgium as set by the Treaty of Versailles. This was significant because Germany was choosing to cooperate rather than being forced — it marked a shift from resentment to reconciliation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n11">In 1926, Germany was admitted to the <dfn class="term" data-def="An international organisation founded after the First World War to maintain peace through collective security and diplomacy.">League of Nations</dfn>, taking a permanent seat on the Council alongside Britain, France, Italy, and Japan. This was a powerful symbol: Germany was being welcomed back into the international community after the humiliation of Versailles. Stresemann won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926 for his work at Locarno.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="From memory, state the dates of the Locarno Pact and League of Nations entry. Explain what Germany accepted and what it did not.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Locarno Pact (1925) and League of Nations membership (1926) restored Germany’s international standing. However, Germany only accepted its <strong>western</strong> borders — it did not accept its eastern borders with Poland, leaving future conflict possible.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Was It Really a “Golden Age”?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The recovery had serious weaknesses. Germany’s prosperity depended almost entirely on American loans. If the loans were recalled, the economy would collapse — which is exactly what happened after the Wall Street Crash of 1929. Unemployment never fell below 1 million during the “Golden Years.” Farmers suffered from falling food prices throughout the period. Many middle-class Germans had not recovered from the hyperinflation of 1923 and remained bitter.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Strengths and Weaknesses of the Recovery</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Strengths:</strong> Industrial output recovered and surpassed pre-war levels by 1928. Exports rose. New housing was built. Wages increased for many workers. Germany was readmitted to the international community.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Weaknesses:</strong> Dependence on American loans made recovery fragile. Unemployment stayed above 1 million. Farmers struggled with low prices. Extremist parties (Nazis and Communists) still won seats in the Reichstag. The middle classes had not recovered their savings from hyperinflation. 28 coalition governments in 14 years showed ongoing political instability.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Changes in Society and Culture</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The Weimar period saw dramatic social and cultural changes, especially in cities like Berlin. Women gained new freedoms: they could vote, enter professions, and enjoyed greater social independence. Some women cut their hair short, wore more daring fashions, and smoked in public — the image of the “New Woman.” By 1925, 36% of the German workforce was female.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Culturally, Germany became a centre of innovation. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An influential German art and design school (1919–33) that pioneered modernist architecture, combining art, craft, and technology.">Bauhaus</dfn> school, founded by Walter Gropius in 1919, revolutionised architecture and design with its clean, functional style. Expressionist art flourished, with artists like Otto Dix and George Grosz producing powerful (and often controversial) work. The German film industry thrived — Fritz Lang’s <em>Metropolis</em> (1927) became one of the most influential films ever made. Cabaret culture in Berlin pushed social boundaries.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Conservative Opposition to Cultural Change</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">Not everyone welcomed these changes. Many conservative and rural Germans saw the new culture as immoral and decadent. The Nazis later called Weimar art “degenerate” and blamed it on Jewish influence. The gap between progressive urban culture and traditional rural values deepened political divisions. For many older and more conservative Germans, the cultural changes of the 1920s were another reason to distrust the Republic.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Without looking, describe two examples of Weimar cultural change and explain how progressives and conservatives viewed them differently.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The “Golden Years” saw significant cultural innovation (Bauhaus, expressionist art, cinema) and changes in women’s roles, but these changes divided opinion — conservatives saw them as evidence of moral decline, deepening the political divide.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Stresemann stabilised the economy with the Rentenmark, the Dawes Plan, and the Young Plan, and restored Germany’s international standing through the Locarno Pact and League of Nations membership.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The recovery was fragile — it depended on American loans, unemployment stayed high, farmers struggled, and the middle classes had not recovered from hyperinflation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Cultural changes (Bauhaus, expressionism, the “New Woman”) were celebrated by progressives but condemned by conservatives, deepening political divisions that extremists would later exploit.</li></ul>
