--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why France invaded the Ruhr, how hyperinflation destroyed savings, and its impact on German society.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The French Occupation of the Ruhr</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In January 1923, Germany fell behind on its <dfn class="term" data-def="Compensation payments that Germany was forced to pay to the Allies under the Treaty of Versailles, set at £6.6 billion in 1921.">reparations</dfn> payments. France and Belgium responded by sending troops into the <dfn class="term" data-def="Germany's most important industrial region, located in western Germany along the Rhine and Ruhr rivers, rich in coal, iron, and steel.">Ruhr</dfn> — Germany’s industrial heartland — to seize goods, raw materials, and factory output as payment. The French argued they were entitled to take what Germany owed by force.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The German government under Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno called for <dfn class="term" data-def="A policy of non-violent resistance where German workers in the Ruhr refused to work for the French occupiers, supported by government wages.">passive resistance</dfn> — workers in the Ruhr were told to stop working and refuse to cooperate with the French. The government promised to continue paying their wages. This policy was popular because it united Germans against a common enemy, but it was economically catastrophic.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="From memory, write the chain of events that led to hyperinflation in five steps.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>France invaded the Ruhr in January 1923 because Germany missed reparations payments. The German government called for passive resistance, but paying idle workers while receiving no industrial output forced the government to print more and more money — triggering hyperinflation.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">The occupation was humiliating and violent. French troops killed over 130 Germans during the occupation, including workers shot during protests. Around 150,000 Germans were expelled from their homes in the Ruhr. Factories and mines were seized. The loss of the Ruhr’s industrial output was devastating — the region produced about 80% of Germany’s coal, iron, and steel. Without this output, the German economy ground to a halt.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Causes of Hyperinflation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="An extreme and rapid rise in prices where money loses its value almost completely, as happened in Germany in 1923.">Hyperinflation</dfn> did not happen overnight. Its roots lay in the war. Between 1914 and 1918, the German government had borrowed heavily and printed money to fund the war effort rather than raising taxes. By the end of the war, the amount of money in circulation had increased massively but the amount of goods available had not — so prices rose.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The Treaty of Versailles made things worse. Reparations payments drained the economy. When the Ruhr was occupied in 1923 and the government called for passive resistance, it had to pay the wages of millions of idle workers. The only way to do this was to print even more money. The result was a catastrophic spiral: more money in circulation meant each banknote was worth less, so prices rose even further, so the government printed even more money.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Scale of Hyperinflation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The numbers were staggering. In January 1921, a loaf of bread cost 0.63 marks. By January 1923, it cost 250 marks. By September 1923, it cost 1.5 million marks. By November 1923, a single loaf cost an almost incomprehensible 200 billion marks. Workers were paid twice a day and rushed to spend their wages before prices rose again. People carried cash in wheelbarrows and used banknotes as wallpaper because the paper was worth more than the printed value.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down the price of a loaf of bread at three key dates during hyperinflation.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The price of a loaf of bread rose from 0.63 marks in January 1921 to 200 billion marks by November 1923. German currency became effectively worthless within months.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Winners and Losers of Hyperinflation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Hyperinflation did not affect everyone equally. Understanding who gained and who lost is essential for exam questions about the impact on German society.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Who Lost from Hyperinflation?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Savers</strong> were devastated. People who had carefully saved money their entire lives found their savings were now worthless. A life’s savings might not buy a cup of coffee. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The social class of professionals, shopkeepers, and white-collar workers who occupied the economic position between the working class and the wealthy.">middle classes</dfn> (Mittelstand) were hit hardest — they had savings, fixed incomes, and pensions that lost all value. <strong>Pensioners and those on fixed incomes</strong> suffered terribly because their payments stayed the same while prices rocketed. <strong>Wage earners</strong> struggled because, even when wages were adjusted, they could never keep pace with rising prices.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Who Gained from Hyperinflation?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>People with debts</strong> benefited because they could repay loans in worthless currency. A mortgage that would have taken years to pay off could be settled for the price of a loaf of bread. <strong>Businesspeople and industrialists</strong> who owned physical assets (factories, land, goods) saw those assets hold their value while debts melted away. <strong>Foreign visitors</strong> with hard currency could buy German goods and property at rock-bottom prices. Some shrewd speculators made fortunes.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Impact on German Society</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The social consequences of hyperinflation were profound. The middle classes — the backbone of any stable democracy — felt betrayed by the Republic. Many had supported the war, saved responsibly, and played by the rules, only to lose everything. Their bitterness and resentment made them receptive to <dfn class="term" data-def="Extreme political views that reject the existing system entirely, found at both the far left (communism) and far right (fascism/Nazism) of the political spectrum.">extremist</dfn> political movements in the years that followed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Crime and social breakdown increased. Food riots broke out in cities. Theft became common as people struggled to feed their families. There was a widespread feeling that the Weimar government was incompetent and unable to protect its citizens. This loss of faith in democratic government would have lasting consequences.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Explain from memory how hyperinflation in 1923 later helped the Nazis gain support.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Hyperinflation destroyed middle-class savings and faith in the Weimar Republic. The bitterness this created among the Mittelstand was a key reason many later turned to extremist parties, including the Nazis, in the early 1930s.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">France occupied the Ruhr in January 1923 because Germany missed reparations payments. Passive resistance worsened the economic crisis.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Hyperinflation was caused by wartime borrowing, reparations, and the government printing money to pay striking Ruhr workers. Bread rose from 0.63 marks to 200 billion marks.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The middle classes (savers, pensioners, those on fixed incomes) were devastated, while those with debts or physical assets benefited. The crisis destroyed faith in the Weimar Republic.</li></ul>
