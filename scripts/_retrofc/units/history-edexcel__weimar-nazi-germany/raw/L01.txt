<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Germany at the End of the First World War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By autumn 1918, Germany was losing the war. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A naval blockade imposed by Britain that prevented food and supplies reaching Germany, causing severe shortages.">British naval blockade</dfn> had caused widespread food shortages — an estimated 750,000 German civilians died from starvation and related diseases during the war. Soldiers were exhausted after four years of fighting, morale had collapsed, and the German army was in retreat on the Western Front. The entry of the United States into the war in 1917 had tipped the balance decisively against Germany.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">On the home front, conditions were desperate. The winter of 1917–18 became known as the “Turnip Winter” because turnips were one of the few foods still available. Fuel was scarce, and many Germans blamed the Kaiser and the military leadership for continuing a war that could not be won.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the three key problems Germany faced at the end of WWI. Write a chain linking them together.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>By October 1918, Germany faced military defeat, mass starvation from the British blockade, and collapsing morale among both soldiers and civilians. These combined pressures made political change inevitable.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Political Unrest and Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In late October 1918, German sailors at Kiel mutinied, refusing to sail out for a final battle against the British Royal Navy. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A refusal by soldiers or sailors to obey orders, often involving violent rebellion against their commanders.">mutiny</dfn> spread rapidly. Workers and soldiers across Germany formed <dfn class="term" data-def="Councils of workers and soldiers that formed during the German Revolution of 1918, inspired by the Russian soviets.">councils</dfn> (similar to the Russian soviets) and took control of cities including Hamburg, Bremen, and Munich. By early November, a full-scale revolution was under way.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">On 29 October 1918, naval commanders ordered the German fleet to sail from Kiel for a final attack on the British navy. Sailors saw this as a suicide mission and refused to obey. When the authorities arrested around 1,000 sailors, their comrades rose up in protest. Within days, 40,000 sailors had joined the revolt. The mutiny triggered uprisings across Germany as workers and soldiers demanded peace and political change.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Kaiser <dfn class="term" data-def="The German Emperor who ruled Germany from 1888 until his forced abdication on 9 November 1918.">Wilhelm II</dfn> had ruled Germany as an autocratic monarch since 1888. As revolution spread, his position became impossible. On 9 November 1918, Chancellor Max von Baden announced the Kaiser’s abdication without the Kaiser’s permission. Wilhelm fled to the Netherlands, where he lived in exile until his death in 1941. Germany’s monarchy was over.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="From memory, state the date of the Kaiser's abdication and the unusual detail about how it was announced.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918. His abdication was announced by Chancellor Max von Baden before the Kaiser had actually agreed, showing the urgency of the political crisis.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Armistice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">On 11 November 1918, the new government signed an <dfn class="term" data-def="An agreement to stop fighting, effectively ending the First World War on 11 November 1918.">armistice</dfn> with the Allies, bringing the fighting to an end. The armistice required Germany to withdraw its troops from occupied territories, surrender its navy and heavy weapons, and allow Allied forces to occupy the Rhineland. Many Germans felt humiliated by these terms, but the country had no choice — continuing the war was impossible.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The New Republic</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">On 9 November 1918, Friedrich Ebert, leader of the Social Democratic Party (SPD), became Germany’s new leader. In January 1919, elections were held for a <dfn class="term" data-def="A national assembly elected in January 1919 to draw up a new constitution for Germany, meeting in the city of Weimar.">National Assembly</dfn>, which met in the city of Weimar (Berlin was too dangerous due to ongoing street fighting). Ebert was elected as the first President of the new republic. The assembly’s main task was to create a new democratic constitution for Germany.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Weimar, Not Berlin?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">The National Assembly met in Weimar rather than Berlin because the capital was gripped by political violence. Communist revolutionaries (the Spartacists) were fighting government forces in the streets. Weimar was a smaller, safer city with strong cultural associations — it was the home of Goethe and Schiller, great German writers, which gave the new republic a sense of intellectual respectability.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Weimar Constitution: Strengths and Weaknesses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The new constitution was one of the most democratic in the world. All men and women over 20 could vote. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The German parliament, consisting of the Reichstag (elected lower house) and the Reichsrat (upper house representing the states).">Reichstag</dfn> (parliament) was elected using proportional representation, and a Bill of Rights guaranteed freedoms of speech, religion, and equality before the law.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two features of the Weimar Constitution and explain how each was both a strength and a weakness.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Weimar Constitution had key strengths (universal suffrage, Bill of Rights, elected parliament) but also critical weaknesses: proportional representation led to unstable coalition governments, and Article 48 allowed the President to rule by decree in an emergency — a power that could be dangerously abused.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Strengths and Weaknesses of the Constitution</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Strengths:</strong> Universal suffrage for men and women over 20. A Bill of Rights protected individual freedoms. The President was directly elected by the people. The Reichstag had the power to pass laws, and the government was accountable to it. Germany’s 18 regions (Länder) kept local powers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Weaknesses:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="An electoral system where the number of seats a party wins is proportional to the number of votes it receives, often leading to many small parties in parliament.">Proportional representation</dfn> meant small parties won seats easily, making it almost impossible for one party to gain a majority. This led to weak coalition governments that often disagreed and collapsed. <strong>Article 48</strong> allowed the President to suspend the Reichstag and pass laws by decree in an “emergency” — but the constitution did not clearly define what counted as an emergency, leaving this open to abuse.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Germany’s military defeat, the naval blockade, and collapsing morale led to revolution in November 1918, forcing the Kaiser to abdicate and the armistice to be signed.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The Weimar Republic was established in 1919 with a democratic constitution, but proportional representation created weak coalition governments, and Article 48 gave the President dangerous emergency powers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">The republic was born out of defeat and revolution, meaning it faced hostility from both the political left and right from the very beginning.</li></ul>
