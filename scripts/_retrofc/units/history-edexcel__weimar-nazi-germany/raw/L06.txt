--- DESCRIPTION ---
How economic crisis and clever propaganda turned the Nazis from a fringe party into a mass movement.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Wall Street Crash and Its Impact on Germany</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In October 1929, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The collapse of share prices on the New York stock exchange in October 1929, triggering a worldwide economic depression.">Wall Street Crash</dfn> sent shockwaves across the world. Share prices on the New York stock exchange collapsed, and American banks demanded the repayment of their loans to Germany. Under the Dawes Plan, Germany had borrowed heavily from the USA to fund its economic recovery during the 1920s. When this money was recalled, German businesses closed, banks failed, and unemployment soared.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="From memory, state Germany's unemployment figure by January 1932 and explain its political impact.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>By January 1932, unemployment in Germany had reached over <strong>6 million</strong> — roughly one in three of the workforce. Millions more were on short-time working or reduced wages.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Weimar government struggled to respond. Chancellor Brüning cut government spending and raised taxes, which made the depression worse. People lost faith in democracy. Desperate and angry, many Germans turned to <dfn class="term" data-def="Parties that reject the current political system entirely and want to replace it with something radically different.">extremist parties</dfn> that promised radical solutions — the Nazis on the far right and the Communists on the far left.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Growth of the Communist Party</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The German Communist Party (KPD), which wanted to overthrow capitalism and create a workers’ state modelled on the Soviet Union.">Communist Party (KPD)</dfn> grew rapidly during the Depression. By November 1932, the KPD won 100 seats in the Reichstag. The Communists drew support from the unemployed and industrial workers, promising to redistribute wealth and nationalise industry. Their growth terrified Germany’s industrialists, landowners, and middle classes, who feared a Communist revolution like the one in Russia in 1917.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">The stronger the Communists became, the more frightened the middle classes and elites grew. Many wealthy industrialists began funding the Nazi Party because Hitler promised to crush communism. Ordinary middle-class Germans also turned to the Nazis as a barrier against a Communist takeover. Fear of communism was one of the Nazis’ most effective recruitment tools — they positioned themselves as the only party strong enough to stop the “Red threat.”</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Appeal of Hitler and the Nazi Party</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Hitler was a gifted public speaker who could whip up a crowd’s emotions. He offered simple solutions to complex problems, blaming Germany’s suffering on the Treaty of Versailles, the Weimar politicians (the “November Criminals”), and Jewish people. His message appealed to different groups for different reasons:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>The unemployed</strong> were promised jobs through public works and rearmament.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>The middle classes</strong> were offered protection from communism and a return to traditional values.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Farmers</strong> were promised higher food prices and protection from foreign imports.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Industrialists</strong> were promised the destruction of trade unions and Communist organisations.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Nationalists</strong> were promised the reversal of the Treaty of Versailles and the restoration of German greatness.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down what Hitler promised to three different groups in German society.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Nazis’ appeal was deliberately broad. Hitler told each audience what it wanted to hear, offering <strong>something for everyone</strong> while keeping his core message of national renewal vague enough to avoid alienating potential supporters.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Goebbels and Nazi Propaganda</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="Joseph Goebbels, the head of Nazi propaganda from 1930, who used modern media techniques to spread the Nazi message across Germany.">Joseph Goebbels</dfn> became head of Nazi propaganda in 1930. He was a master of mass communication and used every available medium to spread the Nazi message. Goebbels organised rallies, torchlight parades, and mass meetings that created an atmosphere of power and excitement. He pioneered the use of radio, film, and posters, and ensured that Nazi slogans like “Work and Bread” were plastered across Germany.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Hitler himself flew across Germany by aeroplane during election campaigns — the “Hitler over Germany” tour of 1932 saw him visit 20 cities in just seven days. This was revolutionary for the time and helped create the image of a dynamic, modern leader who was everywhere at once.</p>
<div class="collapsible">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Mass rallies</strong> — huge, carefully staged events with banners, music, and marching that made the Nazis look powerful and disciplined. <strong>Posters and leaflets</strong> — simple, bold messages targeting specific groups (workers, farmers, women). <strong>Radio</strong> — Goebbels recognised radio as the most important medium for reaching millions. <strong>Scapegoating</strong> — blaming Jews, Communists, and the Weimar government for Germany’s problems gave people a clear enemy. <strong>Hitler’s image</strong> — carefully crafted as a strong, decisive leader who could save Germany.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The Role of the SA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sturmabteilung (SA), also known as the Brownshirts — the Nazi Party’s paramilitary force used to intimidate opponents and protect Nazi meetings.">SA (Sturmabteilung)</dfn>, or Brownshirts, played a vital role in the Nazis’ rise. By 1932, the SA had over 400,000 members. They disrupted Communist and Social Democrat meetings, beat up political opponents, and protected Nazi rallies. The SA’s street violence gave many Germans the impression that the Weimar Republic was collapsing into chaos — and that only the Nazis could restore order. Ironically, the Nazis were creating much of that chaos themselves.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">The Presidential Elections of 1932</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">In March 1932, Hitler stood against the ageing President Hindenburg in the presidential election. Hindenburg won with 53% of the vote, but Hitler gained an impressive 36.8% — over 13 million votes. This showed how far the Nazis had come from their dismal performance in the 1928 elections, when they won just 2.6% of the vote. Hitler was now a major national figure.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this and state the number of Nazi seats in 1928 and July 1932. Explain what caused this growth.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In the <strong>July 1932 Reichstag election</strong>, the Nazis became the <strong>largest party</strong> with 230 seats (37.3% of the vote). In November 1932, their support dipped slightly to 196 seats (33.1%), but they remained the biggest party in the Reichstag.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n28">The Wall Street Crash of 1929 caused mass unemployment in Germany, destroying faith in the Weimar Republic and driving voters towards extremist parties like the Nazis and Communists.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">Nazi support grew because Hitler offered something to every group, Goebbels ran brilliant propaganda campaigns, and the SA created chaos that made democracy look weak.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">By July 1932, the Nazis were the largest party in the Reichstag with 230 seats — a dramatic rise from just 12 seats in 1928.</li></ul>
