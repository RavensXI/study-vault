<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Kamikaze — Beatrice Garland</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A London-based poet and NHS clinician whose first poetry collection was published in 2013.">Beatrice Garland</dfn> wrote <em>Kamikaze</em> about a Japanese pilot during World War Two who was sent on a suicide mission but turned back. <dfn class="term" data-def="Japanese pilots in World War Two who deliberately crashed their planes into enemy ships as a suicide attack, considered a great honour.">Kamikaze</dfn> pilots were trained to fly their aircraft into enemy ships — it was portrayed as the highest patriotic honour. By the end of the war, nearly 4,000 kamikaze pilots had died.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The poem is narrated by the pilot’s daughter, recounting the story “later to her children.” The pilot himself has no voice in the poem — his silence mirrors the way his family silenced him after his return. Garland uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="The perspective or voice from which a poem or story is told.">narrative voice</dfn> to explore what happens when a soldier chooses life over duty.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what four items did the pilot take on his mission according to the poem's description?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The pilot “embarked at sunrise with a flask of water, a samurai sword in the cockpit, a shaven head full of powerful incantations and enough fuel for a one-way journey into history.” The details — the sword, the shaven head, the “one-way journey” — all emphasise that he was prepared to die. The word “incantations” suggests he has been influenced by patriotic propaganda, almost as if he is under a spell.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Pilot’s Decision</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The daughter imagines that “half way there” her father looked down and saw “the little fishing boats strung out like bunting on a green-blue translucent sea.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech comparing two things using ‘like’ or ‘as’.">simile</dfn> comparing fishing boats to bunting is ironic — bunting suggests celebration, but there will be no victorious return for this pilot. The “green-blue translucent sea” is beautiful and peaceful, contrasting sharply with the violent mission he was on.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Beneath the boats, the pilot sees fish “arcing in swathes like a huge flag waved first one way then the other.” A flag is a powerful symbol of national identity — the very thing the pilot was supposed to die for. But here, nature provides its own ‘flag’ that seems to call him back to life. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Repetition of the ‘s’ sound, often creating a hushed, sinister, or soothing effect.">sibilance</dfn> in “dark shoals of fishes flashing silver as their bellies swivelled towards the sun” creates a smooth, flowing sound that reflects the gentle movements of the fish and the beauty of nature.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The pilot then “remembered” his childhood — building cairns of “pearl-grey pebbles” on the shore with his brothers, watching his grandfather’s fishing boat come safe to land. These innocent memories of family and nature contrast with the violence of his mission. The colours “silver” and “pearl-grey” make nature sound precious and valuable — worth living for.</p>

  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">When the pilot returns, his wife “never spoke again in his presence, nor did she meet his eyes.” The neighbours “treated him as though he no longer existed.” In Japanese culture, failing to complete a kamikaze mission brought immense <dfn class="term" data-def="A painful feeling of humiliation caused by the consciousness of wrong or foolish behaviour, or failure to meet expectations.">shame</dfn> on the whole family. His wife’s silence is her expression of that shame.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">At first, only the children “still chattered and laughed” — they are too innocent to understand the cultural expectations. But “gradually we too learned to be silent, to live as though he had never returned.” The verb ‘learned’ is devastating: the children were taught to reject their own father.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11">The poem ends with a haunting question: “he must have wondered which had been the better way to die.” This suggests the pilot faces a form of living death — shunned by his family and community, he might as well have completed the mission. The patriotism that should have led to his glorious death instead destroyed his family from within.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Emigrée — Carol Rumens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="An English poet, lecturer, and translator whose 1993 collection included The Emigrée.">Carol Rumens</dfn> wrote <em>The Emigrée</em> about a speaker who left a city as a child and clings to an idealised memory of it. The title uses the feminine French form, telling us the speaker is female. The city may represent a real place, or it could symbolise a person, a time, or an emotion that the speaker has been forced to leave behind.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The poem opens: “There once was a country… I left it as a child but my memory of it is sunlight-clear.” The fairy-tale opening (“There once was”) suggests loss, while “sunlight-clear” establishes the poem’s central motif — light. Throughout the poem, the speaker’s memories are always associated with brightness and warmth, even as reality darkens.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close the box and explain: what does the verb 'branded' suggest about the emigrée's memories of sunlight in her homeland?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“It may be at war, it may be sick with tyrants, but I am branded by an impression of sunlight.” The verb ‘branded’ is violent — it suggests the positive memory has been burned into her permanently, like a mark on skin. Nothing can change her childhood view, not even war or oppression.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Key Techniques in The Emigrée</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Rumens uses <dfn class="term" data-def="Giving human qualities or characteristics to something non-human.">personification</dfn> to bring the city to life: “my city comes to me in its own white plane. It lies down in front of me, docile as paper.” The city becomes a living companion that the speaker can love and protect. The adjective ‘docile’ (gentle, easily led) and the simile ‘as paper’ suggest the memory is both fragile and controllable — unlike the real city, which has been changed by conflict.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">“Time rolls its tanks” is a powerful metaphor that personifies time as an invading military force. The “frontiers” that “rise between us, close like waves” combine military and natural imagery to show how the speaker is being cut off from her homeland — by politics, by distance, and by the passage of time.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Threat and Defiance in the Final Stanza</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The final stanza introduces a threatening ‘they’ who “accuse me of absence” and “circle me.” The repetition of ‘they’ creates a menacing, oppressive atmosphere. “They accuse me of being dark in their free city” suggests racism or xenophobia — the speaker is treated as an outsider in her new home.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Yet the poem ends defiantly: “My city hides behind me. They mutter death, and my shadow falls as evidence of sunlight.” Even her shadow — caused by sunlight — is proof that light exists. The speaker refuses to let threats destroy her positive memory. The final word, “sunlight,” echoes throughout the poem, symbolising hope, identity, and the enduring power of memory.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Comparing Kamikaze and The Emigrée</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Both poems explore how memory and identity are shaped by forces beyond the individual’s control. In <em>Kamikaze</em>, the pilot’s beautiful childhood memories pull him away from his mission — but society punishes him for choosing life. In <em>The Emigrée</em>, the speaker’s memories protect and sustain her against hostility in her new home.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The poems present contrasting views of silence. The pilot in <em>Kamikaze</em> is silenced by shame — he has no voice in the poem and his family learns “to be silent.” The speaker in <em>The Emigrée</em> refuses to be silenced — she holds onto her language (“I can’t get it off my tongue. It tastes of sunlight”) and her memory as acts of resistance against those who would erase her identity.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26"><em>Kamikaze</em> explores how a pilot’s decision to reject his suicide mission leads to family shame and social death — Garland uses nature imagery, narrative voice, and silence to convey the devastating consequences of defying duty.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><em>The Emigrée</em> presents memory as a source of power and resistance — Rumens uses light imagery, personification of the city, and a defiant first-person voice to show that identity cannot be erased by threat or distance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Both poems explore the tension between individual identity and external forces (patriotism, political oppression), but the pilot is destroyed by society’s expectations while the emigrée resists them.</li></ul>
