How Armitage and Duffy present the lasting psychological effects of conflict on those who witness it.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Remains — Simon Armitage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A poet from Huddersfield who often writes about ordinary people and modern life, including the effects of war on soldiers.">Simon Armitage</dfn> wrote <em>Remains</em> based on the real testimony of a soldier named Guardsman Tromans, who served in Iraq. The poem describes a moment when three soldiers shoot a looter — and the guilt that haunts the speaker long after.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The poem opens with a casual, almost dismissive tone: “On another occasion, we get sent out to tackle looters raiding a bank.” The conversational voice makes it sound like a soldier retelling a story to a mate. Armitage uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="Informal, everyday language that imitates natural speech patterns.">colloquial</dfn> register deliberately — it mirrors how real soldiers speak and makes the horror feel more authentic.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which exact phrase about being armed is repeated to show the speaker's doubt and guilt?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The phrase “probably armed, possibly not” is repeated later in the poem. This <dfn class="term" data-def="When a word or phrase is used more than once for emphasis or to highlight a key idea.">repetition</dfn> emphasises the speaker’s doubt and guilt — he can never be sure whether the man he killed was actually dangerous.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Key Techniques in Remains</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Armitage uses violent imagery to show the horror of the shooting: “I see every round as it rips through his life.” The verb ‘rips’ has connotations of destruction and suggests the fragility of human life. The metaphor “rips through his life” implies not just physical death but the total erasure of a person.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">After the shooting, a fellow soldier “tosses his guts back into his body” before the looter is “carted off in the back of a lorry.” The cold, unfeeling verbs ‘tosses’ and ‘carted’ show how the other soldiers treat the dead man as inconsequential — but the speaker cannot detach himself in the same way.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The “blood-shadow” that “stays on the street” acts as a visual metaphor. The compound noun fuses blood with shadow, suggesting the memory has become inseparable from the speaker — like a shadow that follows him everywhere, it cannot be washed away.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The poem shifts dramatically when the soldier returns home “on leave.” He tries to forget: “Sleep, and he’s probably armed, possibly not. Dream, and he’s torn apart by a dozen rounds.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="A pause in the middle of a line of poetry, created by punctuation, that disrupts the rhythm and forces the reader to stop.">caesura</dfn> between ‘Sleep’ and ‘Dream’ creates a short, sharp rhythm that mimics the sudden shock of flashbacks — a common symptom of <dfn class="term" data-def="Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, a mental health condition triggered by experiencing or witnessing a terrifying event.">PTSD</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The final lines are devastating: “the drink and the drugs won’t flush him out — he’s here in my head when I close my eyes.” The metaphor of the dead man being “dug in behind enemy lines” turns the speaker’s own mind into a war zone. The closing image — “his bloody life in my bloody hands” — uses the double meaning of ‘bloody’ (literal blood and the colloquial swear word) to express both guilt and frustration.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">War Photographer — Carol Ann Duffy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Scottish poet who served as the UK Poet Laureate from 2009 to 2019, known for giving voices to overlooked perspectives.">Carol Ann Duffy</dfn> wrote <em>War Photographer</em> based on her friendship with real war photographers. The poem follows a photographer developing his pictures in a darkroom, remembering the suffering he witnessed abroad.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The opening stanza establishes a sombre, ritualistic mood: “In his darkroom he is finally alone with spools of suffering set out in ordered rows.” The <dfn class="term" data-def="Repetition of the ‘s’ sound, often creating a hushed, sinister, or soothing effect.">sibilance</dfn> in ‘spools of suffering set’ creates a hushed, almost reverential atmosphere. Duffy compares the photographer to “a priest preparing to intone a Mass” — suggesting his work is sacred and solemn.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the three war zone place names separated by full stops, plus the biblical phrase about grass.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Belfast. Beirut. Phnom Penh. All flesh is grass.” The three place names are separated by full stops (<dfn class="term" data-def="The use of full stops to separate individual words or short phrases within a line, creating pauses and emphasis.">caesura</dfn>), forcing the reader to pause on each war zone. The biblical allusion “All flesh is grass” (Isaiah 40:6) reminds us that human life is brief and fragile.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Key Techniques in War Photographer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Duffy structures the poem in four equal stanzas of six lines with a regular ABBCDD <dfn class="term" data-def="The pattern of rhyming words at the end of lines in a poem.">rhyme scheme</dfn>. This rigid, controlled form contrasts with the chaos of conflict — it reflects both the “ordered rows” of photographs and the photographer’s attempt to impose calm on traumatic memories.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The contrast between home and war zone is central. “Rural England” represents “ordinary pain which simple weather can dispel” — problems in England are trivial compared to “fields which don’t explode beneath the feet of running children in a nightmare heat.” This juxtaposition highlights the photographer’s alienation: he exists between two worlds but belongs to neither.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Public Indifference and the Final Stanza</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Duffy criticises the British public’s shallow response to suffering. The editor will “pick out five or six” images from “a hundred agonies in black-and-white” — reducing vast human suffering to a handful of pictures for a “Sunday’s supplement.” Readers’ “eyeballs prick with tears between the bath and pre-lunch beers” — any sadness is fleeting and squeezed between comfortable routines.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">The poem ends with the photographer on an aeroplane, staring “impassively at where he earns his living and they do not care.” The pronoun ‘they’ is accusatory — it separates the photographer from the public and suggests anger and isolation. The cyclical structure (returning to the war zone) implies nothing changes and the cycle of suffering continues.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Comparing Remains and War Photographer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Both poems explore how conflict traumatises those who witness it, even after they return home. In <em>Remains</em>, the soldier tries “drink and drugs” to forget but fails. In <em>War Photographer</em>, the photographer’s hands “did not tremble then though seem to now” — the trauma surfaces when he is safe, not when he is in danger.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">However, the speakers have different relationships to the violence. The soldier in <em>Remains</em> was a direct participant: he pulled the trigger and feels overwhelming guilt (“his bloody life in my bloody hands”). The photographer was a witness and documenter — his moral conflict comes from photographing suffering rather than helping. Both are powerless against the psychological scars that follow them home.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25"><em>Remains</em> uses colloquial voice, repetition, and caesura to convey a soldier’s guilt and PTSD — the memory of violence becomes inescapable.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><em>War Photographer</em> uses a rigid rhyme scheme, religious imagery, and contrast between home and war zone to show a photographer’s isolation and the public’s indifference to suffering.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Both poems show that the psychological effects of conflict last long after the violence ends, but the soldier’s guilt as a participant differs from the photographer’s moral conflict as a witness.</li></ul>
