--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Five Major Themes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every essay on <em>Frankenstein</em> connects to one or more key themes. The five most commonly tested are: <strong>dangerous knowledge and ambition</strong>, <strong>creation and responsibility</strong>, <strong>monstrosity and prejudice</strong>, <strong>isolation and companionship</strong>, and <strong>nature versus nurture</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list the five themes and one quotation for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Dangerous Knowledge:</strong> "So much has been done — far more will I achieve" (Victor's Promethean ambition). <strong>Creation &amp; Responsibility:</strong> "A new species would bless me as its creator" (Victor wants worship, not responsibility). <strong>Monstrosity:</strong> "I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open" (the pronoun 'it' dehumanises from birth). <strong>Isolation:</strong> "I am alone and miserable" (the Creature's plea for a companion). <strong>Nature vs Nurture:</strong> "From that moment I declared everlasting war against the species" (society creates the monster).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">What the 19th-Century Novel Question Tests</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Your response is assessed on three strands: your critical response to the text with well-chosen references (AO1), your analysis of the writer's methods — language, structure and form (AO2), and your understanding of context (AO3). You will receive a printed extract (~400 words) and must write about the extract AND the novel as a whole. Check your exam board's specimen paper and mark scheme for the exact weightings and timings that apply to your exam.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: for each AO, note one thing your paragraph must do to hit it.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>AO1 — Critical response:</strong> Build a clear argument supported by well-chosen references from the extract AND the whole novel. <strong>AO2 — Writer's methods:</strong> Analyse Shelley's methods — language, structure, narrative voice, symbolism. <strong>AO3 — Context:</strong> Embed context (Enlightenment, Romanticism, Gothic, biography). Never bolt context on as a separate paragraph.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Essay Structure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Use <dfn class="term" data-def="Point, Evidence, Analyse Language, Link to Context, Evaluate — a paragraph structure for literature essays.">PEALCE</dfn>: Point → Evidence → Analyse Language → Link to Context → Evaluate. Balance extract analysis (2–3 paragraphs) with whole-novel references (2–3 paragraphs). The biggest mark differentiator is close analysis of methods — analyse what they do, don't just identify them.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Example Paragraph: The Creation Scene</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Point:</strong> Shelley presents Victor's rejection of the Creature as an act of parental failure. <strong>Evidence:</strong> "I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open; it breathed hard." <strong>Analysis:</strong> The pronoun "it" immediately dehumanises the Creature, denying it personhood. The focus on a single "dull yellow eye" reduces the being to one unsettling detail, reflecting Victor's inability to see beyond surface appearance. The passive phrasing — "it breathed" rather than "he breathed" — distances Victor from responsibility. <strong>Context:</strong> Shelley, whose own mother died in childbirth, was deeply aware that creation carries moral obligations. Victor's rejection of a conscious being echoes the Enlightenment's blind faith in progress without consideration of consequences. <strong>Evaluate:</strong> This moment establishes Shelley's central argument: the true horror is not the Creature's appearance but the creator's refusal to take responsibility.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Prepare quotations for five themes: dangerous knowledge, creation/responsibility, monstrosity/prejudice, isolation/companionship, nature vs nurture. 2–3 quotations per theme.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The 30-mark question needs balanced extract + whole-novel analysis. Embed context; never bolt it on.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Use PEALCE. Focus on AO2: analyse Shelley’s language choices and their effects on the reader.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">The highest marks go to responses that analyse Shelley’s methods, not just themes. Instead of “Shelley shows science is dangerous,” write “Shelley’s use of the dehumanising pronoun ‘it’ reveals Victor’s instant denial of the Creature’s personhood, critiquing the Enlightenment’s pursuit of knowledge without moral responsibility.” Always explain the effect of specific words on the reader.</p>
