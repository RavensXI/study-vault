<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Mary Shelley and the Birth of Frankenstein</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Mary Shelley began writing <em>Frankenstein</em> in the summer of 1816, when she was just eighteen years old. She was staying at Lake Geneva with her future husband, the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley, and their friend Lord Byron. A wet, stormy summer kept the group indoors, and Byron proposed a ghost-story competition. Out of that challenge came one of the most influential novels in English literature. Shelley published <em>Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus</em> anonymously in 1818 — many readers initially assumed a man had written it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Shelley’s life was shaped by loss. Her mother, the feminist writer <dfn class="term" data-def="Mary Wollstonecraft (1759–1797), author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman; she died from complications following Mary Shelley’s birth.">Mary Wollstonecraft</dfn>, died eleven days after giving birth to her. Shelley’s own first child died in infancy. These experiences of birth, death, and the fragility of life permeate the novel. Victor Frankenstein’s attempt to create life — and the catastrophic consequences — reflects Shelley’s deeply personal understanding that the line between creation and destruction is dangerously thin.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three biographical details about Shelley connect to Frankenstein?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shelley’s three biographical links: (1) her mother died days after childbirth, giving Shelley a lifelong awareness of how creation and death are intertwined; (2) her own infant daughter died, intensifying her sensitivity to loss; (3) she was surrounded by radical thinkers (Percy Shelley, Byron, her father William Godwin) who debated science, religion, and the boundaries of human knowledge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Enlightenment and Scientific Progress</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The late 18th and early 19th centuries were a period of rapid scientific discovery. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An intellectual movement of the 17th–18th centuries emphasising reason, science, and individual rights over tradition and superstition.">Enlightenment</dfn> championed human reason as the path to understanding the natural world. Scientists like Luigi Galvani experimented with <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrical experiments on dead tissue; Galvani showed that electrical impulses could make a dead frog’s legs twitch, raising questions about whether electricity could reanimate the dead.">galvanism</dfn> — using electricity to stimulate dead tissue — raising the question of whether science could conquer death itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Shelley was fascinated by these debates. In her 1831 preface, she described conversations between Percy Shelley and Byron about “the nature of the principle of life” and whether a corpse could be “re-animated.” Victor Frankenstein embodies the Enlightenment scientist pushed to dangerous extremes: he believes that reason and knowledge can overcome all limits, including death. Shelley’s novel asks a profoundly modern question: just because science <em>can</em> do something, does that mean it <em>should</em>?</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The novel’s subtitle — <em>The Modern Prometheus</em> — references the Greek myth of <dfn class="term" data-def="A Titan from Greek mythology who stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity; as punishment, Zeus chained him to a rock where an eagle ate his liver daily, only for it to regenerate each night.">Prometheus</dfn>, who stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity. Zeus punished Prometheus with eternal torment. Shelley draws a direct parallel: Victor steals the “secret of life” from nature and is punished with the destruction of everything he loves. The myth warns against overreaching — crossing boundaries that were not meant to be crossed. Victor, like Prometheus, brings forbidden knowledge into the world and suffers catastrophically for it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Romanticism and the Sublime</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A literary and artistic movement of the late 18th and early 19th centuries that valued emotion, nature, imagination, and the individual over reason and social convention.">Romanticism</dfn> was the dominant literary movement when Shelley wrote <em>Frankenstein</em>. Romantic writers like Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, and Percy Shelley celebrated nature, emotion, and the power of the imagination. They also explored the concept of the <dfn class="term" data-def="An overwhelming experience of nature’s power and vastness that inspires both awe and terror; in Frankenstein, the sublime Alps and Arctic represent forces beyond human control.">sublime</dfn> — the overwhelming awe and terror inspired by nature’s power. Shelley uses sublime landscapes throughout the novel: the Alpine mountains, the Arctic ice, and violent storms.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">These landscapes serve as a counterpoint to Victor’s scientific ambition. Nature is vast, beautiful, and utterly indifferent to human plans. Victor repeatedly seeks solace in the Alps after his creations cause disaster, but nature does not provide easy comfort — it reminds him of his own insignificance. Shelley suggests that Victor’s fatal flaw is his refusal to accept natural limits: he tries to conquer death, control creation, and master nature, and nature punishes him for his arrogance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down three ways Shelley uses the Romantic movement’s ideas in Frankenstein.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Shelley uses Romanticism in three key ways: (1) sublime landscapes (Alps, Arctic) represent forces beyond human control; (2) the Creature’s emotional sensitivity and love of nature align him with Romantic ideals more than Victor does; (3) Victor’s scientific rationalism is presented as dangerous precisely because it ignores emotion, empathy, and natural limits — the qualities Romanticism valued most.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Gothic Tradition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><em>Frankenstein</em> is also a <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of fiction featuring dark settings, the supernatural, extreme emotions, and disturbing subject matter; Frankenstein uses Gothic conventions to explore the horror of unnatural creation.">Gothic</dfn> novel. Gothic fiction features dark settings, the supernatural, extreme emotions, transgression, and the blurring of boundaries between life and death. Shelley uses Gothic conventions throughout: the midnight creation scene with its “dull yellow eye” opening, the charnel houses and graveyards where Victor collects body parts, the murders committed in darkness, and the pursuit across desolate Arctic ice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">But Shelley’s Gothic is also intellectual. The real horror in <em>Frankenstein</em> is not the Creature’s appearance but Victor’s moral failure. He creates a being with human consciousness and then abandons it. The Gothic terror comes from the recognition that the true monster might not be the Creature but the creator who refuses to take responsibility for what he has made.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><em>Frankenstein</em> is often called the first science fiction novel. Unlike earlier Gothic works that relied on supernatural explanations (ghosts, curses), Shelley grounds her horror in science: Victor uses galvanism and anatomy to create life. The novel imagines the consequences of a plausible (for the time) scientific breakthrough, making it a forerunner of modern science fiction’s “what if?” approach. Shelley anticipated debates about genetic engineering, artificial intelligence, and bioethics that are still urgent today.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Shelley wrote <em>Frankenstein</em> at 18, drawing on personal experience of loss (her mother’s death, her infant’s death) and the scientific debates of her circle (galvanism, the “principle of life”).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The subtitle “The Modern Prometheus” frames Victor’s story as one of overreaching: stealing forbidden knowledge and suffering catastrophic punishment.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The novel blends Gothic horror, Romantic landscapes, and Enlightenment science to ask a question that remains urgent: just because we can, should we?</li></ul>
