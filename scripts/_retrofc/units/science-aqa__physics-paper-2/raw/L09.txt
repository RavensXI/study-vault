--- DESCRIPTION ---
Calculating moments and momentum, and how levers and gears multiply forces.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Moments and Turning Forces</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The turning effect of a force about a pivot point, calculated as force multiplied by perpendicular distance from the pivot.">moment</dfn> is the turning effect of a force around a fixed point called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The fixed point around which rotation occurs.">pivot</dfn>. The size of the moment depends on the force applied and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$M = F \times d$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">where \(M\) is the moment in newton metres (Nm), \(F\) is the force in newtons (N) and \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from the pivot in metres (m).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why you measure perpendicular distance from pivot to force line, not along the angled object.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The distance must be the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force — not the distance along the object itself if the force is applied at an angle.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n6">If an object is balanced (in equilibrium), the total clockwise moment about any pivot equals the total anticlockwise moment. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="When an object is in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about any pivot.">principle of moments</dfn>.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">Clockwise moment = Anticlockwise moment</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">$$500 \times d = 400 \times 2.0$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">$$d = \frac{800}{500} = 1.6 \text{ m from the pivot}$$</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Levers and Gears</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rigid bar that rotates about a pivot, used to multiply force or increase the distance moved by a load.">lever</dfn> is a simple machine that uses moments to multiply forces. By applying a small force at a large distance from the pivot, a larger force can be produced at a shorter distance from the pivot on the other side. Examples include crowbars, wheelbarrows and scissors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The longer the lever arm (distance from effort to pivot), the less force is needed to produce the same turning moment. This is why a long spanner makes it easier to undo a tight bolt — the larger distance means a smaller force produces the same moment.</p>



<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="Toothed wheels that interlock and transmit rotational forces; a larger gear turns more slowly but with greater force.">Gears</dfn> are toothed wheels that transmit rotational motion and force. When two gears are meshed together, they turn in opposite directions. A large gear driving a small gear increases the speed of rotation but reduces the force. A small gear driving a large gear decreases the speed but increases the force (the turning moment).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and describe how a small gear driving a large gear increases moment using radius and distance from axle.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Gears are force multipliers: a small gear driving a large gear increases the moment because the larger gear has a greater radius, so the force acts at a larger distance from the axle.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Momentum</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The product of an object's mass and velocity; a measure of how difficult it is to stop a moving object.">Momentum</dfn> is a property of all moving objects. It is calculated using:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">$$p = m \times v$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">where \(p\) is momentum in kilogram metres per second (kg m/s), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg) and \(v\) is velocity in metres per second (m/s). Momentum is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Conservation of Momentum</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">In a closed system (where no external forces act), the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after. This is the <dfn class="term" data-def="In a closed system, the total momentum before an event equals the total momentum after the event.">conservation of momentum</dfn>. It applies to all collisions and explosions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">In a collision between two objects: \(m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'\). In an explosion where objects start at rest, the total momentum is zero before and after — so the objects move in opposite directions with equal and opposite momenta.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Force, Momentum and Safety</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The force acting on an object is equal to the rate of change of momentum:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">$$F = \frac{m \Delta v}{\Delta t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">This means that to reduce the force during a collision, you can increase the time over which the momentum changes. Car safety features exploit this principle: crumple zones deform slowly, increasing the collision time and reducing the force on passengers. Seat belts stretch slightly, air bags inflate to cushion impact, and cycle helmets contain foam that compresses on impact — all increasing the time of deceleration.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">A 1200 kg car travelling at 15 m/s collides with a stationary 800 kg car. After the collision, they move off together. Calculate their shared velocity.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">Total momentum before = \(1200 \times 15 + 800 \times 0 = 18{,}000\) kg m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Total momentum after = \((1200 + 800) \times v = 2000v\)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29">$$v = \frac{18{,}000}{2000} = 9 \text{ m/s}$$</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">The moment of a force is \(M = F \times d\); for equilibrium, total clockwise moments equal total anticlockwise moments (principle of moments).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Levers and gears are force multipliers that use moments to increase the output force from a smaller input force.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Momentum (\(p = mv\)) is conserved in a closed system; car safety features increase collision time to reduce force on passengers.</li>
</ul>

For moment questions, draw a diagram showing forces and distances from the pivot — this helps avoid errors with perpendicular distances.</p>
