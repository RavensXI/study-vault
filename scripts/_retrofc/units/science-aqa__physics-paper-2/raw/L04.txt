Newton's three laws of motion, inertia, F = ma calculations, and terminal velocity.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Newton’s First Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity, unless acted on by a resultant force.">Newton’s first law</dfn> states that an object will remain at rest or continue to move at a constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">This means that if all forces on an object are balanced (resultant force = 0), the object will not change its motion. A stationary object stays still. A moving object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why a heavy object is harder to push into motion than a light one using the term inertia."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><dfn class="term" data-def="The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. Objects with more mass have more inertia.">Inertia</dfn> is the tendency of an object to resist a change in motion. The more mass an object has, the greater its inertia. This is why a heavy object is harder to push into motion or bring to a stop than a light one.</p>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Newton’s Second Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force and inversely proportional to its mass: F = ma.">Newton’s second law</dfn> states that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">$$F = m a$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Where \(F\) is the resultant force in newtons (N), \(m\) is mass in kilograms (kg), and \(a\) is acceleration in m/s\(^2\).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">This tells us three things: a larger force produces a greater acceleration; a larger mass requires a greater force for the same acceleration; and if the resultant force is zero, there is no acceleration.</p>

<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10">A 1200 kg car has a driving force of 4800 N and friction of 1200 N. Calculate the acceleration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Step 1: Resultant force = 4800 − 1200 = 3600 N</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Step 2: \(a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{3600}{1200} = 3\) m/s\(^2\)</p>
</div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Newton’s Third Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="When two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other.">Newton’s third law</dfn> states that whenever two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces on each other. These forces act on different objects and are always the same type of force.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the four key features of Newton's third law force pairs, including their directions and magnitudes."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Newton’s third law pairs always: act on two different objects, are the same size, act in opposite directions, and are the same type of force. For example, the Earth pulls you down with gravity, and you pull the Earth up with the same gravitational force.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Common Misconceptions About Newton’s Third Law</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">Students often confuse balanced forces with Newton’s third law pairs. Balanced forces act on the <strong>same</strong> object (e.g. weight and normal force on a book). Newton’s third law pairs act on <strong>different</strong> objects (e.g. the book pushes the table down, the table pushes the book up).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Another misconception: “if the forces are equal and opposite, the object can’t move.” This is wrong because the two forces act on different objects. A rocket accelerates because the rocket pushes gas backwards and the gas pushes the rocket forwards — these act on different objects.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Terminal Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">When an object falls through a fluid (air or water), it initially accelerates due to gravity. As its speed increases, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A force that opposes the motion of an object through a fluid, increasing with speed.">drag force</dfn> (air resistance) increases. Eventually, the drag force equals the weight and the resultant force becomes zero.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">At this point, the object reaches <dfn class="term" data-def="The maximum constant velocity an object reaches when the drag force equals its weight during freefall.">terminal velocity</dfn> — it continues to fall at a constant speed because the forces are balanced.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Skydiver Example</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21">When a skydiver jumps, they accelerate because weight is greater than air resistance. As speed increases, air resistance increases, so acceleration decreases. At terminal velocity (~55 m/s), forces are balanced. When the parachute opens, air resistance suddenly increases above the weight, so the skydiver decelerates. A new, lower terminal velocity (~5 m/s) is reached.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n24">First law: no resultant force means no change in motion; inertia resists changes in velocity</li><li data-narration-id="n25">Second law: \(F = ma\); larger forces produce greater acceleration, larger masses accelerate less</li><li data-narration-id="n26">Third law: equal and opposite forces on different objects; terminal velocity is reached when drag equals weight</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">The most common error in \(F = ma\) questions is forgetting to calculate the resultant force first. If driving force and friction are both given, subtract friction from the driving force to get the resultant, then use \(F = ma\). For Newton’s third law, always identify the two objects and state that the forces are equal in size, opposite in direction, and act on different objects.</p>
