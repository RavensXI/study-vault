--- DESCRIPTION ---
How lenses form images, colour filters work, and objects emit radiation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Convex and Concave Lenses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thicker in the middle than at the edges, which converges parallel rays of light to a focal point.">convex lens</dfn> (converging lens) is thicker in the middle than at the edges. It bends parallel rays of light inward so they meet at a single point called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The point where parallel rays of light converge after passing through a convex lens or the point from which they appear to diverge after passing through a concave lens.">principal focus</dfn> (or focal point). The distance from the centre of the lens to the principal focus is called the focal length.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A lens that is thinner in the middle than at the edges, which diverges parallel rays of light.">concave lens</dfn> (diverging lens) is thinner in the middle than at the edges. It spreads parallel rays of light outward so they appear to come from a single point behind the lens. Concave lenses always produce virtual, upright, diminished images.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: What type of image does a concave lens always produce and what are its three characteristics?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A convex lens can produce real or virtual images depending on the position of the object. A concave lens always produces a virtual, upright, diminished image regardless of object position.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n5">To draw a ray diagram for a convex lens, you need three standard rays from the top of the object: (1) a ray parallel to the principal axis, which refracts through the principal focus on the other side; (2) a ray through the centre of the lens, which passes straight through without bending; (3) a ray through the principal focus on the near side, which refracts to become parallel to the principal axis. Where the refracted rays meet is where the image forms.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">If the object is beyond twice the focal length (\(> 2F\)), the image is real, inverted and diminished. At exactly \(2F\), the image is real, inverted and the same size. Between \(F\) and \(2F\), the image is real, inverted and magnified. Inside \(F\), the image is virtual, upright and magnified (this is how a magnifying glass works).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Magnification</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The magnification produced by a lens tells you how much larger or smaller the image is compared to the object:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image height}}{\text{object height}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A magnification greater than 1 means the image is larger than the object. A magnification less than 1 means the image is smaller. Magnification has no units.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Visible Light and Colour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">White light is a mixture of all the colours of the visible spectrum (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet). When white light passes through a prism, each colour is refracted by a slightly different amount — violet is refracted the most and red the least — causing the light to spread into a spectrum. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The separation of white light into its component colours using a prism or diffraction grating.">dispersion</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Colour filters work by absorbing certain wavelengths and transmitting others. A red filter absorbs all colours except red, which it transmits. An opaque object appears a particular colour because it reflects that wavelength and absorbs all others. A red object in green light appears black because there is no red light to reflect.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Colour Mixing and Filters</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">The three primary colours of light are red, green and blue. Combining all three in equal proportions gives white light. Combining two primary colours gives a secondary colour: red + green = yellow, red + blue = magenta, green + blue = cyan. This is additive colour mixing and is used in TV and phone screens.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Colour filters and pigments use subtractive colour mixing. A cyan filter absorbs red and transmits blue and green. Placing a cyan filter and a yellow filter together absorbs both red and blue, transmitting only green.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Black Body Radiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">All objects emit and absorb <dfn class="term" data-def="Electromagnetic radiation emitted by all objects due to their temperature; hotter objects emit more radiation and at shorter wavelengths.">thermal radiation</dfn> (infrared radiation). The hotter an object, the more radiation it emits per second and the shorter the peak wavelength of the radiation. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A theoretical perfect absorber and emitter of radiation; it absorbs all radiation that falls on it and emits a characteristic spectrum depending only on its temperature.">black body</dfn> is a theoretical object that absorbs all radiation that falls on it and emits a characteristic spectrum that depends only on its temperature.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: As black body temperature increases, does peak wavelength shift to longer or shorter wavelengths?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>As the temperature of a black body increases, the peak wavelength shifts towards shorter wavelengths (from infrared to red to white to blue). The total energy radiated per second also increases dramatically.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n19">At room temperature, objects emit mainly infrared radiation (invisible). As they heat up, they begin to glow red (about 500°C), then orange, yellow and eventually white-hot at very high temperatures. Stars are approximate black bodies — a red star is cooler than a yellow star, which is cooler than a blue-white star.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Earth’s Temperature Balance</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">The Earth absorbs short-wavelength radiation from the Sun (visible light and UV) and re-emits longer-wavelength infrared radiation. If the rate of absorption equals the rate of emission, the temperature remains stable. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb some of the outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it back towards the Earth, increasing the surface temperature. This is the greenhouse effect — a natural process that makes Earth habitable, but increasing greenhouse gas concentrations are enhancing it, causing global warming.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Convex lenses converge light and can form real or virtual images; concave lenses diverge light and always form virtual, diminished images.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">White light disperses into a spectrum through a prism; colour filters absorb some wavelengths and transmit others.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">All objects emit thermal radiation; hotter objects emit more radiation at shorter peak wavelengths, as described by the black body radiation curve.</li>
</ul>
