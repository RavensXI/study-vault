Our solar system, life cycle of stars, and evidence for the Big Bang.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Our Solar System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Our <dfn class="term" data-def="The Sun and all the objects that orbit it, including eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets.">solar system</dfn> consists of the Sun (a main sequence star), eight planets, dwarf planets (such as Pluto), moons, asteroids and comets. The four inner planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) are small, rocky and relatively close to the Sun. The four outer planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune) are much larger gas giants, further from the Sun.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">All planets orbit the Sun in roughly the same plane, in the same direction, in slightly elliptical orbits. The Sun contains over 99% of the mass of the solar system. <dfn class="term" data-def="A force of attraction between any two objects with mass; it keeps planets in orbit around the Sun.">Gravity</dfn> provides the centripetal force needed to keep planets in orbit around the Sun, and moons in orbit around planets.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain why gravitational force equals centripetal force in stable orbits and why closer planets orbit faster.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For a stable circular orbit, the gravitational force provides exactly the right centripetal force. A planet closer to the Sun orbits faster because the gravitational pull is stronger and the orbital radius is smaller.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5">Natural satellites (moons) orbit planets. Earth has one moon, while Jupiter has over 90 known moons. <dfn class="term" data-def="Small rocky bodies that orbit the Sun, mostly found in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.">Asteroids</dfn> are small, irregular rocky bodies that mostly orbit the Sun in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. <dfn class="term" data-def="A body of ice, dust and rock that orbits the Sun in a highly elliptical orbit; it develops a tail when close to the Sun.">Comets</dfn> are bodies of ice and dust with highly elliptical orbits. When a comet approaches the Sun, it heats up and develops a glowing tail of gas and dust that always points away from the Sun (due to the solar wind).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6">The speed of a comet changes throughout its orbit. It moves fastest when closest to the Sun (perihelion) because the gravitational pull is strongest, and slowest when furthest from the Sun (aphelion).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The Life Cycle of Stars</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">All stars begin as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A large cloud of gas and dust in space from which stars form when gravity causes it to collapse.">nebula</dfn> — a large cloud of hydrogen gas and dust. Gravity causes the cloud to collapse and compress. As it contracts, it heats up. When the temperature at the core reaches about 15 million °C, hydrogen nuclei begin to fuse into helium — nuclear fusion begins and a star is born. The star enters its main sequence phase, where it spends most of its life.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">During the main sequence, the outward pressure from nuclear fusion is balanced by the inward pull of gravity. This balance keeps the star stable. The Sun is currently in its main sequence phase and has been for about 4.6 billion years.</p>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the stellar evolution sequence for Sun-sized stars versus massive stars, including supernovae and final remnants.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The fate of a star depends on its mass. Stars about the size of the Sun become red giants, then white dwarfs. Stars much more massive than the Sun become red supergiants, then explode as supernovae, leaving behind a neutron star or black hole.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Sun-sized stars:</strong> When hydrogen fuel runs low, the core contracts and the outer layers expand and cool, forming a red giant. The star then sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving behind a dense, hot <dfn class="term" data-def="The small, dense remnant of a sun-sized star after it has shed its outer layers; it gradually cools over billions of years.">white dwarf</dfn> that gradually cools over billions of years.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Massive stars:</strong> These swell into red supergiants, then collapse catastrophically in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A massive explosion that occurs when a large star runs out of fuel and its core collapses; it briefly outshines an entire galaxy.">supernova</dfn> — an explosion so bright it can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. The remnant core may form a <dfn class="term" data-def="An extremely dense remnant of a massive star after a supernova, composed almost entirely of neutrons.">neutron star</dfn> (incredibly dense, about 10 km across) or, if massive enough, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape.">black hole</dfn>. The elements created inside stars and scattered by supernovae form the raw materials for new stars, planets and even life.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Red-Shift and the Big Bang</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">When we observe light from distant galaxies, we find that the absorption lines in their spectra are shifted towards the red end of the spectrum compared to where we would expect them. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The increase in wavelength (shift towards red) of light from galaxies that are moving away from us, caused by the expansion of the universe.">red-shift</dfn>. It occurs because the galaxies are moving away from us, stretching the wavelength of the light. The further away a galaxy is, the greater its red-shift — meaning it is moving away faster. This relationship is known as Hubble’s Law.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The observation that all distant galaxies show red-shift (and that more distant galaxies show greater red-shift) is strong evidence that the universe is expanding. If we imagine reversing this expansion, all matter would converge to a single point. This leads to the <dfn class="term" data-def="The theory that the universe began from an extremely hot, dense point approximately 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.">Big Bang</dfn> theory — the idea that the universe began from an extremely hot, dense point approximately 13.8 billion years ago and has been expanding ever since.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The <dfn class="term" data-def="Low-energy microwave radiation that fills the entire universe; it is the remnant heat from the Big Bang, now cooled to about 2.7 K.">cosmic microwave background radiation</dfn> (CMBR) is low-energy microwave radiation that fills the entire universe in every direction. It was first detected in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson. The CMBR is the cooled remnant of the intense radiation released shortly after the Big Bang, now at a temperature of about 2.7 K (−270.5°C). Its existence provides strong supporting evidence for the Big Bang theory.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Together, red-shift and the CMBR provide two independent lines of evidence for the Big Bang. The discovery of the CMBR was predicted by Big Bang theory before it was detected — a successful prediction that greatly strengthened confidence in the theory.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Dark Matter and Dark Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Observations of galaxy rotation suggest that galaxies contain much more mass than we can see — stars at the edges of galaxies rotate faster than expected. Scientists propose that this “missing mass” is <dfn class="term" data-def="A form of matter that does not emit or absorb light but exerts gravitational effects; it makes up about 27% of the universe.">dark matter</dfn>, which does not emit or absorb electromagnetic radiation but does exert gravitational effects. Observations also show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating. The unknown cause of this accelerating expansion is called <dfn class="term" data-def="A mysterious form of energy that is causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate; it makes up about 68% of the universe.">dark energy</dfn>. Together, dark matter and dark energy make up about 95% of the total mass-energy of the universe.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The solar system contains the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids and comets, all held in orbit by gravity.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">A star’s life cycle depends on its mass: Sun-sized stars end as white dwarfs, while massive stars explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Red-shift of distant galaxies and the cosmic microwave background radiation are the two key pieces of evidence that the universe began with a Big Bang and is still expanding.</li>
</ul>
