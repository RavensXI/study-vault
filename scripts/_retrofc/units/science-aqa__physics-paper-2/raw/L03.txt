--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Speed and Distance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="How fast an object is moving, measured in metres per second (m/s). It is a scalar quantity.">Speed</dfn> is a scalar quantity — it tells you how fast something is moving but not the direction. The equation for speed is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$s = v t$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Where \(s\) is distance in metres, \(v\) is speed in m/s, and \(t\) is time in seconds. This can be rearranged to \(v = \frac{s}{t}\).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down the typical speeds for walking, running, cycling, car in town, car on motorway, train, aeroplane, and sound in air."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Typical speeds: walking ~1.5 m/s, running ~3 m/s, cycling ~6 m/s, car in town ~13 m/s, car on motorway ~30 m/s, train ~55 m/s, aeroplane ~250 m/s, sound in air ~330 m/s.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Velocity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speed in a given direction; a vector quantity measured in m/s.">Velocity</dfn> is a vector quantity. It has both magnitude (speed) and direction. An object moving in a circle at constant speed has a changing velocity because its direction is always changing.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">The difference between speed and velocity matters in physics: two cars travelling at 30 m/s in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Acceleration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate of change of velocity, measured in m/s².">Acceleration</dfn> is how quickly an object’s velocity changes. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{t} = \frac{v - u}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Where \(a\) is acceleration in m/s\(^2\), \(v\) is final velocity, \(u\) is initial velocity, and \(t\) is time in seconds. A negative acceleration means the object is decelerating (slowing down).</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the equation v² = u² + 2as and explain when you would use this instead of other kinematic equations."><div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>For uniform (constant) acceleration, use the equation: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), where \(s\) is the distance travelled. This is particularly useful when time is not given in the question.</p>
</div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n14">A car accelerates from 10 m/s to 30 m/s in 5 seconds. Calculate the acceleration.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">\(a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{30 - 10}{5} = \frac{20}{5} = 4\) m/s\(^2\)</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Distance-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph plotting distance on the y-axis against time on the x-axis; the gradient represents speed.">distance-time graph</dfn> shows how far an object has travelled over time. The gradient (slope) of the line gives the speed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n18">A horizontal line means the object is stationary. A straight diagonal line means constant speed. A curve getting steeper means the object is accelerating. The steeper the line, the faster the object is moving.</p>

<div class="higher-only">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">To find the speed at a specific moment on a curved distance-time graph, draw a tangent to the curve at that point. Then calculate the gradient of the tangent: \(\text{speed} = \frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}\).</p>
</div></div>
</div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Velocity-Time Graphs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A graph plotting velocity on the y-axis against time on the x-axis; the gradient gives acceleration and the area under the line gives distance.">velocity-time graph</dfn> shows how velocity changes over time. The gradient gives the acceleration. The area under the line gives the total distance travelled.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n22">A horizontal line means constant velocity (no acceleration). A line sloping upwards means acceleration. A line sloping downwards means deceleration. The area under the graph can be found by splitting it into rectangles and triangles.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">Use a dynamics trolley on a ramp with a light gate or ticker tape timer. Vary the force (by changing the mass on a pulley) while keeping the total mass constant. Measure the acceleration for each force. Plot a graph of force against acceleration — a straight line through the origin shows that \(F = ma\).</p>
</div></div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Speed is scalar (magnitude only); velocity is vector (magnitude and direction); acceleration = \(\frac{v - u}{t}\)</li><li data-narration-id="n27">Distance-time graphs: gradient = speed; velocity-time graphs: gradient = acceleration, area = distance</li><li data-narration-id="n28">For uniform acceleration without time: \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">Read graph questions carefully: on a distance-time graph the gradient is speed, but on a velocity-time graph the gradient is acceleration and the area underneath is distance. If asked to “determine” a value from a curved graph, draw a tangent at the specified point and calculate the gradient of the tangent.</p>
