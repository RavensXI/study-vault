<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Periodic Table: Order from Evidence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The <dfn class="term" data-def="An arrangement of all known elements in order of atomic number, organised into groups (vertical columns) and periods (horizontal rows) so that elements with similar properties align.">periodic table</dfn> is one of the most powerful tools in chemistry because it predicts properties before a substance is even made. Elements are arranged in order of increasing <strong>atomic number</strong> (the number of protons), and the arrangement reveals deep patterns in reactivity, bonding, and physical properties. Elements in the same vertical column &mdash; a <dfn class="term" data-def="A vertical column in the periodic table containing elements with the same number of outer-shell electrons and therefore similar chemical properties.">group</dfn> &mdash; share the same number of outer-shell electrons and behave chemically in similar ways. Elements in the same horizontal row &mdash; a <dfn class="term" data-def="A horizontal row in the periodic table; all elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.">period</dfn> &mdash; have the same number of electron shells.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Mendeleev and the Predictive Power of Patterns</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n3">Dmitri Mendeleev (1834&ndash;1907) published an early version of the periodic table in 1869, arranging elements primarily by atomic mass and grouping them by similar properties. Where the pattern demanded it, he left gaps &mdash; predicting the existence and properties of undiscovered elements. When these elements were later discovered (such as gallium in 1875 and germanium in 1886), their properties closely matched Mendeleev&rsquo;s predictions. This was powerful evidence that the periodic pattern was real, not coincidental. The later shift to ordering by atomic number resolved anomalies where ordering by mass alone would have placed elements in the wrong group (e.g. iodine and tellurium). Mendeleev&rsquo;s willingness to leave gaps and make falsifiable predictions is a model of good scientific reasoning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Group 1: The Alkali Metals</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Group 1 elements &mdash; lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium &mdash; are known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactive metals in Group 1 of the periodic table, each with one outer-shell electron that they lose readily to form +1 ions.">alkali metals</dfn>. They each have <strong>one electron</strong> in their outer shell. This single electron is lost easily during reactions, making them highly reactive. All form +1 ions and dissolve in water to produce alkaline solutions (hence their name). They are soft metals: lithium, sodium and potassium can all be cut with a knife. Their low density means lithium, sodium and potassium float on water.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">When alkali metals react with water, they produce a metal hydroxide and hydrogen gas. The general equation is: \(2M(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2MOH(aq) + H_2(g)\). The reaction becomes more vigorous going down the group: lithium fizzes gently, sodium melts into a ball and fizzes energetically, potassium ignites and burns with a lilac flame. <strong>Reactivity increases down Group 1</strong> because the outer electron is further from the nucleus, experiences greater <dfn class="term" data-def="The reduction in the attractive force between the nucleus and the outermost electron caused by inner electron shells acting as a barrier.">shielding</dfn> from inner electrons, and is therefore more easily lost.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: (1) what Group 1 metals all have in common, (2) the trend in reactivity going down the group, and (3) the reason for that trend in terms of electron structure.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Group 1 metals have one outer-shell electron. <strong>Reactivity increases going down the group</strong> because the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner shells, making it easier to lose. Melting points decrease going down the group.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Group 7: The Halogens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Group 7 elements &mdash; fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine &mdash; are called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The reactive non-metal elements in Group 7, each with seven outer-shell electrons, which they tend to gain to form &minus;1 ions.">halogens</dfn>. They each have <strong>seven electrons</strong> in their outer shell and need to gain one more electron to achieve a full outer shell, forming &minus;1 ions. Halogens exist as diatomic molecules (\(F_2\), \(Cl_2\), \(Br_2\), \(I_2\)). Physical properties change systematically down the group: fluorine is a pale yellow gas, chlorine is a green gas, bromine is a red-brown liquid at room temperature, and iodine is a dark grey solid that produces a purple vapour when warmed. Melting and boiling points <strong>increase</strong> going down the group as molecules become larger and intermolecular forces grow stronger.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Reactivity decreases going down Group 7</strong> because the outer shell is further from the nucleus and more shielded. This means the attraction for an incoming electron is weaker, so it becomes harder to gain the additional electron needed to reach a full outer shell.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Halogen Displacement Reactions</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">A more reactive halogen can displace a less reactive halogen from a solution of its salt. For example, chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution because chlorine is more reactive than bromine: \(Cl_2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) \rightarrow 2KCl(aq) + Br_2(aq)\). The solution turns from colourless to orange-brown as bromine is released. Bromine can similarly displace iodine from potassium iodide solution. However, bromine cannot displace chlorine from potassium chloride because bromine is less reactive than chlorine. These displacement reactions provide direct experimental evidence for the reactivity trend in Group 7.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Group 1 reactivity INCREASES going down (losing electrons becomes easier); Group 7 reactivity DECREASES going down (gaining electrons becomes harder). Write both trends and their electron-structure explanations without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Group 7 reactivity <strong>decreases going down the group</strong> because the outer shell is further from the nucleus, making it harder to attract and gain the additional electron. Boiling points increase going down as molecules get larger and intermolecular forces increase.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Group 0: The Noble Gases</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Group 0 elements &mdash; helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon &mdash; are called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The very unreactive elements in Group 0, which have full outer electron shells and exist as individual (monatomic) atoms.">noble gases</dfn>. They have <strong>full outer electron shells</strong>, which gives them great stability. They have no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons, so they exist as individual atoms (monatomic) and do not readily form compounds. Boiling points increase going down the group as atoms become larger and the weak intermolecular forces between them become stronger.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Noble gases have useful applications precisely because of their unreactivity: helium is used in balloons and airships (low density, non-flammable); neon is used in advertising signs (produces a distinctive red-orange glow when electricity passes through it); argon is used in light bulbs and in welding to create an inert atmosphere that prevents oxidation of the hot metal or filament.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Electronic Structure Explains All Group Trends</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The patterns across Groups 1, 7 and 0 all share the same root explanation: <dfn class="term" data-def="The arrangement of electrons in the energy levels of an atom, written as numbers separated by commas.">electronic configuration</dfn>. Going down any group, atoms have more electron shells. In Group 1, the single outer electron is progressively further from the nucleus and more shielded &mdash; it is easier to remove, so reactivity increases. In Group 7, the outer shell&rsquo;s pull on an incoming electron is progressively weaker &mdash; gaining an electron becomes harder, so reactivity decreases. In Group 0, the full outer shell provides stability at every period &mdash; all noble gases are unreactive for the same reason. Knowing the electronic configuration of an atom tells you which group it belongs to and predicts how it will behave chemically, even for elements you have never studied before.</p>

    <span>Using Electron Structure to Predict Properties</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">An unknown element has electronic configuration 2, 8, 7. You can deduce: it has 3 electron shells (period 3), 7 outer-shell electrons (Group 7), so it is a halogen. It will: exist as a diatomic molecule, form &minus;1 ions, be less reactive than chlorine (which has configuration 2, 8, 7 at period 3 &mdash; wait, that IS chlorine: 17 electrons). For a period-4 halogen (e.g. bromine, 2, 8, 18, 7), it would be less reactive than chlorine for the reason above. This kind of reasoning from structure to properties is the core skill the periodic table tests &mdash; you are expected to apply it to unfamiliar elements in novel contexts.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Elements in the same group share the same number of outer-shell electrons, giving them similar chemical properties; reactivity trends within groups are explained by how easily electrons are gained or lost as you move down.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Group 1 (alkali metals) &mdash; reactivity increases down the group (easier to lose the outer electron); Group 7 (halogens) &mdash; reactivity decreases down the group (harder to gain an electron); Group 0 (noble gases) &mdash; unreactive due to full outer shells.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Mendeleev&rsquo;s decision to leave gaps and predict undiscovered elements, later confirmed by experiment, demonstrates how scientific models earn confidence through verifiable predictions.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">When explaining reactivity trends, always give the <em>reason</em> in terms of electron structure &mdash; simply stating &lsquo;the outer electron is further away&rsquo; is not enough without explaining that this makes it easier to lose (Group 1) or harder to gain (Group 7). In displacement questions, always state which halogen is more reactive and why, then predict whether displacement occurs. &lsquo;Suggest&rsquo; questions may give you an unfamiliar Group 1 or Group 7 element and ask you to apply the trend &mdash; treat it the same way, just check its position in the group relative to elements you know.</p>
