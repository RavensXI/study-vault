--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sub-atomic particles, isotopes, and how the atomic model evolved from Dalton to Bohr.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Structure of the Atom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">An <dfn class="term" data-def="The smallest part of an element that can exist, made up of a nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons.">atom</dfn> is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element. Atoms are extraordinarily small &mdash; a typical atomic radius is around \(1 \times 10^{-10}\) m. At the centre of each atom is a tiny, dense <dfn class="term" data-def="The central region of an atom, containing protons and neutrons and carrying an overall positive charge.">nucleus</dfn>. The nucleus contains two types of subatomic particle: <strong>protons</strong>, which carry a positive charge, and <strong>neutrons</strong>, which carry no charge. <dfn class="term" data-def="Negatively charged subatomic particles that occupy energy levels around the nucleus of an atom.">Electrons</dfn> orbit the nucleus in energy levels (also called shells) and carry a negative charge. The nucleus accounts for almost all of the mass of an atom, yet it occupies only a tiny fraction of the atom&rsquo;s volume &mdash; the rest is essentially empty space.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the charge and relative mass of each subatomic particle from memory: proton, neutron, electron. This appears in nearly every atomic structure question.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Subatomic particles: <strong>Proton</strong> &mdash; charge +1, relative mass 1. <strong>Neutron</strong> &mdash; charge 0, relative mass 1. <strong>Electron</strong> &mdash; charge &minus;1, relative mass approximately \(\frac{1}{1836}\) (negligible). The nuclear radius is far smaller than the atomic radius &mdash; most of an atom is empty space.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Atomic Number, Mass Number and Neutrons</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; it defines which element the atom is.">atomic number</dfn> (also called the proton number) tells you how many protons are in the nucleus. It uniquely identifies the element &mdash; all atoms of the same element have the same atomic number. In a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so the atom has no overall charge. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.">mass number</dfn> is the total count of protons plus neutrons in the nucleus. To find the number of neutrons:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">$$\text{neutrons} = \text{mass number} - \text{atomic number}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">For example, a chlorine atom with mass number 35 and atomic number 17 has \(35 - 17 = 18\) neutrons, 17 protons, and 17 electrons. Standard notation places the mass number top-left and the atomic number bottom-left of the symbol: \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Isotopes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Atoms of the same element that have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, giving them different mass numbers but identical chemical properties.">Isotopes</dfn> are atoms of the same element with <strong>different numbers of neutrons</strong>. Because the number of protons (and therefore electrons) is unchanged, isotopes have identical chemical properties. Only their mass differs. For example, carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Both are carbon; both react in the same way chemically. Chlorine exists in nature as two isotopes &mdash; chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 &mdash; which is why the relative atomic mass of chlorine given in the periodic table is approximately 35.5 (a weighted average of the two). Isotopes are important in applications ranging from medical imaging to carbon dating.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons and therefore carries an overall electric charge. Losing electrons produces a <strong>positive ion</strong> (cation) because there are now more protons than electrons. Gaining electrons produces a <strong>negative ion</strong> (anion). The nucleus does not change when an ion forms &mdash; only the electron count changes. For example, sodium loses one electron to form \(\text{Na}^+\) and chlorine gains one electron to form \(\text{Cl}^-\). The charges are shown as superscripts: \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) has lost two electrons; \(\text{O}^{2-}\) has gained two.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">How the Atomic Model Developed</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Our current understanding of the atom was not established in a single discovery. It developed gradually over more than a century as new experimental evidence challenged existing ideas and forced scientists to revise their models. This process illustrates a key principle of science: a model is only useful as long as it fits the available evidence. When new evidence arrives that a model cannot explain, the model must change. This is not a failure &mdash; it is how science makes progress.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n13">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Early 1800s</div>
    <h4>Dalton&rsquo;s Model</h4>
    <p>John Dalton proposed that atoms were tiny, solid, indivisible spheres. He argued that all atoms of the same element were identical and that atoms of different elements had different masses. This model explained why elements always combine in fixed ratios, but gave no internal structure for the atom.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1897</div>
    <h4>Thomson&rsquo;s <dfn class="term" data-def="An atomic model proposed by J.J. Thomson in which electrons are embedded in a sphere of uniform positive charge, like plums in a pudding.">Plum Pudding Model</dfn></h4>
    <p>J.J. Thomson discovered the electron by experimenting with cathode rays. He realised atoms contained smaller, negatively charged particles and proposed a new model: a ball of diffuse positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it &mdash; like plums in a pudding. This was the first model to include subatomic particles.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1909&ndash;1911</div>
    <h4>Rutherford&rsquo;s Nuclear Model</h4>
    <p>Ernest Rutherford, working with Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, fired positively charged alpha particles at a thin gold foil. The result was unexpected: most particles passed straight through, but some were deflected at large angles and a small number bounced almost directly back. The plum pudding model predicted that alpha particles would pass through the diffuse positive charge with only minor deflections. The actual results could only be explained if the positive charge and most of the mass were concentrated in a tiny, dense region &mdash; the nucleus. Rutherford&rsquo;s nuclear model replaced the plum pudding model.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">1913</div>
    <h4>Bohr&rsquo;s Model</h4>
    <p>Niels Bohr refined the nuclear model by showing that electrons could not orbit at any distance &mdash; they were confined to specific <strong>energy levels</strong> (fixed shells) at set distances from the nucleus. Electrons in a given shell have a fixed energy; they can absorb energy and jump to a higher shell, or fall back and release energy as light. Bohr&rsquo;s model explained the emission spectra of elements, which the Rutherford model could not.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Explain what the gold-foil experiment showed, and why it disproved the plum pudding model. Write two sentences without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The alpha particle scattering experiment showed that most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil (atom is mostly empty space) but some bounced back sharply (small, dense, positive nucleus). This disproved Thomson&rsquo;s plum pudding model and led to the nuclear model.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Models as Tools: Strengths and Limitations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Each atomic model was the best explanation of the evidence available at the time. Dalton&rsquo;s model was useful for explaining chemical reactions but could not account for electrical phenomena. Thomson&rsquo;s model incorporated electrons but placed them in the wrong configuration. Rutherford&rsquo;s model identified the nucleus but could not explain why electrons did not spiral into it. Bohr&rsquo;s model resolved this by proposing fixed energy levels. Later developments in quantum mechanics produced an even more sophisticated picture &mdash; but at GCSE, the Bohr model is the accepted working model. Understanding that models change with evidence is as important as knowing the models themselves.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Electronic Configuration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Electrons occupy shells around the nucleus. The first shell holds a maximum of <strong>2</strong> electrons; the second and third shells each hold a maximum of <strong>8</strong>. Electrons always fill the lowest available shell first. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The arrangement of electrons in the energy levels of an atom, written as numbers separated by commas.">electronic configuration</dfn> of an element determines how it reacts chemically.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Use the atomic number to find the total number of electrons in a neutral atom. Fill shells in order: first shell up to 2, then second up to 8, then third up to 8. Examples: sodium (atomic number 11) &rarr; 2, 8, 1. Calcium (atomic number 20) &rarr; 2, 8, 8, 2. The number of electrons in the outer shell equals the group number for Groups 1&ndash;7. This is why elements in the same group have similar chemical behaviour: they have the same number of outer-shell electrons available for bonding.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Atoms consist of a nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in shells; protons are positive, neutrons are neutral, electrons are negative with negligible mass.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different mass numbers (different neutron counts); they have identical chemical properties.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Atomic models evolved from Dalton (solid sphere) &rarr; Thomson (plum pudding) &rarr; Rutherford (nuclear, from gold-foil experiment) &rarr; Bohr (fixed electron shells), each driven by new experimental evidence.</li>
</ul>

For isotopes, be precise: same element (same proton number), different mass number (different neutron number), same chemical properties. The words &lsquo;same&rsquo; and &lsquo;different&rsquo; must appear in your answer to score well on comparison questions.</p>
