--- DESCRIPTION ---
Sources of clean water, treatment processes, and how chemistry helps supply communities safely.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Water Treatment Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Access to safe water is one of the most fundamental public health challenges in the world. In the UK, water from rivers, lakes and reservoirs contains dissolved minerals, suspended solids and potentially harmful microorganisms. It must be processed before it is safe to drink. In many parts of the world &mdash; particularly arid regions and areas affected by drought or contamination &mdash; obtaining even basic safe water is a daily challenge that chemistry helps address. This lesson covers how <dfn class="term" data-def="Water that is safe to drink, with sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and harmful microorganisms.">potable water</dfn> is produced from fresh water sources, how seawater is treated in water-scarce regions, and how pollution from human activity is managed.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">What &lsquo;Potable&rsquo; Means</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Potable water is not the same as <strong>pure</strong> water in the chemical sense. Pure water contains only \(H_2O\) molecules. Potable water still contains dissolved mineral salts and trace amounts of other substances &mdash; but at concentrations low enough that it is safe for human consumption. Standards for potable water specify maximum concentrations of dissolved substances, levels of microorganisms, and other quality parameters. In the UK, these standards are set and enforced by regulators, and tap water routinely meets them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the difference between potable water and pure water in one sentence each. This is a common exam trap.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Potable water is safe to drink but is not chemically pure &mdash; it still contains dissolved minerals. Pure water contains only \(H_2O\). The key measure for potability is that harmful substances are below safe thresholds.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Producing Potable Water from Fresh Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In the UK and most countries with reliable fresh water sources, potable water is produced from rivers, lakes and reservoirs through a two-stage treatment process: <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of passing water through filter beds of sand and gravel to remove suspended solid particles.">filtration</dfn> followed by <dfn class="term" data-def="The killing of harmful microorganisms in water, typically by adding chlorine, using ozone or exposing to ultraviolet light.">sterilisation</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Treatment Process Step by Step</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Step 1 &mdash; Filtration:</strong> Water is passed through beds of sand and gravel. The physical structure of these beds traps suspended particles &mdash; silt, organic debris and some microorganisms &mdash; leaving the water visually clear. This is a physical process, not a chemical one, and it does not remove dissolved substances or microorganisms that have passed through.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Step 2 &mdash; Sterilisation:</strong> Chlorine is most commonly added to kill remaining bacteria and other harmful microorganisms. Chlorine is favoured because it is effective, relatively inexpensive, and &mdash; crucially &mdash; it continues to protect water against microbial regrowth as it travels through the distribution network to homes and businesses. Alternatively, ozone (\(O_3\)) or ultraviolet (UV) light can be used; both kill microorganisms effectively but do not provide the lasting protection in the pipes that chlorine does.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Desalination: Water for Arid Regions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In countries or regions where fresh water is scarce &mdash; such as parts of the Middle East, North Africa and island communities &mdash; seawater or <dfn class="term" data-def="Water that contains dissolved salts at levels between fresh water and seawater, found in estuaries or certain groundwater sources.">brackish water</dfn> must be processed to make it potable. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing dissolved salts from seawater or brackish water to produce fresh, potable water.">desalination</dfn>. Two methods are used:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Distillation and Reverse Osmosis</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Distillation:</strong> Seawater is boiled and the steam is collected and condensed. Because the dissolved salts remain in the boiling vessel, the condensed water is essentially pure. This method is energy-intensive, which makes it expensive, but it produces very pure water and is well understood.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Reverse osmosis:</strong> Water is forced at high pressure through a semi-permeable membrane with very small pores that block the dissolved salt ions but allow water molecules to pass through. This approach is more energy-efficient than distillation but the membranes require careful maintenance and can become fouled with impurities. Reverse osmosis is increasingly used in large-scale desalination plants because its energy cost is lower and the technology has improved considerably.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Both methods produce concentrated brine (salt water) as a by-product that must be disposed of carefully to avoid environmental damage to marine ecosystems near the outfall. In the UK, where rainfall is generally plentiful, desalination is rarely needed and fresh-water treatment is far more cost-effective. The choice of method is always a balance of economic cost, energy availability and environmental impact.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Write one sentence explaining why desalination is used in arid regions but not commonly in the UK. Include the key limiting factor.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Desalination (by distillation or reverse osmosis) removes dissolved salts from seawater to make it potable. Both methods require significant energy, making desalination expensive &mdash; it is used where fresh water is scarce and no cheaper alternative exists.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Treating Waste Water and Controlling Pollution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Human activity produces large volumes of waste water from homes, industry and agriculture. This water cannot be returned to rivers or seas untreated without causing serious harm to aquatic ecosystems and human health. Waste water treatment is a multi-stage process involving physical and biological steps:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In sewage treatment, waste water is first screened to remove large solids. It then passes into settlement tanks where smaller solid particles sink as sludge. The remaining liquid undergoes <strong>biological treatment</strong> in which aerobic bacteria break down dissolved organic compounds. The cleaned water (effluent) is filtered and sterilised before being returned to rivers. The sludge removed at earlier stages is treated separately &mdash; it may be digested by bacteria (anaerobic digestion, which also produces methane as a useful biogas), dried, and in some cases used as agricultural fertiliser.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Industrial and Agricultural Pollution</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Industrial waste water may contain dissolved heavy metals, toxic organic compounds or acids that cannot be removed by standard biological treatment. These require specialist chemical treatment &mdash; for example, precipitation reactions to remove dissolved metals, or activated carbon filtration to adsorb organic pollutants. Agricultural run-off containing excess <dfn class="term" data-def="Compounds containing nitrogen or phosphorus applied to soil to promote plant growth.">fertilisers</dfn> is another serious problem. When nitrates and phosphates enter waterways, they cause <strong>eutrophication</strong>: algae grow rapidly on the excess nutrients, block sunlight from aquatic plants, then die and decompose. The decomposition uses up dissolved oxygen, suffocating fish and other aquatic life. Controlling fertiliser use and managing run-off from fields is therefore important not just for farming economics but for ecosystem health.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Explain the sequence of events from agricultural fertiliser run-off to oxygen depletion in a river. Write it as a cause-and-effect chain.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In waste water treatment, physical screening and sedimentation remove solids; biological treatment by aerobic bacteria removes dissolved organics. Untreated agricultural run-off can cause eutrophication, depleting oxygen and killing aquatic life.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Potable water is safe to drink but not chemically pure; it is produced from fresh water by filtration (removes solids) then sterilisation (kills microorganisms, usually with chlorine).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Where fresh water is scarce, desalination by distillation or reverse osmosis converts seawater to potable water, but both methods are energy-intensive and expensive.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Waste water is treated by screening, sedimentation and biological breakdown before return to waterways; agricultural fertiliser run-off causes eutrophication if not controlled.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">When describing water treatment, use process order: filtration first (physical, removes solids), then sterilisation (kills microorganisms). A very common error is to name the steps in the wrong order or to confuse filtration with sterilisation. In &lsquo;suggest&rsquo; questions about water supply in an unfamiliar context (e.g. a remote island), apply the principles: is fresh water available? If not, desalination is needed; which method is more practical given energy supply? Show you are applying the science, not just listing facts.</p>
