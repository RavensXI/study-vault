--- DESCRIPTION ---
How atoms combine, what determines bond type, and how to draw and describe each.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Atoms Form Bonds</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Atoms are most stable when they have a full outer shell of electrons. Most atoms do not naturally achieve this on their own, so they combine with other atoms through chemical bonding. All chemical bonds involve <strong>electrostatic forces</strong> &mdash; attractions between positive and negative charges. The type of bond that forms depends on whether the atoms are metals or non-metals and on how many outer-shell electrons they have. There are three main types: <dfn class="term" data-def="A bond formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal atom to a non-metal atom, producing oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction.">ionic bonding</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A bond in which two non-metal atoms share a pair of electrons, so that both achieve a full outer shell.">covalent bonding</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="The attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a surrounding sea of delocalised electrons.">metallic bonding</dfn>.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Ionic Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Ionic bonding forms between a <strong>metal</strong> and a <strong>non-metal</strong>. The metal atom <strong>transfers</strong> one or more electrons to the non-metal atom. The metal becomes a positively charged ion (cation) because it now has more protons than electrons. The non-metal becomes a negatively charged ion (anion) for the opposite reason. The resulting opposite charges attract each other strongly: this electrostatic force of attraction is the ionic bond.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">In sodium chloride (NaCl): sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one electron to become \(\text{Na}^+\) (2, 8), and chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains that electron to become \(\text{Cl}^-\) (2, 8, 8). Both ions now have full outer shells. In magnesium oxide (MgO): magnesium (2, 8, 2) loses two electrons to form \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\), and oxygen (2, 6) gains two to form \(\text{O}^{2-}\). In any <dfn class="term" data-def="A compound composed of positive and negative ions arranged in a regular three-dimensional lattice held together by strong electrostatic forces.">ionic compound</dfn>, billions of ions pack together in a regular three-dimensional arrangement called a giant ionic lattice, where every ion is surrounded by oppositely charged neighbours in all directions.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Draw a dot-and-cross diagram from memory for sodium chloride: sodium loses one electron (shown leaving), chloride gains it. Label the charges on each ion in square brackets.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In ionic bonding, electrons are <strong>transferred</strong> from metal to non-metal. Both ions achieve full outer shells. Ionic compounds form giant lattices held together by strong electrostatic attraction between opposite charges.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Covalent Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Covalent bonding forms between <strong>non-metal</strong> atoms. Instead of transferring electrons, the atoms <strong>share</strong> pairs of electrons. Each shared pair counts as one covalent bond. By sharing, each atom achieves a full outer shell without losing or gaining electrons overall. A single shared pair is a single bond. Two shared pairs form a double bond (e.g. \(O_2\), \(CO_2\)). Three shared pairs form a triple bond (e.g. \(N_2\)).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In water (\(H_2O\)): the oxygen atom shares one pair of electrons with each of two hydrogen atoms, forming two single covalent bonds. In chlorine gas (\(Cl_2\)): two chlorine atoms share one pair of electrons. In carbon dioxide (\(CO_2\)): each oxygen forms a double bond with the central carbon atom. Covalent compounds include simple small molecules (like \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\), \(CH_4\)) and very large networks called giant covalent structures (like diamond and silicon dioxide).</p>

For covalent bonds, use dots for electrons from one atom and crosses for electrons from the other; draw the outer shells overlapping and place the shared pairs in the overlap. For ionic bonds, show the electron being transferred and enclose each ion in square brackets with its charge written outside. These diagrams are models &mdash; they show electron positions but not the three-dimensional shape, bond angles, or relative atomic sizes. Different models (ball-and-stick, space-filling) address these limitations but introduce others. No single model captures everything about a bond; choosing the right one depends on what you need to show.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In covalent bonding, non-metal atoms <strong>share</strong> electron pairs. Each shared pair is one bond. Both atoms count the shared electrons as their own, giving both a full outer shell.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Metallic Bonding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In a metal, atoms are arranged in a regular lattice structure. The outer-shell electrons are not bound to any individual atom &mdash; they are <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrons that are free to move throughout a metal structure, not attached to any particular atom.">delocalised</dfn> and free to move throughout the entire metallic structure. This creates a &ldquo;sea&rdquo; of mobile electrons surrounding a lattice of positive metal ions. The strong electrostatic attraction between the positive ions and the negative delocalised electrons holds the structure together. This is metallic bonding.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The delocalised electrons explain many properties of metals: they conduct electricity because electrons can move freely to carry charge; they conduct heat well because electrons can transfer energy; metals are malleable and ductile because the positive ions can slide past each other without breaking the bonds with the electron sea. The more delocalised electrons per atom (e.g. aluminium gives three, copper gives one), the stronger the metallic bonding and the higher the melting point.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Comparing Bond Types and Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The type of bond present in a substance determines its structure, which in turn determines its bulk properties. Understanding this link is essential:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Bond Type, Structure and Properties</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Giant ionic lattice</strong> (e.g. NaCl, MgO) &mdash; high melting and boiling points because a large amount of energy is needed to overcome the many strong electrostatic attractions throughout the lattice. Conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water (ions are free to move) but not when solid (ions are fixed in position).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Simple molecular structure</strong> (e.g. \(H_2O\), \(CO_2\), \(CH_4\)) &mdash; low melting and boiling points because the weak intermolecular forces between molecules require little energy to overcome. The covalent bonds within each molecule are strong, but they are not broken on melting. Does not conduct electricity (no free ions or electrons). The stronger the intermolecular forces (larger molecules), the higher the boiling point.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Giant covalent structure</strong> (e.g. diamond, graphite, silicon dioxide) &mdash; very high melting and boiling points because melting requires breaking many strong covalent bonds throughout the whole structure. Diamond does not conduct electricity (all electrons are used in bonding); graphite does conduct electricity because it has one delocalised electron per carbon atom.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Metallic structure</strong> &mdash; high melting and boiling points (especially for metals with more delocalised electrons per atom). Excellent electrical and thermal conductors due to delocalised electrons. Malleable and ductile because ions can slide.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n21">The periodic table gives you the starting information. Metals on the left &mdash; metallic bonding. Non-metals on the right &mdash; covalent bonding between non-metals, ionic bonding when a metal and non-metal combine. Group 0 elements do not bond. From bond type, you can predict structure; from structure, you can predict properties. This logical chain &mdash; position in table → electron arrangement → bond type → structure → properties &mdash; is the engine of chemical prediction.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Ionic bonding involves electron <em>transfer</em> from metal to non-metal, forming a giant ionic lattice; covalent bonding involves electron <em>sharing</em> between non-metals; metallic bonding involves a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Bond type determines structure (giant lattice, simple molecular, metallic) which determines properties (melting point, electrical conductivity, malleability).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Dot-and-cross diagrams model how electrons are arranged in bonds; they are useful but limited models that show electron positions but not three-dimensional shape.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">When asked to explain a property (e.g. &lsquo;why does magnesium oxide have a high melting point?&rsquo;), always state the bond type, then the structural consequence (giant ionic lattice), then the reason the property follows (many strong electrostatic attractions must be overcome). Three-step explanation. For dot-and-cross diagrams, use square brackets and show charges for ionic compounds. Do not forget to show lone pairs as well as bonding pairs in covalent diagrams &mdash; the outer shell must be complete on each atom.</p>
