--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why some reactions release heat and others absorb it, and how to measure energy change.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy and Chemical Reactions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every chemical reaction involves a change in energy. This energy is transferred between the <strong>system</strong> (the reacting chemicals) and the <strong>surroundings</strong>. Energy is never created or destroyed during a reaction &mdash; it is only transferred. Two categories describe the direction of this transfer, and understanding them is fundamental to chemistry.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that transfers energy to the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to rise.">Exothermic reactions</dfn> release energy to the surroundings &mdash; the surroundings get warmer. Familiar examples include combustion (burning wood or gas), neutralisation reactions (acid + alkali), and the reactions that power hand warmers. <dfn class="term" data-def="A reaction that takes in energy from the surroundings, causing the temperature of the surroundings to fall.">Endothermic reactions</dfn> absorb energy from the surroundings &mdash; the surroundings get colder. Examples include thermal decomposition reactions, and the dissolving of certain salts in water. Sports cold packs are a practical application: when activated, an endothermic reaction absorbs heat from the surroundings (including an injured limb), cooling the area.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Remember: EXO = EXIT — energy exits to the surroundings, temperature rises. ENDO = ENTER — energy enters from the surroundings, temperature falls. Connect the prefix to the direction of energy transfer.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Exothermic reactions transfer energy <em>to</em> the surroundings &mdash; temperature of surroundings rises. Endothermic reactions take in energy <em>from</em> the surroundings &mdash; temperature of surroundings falls. Both involve transfer, never creation or destruction, of energy.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Measuring Energy Changes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">You can determine whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic by measuring the temperature change of the surroundings &mdash; typically a solution in a container. If the temperature rises, the reaction is exothermic; if it falls, endothermic. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A simple technique for measuring the heat energy released or absorbed during a chemical reaction by monitoring temperature change of a surrounding solution.">calorimetry</dfn> experiment uses a polystyrene cup (a good insulator) to minimise heat loss to the environment, giving a more accurate measurement. You record the starting temperature, the maximum or minimum temperature reached, and calculate the temperature change: ΔT = Tₙₐˣ &minus; TₓₜₐⱿₜ.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">A student adds excess magnesium powder to 50&nbsp;cm<sup>3</sup> of dilute hydrochloric acid in a polystyrene cup. The temperature rises from 19&deg;C to 38&deg;C. The equation for calculating the heat energy released is:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">$$Q = mc\Delta T$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">where \(Q\) is heat energy (joules), \(m\) is the mass of the solution (grams), \(c\) is the specific heat capacity of the solution (we assume it is the same as water: 4.18&nbsp;J/g/&deg;C), and \(\Delta T\) is the temperature change (degrees Celsius).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">Step 1 &mdash; Mass: 50&nbsp;cm<sup>3</sup> of water has a mass of approximately 50&nbsp;g.
Step 2 &mdash; Temperature change: \(\Delta T = 38 - 19 = 19\)&deg;C.
Step 3 &mdash; Calculate Q: \(Q = 50 \times 4.18 \times 19 = 3971\)&nbsp;J = approximately 3.97&nbsp;kJ.
This is exothermic (temperature rose). The polystyrene cup reduces heat loss, making the result more accurate, but some heat will still escape so the true value is slightly higher.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Reaction Profiles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram showing the relative energy levels of reactants and products and the activation energy barrier as a reaction progresses.">reaction profile</dfn> is a diagram that shows the energy content of the reactants and products, and the energy barrier that must be overcome for the reaction to proceed. The vertical axis represents energy; the horizontal axis represents the progress of the reaction (sometimes called the reaction pathway). The diagram is not a time graph &mdash; it shows energy levels, not rates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Every reaction profile includes a peak &mdash; the top of the energy barrier. The difference between the reactant energy level and the peak is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a reaction to occur.">activation energy</dfn>. This is the energy needed to start breaking bonds in the reactants. For an <strong>exothermic</strong> reaction, the products are at a lower energy level than the reactants; the overall energy change is negative (energy is released). For an <strong>endothermic</strong> reaction, the products are at a higher energy level than the reactants; the overall energy change is positive (energy is absorbed).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Drawing Reaction Profiles &mdash; What to Label</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">When drawing or annotating a reaction profile, you must label four things: (1) the <strong>reactants</strong> energy level (the starting plateau); (2) the <strong>products</strong> energy level (the ending plateau); (3) the <strong>activation energy</strong> (the arrow from reactant level up to the peak); (4) the <strong>overall energy change</strong> (the arrow from reactant level to product level). For exothermic reactions, the product level is lower &mdash; the overall energy change arrow points downward. For endothermic reactions, the product level is higher &mdash; the arrow points upward.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A common mistake is forgetting that the activation energy always starts from the <em>reactant</em> energy level (not from zero), and that the overall energy change is measured from <em>reactants to products</em> (not from zero to products). Catalysts reduce the activation energy &mdash; they provide an alternative reaction pathway with a lower energy barrier &mdash; so they lower the peak of the profile without changing the energy levels of reactants or products.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Draw an exothermic and an endothermic reaction profile from memory. Label reactants, products, activation energy and overall energy change on each one. Check your diagrams: which energy level is higher in each case?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Activation energy is the minimum energy needed for particles to react &mdash; shown on a reaction profile as the gap from the reactant level to the peak. Exothermic: products lower than reactants. Endothermic: products higher than reactants. A catalyst lowers the activation energy without changing reactant or product energy levels.</p>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Bond Energies (Higher)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">At the particle level, energy changes arise from the making and breaking of chemical bonds. Breaking <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy required to break one mole of a specific type of chemical bond; also the energy released when that bond is formed.">bond energy</dfn> requires energy — breaking bonds is endothermic (an endothermic step &mdash; you must put energy in to pull atoms apart). Making bonds <strong>releases</strong> energy (an exothermic step &mdash; energy is given out when new bonds form). The overall energy change of a reaction depends on the balance between these two processes.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How to Calculate Energy Changes Using Bond Energies</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Step 1: Count all the bonds broken in the reactants and add up the energy required to break them (using values given in the question).
Step 2: Count all the bonds formed in the products and add up the energy released when they form.
Step 3: Calculate the overall energy change:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">$$\text{energy change} = \text{energy to break bonds} - \text{energy released making bonds}$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">If the result is <strong>negative</strong>, the reaction is exothermic (more energy released making bonds than required breaking them). If <strong>positive</strong>, the reaction is endothermic. For example, for \(H_2 + Cl_2 \rightarrow 2HCl\): energy to break bonds = H&ndash;H (436&nbsp;kJ/mol) + Cl&ndash;Cl (242&nbsp;kJ/mol) = 678&nbsp;kJ/mol. Energy released making bonds = 2 &times; H&ndash;Cl (431&nbsp;kJ/mol) = 862&nbsp;kJ/mol. Energy change = 678 &minus; 862 = &minus;184&nbsp;kJ/mol. Negative, so exothermic.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Exothermic reactions transfer energy to the surroundings (temperature rises); endothermic reactions absorb energy from the surroundings (temperature falls). Energy is transferred, never created or destroyed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">A reaction profile shows the energy levels of reactants and products and the activation energy barrier. Exothermic profiles have products at a lower energy level than reactants; endothermic profiles have products at a higher level.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">At the particle level: breaking bonds requires energy; making bonds releases energy. The overall energy change is determined by whether more energy is released (making bonds) or absorbed (breaking bonds).</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">A very common error is confusing the direction of the activation energy arrow and the overall energy change arrow on a reaction profile. Practise drawing both an exothermic and an endothermic profile and labelling all four features. For bond energy calculations (Higher), a frequent mistake is calculating the energy change the wrong way round &mdash; always write the formula first: energy change = energy to break bonds &minus; energy released making bonds. If your answer is negative, the reaction is exothermic.</p>
