<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Summer That Created a Monster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Mary Shelley began writing <em>Frankenstein</em> in the summer of 1816, when she was just eighteen years old. She was staying at the Villa Diodati near Lake Geneva with her future husband Percy Bysshe Shelley, the poet Lord Byron, and Byron’s physician John Polidori. Volcanic ash from the eruption of Mount Tambora in 1815 had darkened the skies across Europe, creating what became known as the “Year Without a Summer.” Trapped indoors by relentless rain, Byron challenged the group to each write a ghost story. From that challenge, Shelley produced what many consider the first <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of fiction that uses scientific or technological concepts as a central element of the plot, exploring the consequences of human innovation.">science fiction</dfn> novel.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Shelley later described a waking nightmare that inspired the novel: she imagined “the pale student of unhallowed arts kneeling beside the thing he had put together.” This image — a creator horrified by his own creation — became the emotional core of <em>Frankenstein</em>. The novel was published anonymously in 1818, and many readers assumed it had been written by a man. It was only in the 1831 revised edition that Shelley was openly credited as the author.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the key contextual details and how they connect to the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mary Shelley was only eighteen when she began <em>Frankenstein</em> during the summer of 1816. The “Year Without a Summer” created an atmosphere of darkness and unease that directly influenced the novel’s Gothic tone. The story was born from a ghost story competition at the Villa Diodati.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Galvanism and the Science of Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In the early nineteenth century, scientists were experimenting with electricity and its effects on the human body. <dfn class="term" data-def="Scientific experiments using electrical currents to stimulate movement in dead tissue, named after Luigi Galvani, who made frog legs twitch with electricity in 1780.">Galvanism</dfn> — named after the Italian scientist Luigi Galvani — involved applying electrical currents to dead animals and even human corpses to make their muscles twitch. In 1803, Giovanni Aldini publicly applied electrical currents to the body of an executed criminal, causing the corpse’s jaw to clench and its eye to open. Audiences were both horrified and fascinated.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">These experiments raised a terrifying question: could science actually reanimate the dead? Shelley was aware of these developments through Percy Shelley, who was deeply interested in science and had conducted his own electrical experiments at Oxford. Victor Frankenstein’s ambition to “bestow animation upon lifeless matter” is rooted in real scientific debates of the period. The novel asks whether such ambition is heroic or catastrophically irresponsible.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><em>Frankenstein</em> was written during the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary and artistic movement of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries that valued emotion, imagination, the sublime power of nature, and individual experience over reason and scientific rationalism.">Romantic</dfn> period. Romantic writers like Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, and Percy Shelley championed the power of nature, emotion, and imagination over cold rationalism. They believed that the natural world had a restorative, almost spiritual force, and that humanity’s attempts to dominate nature through science and industry were dangerous.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Shelley uses this Romantic framework throughout the novel. The Swiss Alps, the Rhine Valley, and the Arctic wilderness are presented as awe-inspiring and sublime. Victor finds temporary peace in nature after his worst moments of guilt. But crucially, his scientific ambition represents the opposite of Romantic values — he tries to conquer nature rather than respect it, and the consequences are devastating. The Creature, denied access to human society, finds his only comfort in the natural world, making him in some ways more “Romantic” than his creator.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Gothic Tradition</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><em>Frankenstein</em> belongs to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary genre that combines horror, mystery, and the supernatural, often exploring the dark side of human nature and featuring transgression, isolation, and extreme settings.">Gothic</dfn> literary tradition. Gothic novels typically feature dark and isolated settings, transgressive acts that violate natural or moral boundaries, supernatural or monstrous elements, and intense psychological states. Earlier Gothic works like Horace Walpole’s <em>The Castle of Otranto</em> (1764) and Ann Radcliffe’s <em>The Mysteries of Udolpho</em> (1794) established many of these conventions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Shelley draws on Gothic conventions but also transforms them. The “monster” of her novel is not a supernatural demon but a scientifically created being. The horror comes not from the Creature itself but from Victor’s act of creation and subsequent abandonment. The settings — laboratories, charnel houses, Arctic wastelands, and remote mountain peaks — reflect the characters’ psychological states. The novel is Gothic because it explores transgression, isolation, and the consequences of overreaching human ambition.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall the contextual facts and explain how they shape the text.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><em>Frankenstein</em> blends Gothic horror, Romantic values, and early science fiction. Shelley uses Gothic conventions — isolation, transgression, monstrous creation — but grounds them in real scientific debates about Galvanism. The novel warns that science without moral responsibility leads to destruction.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Industrial Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Shelley wrote during the early <dfn class="term" data-def="The period of rapid industrial and technological change in Britain from the late eighteenth century, transforming society from agricultural to factory-based and raising questions about the human cost of progress.">Industrial Revolution</dfn>, when Britain was being transformed by new technologies, factories, and scientific discoveries. While many celebrated this progress, others feared its consequences: the dehumanisation of workers, the destruction of the natural landscape, and the arrogance of believing that technology could solve every problem.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Victor Frankenstein embodies this anxiety. He is brilliant and ambitious, but he never stops to consider whether he <em>should</em> create life — only whether he <em>can</em>. His famous warning, “Learn from me … how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge,” speaks directly to Shelley’s concern that unchecked scientific progress, without ethical reflection, leads to catastrophe. This theme remains strikingly relevant today.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Mary Shelley’s life was marked by loss. Her mother, the feminist writer Mary Wollstonecraft, died just eleven days after giving birth to her. Shelley grew up without a mother — a theme of absent and failed parenting that runs through <em>Frankenstein</em>. Before she began writing the novel, she had already lost her first child, a premature baby girl who died at just two weeks old. She wrote in her journal: “Dream that my little baby came to life again.” The novel’s central image — a creator who brings something to life and then recoils in horror — resonates powerfully with Shelley’s own experiences of creation, loss, and the desperate wish to reverse death.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Closed Book Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">For Edexcel 1ET0 Component 2, Section A, you will answer a two-part question on <em>Frankenstein</em>. Part (a) asks you to analyse an extract of around 400 words, focusing on how Shelley uses language and structure to create meaning (AO1 + AO2). Part (b) asks you to explore a theme, character, or idea across the whole novel (AO1 + AO2). This is a <dfn class="term" data-def="An exam condition where students do not have a copy of the text and must rely on memorised quotations and knowledge of the whole novel.">closed book</dfn> exam, so you must memorise key quotations. Context is not separately assessed, but weaving contextual understanding into your analysis strengthens your response and lifts your AO1 marks for a personal, informed interpretation.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">Shelley wrote <em>Frankenstein</em> aged eighteen, during the “Year Without a Summer” (1816), inspired by a ghost story competition and real scientific experiments in Galvanism.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The novel blends Gothic horror, Romantic values, and science fiction, reflecting anxieties about the Industrial Revolution and unchecked scientific ambition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Shelley’s personal losses — her mother’s death and the loss of her first child — deeply inform the novel’s themes of creation, abandonment, and the consequences of playing God.</li></ul>
