<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Walton’s Letters: The Frame Narrative</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens not with Victor Frankenstein but with Captain Robert Walton, an Arctic explorer writing letters to his sister Margaret Saville. Walton is on a voyage to the North Pole, driven by an intense desire for discovery and glory. He describes himself as having “voluntarily endured cold, famine, thirst, and want of sleep” in pursuit of his goal. This language of self-sacrifice immediately parallels Victor’s own obsessive ambition. Shelley uses Walton as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A secondary character whose experiences and qualities mirror or contrast with those of the main character, highlighting key themes.">foil</dfn> for Victor — both men are consumed by ambition, isolated from society, and willing to risk everything for knowledge.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Walton’s letters establish the novel’s <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative structure where one story is embedded within another, like nesting dolls. In Frankenstein, Walton’s letters contain Victor’s story, which contains the Creature’s story.">frame narrative</dfn>. Within his letters, Victor tells his story; within Victor’s story, the Creature tells his. This three-layered structure forces the reader to question whose version of events to trust. Walton is not a neutral observer — he admires Victor and describes him as “noble” and “godlike,” yet the story Victor tells reveals a man who is anything but.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain both sides of the contrast described here from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Walton’s frame narrative introduces key themes before Victor even appears: ambition, isolation, and the dangerous pursuit of knowledge. Walton tells Margaret, “I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited.” His language of desire and obsession mirrors Victor’s — Shelley warns us from the first page that unchecked ambition leads to suffering.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Victor’s Childhood and Early Obsession</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Victor grows up in Geneva in a loving, privileged household. His parents are affectionate, his childhood companion Elizabeth Lavenza is devoted to him, and his best friend Henry Clerval provides warmth and companionship. Shelley presents this as an ideal upbringing — everything the Creature will later be denied. Yet even in childhood, Victor shows a dangerous tendency. He becomes obsessed with the works of Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus, and Albertus Magnus — medieval <dfn class="term" data-def="Pre-scientific practices that attempted to transform base metals into gold and discover the elixir of life. Victor’s early fascination with alchemists foreshadows his later desire to conquer death.">alchemists</dfn> who sought to discover the secret of life and the power to overcome death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Victor’s father dismisses these books as “sad trash” but fails to explain why. Shelley implies that this dismissal, without proper guidance, allows Victor’s obsession to grow unchecked. Victor later reflects: “I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation.” The word “ardour” suggests both passion and burning, while “far exceeded moderation” acknowledges that his desire was excessive from the start. His ambition is presented not as something that develops suddenly but as an innate characteristic that was never properly managed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">A pivotal moment in Victor’s childhood is witnessing a lightning bolt destroy an oak tree. This dramatic natural event introduces him to the power of electricity and modern science, replacing his interest in alchemy with a fascination for the “new philosophers” — the real scientists of the Enlightenment. The lightning bolt is deeply symbolic: it represents both the awesome power of nature and the destructive potential of the scientific knowledge Victor will pursue. Shelley uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A warning or indication of a future event in a narrative. Shelley uses foreshadowing throughout to signal the catastrophe Victor’s ambition will cause.">foreshadowing</dfn> here — the same natural force that destroys the tree will, metaphorically, destroy Victor’s life.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Ingolstadt: The University Years</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Victor leaves Geneva for the University of Ingolstadt, where two professors shape his destiny. Professor Krempe dismisses the alchemists Victor admires as charlatans, humiliating him publicly. But Professor Waldman takes a different approach, praising modern chemistry for having “acquired new and almost unlimited powers” and describing scientists who “penetrate into the recesses of nature and show how she works in her hiding-places.” This language of penetration and exposure is significant — it frames science as an act of violation, of forcing nature to reveal her secrets.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Waldman’s words ignite Victor’s ambition. Victor throws himself into his studies with terrifying intensity, abandoning his family, his friends, and his health. He describes himself as having “pursued nature to her hiding-places,” echoing Waldman’s violent language. For two years, he barely leaves his laboratory, cutting himself off from the people who love him. Shelley makes clear that Victor’s isolation is a <em>choice</em> — he actively rejects human connection in favour of his obsessive work.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and describe Victor development across the text in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Victor’s obsession builds gradually: from childhood alchemy, through the lightning bolt, to Waldman’s inspiring lecture. At Ingolstadt, he isolates himself for two years, neglecting family and health. He later admits: “I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation.” Shelley shows that Victor’s downfall begins not with the creation itself but with the unchecked ambition that drives him towards it.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Drive to Create Life</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Victor’s goal becomes nothing less than the conquest of death itself. He begins collecting body parts from “the <dfn class="term" data-def="A building or vault where the bodies of the dead are stored. Victor’s work in charnel houses and dissecting rooms reflects the Gothic tradition of transgressing the boundary between life and death.">charnel house</dfn>” and “the dissecting room,” working among dead bodies in scenes that are both Gothic and deeply unsettling. He describes this period as one where “my eyes were insensible to the charms of nature” and the “summer months passed while I was thus engaged, heart and soul, in one pursuit.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Shelley presents Victor’s ambition as fundamentally self-centred. He imagines that “a new species would bless me as its creator and source.” He does not want to help humanity — he wants to be worshipped. The word “bless” gives him a God-like status, while “source” positions him as the origin of a new form of life. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Excessive pride or arrogance, especially when a character believes they can rival or surpass God or nature. Victor’s desire to create life is the novel’s central act of hubris.">hubris</dfn> — the belief that he can play God — is the novel’s central transgression, and Shelley makes clear it will have devastating consequences.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Shelley deliberately parallels Walton and Victor. Both men are isolated from loved ones, driven by an obsessive desire for discovery, and willing to sacrifice everything for glory. Walton writes: “I would sacrifice my fortune, my existence, my every hope, to the furtherance of my enterprise.” Victor’s story serves as a warning to Walton — and by extension, to the reader — about where such unchecked ambition leads. The critical question is whether Walton will learn from Victor’s mistakes or repeat them. His ultimate decision to turn back from the Arctic suggests he does learn, making him the only character in the novel who heeds Victor’s warning.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n19">Walton’s frame narrative introduces ambition and isolation as key themes. His admiration for Victor creates an unreliable perspective that the reader must navigate.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Victor’s obsession builds from childhood alchemy through the lightning bolt to Waldman’s lecture. His two years of isolation at Ingolstadt show ambition consuming his humanity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">Victor’s hubris is revealed by his desire for a “new species” to “bless me as its creator” — he wants not to serve humanity but to be worshipped as a god.</li></ul>
