--- DESCRIPTION ---
Where materials come from, how they are processed, and how LCA measures environmental impact.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Do Materials Come From?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every material in a product starts as a raw resource extracted from the earth, grown in a field, or harvested from an animal. You need to know the <dfn class="term" data-def="The natural raw material from which a workable material is derived, e.g. trees for timber, ore for metal.">primary source</dfn> of each material category and the main processes involved in converting it into a workable form.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Sources & Origins by Category</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Papers & Boards</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n4">Paper is made from <dfn class="term" data-def="The structural carbohydrate that forms the cell walls of plants, used as the raw fibre in paper production.">cellulose</dfn> fibres derived from wood (mainly softwood trees like spruce and pine) and sometimes grasses such as bamboo. Logs are chipped, pulped in water with chemicals, and the resulting slurry is pressed, dried and rolled into sheets. Recycled paper skips the tree-felling stage by re-pulping waste paper, though each cycle shortens the fibres and reduces quality.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Timber-Based Materials</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n5">Trees are felled and converted into usable planks through <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of cutting a felled tree trunk into planks or boards using different sawing methods.">conversion</dfn> (sawing methods include through-and-through and quarter sawn). The green timber must then be <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of reducing the moisture content of freshly cut timber so it does not warp, split or decay.">seasoned</dfn> — either air-dried (stacked outdoors for months) or kiln-dried (heated in a controlled chamber for days). Manufactured boards like MDF, plywood and chipboard are made from timber waste — fibres, veneers or chips bonded with resin.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Metal-Based Materials</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6">Metals are extracted from <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock that contains a high enough concentration of metal to make extraction economically worthwhile.">ore</dfn> mined from the earth. The ore is refined — iron ore is smelted in a blast furnace to produce pig iron, which is then converted into steel. Aluminium is extracted from bauxite ore using electrolysis, an extremely energy-intensive process. Copper ore is smelted and electrolytically refined to high purity. Alloys (e.g. brass = copper + zinc, stainless steel = iron + chromium + nickel) are created by mixing metals to improve properties.</p>
</div></div>
</div>



<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Polymers</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">Most polymers come from <dfn class="term" data-def="A fossil fuel extracted from underground deposits and refined into fuels, chemicals and polymer feedstocks.">crude oil</dfn>. Crude oil is separated into fractions by <dfn class="term" data-def="Heating crude oil in a column so different hydrocarbons condense at different temperatures, separating them into fractions.">fractional distillation</dfn>. The naphtha fraction is then broken down further by <dfn class="term" data-def="Breaking long hydrocarbon chains into shorter, more useful molecules (monomers) used to make polymers.">cracking</dfn> to produce monomers like ethene and propene. These monomers are then polymerised — joined into long chains — to create materials such as polyethylene, polypropylene and PVC.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Textile-Based Materials</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">Textile fibres come from three main sources. <strong>Animal sources:</strong> wool from sheep, silk from silkworms. <strong>Vegetable sources:</strong> cotton from the cotton plant, linen from flax. <strong>Chemical/synthetic sources:</strong> polyester and nylon from crude oil (same petrochemical route as polymers). Raw fibres are cleaned, carded (aligned), spun into yarn, and then woven or knitted into fabric.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name the primary source and one key processing step for timber, metals and polymers.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>You must know the primary source <strong>and</strong> the main conversion process for each material category: paper (cellulose from wood → pulping), timber (trees → conversion & seasoning), metals (ore → smelting/electrolysis), polymers (crude oil → fractional distillation & cracking), textiles (animal/vegetable/chemical → spinning).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A systematic method for evaluating the environmental impact of a product at every stage of its life, from raw material extraction to disposal.">life cycle assessment</dfn> (LCA) examines the environmental impact of a product at every stage: raw material extraction, processing, manufacture, distribution, use, and end-of-life disposal. It considers energy consumption, water use, carbon emissions, pollution and waste at each stage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and draw the five stages of an LCA from memory in order.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five stages of an LCA are: <strong>1. Raw material extraction</strong> → <strong>2. Processing & manufacture</strong> → <strong>3. Distribution & transport</strong> → <strong>4. Use & maintenance</strong> → <strong>5. End-of-life disposal</strong> (recycle, landfill or incinerate).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Why LCA Matters for Designers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">An LCA helps designers make informed choices. If the biggest environmental impact of a kettle is the energy used to boil water (the "use" stage), then improving insulation to keep water hot longer would have a bigger effect than switching to a lighter plastic for the casing. LCA data can also reveal hidden problems — for example, a "biodegradable" polymer that only breaks down under industrial composting conditions, not in a home compost bin.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Know the source of each material:</strong> paper from cellulose/wood, timber from trees (conversion + seasoning), metals from ore (smelting), polymers from crude oil (distillation + cracking), textiles from animal/vegetable/chemical sources (spinning).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n18"><strong>LCA has five stages:</strong> extraction, processing, distribution, use, and end-of-life — each with its own environmental impact.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19"><strong>LCA guides design decisions:</strong> by revealing where the biggest environmental costs are, designers can focus improvements where they matter most.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n15">LCA questions often ask you to <strong>identify which stage has the greatest environmental impact</strong> for a specific product. Think carefully about the product’s life — for a car, manufacture and fuel use dominate; for a paper cup, raw material extraction and disposal are key. Always explain <em>why</em> a particular stage has the most impact.</p>
