Prototype, batch, mass and continuous production — choosing the right scale for the job.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Scales of Production?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not every product is made the same way. A one-off wedding cake and a million identical Mars bars require completely different manufacturing approaches. You need to know four scales of production: <strong>prototype</strong>, <strong>batch</strong>, <strong>mass</strong> and <strong>continuous</strong>. You must know what each one means, when it is used, and which processes suit each scale.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name all four scales of production with one example product for each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four scales of production are: <strong>prototype</strong> (one-off), <strong>batch</strong> (set quantity), <strong>mass</strong> (large volume on an assembly line) and <strong>continuous</strong> (24/7 non-stop production).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Prototype (One-Off) Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A single, often handmade product created to test a design before committing to full production.">prototype</dfn> is a single item made to test a design. It is usually handmade using general-purpose tools and may take a long time to produce. The cost per unit is high because tooling and labour are not spread across many products. Examples include an architectural model, a bespoke piece of jewellery, or a 3D-printed concept model for a new phone case.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Batch Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Manufacturing a set quantity of identical products before the equipment is reconfigured for a different product.">Batch production</dfn> involves making a set number of identical items. Between batches, the equipment is reset or retooled for a different product. A bakery might produce 200 loaves of white bread, then switch to 150 wholemeal rolls. This is common in textiles (seasonal clothing runs), furniture (limited-edition ranges) and food manufacturing. Jigs and templates are used to maintain consistency within each batch.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Mass Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Producing large quantities of identical products on an assembly line, often with significant automation.">Mass production</dfn> uses assembly lines, automation and dedicated machinery to produce thousands or millions of identical items. The high initial investment in tooling (e.g. injection mould tools costing tens of thousands of pounds) is justified by the low unit cost when spread over huge volumes. Cars, smartphones, plastic bottles and printed circuit boards are all mass-produced. Quality control is built into the line — automated sensors check dimensions, weight and surface finish.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Mass Production Across Material Categories</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Timber:</strong> CNC routers and automated lathes produce identical wooden components at high speed. Flat-pack furniture uses standard panels cut and drilled by machine.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Metals:</strong> Stamping, pressing and die casting produce thousands of identical metal parts. Robotic welding assembles car bodies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Polymers:</strong> Injection moulding is the most common mass-production process — molten polymer is forced into a mould, cooled and ejected. Cycle times can be as short as 30 seconds.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Textiles:</strong> Computer-controlled cutting tables and industrial sewing machines produce garments at volume. Automated looms weave fabric continuously.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Papers & boards:</strong> Offset lithography prints millions of identical sheets. Die-cutting machines stamp out packaging nets at high speed.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Continuous Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Non-stop, 24-hour production of a single product, often involving chemical or process industries.">Continuous production</dfn> runs 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. It is used when stopping and restarting the process would be extremely expensive or impractical. Examples include paper mills (continuous rolls of paper), steel production (blast furnaces run non-stop for years), oil refineries and electricity generation. The unit cost is the lowest of all scales, but flexibility is very limited — the line makes one product and changing it is costly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain the key difference between mass and continuous production.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The main difference between mass and continuous production is that <strong>mass production</strong> can be stopped and restarted (e.g. at the end of a shift), while <strong>continuous production</strong> runs non-stop because halting the process would damage equipment or waste materials.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Choosing the Right Scale</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The choice of production scale depends on several factors: the number of products needed, the acceptable unit cost, the level of customisation required, and the available budget for tooling. A hand-thrown ceramic mug is beautiful but expensive; an injection-moulded plastic cup is cheap but lacks character. Designers must match the scale to the market.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Comparing the Four Scales</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Prototype:</strong> 1 item, highest unit cost, most flexible, general tools, skilled labour.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Batch:</strong> Tens to thousands, moderate unit cost, some flexibility (can switch products between batches), jigs and templates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Mass:</strong> Thousands to millions, low unit cost, high tooling investment, assembly line, significant automation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Continuous:</strong> Non-stop output, lowest unit cost, no flexibility, process cannot easily be stopped.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Four scales:</strong> prototype (one-off), batch (set quantity), mass (assembly line) and continuous (24/7 non-stop) — each suits different product volumes and budgets.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Unit cost falls as volume rises</strong> because tooling and setup costs are spread across more products.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Scale determines process:</strong> hand tools for prototypes, jigs for batch, injection moulding for mass, and rolling mills for continuous.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">When a question asks you to recommend a scale of production, always <strong>name the scale</strong>, <strong>justify your choice</strong> by linking to the product volume and market, and <strong>name a suitable manufacturing process</strong> for that scale. For example: “Batch production using a jig on a pillar drill would be suitable because only 500 units are needed per month.”</p>
