--- DESCRIPTION ---
How designers choose the right material by balancing function, cost, ethics and aesthetics.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Material Selection Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every product begins with a choice: what material should it be made from? A designer must weigh up a range of factors before committing to timber, metal, polymer, textile or paper. The wrong choice can make a product too expensive, too fragile, or harmful to the environment. Exam questions on material selection often ask you to <dfn class="term" data-def="To give reasons for choosing a particular material based on its properties and the product requirements.">justify</dfn> why a specific material is suitable for a given product.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six factors that influence material selection from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The six main factors in material selection are: <strong>functionality</strong>, <strong>aesthetics</strong>, <strong>availability</strong>, <strong>cost</strong>, <strong>environmental impact</strong>, and <strong>social & ethical considerations</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Functionality & Ease of Working</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="How well a material performs the job it is intended to do in a product.">Functionality</dfn> is the starting point. A material must be fit for purpose. A kitchen chopping board needs a food-safe, durable surface — hardwood such as beech works well because it resists scoring and does not taint food. A children's toy needs impact resistance and no sharp edges, making injection-moulded ABS polymer a common choice. Ease of working also matters: if a material is difficult to cut, shape or join, production costs rise.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Aesthetics: Finish, Texture & Colour</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="The visual and tactile qualities of a material, including its colour, texture and surface finish.">Aesthetics</dfn> influence consumer appeal. Oak has a warm grain that suits furniture; brushed stainless steel gives kitchen products a modern feel; polyester fabric can be dyed in vibrant colours for sportswear. Designers match the look and feel of a material to the target market and product purpose.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Availability & Cost</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="How easy it is to source a material from suppliers and whether it is in regular stock.">Availability</dfn> determines whether a material can be sourced reliably. Exotic hardwoods like ebony are beautiful but rare, which drives up cost and raises sustainability concerns. Mild steel, by contrast, is widely available and relatively cheap. Cost also depends on volume: <dfn class="term" data-def="Purchasing materials in large quantities to reduce the unit price.">bulk buying</dfn> reduces the per-unit price, which matters for mass-produced items.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Timber:</strong> Softwoods (pine, spruce) are cheaper than hardwoods (oak, mahogany). MDF and plywood offer a low-cost manufactured alternative.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Metals:</strong> Mild steel is inexpensive; aluminium costs more but is lighter. Copper and brass are costlier still.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Polymers:</strong> Polypropylene and HDPE are among the cheapest. Acrylic and nylon cost more but offer better mechanical properties.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Textiles:</strong> Cotton and polyester are affordable. Silk, wool and Kevlar are premium-priced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Papers & boards:</strong> Cartridge paper and corrugated card are low-cost. Foil-lined board for food packaging costs more due to added barrier layers.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Environmental Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Designers must consider a material's environmental impact throughout its life. Can it be recycled at end of life? Is it made from renewable sources? Timber from <dfn class="term" data-def="The Forest Stewardship Council, an organisation that certifies sustainably managed forests.">FSC</dfn>-certified forests is a responsible choice. Recycled aluminium uses only 5% of the energy needed to produce virgin aluminium, making it a strong environmental option for drinks cans and packaging.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain why recycled aluminium is considered environmentally friendly.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Selecting materials with <strong>end-of-life disposal</strong> in mind is a key exam theme. Products that can be recycled, composted or disassembled for reuse score highly in sustainability assessments.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Social, Cultural & Ethical Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Social responsibility means ensuring a material does not cause harm during extraction, processing or disposal. Products should be safe for workers to manufacture and safe for consumers to use. Cultural sensitivity matters too — certain materials or animal-derived products may be inappropriate for some markets. Ethical sourcing, such as buying FSC timber or Fairtrade cotton, shows a designer is thinking beyond profit.</p>

<div class="collapsible">

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">A furniture company choosing timber should look for the FSC logo, which guarantees the wood comes from responsibly managed forests. A clothing brand might use Fairtrade cotton to ensure farmers receive a fair wage. In metals, conflict minerals (such as cobalt mined in unsafe conditions) are a growing concern — responsible manufacturers audit their supply chains.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Putting It All Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">In practice, material selection is always a trade-off. A lightweight polymer might be cheap and easy to mould, but it is not biodegradable. A natural hardwood is attractive and renewable, but expensive and harder to work. The best designers balance all six factors — functionality, aesthetics, availability, cost, environment and ethics — to find the optimum material for each product.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Six selection factors:</strong> functionality, aesthetics, availability, cost, environmental impact, and social/ethical considerations must all be weighed when choosing a material.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Trade-offs are inevitable:</strong> no single material scores top marks on every factor — designers must prioritise based on the product’s purpose and target market.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>End-of-life matters:</strong> choosing recyclable, reusable or biodegradable materials reduces a product’s environmental footprint.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n23">When a question asks you to <strong>justify</strong> a material choice, structure your answer around at least three selection factors (e.g. functionality, cost, environment). Name the specific material, state the property that makes it suitable, and link that property to the product’s requirements. Avoid vague statements like “it is strong” — say <em>why</em> strength matters for that product.</p>
