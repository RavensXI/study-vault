--- DESCRIPTION ---
How DNA mutations create genetic variation, and why context decides if a change is harmful or not.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Mutation?</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n2">The base sequence of DNA is not perfectly stable. Errors can arise when DNA is copied before cell division, or damage can be caused by environmental factors such as radiation or certain chemicals. A change to the base sequence of DNA is called a <dfn class="term" data-def="A change in the base sequence of DNA. Mutations create new genetic variants and are the ultimate source of all genetic variation.">mutation</dfn>. Mutations are the source of all new genetic variants &mdash; without them, there would be no variation between individuals of the same species.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n3">There are three main types of mutation at the single-base level:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n4"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A mutation in which one base is replaced by a different base, changing a single codon.">Substitution</dfn>:</strong> one base is replaced by a different base (e.g. A replaced by C).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n5"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A mutation in which an extra base is added into the DNA sequence, causing a frameshift from that point onwards.">Insertion</dfn>:</strong> an extra base is added into the sequence.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n6"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A mutation in which a base is removed from the DNA sequence, causing a frameshift from that point onwards.">Deletion</dfn>:</strong> a base is removed from the sequence.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name the three types of mutation, then explain which two cause a frameshift and why that matters.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Substitution mutations change only one codon (triplet). Insertion and deletion mutations shift the reading frame &mdash; every triplet <em>after</em> the mutation point is disrupted, potentially altering every amino acid from that point onwards in the protein chain.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Mutations in Coding DNA</h2>
<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n9">If a mutation occurs in a <dfn class="term" data-def="DNA sequences that contain the instructions for making a protein &mdash; the sections of a gene that are read and translated during protein synthesis.">coding region</dfn> of DNA (within a gene), it may alter the amino acid sequence of the protein produced. There are three possible outcomes:</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Possible Effects of a Mutation in a Gene</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <div class="higher-only">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Harmful:</strong> the mutant protein folds incorrectly or cannot carry out its function. Enzymes may no longer catalyse their reaction; structural proteins may weaken tissues. Example: sickle cell disease, caused by a single base substitution in the gene for haemoglobin, which changes one amino acid and causes the protein to form rigid rods that distort red blood cells.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Neutral:</strong> the mutation either changes a codon to a synonymous codon (one that codes for the same amino acid &mdash; because the genetic code is redundant), or changes an amino acid in a region of the protein that is not critical to its function. The protein still works normally. Many mutations fall into this category.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Beneficial:</strong> rarely, a mutation produces a protein that functions better in particular conditions, or confers a survival advantage on the organism. Natural selection can then spread this variant through a population over many generations. Example: mutations giving bacteria resistance to an antibiotic are beneficial to the bacterium in an environment where that antibiotic is present.</p>
    </div>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Mutations in Non-Coding DNA</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Not all mutations occur within genes. Large portions of the genome are <dfn class="term" data-def="DNA sequences that do not code for proteins but have regulatory roles, controlling when and how much specific genes are expressed.">non-coding DNA</dfn>. These regions control whether genes are switched on or off &mdash; they regulate <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which the information in a gene is used to produce a functional protein. A gene is expressed when it is actively being read and its protein is being made.">gene expression</dfn>.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<p data-narration-id="n15">If a mutation occurs in a non-coding regulatory region, it does not alter the protein sequence directly. Instead, it alters the <em>amount</em> of protein produced, or <em>when</em> the gene is active. For instance, a mutation might silence a tumour-suppressor gene &mdash; the protein remains identical in structure, but the cell no longer produces enough of it to restrain cell division. This is one of the ways cancer can arise from mutations in non-coding regions rather than in the protein-coding sequence itself.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Explain to someone: how can a mutation in non-coding DNA affect the phenotype if it doesn't change the protein sequence?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Mutations in non-coding DNA can affect phenotype by altering gene expression &mdash; changing how much of a protein is made, or whether it is made at all &mdash; without changing the protein&rsquo;s structure. This means the same mutation can be harmful, neutral, or beneficial depending on the context: which gene is affected, in which tissue, and in what environment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Context Determines Consequence</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The same mutation can have entirely different consequences depending on context &mdash; a central idea in understanding genetic variation. The sickle cell allele, for example, is harmful when a person inherits two copies (causing sickle cell disease) but protective against severe malaria when only one copy is inherited. This explains why the allele remains common in populations from malaria-endemic regions: natural selection has maintained it because it confers a survival advantage in heterozygous individuals, even though it is harmful in homozygous form.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19">The environment plays a critical role in determining whether a mutation is disadvantageous. A mutation that impairs a metabolic pathway may be fatal in one nutritional environment but perfectly compatible with life in another where an alternative food source compensates. This interplay between genetic variant and environment underpins much of evolutionary biology and is central to understanding why genetic variation within a species is maintained rather than being eliminated by natural selection.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">It is also important to remember that mutations occur randomly &mdash; they are not directed towards any goal. A bacterium does not develop antibiotic resistance because it &lsquo;wants&rsquo; to survive; rather, random mutations arise all the time, and when an antibiotic is introduced, individuals that already carry a resistance mutation survive and reproduce while others die. The resistance spreads through natural selection, not through any purposeful change. This distinction between random mutation and directed adaptation is one of the most commonly misunderstood aspects of evolution.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n21">Every person&rsquo;s genome contains millions of single-base variants compared to any other person&rsquo;s genome. The vast majority of these are neutral &mdash; they have accumulated over thousands of generations without affecting survival or reproduction. A small number influence disease risk, drug response, or physical characteristics. Modern genome sequencing allows scientists to identify which variants are associated with which outcomes &mdash; but association does not mean direct causation, and most complex traits involve hundreds of variants acting together.</p>

</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Mutations are changes to the DNA base sequence. Substitution changes one base; insertion adds a base; deletion removes a base. Insertions and deletions cause frameshifts, disrupting all subsequent codons.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Mutations in coding DNA may alter the amino acid sequence of a protein, potentially making it non-functional (harmful), unchanged in function (neutral), or better adapted (rarely, beneficial). The outcome depends on which amino acid is affected and whether it is in a critical region.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Mutations in non-coding regulatory DNA can change gene expression without altering the protein sequence itself. Whether a mutation is harmful, neutral or beneficial often depends on the environment and genetic context.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">When explaining the effect of an insertion or deletion mutation, use the term &lsquo;frameshift&rsquo; and explain that <em>all</em> subsequent triplets are altered &mdash; this is the key difference from substitution, which only changes one codon. For questions asking you to evaluate whether a mutation is harmful, neutral or beneficial, always link to the <em>context</em>: a mutation that is harmful in one environment may be beneficial in another. Do not say mutations &lsquo;always&rsquo; cause disease.</p>
