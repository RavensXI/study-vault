--- DESCRIPTION ---
How we use natural resources, potable water treatment, and sustainable development.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Natural Resources and Products</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Humans use the Earth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Materials from the Earth that are used by humans, including water, land, minerals, fossil fuels and the atmosphere. Some are finite (will run out), others are renewable.">natural resources</dfn> to provide warmth, shelter, food, and transport. Some resources are <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources that are being used faster than they can be replaced and will eventually run out, e.g. fossil fuels, metal ores.">finite</dfn> (non-renewable) — they will eventually run out (e.g. fossil fuels, metal ores). Others are <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources that can be replaced at the same rate as they are used, so they will not run out, e.g. timber, cotton.">renewable</dfn> — they can be replaced (e.g. timber, cotton).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Development that meets the needs of current generations without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.">Sustainable development</dfn> means meeting the needs of today’s population without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs. Chemistry plays a vital role in sustainable development by finding alternatives to finite resources and reducing waste.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall the four main strategies for sustainable development, including the example material mentioned.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sustainable development: meet today’s needs without compromising the future. Strategies include using renewable resources, recycling, reducing waste, and developing new materials (e.g. bioplastics).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Potable Water</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Water that is safe to drink. It is not pure water (which contains only H2O) but has sufficiently low levels of dissolved salts and microorganisms.">Potable water</dfn> is water that is safe to drink. It is not the same as pure water — it contains low levels of dissolved substances. In the UK, potable water is produced from fresh water (rivers, reservoirs) by:</p>

<ol>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Filtration</strong> — passing through filter beds to remove large debris, gravel and sand.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sedimentation</strong> — allowing fine particles to settle out.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Sterilisation</strong> — adding chlorine, or using UV light or ozone to kill bacteria and make water safe.</li>
</ol>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">In areas with limited fresh water, sea water can be made potable by <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of removing salt and other dissolved substances from sea water to produce potable water. Methods include distillation and reverse osmosis.">desalination</dfn>. Methods include <strong>distillation</strong> (boiling the water and condensing the steam) and <strong>reverse osmosis</strong> (forcing water through a membrane that blocks salt). Both methods require significant energy, making them expensive.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list the three stages of UK potable water treatment and name the three sterilisation methods used.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK potable water: fresh water → filtration → sedimentation → sterilisation (chlorine/UV/ozone). Desalination converts sea water but requires a lot of energy. Potable ≠ pure — it still contains some dissolved substances.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Waste Water Treatment</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Used water from homes, industry and agriculture that must be treated before being released back into the environment to prevent pollution.">Waste water</dfn> (sewage and industrial water) must be treated before release into the environment. The process includes:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sewage Treatment Steps</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Screening:</strong> large objects (rags, sticks) are removed by passing water through screens.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Sedimentation:</strong> solids settle out as sludge. The liquid effluent is separated.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Biological treatment:</strong> aerobic bacteria break down organic matter in the effluent. The sludge is also digested anaerobically by bacteria, producing biogas (methane) which can be used as fuel.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Final treatment:</strong> the cleaned water is tested and released back into rivers or the sea.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Life Cycle Assessments and Recycling</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n19">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An assessment of the environmental impact of a product at every stage: extracting raw materials, manufacturing, using the product, and disposal.">life cycle assessment</dfn> (LCA) evaluates the environmental impact of a product at every stage: extracting raw materials, manufacturing and packaging, use, and disposal (landfill, recycling, or incineration). LCAs help companies make products more sustainable.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n20">Reducing, reusing and recycling materials conserves finite resources, reduces energy consumption, and decreases the amount of waste going to landfill. However, recycling has its own costs (collection, sorting, processing energy) and LCAs can be subjective.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Sustainable development balances current needs with future resources; chemistry develops alternatives to finite resources.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Potable water: filtration &rarr; sedimentation &rarr; sterilisation. Desalination for sea water (energy-intensive).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Life cycle assessments evaluate environmental impact at every stage; reduce, reuse, recycle to conserve resources.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Remember that potable water is NOT the same as pure water. In the exam, you may be asked to compare the processes for producing potable water from fresh water vs sea water. The key difference is that desalination (from sea water) requires much more energy because dissolved salts must be removed, whereas fresh water treatment mainly removes debris and pathogens.</p>
