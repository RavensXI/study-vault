--- DESCRIPTION ---
How crude oil is separated by fractional distillation, alkane properties, and cracking.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Crude Oil and Hydrocarbons</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A finite resource formed from the remains of ancient marine organisms over millions of years. It is a mixture of many different hydrocarbons.">Crude oil</dfn> is a finite resource formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient marine organisms. It is a mixture of many different <dfn class="term" data-def="Molecules made up of hydrogen and carbon atoms only. Alkanes are a type of hydrocarbon with single C–C bonds.">hydrocarbons</dfn> (molecules containing only hydrogen and carbon atoms).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The hydrocarbons in crude oil are separated by <dfn class="term" data-def="The separation of crude oil into fractions based on their boiling points. The oil is heated and vapours rise up the column, condensing at different levels.">fractional distillation</dfn>. The crude oil is heated until most of it evaporates. The vapours rise up a tall column that is hot at the bottom and cool at the top. Different fractions condense at different heights depending on their boiling points: smaller molecules condense near the top (low boiling point) and larger molecules condense near the bottom (high boiling point).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall which hydrocarbons (smaller or larger) have lower boiling points and are collected at the top of the column.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation. Smaller hydrocarbons have lower boiling points, are less viscous, more flammable, and are collected near the top of the column. Larger hydrocarbons have higher boiling points and are collected near the bottom.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Alkanes</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">Most hydrocarbons in crude oil are <dfn class="term" data-def="A family of hydrocarbons with single covalent bonds between carbon atoms. Their general formula is CnH(2n+2). Examples: methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6).">alkanes</dfn>. They have the general formula \(C_nH_{2n+2}\). The first four are:</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7">Methane: \(CH_4\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8">Ethane: \(C_2H_6\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9">Propane: \(C_3H_8\)</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">Butane: \(C_4H_{10}\)</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n11">As the carbon chain gets longer: boiling point <strong>increases</strong>, viscosity <strong>increases</strong> (thicker), and flammability <strong>decreases</strong> (harder to ignite). These trends are due to stronger <dfn class="term" data-def="Weak attractive forces between molecules. Larger molecules have stronger intermolecular forces, giving them higher boiling points.">intermolecular forces</dfn> between the larger molecules.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n12">Hydrocarbons are used as fuels because they release energy when burned (combustion). <strong>Complete combustion</strong> (plenty of oxygen) produces carbon dioxide and water:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">$$CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Incomplete combustion</strong> (limited oxygen) produces carbon monoxide (toxic) and/or carbon (soot), as well as water. Carbon monoxide is dangerous because it is colourless, odourless, and binds to haemoglobin, preventing oxygen transport.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Cracking</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Breaking down long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful molecules by heating with a catalyst (catalytic cracking) or with steam (steam cracking).">Cracking</dfn> is the process of breaking down long-chain hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful molecules. There is more demand for shorter-chain hydrocarbons (fuels like petrol) than for longer-chain ones. Cracking also produces <dfn class="term" data-def="Hydrocarbons with at least one carbon-carbon double bond (C=C). They are more reactive than alkanes and are used to make polymers.">alkenes</dfn>, which are used to make polymers and other chemicals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and write the word equation for cracking, then name the two methods and what alkenes are used to make.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cracking: long alkane &rarr; shorter alkane + alkene. Methods: catalytic cracking (hot catalyst) or steam cracking (high temperature + steam). Alkenes have a C=C double bond and are used to make polymers.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Alkenes and Testing for Unsaturation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Alkenes contain a carbon-carbon double bond (\(C=C\)) and have the general formula \(C_nH_{2n}\). They are described as <strong>unsaturated</strong>. You can test for the presence of a \(C=C\) double bond using <strong>bromine water</strong>: it turns from <strong>orange to colourless</strong> with an alkene (the bromine adds across the double bond). Alkanes do not decolourise bromine water because they are <strong>saturated</strong> (no double bonds).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation based on boiling points; smaller molecules collect at the top.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Alkanes: \(C_nH_{2n+2}\), saturated, single bonds. As chain length increases: BP increases, viscosity increases, flammability decreases.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Cracking breaks long alkanes into shorter alkanes + alkenes. Bromine water test: orange &rarr; colourless = alkene present.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">Make sure you can explain the trends in properties as chain length increases and link them to intermolecular forces. For cracking, always give both types of products (a shorter alkane AND an alkene). The bromine water test is a favourite exam question — state the colour change (orange to colourless) and what it shows (presence of a C=C double bond).</p>
