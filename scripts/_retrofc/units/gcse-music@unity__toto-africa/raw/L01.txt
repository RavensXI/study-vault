<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Background to Toto — Africa</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A song recorded by the American rock band Toto in 1981, released as a single in 1982. It is a soft-rock love song with features of African music.">Africa</dfn> is a song recorded by the American rock band Toto in 1981 for their fourth studio album, <em>Toto IV</em>. It is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A style of rock music with a gentle, melodic sound, often featuring synthesizers and smooth vocal harmonies.">soft-rock</dfn> love song with features of African music. The song was written by band members David Paich (born 1954) and Jeff Porcaro (born 1954, died 1992).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Africa was released as the third single from the album on 30th September 1982 through Columbia Records. It reached number one in America for a single week on 5th February 1983. It also reached the top ten in the UK, Canada, Ireland, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Switzerland. In 2012, music magazine NME placed Africa at number 32 on its list of “50 Most Explosive Choruses”.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about instrumentation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Instrumentation: rock band with drum kit and additional percussion, lead and bass guitars, synthesizers, male lead vocals and male backing vocals. The instrumental sonorities include xylophones and marimbas, suggesting the sounds of African pitched percussion instruments.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Overall Musical Elements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Before diving into the introduction and Verse 1, here are the key musical elements that apply across the whole song:</p>
<ul>

  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Time signature:</strong> 2/2 (alla breve).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Dynamics:</strong> Most of the song is <em>mezzo-forte</em>, whilst the choruses are <em>forte</em>.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Form and structure:</strong> Verse–chorus form — Intro, Verse 1, Chorus 1, Link 1, Verse 2, Chorus 2, Link 2, Instrumental, Chorus 3, Chorus 4, Outro.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Introduction (Bars 1–4)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The introduction is in the key of B major (though note the chord revision sheet labels the intro chords in A major context as ♭VII, vi and ii). It is 4 bars long in the vocal score, but the recording also has an additional 8-bar percussion introduction before the scored intro begins. This percussion section uses layered rhythm parts and <dfn class="term" data-def="A short musical pattern that repeats continuously throughout a section or piece.">ostinatos</dfn>, showing African musical influence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The scored introduction contains only three chords: ♭VII (A), vi (G#m) and ii (C#m). It is built from two riffs, each lasting two bars:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 5 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Riff a</strong> (bars 1–2): A distinctive <dfn class="term" data-def="Placing rhythmic emphasis on beats or parts of beats that are not normally stressed, creating a feeling of rhythmic surprise.">syncopated</dfn> rhythm pattern, mostly repeating chord ♭VII (A) and concluding with chords vi (G#m) and ii (C#m). <strong>Riff b</strong> (bars 3–4, with an anacrusis): Uses an ostinato pattern of five notes based on the E major <dfn class="term" data-def="A musical scale with five notes per octave, most commonly notes 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 of the major scale.">pentatonic scale</dfn> over a sustained chord ii (C#m).</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Both riffs have their origins in traditional African music. They use syncopation, a pentatonic scale, and irregular ostinato groupings that cut across the 2/2 time signature. The instrumental sounds — including xylophones and marimbas — imitate the pitched percussion instruments found across the African continent.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/gcse-music/toto-africa/diagram_africa_overview_introduction_and_verse_1.jpg" alt="Diagram: Africa: Overview, Introduction and Verse 1" loading="lazy"></figure>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Riff a is extremely important to the song’s structure. With the exception of the chorus, it appears in every other section. Riff b is developed later in the instrumental section. On the album version, the 4-bar intro section is repeated four times (giving 8 bars percussion + 16 bars of Riff a/b = 24 bars total). The single edit is shorter.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Verse 1 (Bars 5–39)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Verse 1 is 35 bars long and is in B major (with 5 sharps: F#, C#, G#, D# and A#, but all the A notes in this section are A naturals). The verse falls into four phrases:</p>
<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n21">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 1</div>
    <h4>Bars 5–13 (9 bars)</h4>

  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 2</div>

    <p>“She’s coming in twelve thirty flight. Moonlit wings reflect the stars that guide me t’ward salvation.”</p>

  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 3</div>
    <h4>Bars 23–31 (9 bars)</h4>
    <p>“I stopped an old man along the way, hoping to find some old forgotten words or ancient melodies.”</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">Phrase 4</div>
    <h4>Bars 32–39 (8 bars)</h4>
    <p>“He turned to me as if to say, ‘Hurry boy, it’s waiting there for you.’”</p>
  </div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Why Are the Phrases 9 Bars Long?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">A 9-bar phrase is unusual in popular music. The reason is that <dfn class="term" data-def="A short repeated phrase in popular music and jazz, typically used as an introduction or refrain in a song.">Riff a</dfn> begins as the vocal line ends in bar 8 each time. This creates an overlap where the instrumental riff fills the gap before the next vocal phrase starts, adding an extra bar to each phrase.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">The melody for the first three phrases is also unusual. Each phrase falls into two vocal lines of three and five bars, ending with a silent bar. The melody is fairly low in the voice, moves <dfn class="term" data-def="Moving by step from one note to the next adjacent note, rather than by leap.">conjunctly</dfn> (by step), uses a syncopated rhythm and is mostly <dfn class="term" data-def="A vocal style where each syllable of text is set to one note of music.">syllabic</dfn> (one note per syllable).</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Verse 1 Harmony</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which the chords change in a piece of music.">harmonic rhythm</dfn> of the first three phrases is slow — chords change only once per bar, except the last bar where Riff a’s distinctive chord pattern appears. The 9-bar chord pattern for phrases 1–3 is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Bar 5:</strong> I (B) → <strong>Bar 6:</strong> iii (D#m) → <strong>Bar 7:</strong> vi (G#m) → <strong>Bar 8:</strong> Ic (B/F#) → <strong>Bar 9:</strong> ♭VIIc (A/E) → <strong>Bar 10:</strong> iv9d (E/F#) → <strong>Bar 11:</strong> vi (G#m) → <strong>Bars 12–13:</strong> Riff a</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Phrase 4 — The Different Ending</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The final phrase (bars 32–39) is only 8 bars long. It begins the same way as the first three phrases. However, in the fourth bar the melody begins an octave higher than previously stated. A backing vocalist harmonises the melody, moving in <dfn class="term" data-def="Two or more parts moving in the same direction at the same time, maintaining a fixed interval between them.">parallel motion</dfn> at an interval of a 3rd below.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Harmonically, chord vi (G#m) is sustained for an extra bar. Riff a is then stated in bars 36–37, and chord ii (C#m) sustains until the end of the phrase. The chord pattern is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Bar 32:</strong> I (B) → <strong>Bar 33:</strong> iii (D#m) → <strong>Bar 34:</strong> vi (G#m) → <strong>Bar 35:</strong> vi (G#m) → <strong>Bars 36–37:</strong> Riff a → <strong>Bar 38:</strong> ii (C#m) → <strong>Bar 39:</strong> ii (C#m)</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define remember: All F, C in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Remember: All F, C and G chords in the song are sharp minor (#m) chords. The D chords are also sharp minor 7ths (D#m7) but only in the verse and instrumental sections. Watch out for slash chords — the first letter is the chord name, the second letter is the bass note.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n36">The introduction uses two riffs — Riff a (syncopated chords ♭VII, vi, ii) and Riff b (pentatonic ostinato over chord ii) — both influenced by traditional African music through syncopation, pentatonic scales and irregular groupings.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">Verse 1 has four phrases: three 9-bar phrases (unusual length caused by Riff a overlapping with the vocal ending) and one 8-bar phrase with a different harmonic and melodic ending.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">The verse melody is low, conjunct, syncopated and syllabic, with a slow harmonic rhythm (one chord per bar). Phrase 4 adds a backing vocal harmonising a 3rd below and shifts the melody up an octave.</li>
</ul>
