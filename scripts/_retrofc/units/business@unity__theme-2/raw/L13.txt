<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Business Calculations Matter?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the Edexcel GCSE Business exam, you will be expected to perform calculations without being given the formulas. Knowing the key formulas and understanding what the numbers mean is essential — not just for calculation questions, but also for “Discuss” and “Evaluate” questions where you need to interpret financial data and use it as evidence. This lesson covers the five core calculations: <dfn class="term" data-def="The profit a business makes after deducting the direct costs of making or buying its products from sales revenue.">gross profit</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The profit remaining after all costs (cost of sales and operating costs) have been deducted from sales revenue.">net profit</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of sales revenue that is gross profit, showing how much of every £1 of sales is kept after direct costs.">gross profit margin</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of sales revenue that is net profit, showing how much of every £1 of sales the business actually keeps after all costs.">net profit margin</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A calculation that measures the average annual profit of an investment as a percentage of the initial cost, used to compare different projects.">average rate of return</dfn> (ARR).</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: will Edexcel provide formulas? Why should you still write them?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>You will <strong>not</strong> be given formulas in the Edexcel exam. Quoting the correct formula before working through a calculation can earn you valuable marks, even if your final answer is slightly wrong.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Gross Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="The direct costs involved in making or purchasing the products a business sells, such as raw materials, stock purchases, and delivery of stock.">cost of sales</dfn> includes the direct costs tied to the products being sold — the cost of buying stock from a supplier, delivery charges for that stock, and packaging. It does <strong>not</strong> include rent, wages, marketing, or other running costs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The formula for gross profit is:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Without looking, write down the formula to calculate Gross Profit.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Gross Profit = Sales Revenue − Cost of Sales</strong></p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Gross Profit</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">A shop sells 400 hoodies at £28 each. The direct cost per hoodie is £15.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Sales Revenue = 400 × £28 = <strong>£11,200</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Cost of Sales = 400 × £15 = <strong>£6,000</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Step 3:</strong> Gross Profit = £11,200 − £6,000 = <strong>£5,200</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n12">If a business wants to increase gross profit, it can either raise its <dfn class="term" data-def="The total income a business receives from selling its products or services, calculated as price multiplied by quantity sold.">sales revenue</dfn> (by selling more or increasing prices) or reduce its cost of sales (for example, by finding a cheaper supplier).</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Net Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Net profit goes one step further than gross profit. It deducts <dfn class="term" data-def="The indirect costs of running a business, such as rent, salaries, insurance, marketing, and utility bills.">operating costs</dfn> (rent, salaries, insurance, marketing, utility bills) from gross profit to show the money that is actually left at the end.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The formula is:</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and try to recall the 2 formulas for calculating Net Profit.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Net Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Costs</strong></p>
<p>Alternatively: Net Profit = Sales Revenue − Cost of Sales − Operating Costs</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Net Profit (EuroParts)</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17">EuroParts sells 340 batteries at £125 each.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Sales Revenue</strong> = 340 × £125 = <strong>£42,500</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Cost of Sales</strong> = £24,480</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Gross Profit</strong> = £42,500 − £24,480 = <strong>£18,020</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Operating Expenses & Interest</strong> = £14,450</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Net Profit</strong> = £18,020 − £14,450 = <strong>£3,570</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Despite strong gross profit, net profit is much lower because operating expenses consume most of the money.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Businesses often say net profit is the most important profit because it represents the money that is actually left. This leftover money can be paid to owners or shareholders, or reinvested back into the business to fund growth.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_generated_cbc7a951.png" alt="Generated diagram for Business Calculations"><figcaption>Generated diagram for Business Calculations</figcaption></figure>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Gross Profit Margin (GPM%)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Gross profit margin tells you how much gross profit a business makes for every £1 of sales. It is expressed as a percentage, making it easy to compare performance over time or against competitors.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="From memory, what does a GPM of 40% mean in terms of revenue and cost of sales?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Gross Profit Margin (%) = (Gross Profit ÷ Sales Revenue) × 100</strong></p>
<p>A GPM of 40% means for every £1 sold, 40p is gross profit and 60p covers the cost of sales.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Gross Profit Margin</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n28">A business has sales revenue of £18,500 and cost of sales of £13,690.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Gross Profit = £18,500 − £13,690 = <strong>£4,810</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Step 2:</strong> GPM = (£4,810 ÷ £18,500) × 100 = <strong>26.00%</strong></p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Companies use GPM to track performance over time, compare the profitability of different products, set prices, control direct costs, and check competitiveness against industry rivals. If GPM is falling, it could mean supplier prices have risen, the business is discounting too heavily, or it has shifted to selling lower-margin products.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Net Profit Margin (NPM%)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Net profit margin shows how much net profit a business makes for every £1 of sales after <strong>all</strong> costs have been paid — not just the direct cost of sales, but also operating costs like rent, wages, and marketing.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Close this and state what an NPM of 8% means for each pound of sales.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Net Profit Margin (%) = (Net Profit ÷ Sales Revenue) × 100</strong></p>
<p>An NPM of 8% means for every £1 of sales, the business keeps just 8p as actual profit.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: Net Profit Margin</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n35">A business has sales revenue of £435 million, cost of sales of £315 million, and operating costs & interest of £95 million.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Gross Profit = £435m − £315m = <strong>£120m</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Step 2:</strong> Net Profit = £120m − £95m = <strong>£25m</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Step 3:</strong> NPM = (£25m ÷ £435m) × 100 = <strong>5.75%</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n39">This tells us that for every £1 of sales revenue, the business keeps only 5.75p as actual profit. Operating costs are consuming a large share of gross profit.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n40">The key difference between GPM and NPM is what they measure. GPM shows how profitably a business is selling its products (are the direct costs under control?). NPM shows how well the business controls <strong>all</strong> its costs. A business could have a high GPM but a low NPM if its operating costs — such as rent, wages, or marketing — are very high.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n41">Average Rate of Return (ARR)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n42">When a business has limited money and several possible investment projects, it needs a way to compare them. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A financial calculation comparing the average annual profit of an investment to its initial cost, expressed as a percentage.">average rate of return</dfn> (ARR) calculates how profitable an investment will be relative to its cost, expressed as a percentage.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n43" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what are the three steps for calculating ARR (%)?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>ARR (%) = (Average Annual Profit ÷ Cost of Investment) × 100</strong></p>
<p>The 3-step method: (1) Add up all annual profits and divide by the number of years to get average annual profit. (2) Divide average annual profit by the initial cost. (3) Multiply by 100 to get a percentage.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Worked Example: ARR (Jacksons PLC)</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n44">Jacksons PLC is comparing two investment options:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n45"><strong>Project A</strong> — Extend existing factory. Cost: £60m. Lifespan: 25 years. Total profit expected: £720m.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n46"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Average Annual Profit = £720m ÷ 25 = <strong>£28.8m</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n47"><strong>Step 2 & 3:</strong> ARR = (£28.8m ÷ £60m) × 100 = <strong>48.00%</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n48"><strong>Project B</strong> — Purchase new equipment. Cost: £90m. Lifespan: 40 years. Total profit expected: £2,250m.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n49"><strong>Step 1:</strong> Average Annual Profit = £2,250m ÷ 40 = <strong>£56.25m</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n50"><strong>Step 2 & 3:</strong> ARR = (£56.25m ÷ £90m) × 100 = <strong>62.50%</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n51">Project B has the higher ARR (62.50% vs 48.00%), so based purely on the numbers, it would be the better investment. It also generates income for 40 years compared to just 25 years for Project A.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n52">The project with the highest ARR percentage is usually the one a business should choose because it generates the greatest return on the money invested. However, ARR is based on predicted profits, which may turn out to be inaccurate, and it does not account for when the profits arrive — getting money sooner is generally better for cash flow.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n53">Interpreting Financial Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n54">Calculations alone are not enough — the exam also tests your ability to <strong>interpret</strong> what the numbers mean. When you compare financial data across two time periods or two businesses, always look at the trend and explain <strong>why</strong> the figures have changed.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Interpretation Example: Falling GPM Despite Rising Revenue</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n55">A clothing shop has sales of £120,000 this year with a GPM of 30%. Next year, sales rise to £140,000 but GPM falls to 25%.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n56"><strong>This year:</strong> Gross Profit = 30% of £120,000 = £36,000</p>
<p data-narration-id="n57"><strong>Next year:</strong> Gross Profit = 25% of £140,000 = £35,000</p>
<p data-narration-id="n58">Despite sales revenue increasing by £20,000, gross profit actually fell by £1,000. This suggests the business is facing higher direct costs, offering more discounts, or has shifted to selling lower-margin products. Rising revenue does not always mean rising profit.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n59">When interpreting data in the exam, state the calculation, identify the change or trend, and then explain a possible reason. Common mistakes include calculating correctly but then failing to say what the result actually means for the business.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n61">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n62">Five essential formulas: Gross Profit (SR − CoS), Net Profit (GP − OC), GPM% (GP ÷ SR × 100), NPM% (NP ÷ SR × 100), and ARR% (Average Annual Profit ÷ Cost of Investment × 100).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n63">GPM measures how profitably a business sells its products; NPM measures how well it controls all its costs — a high GPM with a low NPM means operating costs are too high.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n64">Always show your working, state the formula, and interpret what the result means for the business — a number without explanation will not earn full marks.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n60">For calculation questions, <strong>always show your working</strong> — you can earn marks for the correct formula and method even if your final number is wrong. Write out the formula first, substitute the numbers, then calculate. For “Discuss” and “Evaluate” questions that include financial data, use the numbers as evidence: calculate the GPM, NPM, or ARR and then interpret what the result means for the business rather than just stating the figure.</p>
