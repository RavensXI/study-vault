--- DESCRIPTION ---
Distribution channels, retailers, e-tailers, and choosing how products reach customers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is ‘Place’ in the Marketing Mix?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In business, <dfn class="term" data-def="The element of the marketing mix concerned with how a product reaches the customer, including distribution channels and selling methods.">place</dfn> refers to how a business gets its product or service to the customer. It covers the methods of distribution a business uses — where and how customers can buy the product. Getting place right is essential because even a brilliant product will fail if customers cannot access it easily.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Edexcel specification requires you to understand two main methods of distribution: <strong>retailers</strong> (traditional bricks-and-mortar shops) and <strong>e-tailers</strong> (businesses selling online through e-commerce). You also need to understand the broader distribution channels that connect manufacturers to consumers.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the other 3Ps of the marketing mix besides Place.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Place</strong> is one of the 4Ps of the marketing mix (Product, Price, Promotion, Place). It answers the question: <em>How does the product get from the manufacturer to the customer?</em></p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Distribution Channels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The route a product takes from the manufacturer to the final consumer, which may involve intermediaries such as wholesalers and retailers.">distribution channel</dfn> is the route a product takes from the manufacturer to the consumer. There are three main distribution channels, each with different advantages and drawbacks.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Channel 1: Direct Selling</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A distribution method where the manufacturer sells products straight to the consumer with no intermediary involved.">Direct selling</dfn> involves only the manufacturer and the consumer — no third party is involved. The manufacturer produces the product and supplies it directly to the customer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Advantages:</strong> The manufacturer keeps all the profit (no middleman takes a cut). They also have greater control and flexibility over how products are distributed and priced.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Drawbacks:</strong> Consumers do not typically buy in large quantities, so total sales volumes tend to be lower. Distribution costs per sale are higher because the manufacturer must deliver to individual customers rather than in bulk.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Example:</strong> Hannah runs a handmade candle business producing 20 candles per week. Direct selling suits her because she has a small output and wants to keep full control over pricing and customer relationships.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Channel 2: Retailing</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the retailing channel, a manufacturer produces goods and sells them in bulk to a <dfn class="term" data-def="A business that sells goods directly to consumers through a physical shop or bricks-and-mortar premises.">retailer</dfn>, who then sells them on to consumers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Advantages:</strong> Retailers have a wider reach, helping the manufacturer sell to more customers. Distribution costs fall because products are delivered in bulk to each retailer rather than individually to each consumer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Drawbacks:</strong> Retailers need to make a profit, so they pay the manufacturer less per unit than a consumer would. This squeezes profit margins for the manufacturer.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Channel 3: Wholesaling</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A business that buys goods from manufacturers in very large quantities and sells them on to retailers, acting as an intermediary in the distribution channel.">Wholesalers</dfn> buy products from manufacturers in very large quantities. They then sell these on to various retailers — many of which are independents — who sell the products to consumers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Advantages:</strong> Manufacturers can guarantee high sales volumes because wholesalers purchase in bulk. Distribution costs are lower because the manufacturer ships to one wholesaler instead of many retailers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Drawbacks:</strong> Two intermediaries are involved (wholesaler and retailer), so profit margins are squeezed even further. The manufacturer also loses control over which retailers end up selling its products.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Retailers — Selling Direct to the Consumer</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">A retailer is a business that sells goods directly to consumers through a physical, ‘bricks-and-mortar’ shop. High street chains like River Island, Tesco, and John Lewis are all retailers. Customers visit the shop, browse products, and take them home immediately.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Without looking, state how independent retailers compete if not on price.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Independent retailers</strong> are shops that are NOT part of a chain. They are usually smaller businesses started by the owner. They often sell niche products at higher prices and compete on customer service rather than price.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Advantages of being a retailer:</strong> Shopping is an enjoyable experience customers can share with friends and family. Customers get instant satisfaction — they leave with the product straight away. Retailers work well for products customers want to see, touch, or try before buying, such as jewellery or caravans. Sales staff can offer personalised help and advice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Disadvantages of being a retailer:</strong> Shops are only open during the day, and customers may be too busy to visit. Customers may have to queue or carry heavy bags. Retailers often charge higher prices than e-tailers because of the cost of running physical premises (rent, rates, staff).</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">E-tailers — Selling Online Through E-Commerce</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A business that sells goods directly to consumers via the internet, operating through a website or app rather than physical premises.">e-tailer</dfn> is a business that sells goods directly to consumers via the internet. <dfn class="term" data-def="The buying and selling of goods and services over the internet.">E-commerce</dfn> has transformed how businesses reach customers. Some e-tailers are purely online (like Amazon), while others are ‘clicks and mortar’ businesses that have both physical stores and an online shop (like Sainsbury’s, Next, and Argos).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Advantages of being an e-tailer:</strong> A business can start with a smaller investment because there are no premises or large staff costs. It can sell a much larger product range than a physical shop. Prices can undercut competitors because fixed costs are lower (no rent on shops). There is huge potential to grow rapidly and reach a global marketplace.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Disadvantages of being an e-tailer:</strong> It is hard to build trust with customers when there is no face-to-face interaction. Website development and maintenance costs can be high. Online security and fraud are ongoing concerns. The business is only as strong as its delivery service — late deliveries can damage its reputation.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall 2 ways e-commerce can increase a business's profit.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>E-commerce can increase a business’s <strong>sales</strong> (the shop is open 24/7 and can serve customers worldwide), reduce <strong>costs</strong> (no rent on physical premises, fewer staff), and therefore boost <strong>profit</strong>. This is why many retailers now also operate as e-tailers.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Choosing the Right Distribution Channel</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Businesses must consider several factors when choosing their distribution channel. A small producer like Hannah’s candle business may prefer direct selling to keep control and maximise profit per unit. A growing business like Luke’s nutrition shop, ‘Peak Protein’, may need to switch from direct selling to retailing or e-tailing to reach more customers and break through a sales plateau.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The right channel depends on the product type, the size of the business, the target market, and cost considerations. Many modern businesses use a combination of channels — for example, selling through their own website (direct), through retailers, and through wholesalers — to maximise their reach.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Case Study: SportShoes.com</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n30">SportShoes.com relies solely on e-commerce with no physical premises. This means lower overhead costs, allowing them to offer competitive prices on trainers. However, they face challenges: free returns increase variable costs, and they miss out on customers who want to try shoes on before buying. If delivery is late, their reputation suffers because they have no physical stores to fall back on.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">Place is about how products reach customers — the three main distribution channels are direct selling, retailing, and wholesaling, each with trade-offs between control, cost, and reach.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Retailers sell through physical shops (instant satisfaction, but higher costs), while e-tailers sell online (lower costs and global reach, but trust and delivery challenges).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">The best distribution channel depends on the product, the business size, and the target market — many businesses now use a combination of channels.</li></ul>
