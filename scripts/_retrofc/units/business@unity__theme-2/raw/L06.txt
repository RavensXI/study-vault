--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses choose pricing strategies and the factors that influence pricing decisions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Pricing?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Deciding the amount that is required as payment for a product or service.">Pricing</dfn> means deciding how much to charge customers for a product or service. It is one of the four elements of the marketing mix (product, price, promotion, place) and has a direct impact on revenue, profit, and how customers perceive a business.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">An important distinction in business is the difference between <strong>price</strong> and <strong>cost</strong>. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The amount a customer pays to buy a product or service.">price</dfn> is what the customer pays. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The expenses a business incurs to produce or acquire a product, including raw materials, labour, and overheads.">cost</dfn> is what the business pays to make or acquire the product, including raw materials, labour, and overheads. A business must set its price higher than its costs in order to make a profit.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and define 'price' and 'cost' in a business context, recalling which one customers pay.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Price ≠ Cost.</strong> In business, the price is what the customer pays and the cost is what the business spends to produce or buy the product. Mixing these up in the exam is a common mistake — always use the correct term.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Pricing Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">A <dfn class="term" data-def="The method a business uses to decide on the price to charge for a product or service.">pricing strategy</dfn> is the method a business uses to set the price of its products. Different strategies suit different situations depending on the market, the product, and the business’s objectives. The Edexcel specification requires you to know five key strategies: price skimming, cost-plus pricing, penetration pricing, competitive pricing, and loss leader pricing.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing strategy where a business sets a high price when a product is first launched and then gradually lowers the price over time.">Price skimming</dfn> involves setting a high price for a new product when it is first launched, then gradually lowering the price over time. It is commonly used in the electronics industry — for example, Microsoft used price skimming when launching the Xbox One. The initial high price targets “early adopters” who are willing to pay a premium to have the product first.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Advantages:</strong> It is a very profitable strategy because early adopters pay top price. The high price suggests quality, which supports the brand image. It also helps the business recoup expensive research and development (R&D) costs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> The high launch price may put some customers off or cause them to delay their purchase. As the price drops, early customers who paid full price may feel frustrated.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Cost-Plus Pricing</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing strategy where the total cost of producing a product is calculated and then a percentage mark-up is added on top to determine the selling price.">Cost-plus pricing</dfn> is calculated by working out the total cost to produce a product and then adding a profit margin on top. This can be done by adding a fixed amount in pounds (e.g. £27 profit per unit) or by adding a percentage mark-up (e.g. 25% on top of the cost). It is most commonly used in the food industry, where ingredient costs are easy to calculate — for example, Pizza Hut can work out exactly how much it costs to make one pizza and then add a mark-up.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Advantages:</strong> It makes sure the business covers all of its costs. It helps the business calculate its break-even point and margin of safety. It is also flexible — the price can be adjusted if the cost of raw materials rises.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> The business may have been able to charge a higher price but doesn’t consider factors like level of demand, meaning potential profit is missed. It can also be difficult to accurately calculate all costs.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing strategy where a business sets a low initial price for a new product to quickly attract customers and gain market share, then raises the price over time.">Penetration pricing</dfn> is the opposite of price skimming. The business sets a low initial price when a product is first launched to attract customers quickly and build market share. Once the product is established, the price is gradually raised. Disney+ used penetration pricing when it launched its streaming service at a low monthly cost to draw subscribers away from competitors like Netflix.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Advantages:</strong> It is a good way for a business to establish itself as a market leader. Low prices encourage word-of-mouth recommendations, which spread quickly. Prices can be raised once the promotion period is over and customers are loyal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Profit margins are very low during the launch period. There is no way to tell whether customers would have bought the product at a higher price anyway. Some customers may leave when prices increase.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Competitive Pricing & Loss Leaders</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing strategy where a business sets its prices at a similar level to its competitors.">Competitive pricing</dfn> means setting prices at a similar level to competitors. This is common in markets where products are very similar, such as petrol stations, where prices only differ by a few pence per litre. Because prices are so close, customers must judge businesses on non-price factors such as quality of service, speed, or extras — for example, a Costa Express machine in a petrol station.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain how competitive pricing can lead to 'shrinkflation', and why this happens.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Competitive pricing</strong> prevents <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where competing businesses repeatedly lower their prices to undercut each other, often reducing profits for all businesses involved.">price wars</dfn> and keeps customers from switching to rival brands. However, it means a higher price may have been possible, and rising costs must be absorbed by the business rather than passed on to the customer. This can lead to <dfn class="term" data-def="When a business reduces the size or quantity of a product while keeping the price the same, so the customer effectively pays more per unit.">shrinkflation</dfn> — where the product gets smaller but the price stays the same.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing strategy where a product is sold below cost to attract customers into a store, with the aim that they will buy other, more profitable items.">Loss leader pricing</dfn> involves selling a product below cost to attract customers into the store. The aim is that customers will then buy other, more profitable items. Supermarkets frequently use loss leaders — for example, Costco is well known for selling its rotisserie chickens at a loss to drive footfall. Loss leaders are often placed at the front of the store to attract passing customers and are frequently seasonal (like disposable barbecues in summer).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Advantages of loss leaders:</strong> They increase sales and footfall. They are a good way to sell out-of-date stock. They attract new customers who may become regular shoppers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Disadvantages of loss leaders:</strong> There is a risk of making a loss if customers only buy the loss leader and never return. Customers may perceive the product as poor quality because of the low price. Profit margins are significantly reduced.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Influences on Pricing Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Businesses do not choose a pricing strategy in isolation. Several external factors influence which strategy is most appropriate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Technology:</strong> Advances in technology make production more efficient, so businesses can produce more in less time and benefit from <dfn class="term" data-def="The cost advantages a business gains when production increases, causing the average cost per unit to fall.">economies of scale</dfn>. As average production costs fall, businesses can pass savings on to consumers as lower prices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Competition:</strong> In highly competitive markets where products are very similar (like petrol), prices tend to be similar. Businesses must differentiate on non-price factors. In less competitive or niche markets, businesses have more freedom to set higher prices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Market segments:</strong> The target market influences pricing. If a business targets wealthy consumers, premium pricing communicates quality and exclusivity. If the target market is price-sensitive (like students), discounts and competitive pricing are more appropriate.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Product life cycle:</strong> Where a product sits on the <dfn class="term" data-def="The stages a product goes through from its launch to its withdrawal from the market: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.">product life cycle</dfn> affects pricing. At <strong>introduction</strong>, prices may be low to encourage trial (penetration) or high to maximise early profit (skimming). During <strong>growth</strong>, small discounts may encourage further purchases. At <strong>maturity</strong>, prices are at their highest as the business harvests profit. In <strong>decline</strong>, heavy discounts clear remaining stock before withdrawal.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the FOUR influences on pricing: can you recall them all for a 6-mark question?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There are four main influences on pricing: <strong>technology</strong> (lower production costs), <strong>competition</strong> (similar prices in competitive markets), <strong>market segments</strong> (pricing to match the target audience), and the <strong>product life cycle</strong> (different prices at each stage). Learn these — they are regularly tested in 3-mark and 6-mark questions.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Psychological Pricing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing technique that makes prices seem lower than they actually are, such as charging £9.99 instead of £10.00.">Psychological pricing</dfn> is a technique that makes prices appear more attractive. The most common example is pricing an item at £9.99 instead of £10.00. Although the actual difference is only 1p, customers perceive the price as significantly cheaper because it starts with a “9” rather than a “10.” This technique is used across almost every retail sector and is often combined with other pricing strategies.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n31">High-Margin vs High-Volume Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Pricing strategies generally fall into two broad categories. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing approach that sets a high price to maximise profit per unit sold, typically used for premium or niche products.">high-margin pricing strategy</dfn> aims to maximise profit per unit by setting a higher price than rivals. This works best for premium or niche products where customers value quality over price. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A pricing approach that sets a low price to maximise the number of units sold, relying on large sales volumes for profit.">high-volume pricing strategy</dfn> sets a lower price to sell as many units as possible, relying on large sales volumes for overall profit. Supermarkets often use high-volume strategies, while luxury brands use high-margin strategies.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n35">There are five key pricing strategies for Edexcel Business: price skimming (high then lower), cost-plus (cost + mark-up), penetration (low then higher), competitive (match rivals), and loss leader (sell below cost to attract customers).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Pricing decisions are influenced by technology, competition, market segments, and the product life cycle — a business must consider all of these when choosing a strategy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">The difference between price (what the customer pays) and cost (what the business spends) is fundamental — never confuse the two in an exam answer.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n33">For “Explain” questions (3 marks) on pricing, always use a chain of reasoning: state the strategy, explain how it works, and then link it to a business outcome (e.g. profit, market share, customer loyalty). For “Discuss” questions (6 marks), you must analyse <strong>both sides</strong> — give at least one advantage and one disadvantage, each developed with a “because… this means… therefore” chain. Always use specific pricing vocabulary: “mark-up,” “profit margin,” “market share,” “footfall.”</p>
