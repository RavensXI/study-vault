Imports, exports, tariffs, trade blocs, and how UK businesses compete globally.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Globalisation?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which the world is becoming increasingly interconnected as a result of massively increased international trade and cultural exchange.">Globalisation</dfn> is the process by which the world is becoming increasingly interconnected because of massively increased trade and cultural exchange. It has led to a huge increase in the production of goods and services around the world. The biggest companies are no longer national firms — they are <dfn class="term" data-def="A business organisation that has operations in multiple countries, also known as an MNC.">multinational corporations</dfn> (MNCs) with offices and factories in many countries. Coca-Cola, for example, operates in 188 countries worldwide.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 benefits globalisation brings to businesses, then state its main drawback.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Globalisation means businesses can sell to customers around the world, source cheaper materials from overseas, and set up operations in different countries. However, it also means they face more competition from international rivals.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Globalisation affects businesses in several important ways. It creates opportunities to access larger markets, find cheaper suppliers, and benefit from economies of scale. At the same time, it increases competition because foreign businesses can now sell their products in the UK more easily. Businesses must adapt their strategies to survive in a global marketplace.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Imports and Exports</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Goods and services that a country buys from other countries.">Imports</dfn> are goods and services that a country buys from other countries. For example, the UK imports electronics, clothing, and oil from overseas. Countries import things they do not produce themselves or cannot make as efficiently. <dfn class="term" data-def="Goods and services that a country sells to other countries.">Exports</dfn> are goods and services that a country sells to other countries. The UK exports medical supplies, cars, gas turbines, and gold to international markets.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Benefits of importing:</strong> Businesses can access a wider variety of raw materials and products. Imports can provide consumers with more choice and often lower prices due to competition. Businesses may find cheaper suppliers overseas, reducing their costs of production.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Drawbacks of importing:</strong> If a country imports more than it exports, it has a <dfn class="term" data-def="When the value of a country’s imports exceeds the value of its exports.">trade deficit</dfn>. Local producers may struggle to compete with cheaper imports, which can lead to job losses in the UK. Relying too much on imports makes a country vulnerable to trade disruptions or price increases.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Benefits of exporting:</strong> Exports create jobs and increase income. Selling to international markets gives businesses access to more customers and higher total sales. This helps diversify a business’s revenue and reduces dependence on the UK market alone.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Drawbacks of exporting:</strong> Businesses face different product regulations and standards in each country. Relying too much on exports can be risky if global demand drops or trade restrictions are introduced.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define 'trade surplus' and 'trade deficit' using 'imports' and 'exports'.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The <strong>balance of trade</strong> compares the value of a country’s imports and exports. A <strong>trade surplus</strong> occurs when exports exceed imports. A <strong>trade deficit</strong> occurs when imports exceed exports.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Case Study: Primark’s Global Expansion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Primark is a UK-based fashion retailer known for affordable clothing. It expanded across Europe, opening stores in Spain, France, Germany, and the Netherlands, before moving into the US market. Primark’s success came from maintaining low prices, opening large flagship stores in key locations, and tailoring its product offerings to local tastes while keeping its core value of affordability.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">However, Primark faced challenges in adapting its supply chain to new markets, managing logistics across different countries, and adjusting its marketing strategy to suit fashion preferences in different cultures. Styles, colours, sizes, and modesty norms differ by country — if Primark did not adapt, stock would not sell and its brand reputation could suffer.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Exchange Rates and International Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">When businesses trade internationally, they deal with different currencies. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency.">exchange rate</dfn> is the price of one currency in terms of another. Changes in exchange rates directly affect the cost of imports and the revenue from exports.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>How Exchange Rate Changes Affect Businesses</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n17">If the <strong>pound weakens</strong> (falls in value), UK exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, so export sales may rise. However, imported raw materials and goods cost more, pushing up costs for UK businesses that rely on imports.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">If the <strong>pound strengthens</strong> (rises in value), UK exports become more expensive abroad, so demand may fall. However, imports become cheaper, which can reduce costs for businesses that buy materials from overseas.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Tariffs and Barriers to International Trade</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A tax placed on imported goods to increase their price and reduce demand for them.">tariff</dfn> is a tax placed on imported goods to increase their price and decrease demand. Governments impose tariffs to raise revenue, restrict imports, and persuade consumers to buy domestically produced goods instead. When a tariff is added, importers pass most or all of the tax into the retail price, widening the price gap between imports and UK-made goods.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Close this and list 2 advantages and 2 disadvantages that tariffs have for UK businesses.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Tariff advantages:</strong> UK goods become cheaper by comparison, so UK businesses sell more. Tariffs protect new businesses from being overwhelmed by international competition. They raise tax revenue for the government. <strong>Tariff disadvantages:</strong> Other countries may impose retaliatory tariffs on UK exports. Tariffs may simply increase prices for consumers without improving UK competitiveness.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Trade Blocs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of countries that agree to trade with each other more freely under common rules, usually with reduced or zero tariffs between members.">trade bloc</dfn> is a group of countries that agree to trade with each other more freely under common rules. Members typically face lower or zero tariffs when trading with each other, making it easier and cheaper to do business within the bloc.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Examples of Major Trade Blocs</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>European Union (EU):</strong> 27 European countries with free trade between members, shared rules, and a common currency (the euro) for some. The EU creates a single market with free movement of goods, services, people, and capital.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>USMCA:</strong> An agreement between the USA, Mexico, and Canada (replaced NAFTA in 2020) that reduces tariffs and trade barriers between the three countries.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>ASEAN Free Trade Area:</strong> Southeast Asian countries working together to promote economic growth and make trade easier in the region.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Trade blocs benefit members through bigger markets, cheaper inputs, more competition (which drives better quality and lower prices), and more foreign investment. However, non-member countries face tariffs and extra paperwork, making them less competitive. Members may also lose some control over their own trade rules.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n28">How Businesses Compete Internationally</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Businesses use the <strong>internet and e-commerce</strong> to trade internationally. Even a small UK business can sell to international markets using a website. If the website is translated into different languages, the business can reach customers in many countries. Delivery firms such as UPS and FedEx handle shipping, so there is no need to invest in expensive shops abroad.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Businesses also compete internationally by <strong>changing the marketing mix</strong>. This means adapting the product, price, promotion, and place to suit different markets. For example, a food company might change flavours or packaging to match local tastes, or a clothing company might adjust sizes and styles for different countries.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Case Study: Dyson’s Move to Singapore</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32">James Dyson moved his business headquarters from the UK to Singapore for several reasons. Singapore is closer to growing Asian markets like China. Dyson already manufactured products in Southeast Asia, so the move made it easier to manage production. Singapore has a skilled workforce of engineers and tech experts, low taxes, and a stable economy. The move also reduced Dyson’s reliance on the UK market amid uncertainty from Brexit.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Case Study: Tesla and Globalisation</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n33">Globalisation allowed Tesla to access much larger international demand, especially in China. Building a $5 billion Shanghai factory let Tesla sell directly to Chinese customers, achieving far higher sales volumes and economies of scale. This helped lower average costs and move closer to its target price of $35,000 for the Model 3.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34">Globalisation also let Tesla buy cheaper parts from around the world, sourcing batteries and components from large Asian suppliers rather than relying solely on the US. However, if governments introduce tariffs on imports from the US, the Model 3 becomes much more expensive in countries like China, and sales could fall significantly.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n37">Globalisation increases trade, competition, and opportunities for businesses to access international markets, but also exposes them to exchange rate risks and foreign competition.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Tariffs and trade blocs are the main barriers and enablers of international trade — they directly affect the price competitiveness of imports and exports.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Businesses compete internationally by using e-commerce, adapting their marketing mix, and sometimes relocating operations to be closer to key markets.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">When answering questions on globalisation, always link your points to the specific business in the question. Do not just state what globalisation is — explain how it affects that business’s costs, sales, or competitiveness. For “Discuss” questions, make sure you consider both sides (e.g. opportunities AND threats of globalisation) and reach a clear conclusion that is justified by your analysis.</p>
