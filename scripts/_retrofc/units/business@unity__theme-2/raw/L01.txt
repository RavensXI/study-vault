--- DESCRIPTION ---
Organic and inorganic growth, mergers, takeovers, PLCs, and sources of finance for expansion.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Do Businesses Want to Grow?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Most businesses do not stay the same size forever. Owners want their business to grow so they can increase profit, gain a larger <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of total sales in a market that is controlled by one business.">market share</dfn>, and build long-term security. A bigger business can negotiate better deals with suppliers, attract more customers, and become harder for competitors to challenge.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the 2 types of growth, with how each happens.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There are two main types of business growth: <strong>organic (internal) growth</strong>, where the business expands from within, and <strong>inorganic (external) growth</strong>, where the business grows by merging with or taking over another company.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Organic Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="When a business expands from within by developing new products, entering new markets, or increasing output, without merging with or taking over another business.">Organic growth</dfn> means the business grows by itself, without merging with or taking over another company. This is sometimes called internal growth. There are several ways a business can achieve organic growth.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Methods of Organic Growth</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>New products through innovation:</strong> A business can grow by making changes to existing products or creating entirely new ones. For example, mobile phones evolved into smartphones — an innovation that improved the original product and opened up huge new markets.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>New products through research and development (R&D):</strong> R&D involves all the activities a business carries out to discover or create new products, often using science and technology. R&D frequently aims to solve consumer problems, which can lead to strong demand for the new product.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>New markets through the marketing mix:</strong> A business can reach new customers by improving its product, adjusting its price, changing where it sells, or using new promotional methods such as social media and digital marketing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>New markets through technology:</strong> Investing in new technology can speed up production or make it more efficient. For example, Ocado uses automated warehouse robots to pick and pack grocery orders faster than human workers could alone.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Expanding overseas into emerging markets:</strong> An <dfn class="term" data-def="A country or region experiencing rapid economic growth and industrialisation but not yet as developed as established economies like the UK or USA.">emerging market</dfn> is a country experiencing rapid economic growth. Tesco expanded into India through a partnership with Tata Group, tapping into a growing consumer base.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Organic growth lets a business keep its company culture and gives the owners full control. Higher production can also lead to lower average costs through economies of scale. However, organic growth is slow and risky — the gap between investing money and seeing a return can be long, and success depends on reliable sales forecasts.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Inorganic Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="When a business grows by joining with another business, either through a merger (two businesses combine) or a takeover (one business buys controlling shares in another).">Inorganic growth</dfn> means the business uses another company to help it grow. This happens through mergers or takeovers and is sometimes called external growth.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_business_growth.jpg" alt="Diagram: Business Growth" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, define merger vs takeover & what hostile means.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A <strong>merger</strong> is when two businesses combine to form one new company. A <strong>takeover</strong> (acquisition) is when one business buys more than 50% of the shares in another. Takeovers can be <strong>friendly</strong> (both sides agree) or <strong>hostile</strong> (the target company’s management opposes the deal).</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Real-World Examples of Inorganic Growth</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Merger — T-Mobile and Orange (2009):</strong> The two mobile networks merged their UK businesses to create a company with 28.4 million customers, later rebranded as EE.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Merger — Disney and Pixar (2006):</strong> Disney bought Pixar for $7.4 billion. The deal combined Disney’s distribution power with Pixar’s creative talent, producing hits like Toy Story and Finding Nemo under one roof.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Merger — GlaxoSmithKline (2000):</strong> SmithKline Beecham and Glaxo Wellcome merged to form one of the world’s largest pharmaceutical companies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Hostile takeover — Kraft and Cadbury (2010):</strong> Kraft bought Cadbury for £11.7 billion against the wishes of Cadbury’s management. Despite promises to protect jobs, Kraft closed the Somerdale factory near Bristol, making hundreds of workers redundant.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Hostile takeover — RBS and NatWest (2000):</strong> The Royal Bank of Scotland took over NatWest for £21 billion, becoming the second-largest bank in Europe. The deal led to 18,000 job losses.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Advantages and Disadvantages of Inorganic Growth</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Advantages:</strong> Eliminates a competitor, gains valuable assets (such as prime locations or brand names), achieves <dfn class="term" data-def="The cost advantages a business gains when it grows larger, allowing it to reduce the average cost per unit through bulk buying, specialisation, or spreading fixed costs.">economies of scale</dfn> through bulk buying, and instantly increases market share.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Very expensive and risky. Different company cultures may clash, leading to communication problems. Legal costs can be high. The deal may need outside investment, which could mean the original owners lose control. Around 80% of all mergers fail.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Public Limited Companies (PLCs)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">As a business grows, it may choose to become a <dfn class="term" data-def="A company whose shares are traded on the stock exchange, allowing any member of the public to buy and sell them. Denoted by ‘plc’ after the company name.">public limited company (plc)</dfn>. PLCs are listed on the stock exchange, meaning anyone can buy shares. There are currently around 1,400 companies on the London Stock Exchange, including household names like Tesco, Barclays, and BP.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Close this and list 2 PLC benefits, plus 2 reasons owners risk losing control.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>PLCs benefit from <strong>limited liability</strong>, easier access to finance through share issues, and bank willingness to lend. However, they must publish annual accounts (visible to competitors), face expensive reporting requirements, and risk the original owners losing control if shareholders gain a 51% majority.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Sources of Finance for Growing Businesses</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Growing businesses need money to fund their expansion. Finance can come from <strong>internal sources</strong> (raised from within the business) or <strong>external sources</strong> (borrowed or raised from outside).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Internal sources</strong> include <dfn class="term" data-def="Profits that a business keeps and reinvests rather than distributing to owners, with no interest to pay and no loss of ownership.">retained profit</dfn> (profits kept in the business and reinvested — cheap because there is no interest to pay) and the sale of assets (selling machinery, vehicles, or premises the business no longer needs). Internal finance does not dilute ownership.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>External sources</strong> include bank loans (regular repayments plus interest), share capital (selling shares to raise funds — no interest, but ownership is shared), and stock market flotation (a private limited company becomes a plc and sells shares to the general public). Netflix, for example, could issue new shares to fund its multi-billion-pound investment in original programming without taking on debt.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n31">Organic growth (innovation, R&amp;D, new markets, technology, overseas expansion) is slower but cheaper and less risky, while inorganic growth (mergers and takeovers) is faster but expensive and often leads to culture clashes.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32">Growing businesses may become PLCs to raise capital through share issues, but this risks losing control and requires expensive public reporting.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Finance for growth comes from internal sources (retained profit, asset sales) or external sources (loans, share capital, stock market flotation), each with trade-offs between cost, control, and risk.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n29">For “Discuss” and “Justify” questions on business growth, always consider <strong>both sides</strong>. A strong answer on organic vs inorganic growth will weigh up speed, cost, risk, and control. Use real business examples (Kraft/Cadbury, Tesco in India, Disney/Pixar) to show application — this is what lifts you from Level 1 to Level 3. Always finish with a justified conclusion that links back to the specific business in the question.</p>
