<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Stock?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business needs to hold materials, components, or finished products so it can keep trading. <dfn class="term" data-def="The products, raw materials, or components held by a business for use in production or for sale to customers.">Stock</dfn> can be described in three ways: <strong>raw materials</strong> waiting to enter production, <strong>work-in-progress</strong> (partly finished goods), and <strong>finished goods</strong> ready for sale. Managing stock well is essential — hold too much and costs rise; hold too little and the business risks running out and losing customers.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Reading a Bar Gate Stock Graph</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A chart that shows the level of stock a business holds over time, including maximum stock, minimum stock, re-order level, and lead time.">bar gate stock graph</dfn> is a visual tool that helps a business plan when to re-order stock. The graph shows stock levels on the vertical axis and time (usually weeks) on the horizontal axis. Each time stock is delivered, the level jumps back up to the top of the chart.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Close this box and name the FOUR features of a bar gate stock graph!">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The four key features of a bar gate stock graph are: <strong>maximum stock level</strong> (the most the business can store), <strong>minimum stock level</strong> (the lowest safe amount, also called buffer stock), <strong>re-order level</strong> (the point at which new stock must be ordered), and <strong>lead time</strong> (the gap between placing the order and the stock arriving).</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For example, a pet shop might hold a maximum of 20 boxes of cat food. Its minimum (buffer) stock level is 5 boxes, and it re-orders when stock falls to 10 boxes. If the re-order happens at week 1.5 and stock arrives at week 2, the lead time is half a week. Businesses use these graphs to avoid running out of stock while keeping storage costs under control.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">Holding large amounts of stock is expensive. The business needs warehouse space (rent), electricity, insurance, security, and staff to manage it all. Perishable stock — such as food — can go out of date before it is sold, creating waste. Cash is also tied up in unsold stock, weakening cash flow. All of these extra costs make it harder for the business to compete on price.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Just in Time (JIT) Stock Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="A stock management method where materials are delivered only when they are needed on the production line, reducing the need to hold buffer stock.">Just in time (JIT)</dfn> is a lean production strategy where parts and materials arrive at the factory just as they are needed. Instead of filling a warehouse, a business using JIT might receive hundreds of small deliveries every day. For example, Kellogg’s cereal ingredients arrive on the production line just before they are mixed, so nothing sits in storage.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_generated_9e989f30.png" alt="Generated diagram for Working with Suppliers"><figcaption>Generated diagram for Working with Suppliers</figcaption></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list TWO advantages and TWO disadvantages of JIT.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Advantages of JIT:</strong> no warehouse costs, improved cash flow (money is not tied up in stock), less waste from out-of-date stock, and reduced storage space needed. <strong>Disadvantages:</strong> the business cannot meet sudden surges in demand, a single late delivery can stop the entire production line, and the business loses bulk-buying discounts because it orders in smaller quantities.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">JIT only works when the business has excellent, reliable relationships with its suppliers. If a delivery is late or the quality is poor, there is no buffer stock to fall back on — production stops completely, costing the business money and damaging its reputation with customers.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Role of Procurement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of purchasing and obtaining raw materials, components, products, or services from suppliers.">Procurement</dfn> is the process by which a business buys the raw materials, components, and services it needs to operate. Getting procurement right means finding the right supplies, from the right supplier, at the right price, delivered at the right time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The organisation and management of transporting raw materials and finished goods to where they are needed.">Logistics</dfn> is closely linked to procurement. It covers three main elements: <strong>transportation</strong> (cargo ships, lorries, planes), <strong>storage</strong> (warehousing goods before distribution), and <strong>distribution</strong> (getting the final product to the customer). Many products — such as clothes and electronics — are manufactured abroad and must travel across the globe before reaching UK shops.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Impact of Logistics and Supply Decisions</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Costs:</strong> Quick, efficient production keeps costs low. Delays can cost a business money through idle time, damaged goods, and limited cash flow. Poor logistics decisions lead to late raw materials, falling productivity, and higher storage costs.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Reputation:</strong> If suppliers regularly deliver late or provide poor-quality materials, the business will struggle to deliver to its own customers on time. This damages its reputation. Conversely, reliable, high-quality supply chains help build a strong brand image.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Customer satisfaction:</strong> Customers expect the right product, at the right place, at the right time. Good logistics decisions ensure this happens, leading to repeat purchases, higher sales, and increased market share. Poor decisions cause stockouts, disappointed customers, and falling profits.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Relationships with Suppliers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The Edexcel specification identifies five key factors that affect supplier relationships: <strong>quality</strong>, <strong>delivery</strong> (cost, speed, and reliability), <strong>availability</strong>, <strong>cost</strong>, and <strong>trust</strong>.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Five Factors in Detail</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Quality:</strong> The quality of supplies must be consistent. If a supplier provides poor-quality raw materials, the finished product will suffer and customers will blame the business, not the supplier. A school buying fruit and vegetables for lunches, for example, expects fresh produce every time.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Delivery:</strong> Reliable delivery is critical, especially for businesses using JIT. If a supplier delivers late or sends faulty goods, the business may let its own customers down. Delivery also involves cost — faster delivery often costs more — and speed, which matters when demand is unpredictable.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Availability:</strong> Even if a supplier offers great quality and price, the arrangement breaks down if the stock is not available when needed. Unavailable stock can stop production entirely and leave shelves empty, driving customers to competitors.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Cost:</strong> The procurement team must find suppliers willing to supply at the right price. The lowest price is not always the best value for money — a slightly more expensive supplier who delivers reliably and consistently may save money in the long run. Once a supply agreement is in place, both sides want it to last to avoid the costs of renegotiation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Trust:</strong> The best supplier relationships are built on trust, joint problem-solving, and open communication. Trust develops through reliable deliveries and consistent quality over time.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="From memory, what YEAR did KFC face UK chicken shortages due to a logistics switch?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>KFC faced a real-world supplier crisis in 2018 when a switch to a new logistics provider caused chicken shortages across the UK. Hundreds of restaurants had to close temporarily. This shows how poor procurement and logistics decisions can damage a business’s reputation, reduce customer satisfaction, and lead to significant lost revenue.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Bar gate stock graphs show maximum stock, minimum stock, re-order level, and lead time — helping businesses decide when and how much to order.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29">JIT stock control eliminates warehouse costs and reduces waste, but relies on excellent supplier relationships and leaves no buffer if deliveries fail.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">Strong supplier relationships depend on five factors — quality, delivery, availability, cost, and trust — and good procurement and logistics decisions directly affect costs, reputation, and customer satisfaction.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For “Discuss” (6-mark) questions on suppliers and stock, always give <strong>both sides</strong> of the argument. Use connective phrases like “this means”, “therefore”, and “as a result” to build chains of reasoning. For example, if asked to discuss the benefits of JIT: “Cash is not tied up in stock, this means the business will have better cash flow. As a result, it can pay its suppliers on time, maintaining a positive relationship.” Then give the other side: “However, the business cannot meet sudden increases in demand…”</p>
