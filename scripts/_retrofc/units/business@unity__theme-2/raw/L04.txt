--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ethics, environmental concerns, and pressure groups shape business decisions and affect profit.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Business Ethics?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Most businesses exist to make a profit for their owners or shareholders. However, <dfn class="term" data-def="The moral principles that guide how a business behaves towards its employees, customers, suppliers, the environment, and the wider community.">business ethics</dfn> means going beyond just making money — it means trying to do the right thing for all <dfn class="term" data-def="Individuals or groups who have an interest in a business, including employees, customers, suppliers, shareholders, the local community, and the government.">stakeholders</dfn>. An ethical business operates in a way that is honest, fair, and responsible.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and list 3 stakeholders ethical businesses consider besides profit.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Ethical businesses consider the wider impact of their decisions on employees, suppliers, customers, the local community, and the environment — not just how much profit they can make.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4">For example, <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of trading that ensures farmers and workers in developing countries receive a fair price for their goods, decent working conditions, and sustainable terms of trade.">Fairtrade</dfn> organisations try to meet the needs of their employees and suppliers by paying fair prices and providing decent working conditions. Companies like Lush source materials ethically, while Google invests in employee wellbeing through benefits such as free food, medical care, gyms, and flexible working hours.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Ways a Business Can Become More Ethical</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">There are several practical steps a business can take to operate more ethically:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Ethical Business Practices</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Paying workers higher wages</strong> — going beyond the legal minimum wage to pay a fair wage that allows employees to live comfortably. The minimum wage is a legal requirement, not an ethical choice.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Improving working conditions</strong> — providing a safe, supportive workplace. Sports Direct was criticised in an MPs’ report for treating staff “not as humans,” showing the reputational damage of poor conditions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Buying Fairtrade supplies</strong> — ensuring suppliers in developing countries receive a fair price for their goods. Fairtrade addresses injustices in normal trade that discriminate against the poorest producers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Reducing pollution and packaging</strong> — disposing of waste responsibly, recycling materials, and cutting unnecessary packaging.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Avoiding child labour</strong> — about 14 million children worldwide are involved in manufacturing goods, including clothing in Bangladesh, footwear in India, and soccer balls in Pakistan. Ethical businesses refuse to use suppliers who exploit children.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Trade-Off Between Ethics and Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Acting ethically often costs a business more money in the short term. Paying higher wages, sourcing Fairtrade materials, or improving working conditions all increase costs. This creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A situation where a business must give up one benefit in order to gain another, such as accepting lower short-term profits to act more ethically or sustainably.">trade-off</dfn> — the business may have to accept lower profits or charge higher prices.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name 2 advantages AND 2 disadvantages of ethical trading.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Advantages of ethical trading include improved reputation, increased customer loyalty, motivated staff, and long-term financial benefits. Disadvantages include higher costs, potential short-term financial loss, and difficulty balancing ethics with the need to remain profitable.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Primark, for example, faced criticism when poor working conditions were discovered in its supply chain. UK consumers will no longer tolerate these practices, so fixing problems overseas can be very expensive. However, businesses that build a strong ethical reputation — like Sainsbury’s with its sustainability commitments — can gain a competitive advantage.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Environmental Considerations for Business</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Businesses must also consider their impact on the <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the natural world and the impact of human activity on its condition, including issues like pollution, waste, and use of natural resources.">environment</dfn>. The main environmental issues for GCSE Business are:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Key Environmental Issues</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Sustainability</strong> — using <dfn class="term" data-def="Resources that can be used again and again without running out, such as solar, wind, and water energy.">renewable resources</dfn> like solar and wind power instead of non-renewable fossil fuels (gas, oil, coal). Sustainable production also means using electric vehicles, avoiding harmful chemicals, and limiting scarce resources.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Traffic congestion</strong> — smaller packaging means fewer lorries on the road, reducing pollution and fossil fuel use.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Recycling and waste disposal</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of converting waste materials into reusable materials or new products, reducing the need for raw resources.">recycling</dfn> is the process of converting waste into reusable material. Businesses have an ethical duty to dispose of waste responsibly rather than dumping it in landfill or the ocean.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Noise and air pollution</strong> — business operations and transport create noise and release harmful substances into the atmosphere. The UK government has strict regulations: businesses likely to pollute the air must gain a permit to trade, or face up to six months in prison and a £20,000 fine.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">The Trade-Off Between Environment, Sustainability, and Profit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Going green can be expensive. Greener production methods cost more, and items like solar panels require a large upfront investment. However, there are clear benefits: businesses can use their green credentials as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A feature or benefit that makes a product or business different from and better than competitors.">unique selling point</dfn> (USP), and investing in greener energy can lead to cost savings over time. There is also a growing “green market” of customers who actively seek environmentally friendly products.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Pressure Groups and the Marketing Mix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="Organisations set up to try to influence what consumers think about a business and its practices, using methods like campaigns, marches, letters to MPs, and raising public awareness.">Pressure groups</dfn> are organisations that campaign to change business behaviour. They use methods like writing to MPs and the press, organising marches, running campaigns, and raising public awareness. Their actions can directly affect a business’s marketing mix.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall how pressure groups affect Product AND Price in the marketing mix.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Pressure groups can impact all four elements of the marketing mix: <strong>Product</strong> (forcing businesses to change or withdraw products), <strong>Price</strong> (pushing up costs through ethical demands), <strong>Promotion</strong> (damaging a brand with bad publicity), and <strong>Place</strong> (changing how products are distributed).</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Greenpeace campaigned against John West because its tuna was caught using destructive fishing methods that harmed sharks and turtles. Greenpeace also pressured Lego to change how it promoted its products due to links with Shell oil. The National Obesity Forum publicly criticised NHS hospitals for selling chocolate, crisps, and sugary drinks in vending machines, pushing them to offer healthier choices instead.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Pressure group activity can lead to damaged brand reputation, forced changes in decision-making, increased costs, tighter government regulation, and loss of market share to competitors. For example, if a pressure group publicises that a business pollutes, consumers may stop buying its products, causing falling revenues.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n32">Business ethics means acting honestly and fairly towards all stakeholders, not just maximising profit — but ethical choices often involve trade-offs with short-term costs.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Environmental considerations include sustainability, recycling, waste disposal, pollution, and traffic congestion — going green can be costly but offers long-term competitive advantages.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Pressure groups can force businesses to change their products, prices, promotion, or distribution by raising public awareness and damaging brand reputation.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n30">When answering questions on ethics and the environment, always link back to the impact on the business. For a “Discuss” question, give arguments for <strong>and</strong> against ethical or environmental action, supported by real business examples like Fairtrade, Primark, or Greenpeace campaigns. For “Explain” questions, use a clear chain of reasoning: state the point, explain how it works, then show the effect on the business (e.g. costs, sales, reputation).</p>
