Promotion strategies including advertising, sponsorship, branding, and digital marketing.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Promotion?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The methods a business uses to persuade, remind, or inform customers to buy its products or services.">Promotion</dfn> is the process of reminding, informing, or persuading customers to buy a product or service. It is one of the four Ps of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of product, price, place, and promotion that a business uses to sell its products.">marketing mix</dfn>, and without it even the best product can fail because customers simply do not know it exists.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Edexcel specification requires you to understand five main promotion strategies — <strong>advertising</strong>, <strong>sponsorship</strong>, <strong>product trials</strong>, <strong>special offers</strong>, and <strong>branding</strong> — plus how technology is used in promotion through <strong>targeted advertising online</strong>, <strong>viral advertising via social media</strong>, and <strong>e-newsletters</strong>.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the five core AND three tech-based promotion strategies from the Edexcel spec.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five core promotion strategies on the Edexcel spec are: advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers, and branding. You must also know three technology-based methods: targeted online advertising, viral advertising, and e-newsletters.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Advertising</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="Messages paid for by businesses that are designed to inform, remind, or influence people who see them.">Advertising</dfn> involves paying for messages that inform and influence customers. A business advertises for several reasons: to give customers information about a new product, to raise awareness of a brand, to remind customers about an existing product, or to persuade customers to switch from a competitor.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Advertising Media</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Newspapers:</strong> Local papers are good for promoting products within a community. National papers reach customers all over the UK, but are more expensive. Both have a limited shelf life — yesterday’s paper is thrown away.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Magazines:</strong> Magazine adverts are popular for launching new products. Magazines have a <em>pass-on readership</em>, meaning more than one person reads the same copy. Niche magazines help target specific market segments, such as fitness or fashion enthusiasts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Television:</strong> TV adverts reach the largest audiences, making them perfect for mass-market products. Specific channels help target particular groups of customers. However, TV advertising is too expensive for most small businesses and start-ups. Streaming services like Netflix and iPlayer also mean fewer people watch traditional TV adverts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Radio:</strong> Radio advertising can remind customers about products, but listeners often switch off or change the station when adverts come on. It tends to be cheaper than TV but less visually impactful.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n11">When choosing which medium to use, a business must consider the cost of the advert, whether it will reach the right <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of consumers who share similar characteristics such as age, income, or lifestyle.">market segment</dfn>, and whether the message will actually encourage customers to buy.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sponsorship</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="When a business pays to have its name or brand associated with a particular individual, event, or team.">Sponsorship</dfn> is when one brand aligns itself with another by paying money to appear alongside it. A business might sponsor a TV programme, a charity, a celebrity, or a sports team. The goal is to boost brand awareness and improve the company’s reputation through positive association.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">For example, Manchester United has a long list of global sponsors that pay millions to have their logos displayed alongside the club’s brand. JD Sports sponsored Bournemouth football club to reach its target market of 16–24 year olds who are interested in sportswear.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name two advantages AND disadvantages of sponsorship for a business.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Sponsorship <strong>advantages</strong>: positive association with a celebrity or sport, builds brand awareness, ideal way to reach a target market. <strong>Disadvantages</strong>: difficult to measure the impact on sales, risky if the sponsored person causes controversy, expensive with no guaranteed returns.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Product Trials and Special Offers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A temporary offering that lets consumers examine, use, or test a product before the business commits to a full launch.">product trial</dfn> is a temporary offering that lets consumers try a product before the business commits resources to a full launch. Trials are carried out to avoid expensive mistakes — they give information on likely demand in the wider market. For example, Tesco runs Home Panels where selected customers test new products and provide feedback.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Temporary reductions in price or added incentives designed to persuade customers to make a purchase.">Special offers</dfn> (also called sales promotions) are designed to persuade customers to buy. Common types include buy-one-get-one-free (BOGOF), discounts, free gifts, and competitions. They can encourage a customer to switch from their regular brand, try something new, or buy multiple items such as 3-for-2 deals.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Are Special Offers Always Good for Business?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">Special offers attract customers in the short term, but they eat into <dfn class="term" data-def="The money a business keeps after all costs have been deducted from revenue.">profit</dfn> because each item is sold for less. If customers only buy when there is a discount and do not come back at full price, the business loses money overall. A business must weigh up whether the short-term boost in sales is worth the long-term reduction in profit margins.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Branding</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The promotion of a product or business through a distinctive name, design, logo, or image that makes it recognisable.">Branding</dfn> is the promotion of a product or business by means of a distinctive name, design, or image. Strong brands are instantly recognisable — think of the Nike swoosh or the Apple logo. Businesses invest heavily in branding because it creates customer loyalty, allows them to charge <dfn class="term" data-def="A higher price that customers are willing to pay because they perceive the product or brand as superior.">premium prices</dfn>, and differentiates them from competitors.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why Bentley can charge premium prices, referencing brand values.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Strong branding adds value because loyal customers are willing to pay more. Bentley, for example, can charge premium prices because customers associate the brand with luxury, quality, and exclusivity.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Technology in Promotion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Targeted advertising online</strong> uses data about a customer’s browsing behaviour to display relevant adverts. If a customer searches for Xbox games, they may see adverts for Xbox games on other websites they visit. The advantage is that the advert reaches someone who is already interested. The disadvantage is that customers may ignore online ads, making it hard to measure effectiveness.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Viral Advertising and E-Newsletters</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25"><dfn class="term" data-def="A strategy that uses social networks to spread a marketing message quickly, like a virus passing from person to person.">Viral advertising</dfn> aims to encourage large numbers of people to share content organically across social media. The best viral adverts are memorable, funny, or surprising. The Old Spice “The Man Your Man Could Smell Like” campaign is a famous example — it gained over 60 million views and boosted body wash sales by 60%.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="An electronic publication sent by a business via email to inform customers about products, offers, or news.">E-newsletters</dfn> are marketing emails sent to customers who have signed up to receive them. Businesses encourage customers to share the newsletter with friends using share buttons, which can make the campaign go viral. E-newsletters are a cost-effective way to keep in touch with existing customers.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Choosing the Right Promotional Mix</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">No single promotion method works for every business. The right choice depends on several factors: can the business afford it, will the target market see it, and will it actually draw customers in rather than put them off? A small local business might use leaflets and local newspaper adverts, while a global brand like KFC might combine TV advertising, social media campaigns, and special offers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The combination of different promotional methods a business uses to communicate with its target market.">promotional mix</dfn> is the combination of methods a business uses. Getting this mix right means reaching the correct market segment with a message that persuades them to buy — and doing so within budget.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n31">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n32">Promotion includes five core strategies (advertising, sponsorship, product trials, special offers, branding) plus three technology-based methods (targeted online ads, viral advertising, e-newsletters).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Each method has advantages and disadvantages — the right choice depends on the business’s budget, target market, and product type.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Strong branding creates customer loyalty and allows businesses to charge premium prices, giving them a competitive advantage.</li></ul>

Always include a counter-argument — for instance, the method might be expensive or difficult to measure. Finish with a conclusion that weighs up both sides.</p>
