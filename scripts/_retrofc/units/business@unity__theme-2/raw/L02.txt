--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why and how business aims and objectives change as businesses grow and evolve over time.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Business Aims and Objectives?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before understanding why aims change, it helps to recap what they are. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A long-term goal or target that gives a business direction and purpose.">business aim</dfn> is a big, long-term goal that gives a business direction — the “where we’re heading” statement. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific, measurable target with a deadline that helps a business achieve its aims.">objective</dfn> is a smaller, measurable target with a number and a deadline that helps the business reach that aim. Think of the aim as a destination on a map and the objectives as the roads you take to get there.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and list the 5 SMART criteria for objectives -- what does each letter stand for?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Aims are broad and long-term (e.g. “become the most popular café in Burnley”). Objectives are SMART — <strong>Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound</strong> (e.g. “increase online sales by 8% by July”).</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Financial and Non-Financial Aims</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Most businesses are set up to make money, so <dfn class="term" data-def="Goals directly concerned with the monetary success of a business, such as survival, profit, sales targets, and market share.">financial aims</dfn> are extremely common. These include survival (covering costs to keep trading), making a profit, hitting sales targets, growing market share, and achieving financial security for the owner.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">However, businesses also have <dfn class="term" data-def="Goals not linked to money, such as personal satisfaction, social responsibility, challenge, and control over decision-making.">non-financial aims</dfn>. These are personal to the entrepreneur or linked to wider social issues. They include social aims (operating ethically), personal satisfaction (turning a hobby into a career), the challenge of building something from scratch, and having control over your own decisions and working hours.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Why Non-Financial Aims Matter for Profit-Making Businesses</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">Even profit-making businesses benefit from non-financial aims. Consumers care about ethical issues, so social aims improve a business’s reputation. Non-financial goals give employees a purpose beyond money, which helps with recruitment and retention. They also give the business a wider sense of purpose that can attract loyal customers.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Why Business Aims and Objectives Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A business will not keep the same aims and objectives forever. As a business evolves — growing larger, facing new challenges, or responding to the world around it — its goals must change too. Edexcel identifies five key reasons why aims and objectives change.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_changes_in_business_aims_and_objectives.jpg" alt="Diagram: Changes in Business Aims and Objectives" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n10" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 of the 5 reasons why business aims and objectives change.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five reasons aims and objectives change are: <strong>market conditions</strong>, <strong>technology</strong>, <strong>business performance</strong>, <strong>legislation</strong>, and <strong>internal reasons</strong>. These are the “why” — the triggers that force a business to rethink its goals.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Five Reasons Explained</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Market conditions:</strong> Changes in the market force businesses to adapt. New competitors entering the market, rising unemployment reducing demand, or shifting consumer preferences can all mean a business must change its aims. For example, if a major competitor closes down, a business might shift its aim from survival to growth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Technology:</strong> New technology can transform an industry overnight. A new production process (like a better bagging technique for crisps) or a revolutionary product (like Dyson’s bagless vacuum cleaner) forces all competitors to change their objectives to keep up with innovation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Performance:</strong> A business’s own results shape its next moves. After a strong year of sales and profit, the owners may set growth objectives — expanding, hiring more staff, and increasing output. After a bad year, they might refocus on survival, cutting costs, or reducing the workforce.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Legislation:</strong> New laws can force a business to change direction. The National Minimum Wage rising each year, for example, increases wage costs. A business may respond by setting new objectives to cut costs elsewhere, reduce staff numbers, or slow down expansion plans.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Internal reasons:</strong> Personal goals of the owner or changes within the business itself can trigger new objectives. As a business expands, the owner might set objectives for higher <dfn class="term" data-def="The percentage of total sales in a market that is controlled by a particular business.">market share</dfn>, increased revenue, or higher production levels.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">How Business Aims and Objectives Change</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The Edexcel specification also requires you to know <em>how</em> aims and objectives change — the practical ways a business adjusts its strategy. There are five main methods.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Focus on Survival or Growth</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18">In its first year, a new business often aims simply to <dfn class="term" data-def="A business's ability to cover its costs and continue trading without making a profit.">survive</dfn> — reaching a sustainable level of sales that covers costs like wages, rent, and rates. The owner may keep prices low to attract customers, accepting little profit at first. Once established, the business can raise prices and shift its objective to making a profit and then to growth — selling more goods, producing more, opening new outlets in the UK (the domestic market) or even expanding overseas (the international market).</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Entering or Exiting Markets</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19">A business may change its objectives to enter a new market — for example, starting to sell products in China. This could involve a joint venture or merger as part of the new plan. Equally, a business may exit a market that is no longer profitable, refocusing its resources on its strongest areas.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Growing or Reducing the Workforce</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">To meet new objectives, a business may change the size of its workforce. A growing strawberry farm might take on more temporary pickers to increase output. Conversely, a business facing rising costs might reduce staff numbers to cut its wage bill and refocus on its core operations.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Increasing or Decreasing the Product Range</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21">A growing business may increase its <dfn class="term" data-def="The number of variations of products or services offered by a business.">product range</dfn> to give customers more choice, boost sales, and increase revenue and profit. For example, a wedding car company might add a campervan to its fleet. On the other hand, a business might <em>decrease</em> its product range to cut out products that are old, obsolete, or not selling. In 2015, Tesco reduced the number of products in its stores from 90,000 by 30% because having too much choice was confusing customers — they had previously stocked 228 types of air freshener.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Real-World Case Studies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Looking at real businesses shows how aims evolve over time as companies grow and the world changes around them.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain how broadband internet impacted Netflix's shift from DVDs to streaming.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p><strong>Netflix</strong> started in 1997 as a DVD rental-by-mail service aiming to offer a convenient alternative to video shops. By the mid-2000s, broadband internet changed the market, so Netflix shifted its aim to becoming a streaming platform. Today it aims to be a global entertainment leader, producing original films and series, with over 230 million subscribers. This shows how technology and market conditions drive changes in aims.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>IKEA</strong> was founded in 1943 with the aim of providing affordable furniture to Swedish consumers. As environmental concerns grew, IKEA shifted its focus towards sustainability, aiming to become a circular and climate-positive company by 2030. Its current objectives include using renewable and recycled materials and reducing its carbon footprint. This shows how legislation and changing consumer values (market conditions) trigger new aims.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Microsoft</strong> was founded in 1975 to develop and sell computer software. Under CEO Satya Nadella in the 2010s, Microsoft shifted to a “cloud-first” strategy, focusing on its Azure cloud computing platform and artificial intelligence. Technology and internal leadership changes drove this transformation, making Microsoft one of the most valuable companies in the world.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n29">Business aims and objectives change over time in response to five key triggers: market conditions, technology, performance, legislation, and internal reasons.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n30">The practical ways businesses change include shifting between survival and growth, entering or exiting markets, adjusting workforce size, and increasing or decreasing the product range.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Real businesses like Netflix, IKEA, and Microsoft show how evolving aims are essential for long-term success.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n27">When answering questions on changing aims and objectives, always separate the <strong>why</strong> (the reason — market conditions, technology, performance, legislation, internal) from the <strong>how</strong> (the method — survival/growth, entering/exiting markets, workforce changes, product range changes). Strong answers use a real business example to illustrate their point. For “Explain” questions, build a chain of reasoning: state the point, explain it, then show the consequence for the business.</p>
