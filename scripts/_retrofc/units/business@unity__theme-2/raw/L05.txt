Design mix, product life cycle, extension strategies, and product differentiation.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is the Design Mix?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a business develops a new product, it must consider three key elements known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three factors a business considers when designing a product: function, aesthetics, and cost.">design mix</dfn>. Getting the balance right between these three elements determines whether the product will succeed in the market. Different products may prioritise one element over the others depending on the <dfn class="term" data-def="A specific group of consumers that a business aims its products or marketing at.">target audience</dfn> and the competitive landscape.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the three elements of the design mix: function, aesthetics, and cost.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The three elements of the design mix are: <strong>function</strong> (how well the product works), <strong>aesthetics</strong> (how the product looks and feels), and <strong>cost</strong> (how much it costs to manufacture). Businesses must balance all three to create a successful product.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><strong>Function</strong> refers to how well a product works and whether it does what it is supposed to do. A product must be reliable and fit for purpose. For example, a smartphone must be able to make calls, send messages, and run apps smoothly. When a product functions well, its reliability can become its <dfn class="term" data-def="A feature or benefit that makes a product different from and better than competing products.">unique selling point</dfn> (USP).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Aesthetics</strong> refers to the visual appearance of a product — how it looks, feels, and appeals to customers. Good aesthetics help a business <dfn class="term" data-def="Making a product or service stand out from competitors by giving it unique features or qualities.">differentiate</dfn> its product from competitors. For example, the Dyson bladeless fan has a distinctive design that sets it apart from traditional fans. When aesthetics are done well, they can become the product’s USP and allow the business to charge a premium price.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Cost</strong> refers to how much it costs to manufacture the product. A business needs production to be financially viable — if costs are too high, the selling price will be too expensive for the target audience. Lower production costs mean savings can be passed on to customers, giving the business a competitive advantage and greater market share.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n7">Different products prioritise different elements. A Bugatti Veyron, the fastest car in the world at 301 mph, prioritises function above all else. Apple’s iPhone prioritises aesthetics — its sleek, minimalist design is a major reason customers choose it over competitors. Budget supermarket own-brand products prioritise cost, keeping prices low by using simple packaging and cheaper ingredients.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Improving the aesthetic element can make a product more attractive compared with rival products, giving it a USP. This may allow the business to charge a premium price and add value. A larger profit margin means more retained profit to reinvest in new products.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Product Life Cycle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Every product goes through a series of stages from its creation to its eventual withdrawal from the market. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The stages a product passes through from development to decline: research and development, introduction, growth, maturity, and decline.">product life cycle</dfn> (PLC). Understanding the PLC helps a business plan its marketing, pricing, and investment decisions at each stage.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_product.jpg" alt="Diagram: Product" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the five stages of the product life cycle in order, starting with Research & Development.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five stages of the product life cycle are: <strong>Research & Development</strong> (designing and testing), <strong>Introduction</strong> (product launched, low sales), <strong>Growth</strong> (sales increase rapidly), <strong>Maturity</strong> (sales at their peak), and <strong>Decline</strong> (sales begin to fall).</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>The Five Stages Explained</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Research & Development (R&D):</strong> The product is designed and market research is carried out to ensure it will satisfy customer needs. This stage is expensive because the business spends money with no revenue coming in. There is always a risk the product may fail.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Introduction:</strong> The product is launched onto the market. Sales are low because customers are not yet aware of it. Advertising is informative to build awareness. Profits may be negative due to high launch costs. Businesses may use price skimming (high initial price) or penetration pricing (low initial price) at this stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Growth:</strong> Sales increase rapidly as the product becomes more popular. The business invests more in marketing and production to meet rising demand. Competition may start to enter the market. The main goal is to expand market share and build brand awareness.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Maturity:</strong> Sales reach their peak and the market becomes saturated. The product is well-established with repeat purchases. Competition is intense. The business may need to consider extension strategies to maintain sales.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Decline:</strong> Sales begin to fall as the product loses popularity, perhaps because new technology or competitor products have replaced it. For example, BlackBerry smartphones were withdrawn in 2016 after being overtaken by touchscreen phones like the iPhone.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n17">One benefit of using the PLC is that managers can concentrate resources on products in the growth stage, giving them a longer maturity phase and greater market share. Products in decline that are making a loss can be withdrawn, freeing up retained profit for investment in new products.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Extension Strategies</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Actions a business takes to extend the life of a product and prevent it from entering the decline stage.">Extension strategies</dfn> are methods businesses use to extend the life of a product before it goes into decline. Rather than withdrawing a product, a business can take action to boost sales and keep the product in the maturity stage for longer.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Four Key Extension Strategies</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>1. Updating packaging:</strong> New packaging can make a product feel fresh and attract customers who may have overlooked it. For example, Uncle Ben’s rice rebranded to Ben’s Original, updating its name and packaging to appeal to modern consumers and signal a more inclusive brand identity.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>2. Adding new features:</strong> Modifying a product by adding new features encourages existing customers to upgrade. The VW Golf has been modified through nine generations since 1974, each time retaining the same core concept but updating the styling, technology, and performance. The iPhone is another classic example — each new version adds features to encourage consumers to buy the latest model.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>3. Changing the target market:</strong> A business can sell the same product to a different segment or audience. Netflix started as a DVD rental service for film enthusiasts but repositioned itself as a family streaming platform. Mountain Dew originally targeted outdoor enthusiasts before repositioning towards gamers.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>4. New advertising campaigns:</strong> A creative advertising campaign can renew interest in a product that has reached saturation. Some brands like Coca-Cola have never reached the decline stage because they continually refresh their advertising to stay relevant.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n24">Product Differentiation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Product <dfn class="term" data-def="Making a product or service stand out from competitors by giving it unique features or qualities.">differentiation</dfn> means making a product or service different from the competition. It is important because it helps a business stand out in a crowded market, attract customers, and potentially charge higher prices.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall 3 ways businesses can differentiate themselves from the list given, like branding or USP.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Businesses can differentiate through: <strong>branding</strong> (name, logo, reputation), <strong>USP</strong> (a unique feature competitors lack), <strong>location</strong> (being in the best position), <strong>design</strong> (visual appeal), <strong>customer service</strong> (superior experience), <strong>quality</strong> (exceeding customer expectations), and <strong>product mix</strong> (offering variety to meet different needs).</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Ways to Differentiate a Product</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Branding:</strong> A brand is a name, logo, slogan, or design associated with a product. Strong branding helps customers recognise and choose a product. Nike and Adidas are great rivals — customers buy their products partly because of brand loyalty.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>USP:</strong> A unique selling point is what makes one product different and better than all similar products. For example, Amazon’s same-day delivery service for Prime members is a USP that competitors struggle to match.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Customer service:</strong> Businesses like Virgin Atlantic differentiate through exceptional customer service, including in-flight Wi-Fi, touchscreen entertainment, and spacious cabins — a stark contrast to budget airlines like Ryanair.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Quality:</strong> <dfn class="term" data-def="The extent to which a product or service meets or exceeds customer expectations and is fit for purpose.">Quality</dfn> means the extent to which a product meets or exceeds customer expectations. High quality allows a business to charge premium prices, builds trust and loyalty through fewer faults and returns, and creates a barrier to entry that competitors find hard to copy.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n31">The benefits of producing high-quality products include: improved brand image, the ability to charge a premium price, increased net profit margin, fewer defects, and fewer customer complaints. A well-managed <dfn class="term" data-def="The complete range of products and services that a business offers to its customers.">product mix</dfn> also differentiates a business by meeting varied customer needs better than rivals, leading to greater loyalty and higher revenue per customer.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n33">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n34">The design mix (function, aesthetics, cost) determines how a product is developed — different products prioritise different elements depending on the target audience.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">The product life cycle has five stages (R&D, introduction, growth, maturity, decline), and extension strategies like new features, updated packaging, or changing the target market can delay decline.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Product differentiation through branding, USP, quality, customer service, and design helps a business stand out, attract customers, and charge higher prices.</li></ul>
