Hierarchies, recruitment processes, training methods, and motivation theories for managing people.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Organisational Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every business needs a clear structure so employees know who they report to, what their responsibilities are, and how decisions flow through the organisation. An <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram or chart showing the lines of authority and layers within a business.">organisational structure</dfn> is a diagram that shows the hierarchy of a business — who is in charge, who reports to whom, and how the business is divided into departments or teams.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and define chain of command, span of control, delegation, and delayering from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Key vocabulary for organisational structures: the <strong>chain of command</strong> is the line of authority from top to bottom; the <strong>span of control</strong> is the number of subordinates a manager is directly responsible for; <strong>delegation</strong> is giving a task to a lower-level employee; and <strong>delayering</strong> is removing one or more layers of management to cut costs and speed up communication.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Hierarchical vs Flat Structures</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An organisational structure with many layers of management, a long chain of command, and narrow spans of control.">hierarchical structure</dfn> has many layers of management. Authority flows from the top down through a long chain of command. Managers typically have a narrow span of control, meaning they supervise only a small number of employees. This means they can get to know their team well and delegate tasks effectively.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">However, communication in a hierarchical structure can be slow because messages must pass through many layers. This can make the business inflexible and slow to respond to changes in the market. It is also expensive because there are more managers and supervisors to pay.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An organisational structure with few layers of management, a short chain of command, and wide spans of control.">flat structure</dfn> has few layers of management and a short chain of command. Managers have a wide span of control. Communication is faster because there are fewer levels for messages to travel through. Employees are often given more responsibility and autonomy, which can be motivating.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The downside is that staff can become overstretched because there is less supervision. Wide spans of control mean managers may lose touch with individual employees, and power struggles can develop when the manager is not around.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Centralised vs Decentralised Decision-Making</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n9">In a <strong>centralised</strong> organisation, decisions are made at head office by senior management. This ensures consistency but can be slow and may not respond well to local customer needs. In a <strong>decentralised</strong> organisation, decision-making power is given to managers and employees closer to the customers. This speeds up decisions and gives local staff more responsibility, but it can lead to inconsistency across the business.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Recruitment Process</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of finding and hiring the best-qualified candidate for a job opening, in a timely and cost-effective manner.">Recruitment</dfn> is the process of finding and hiring the right person for a job vacancy. It follows a clear seven-stage process that ensures the business finds the most suitable candidate.</p>
<figure class="diagram"><img src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/business/theme-2/diagram_organisational_structures_recruitment_and_training.jpg" alt="Diagram: Organisational Structures, Recruitment and Training" loading="lazy"></figure>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the 7 stages of recruitment, starting with identifying the vacancy.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The seven stages of recruitment: (1) identify the vacancy, (2) write a <strong>job description</strong> (duties and responsibilities), (3) write a <strong>person specification</strong> (qualifications, skills, and qualities needed), (4) advertise the vacancy, (5) receive applications and CVs, (6) interview shortlisted candidates, (7) offer the job and issue a contract.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n13">There are different job roles within a business hierarchy. <strong>Directors</strong> sit at the top and make strategic long-term decisions. <strong>Senior managers</strong> are appointed by the board to run day-to-day operations. <strong>Supervisors and team leaders</strong> oversee small teams of workers. <strong>Operational staff</strong> carry out the core work of the business, while <strong>support staff</strong> help the business function (for example, ICT technicians or teaching assistants in a school).</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Internal vs External Recruitment</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Internal recruitment</strong> means filling a vacancy with someone already working in the business. It is cheaper (no advertising costs), quicker (no induction needed), and less risky because the candidate’s track record is known. However, it limits the pool of applicants and may cause resentment among staff who are not chosen.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>External recruitment</strong> means hiring someone from outside the business. This brings fresh ideas and new skills, which can help the business innovate. However, it is more expensive (advertising, interviews, induction) and riskier because the candidate is unknown.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Training and Development</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The action of teaching an employee a skill or type of behaviour to improve their performance.">Training</dfn> is the process of teaching employees new skills or improving existing ones. Businesses that invest in training benefit from higher productivity, better quality work, and a more motivated workforce.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Training</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Formal training</strong> is structured, with set objectives, often delivered by an outside expert. It may lead to a qualification (for example, a food hygiene certificate) but is expensive and takes employees away from their work.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Informal training</strong> takes place on the job, where a colleague shows the employee how to do something. It is cheap and quick but may not be as thorough.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Self-learning</strong> means studying without a teacher, for example through online courses. <strong>Ongoing training</strong> ensures employees continuously develop their skills to keep up with industry changes and new technology.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Businesses also use <strong>target setting</strong> and <strong>performance reviews</strong> to develop employees. Targets should be SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Timely). Performance reviews are formal meetings between an employee and their supervisor to discuss progress, set new targets, and identify training needs.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how training links to motivation and retention.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>There is a direct link between training, motivation, and <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of a business to keep its employees over time, reducing staff turnover.">retention</dfn>. Employees who receive regular training feel valued and are more motivated. Motivated employees are less likely to leave, which reduces recruitment costs and maintains productivity. Retraining is especially important when new technology is introduced.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Motivation Theories</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Understanding what motivates employees helps businesses improve productivity and reduce staff turnover. Three key theorists are studied at GCSE level.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Frederick Taylor — Scientific Management</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n25">Taylor believed that workers are primarily motivated by <strong>money</strong>. He argued that managers should observe workers, break tasks into small steps, and find the most efficient way to do each task. Workers should then be paid <dfn class="term" data-def="A payment system where workers are paid based on the number of items they produce.">piece rate</dfn> — the more they produce, the more they earn. While this can boost output, it ignores the fact that many workers care about more than just pay, and repetitive tasks can be demotivating.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Abraham Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="Maslow's theory that human needs are arranged in a pyramid, from basic survival needs at the bottom to self-fulfilment at the top.">Maslow’s hierarchy of needs</dfn> arranges human needs in five levels: (1) <strong>physiological</strong> — basic needs like food and shelter (met by wages); (2) <strong>safety</strong> — job security and safe working conditions; (3) <strong>social</strong> — belonging, teamwork, and friendships at work; (4) <strong>esteem</strong> — recognition, praise, and promotion; (5) <strong>self-actualisation</strong> — reaching your full potential through challenging and creative work. According to Maslow, each level must be satisfied before a person can move to the next.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Frederick Herzberg — Two-Factor Theory</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n27">Herzberg identified two sets of factors. <strong>Motivators</strong> actually increase job satisfaction and include achievement, recognition, interesting work, responsibility, and promotion. <strong>Hygiene factors</strong> do not motivate on their own, but their absence causes dissatisfaction — these include pay, working conditions, company policy, and relationships with colleagues. According to Herzberg, improving hygiene factors only prevents unhappiness; to truly motivate employees, businesses must provide motivators such as challenging work and opportunities for growth.</p>
</div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n28" data-revision-tip="From memory, compare Taylor's piece rate, Maslow's hierarchy, and Herzberg's motivators/hygiene factors.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Taylor focused on <strong>money</strong> as the key motivator (piece rate). Maslow said workers have a <strong>hierarchy of five needs</strong> from basic survival to self-fulfilment. Herzberg distinguished between <strong>motivators</strong> (which create satisfaction) and <strong>hygiene factors</strong> (which only prevent dissatisfaction). In the exam, you need to be able to compare these theories and apply them to business scenarios.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n30">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n31">Hierarchical structures have many layers and narrow spans of control (good for supervision, slow for communication), while flat structures have few layers and wide spans of control (fast communication, risk of overwork).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32">Recruitment follows seven stages from identifying a vacancy to issuing a contract, and can be internal (cheaper, less risky) or external (fresh ideas, fills skill gaps).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n33">Training improves motivation and retention. Taylor focused on pay, Maslow on a hierarchy of five needs, and Herzberg on motivators vs hygiene factors — each theory offers different insights into what drives employees.</li></ul>
