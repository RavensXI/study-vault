--- DESCRIPTION ---
How businesses use quantitative data, financial information, and market data to make decisions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Quantitative Business Data?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Numerical data presented as statistics, percentages, or in graphs and charts that businesses use to make decisions.">Quantitative data</dfn> is data that is based on numbers. It is displayed in graphs, charts, statistics, or percentages. Businesses rely on quantitative data to make informed decisions — for example, identifying which products sell best and should be promoted, or which product sells least and should be withdrawn from the market.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Reading graphs and charts is a core skill for both Paper 1 and Paper 2. This makes understanding quantitative data one of the most valuable skills you can develop for your GCSE Business exam.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name two examples each of quantitative and qualitative data used by a business.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Quantitative data is about <strong>numbers</strong> — sales figures, percentages, costs, and profits. <dfn class="term" data-def="Data based on opinions, attitudes, and feelings rather than numbers, often gathered through interviews or focus groups.">Qualitative data</dfn> is about <strong>opinions and attitudes</strong> — what people think and feel. Both types are valuable, but they tell a business different things.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Types of Business Data</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Businesses use several categories of data to support, inform, and justify their decisions. Each category provides a different perspective on how the business is performing and what opportunities exist.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Numerical information from a business’s financial statements, including revenue, costs, profit, and cash flow figures.">Financial data</dfn> comes from a business’s own accounts and financial statements. It includes figures such as sales revenue, costs, gross profit, net profit, profit margins, and cash flow. A business can use financial data to monitor how well it is performing, compare results against previous years, and spot trends. For example, if the gross profit margin has fallen from 45% to 38% over two years, the business knows its cost of sales is rising faster than revenue and must take action.</p>

<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Information a business collects about its own products, services, and the effectiveness of its marketing activities.">Marketing data</dfn> is information a business collects about its own products, services, and how well its marketing is working. This includes customer satisfaction ratings, product reviews, questionnaire responses, and website traffic data. Although ratings can be turned into numbers, much marketing data is qualitative because it captures opinions and attitudes. A business can use low satisfaction scores to identify problems and invest in improvements such as customer service training.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Information about the wider market a business operates in, including population changes, income levels, market size, and competitor activity.">Market data</dfn> is information about the wider market a business operates in. It includes demographic data such as population changes, average income, migration patterns, and unemployment rates. It also covers data about a specific product market, including market size, number of competitors, and average prices. Businesses use market data to spot new opportunities and areas for growth — for example, deciding where to open a new store or what price to charge based on competitor pricing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Reading and Interpreting Graphs and Charts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In the exam, you will be expected to read data from graphs and charts and use it to support your answers. Common graph types include bar charts, line graphs, and pie charts. The key skill is being able to extract specific values, identify trends, and perform simple calculations using the data.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list the four steps to follow when reading charts in your business exam.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>When reading charts in the exam: (1) read the title and axis labels carefully, (2) identify the specific data point the question asks about, (3) show your workings for any calculation, and (4) always include units such as £ or %. A common exam calculation is finding a percentage from a pie chart — add up all known segments and subtract from 100%.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n13">For example, if a pie chart shows market shares of 25.6%, 21.7%, 6.5%, 9.3%, and 13.2% for five companies, the remaining company’s share is 100 − 76.3 = 23.7%. Another common calculation is average sales revenue: add together the revenue figures for each period and divide by the number of periods. If sales for four months are £250,000, £450,000, £400,000, and £800,000, the average is £1,900,000 ÷ 4 = £475,000.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Using Financial Information to Understand Business Performance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Financial information helps a business understand how well it is doing. By looking at financial statements and key calculations such as gross profit margin, net profit margin, and average rate of return, a business can assess whether it is becoming more or less profitable over time. <dfn class="term" data-def="Examining financial figures to draw conclusions about a business’s health, performance, and future prospects.">Interpreting financial data</dfn> means going beyond the raw numbers to explain what they tell us about the business.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Financial data is also used to make strategic decisions about the future. The data from financial statements can help a business to forecast cash flow and staffing needs, plan budgets, control costs, and set targets for sales revenue. For example, a company like Superdry or BP publishes annual financial reports that investors, managers, and competitors all analyse to inform their own decisions.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Limitations of Financial Information</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Although financial data is essential, it has significant <dfn class="term" data-def="Restrictions or weaknesses that reduce the reliability or usefulness of financial information for making business decisions.">limitations</dfn> that businesses must be aware of when making decisions.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall three key limitations of relying solely on financial data for business decisions.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Key limitations of financial data: it is a <strong>snapshot in time</strong> (backward-looking), it is <strong>open to human error</strong>, it does not show <strong>qualitative factors</strong> like staff morale or brand reputation, and it can be <strong>manipulated</strong> to present an overly positive picture. A business that relies only on financial data may miss important changes in customer attitudes or market conditions.</p>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n20">For understanding business performance, financial information only shows what has already happened. It tells you nothing about the current trading environment or future trends. For making business decisions, financial data provides a useful starting point but should always be combined with other evidence such as market research, customer feedback, and competitor analysis. For example, a business might have excellent profit margins on paper, but if customer satisfaction is falling, those profits may not be sustainable.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Using Market Data for Business Decisions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Market data helps businesses make strategic decisions by providing information about the external environment. For example, UK population data shows the 65+ age group is projected to grow from 13.8% in 1974 to 24.3% by 2039. A business could use this data to justify expanding into products for older customers, such as healthcare or retirement services.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Similarly, data on average weekly earnings growth can influence business strategy. During 2008–2009, earnings growth fell to approximately −2% to −3%. This meant consumers had less disposable income, reducing demand for non-essential products. Businesses in sectors like fashion or hospitality would need to offer discounts or promotions, potentially reducing their profit margins. Understanding these external trends helps a business prepare and adapt its strategy.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n25">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n26">Quantitative data (numbers) and qualitative data (opinions) both help businesses make decisions — financial data, marketing data, and market data each provide a different perspective on performance.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27">Interpreting graphs and charts is a core exam skill — practice reading values, identifying trends, and performing calculations like averages and percentages.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28">Financial information has important limitations: it is backward-looking, open to human error, and does not capture qualitative factors like customer satisfaction or brand reputation.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n24">When answering questions about business data, always state the <strong>type of data</strong> (financial, marketing, or market), explain <strong>how</strong> the business would use it, and then link to the <strong>impact</strong> on decisions or performance. For calculation questions, always show your workings — you can earn method marks even if your final answer is wrong. For “Discuss” and “Evaluate” questions about limitations, consider both sides: the value of financial data <em>and</em> its weaknesses.</p>
