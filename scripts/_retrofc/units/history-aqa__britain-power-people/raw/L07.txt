--- DESCRIPTION ---
How ideas born in 17th-century England exploded in America and shaped later British reform.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Bankrupt Empire After 1763</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When the Seven Years War ended in 1763 Britain emerged victorious but exhausted. The national debt had nearly doubled. Britain now controlled an enlarged North American empire, and the army needed to defend it had to be paid for. Parliament reasoned that the colonists, having been protected from the French at British expense, should help cover the cost. The thirteen American colonies disagreed. They had no MPs at Westminster, and they argued that taxing them without their consent broke a principle that ran from Magna Carta through the Civil War &mdash; no taxation without representation.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">From Stamps to Tea, 1765-1773</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The first direct tax was the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1765 British law that required printed materials in the American colonies to be produced on stamped paper carrying a tax; widely resisted and repealed in 1766.">Stamp Act</dfn> of 1765, which required printed materials in the colonies &mdash; newspapers, legal documents, even playing cards &mdash; to carry a tax stamp. The colonial response was immediate. Mobs intimidated stamp distributors, the Stamp Act Congress drafted a unified protest, and merchants boycotted British goods. Parliament repealed the act in 1766 but issued the Declaratory Act asserting its right to legislate for the colonies &lsquo;in all cases whatsoever&rsquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The Townshend Duties of 1767 reignited the dispute by taxing imports including tea, glass, paint and paper. British troops were sent to Boston in 1768 to keep order; on 5 March 1770 they fired on a hostile crowd, killing five &mdash; the Boston Massacre. In December 1773, colonists disguised as Mohawks tipped 342 chests of East India Company tea into Boston harbour &mdash; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The December 1773 protest in which colonists destroyed 342 chests of East India Company tea in Boston harbour rather than pay the duty on it.">Boston Tea Party</dfn>. Parliament's response, the Coercive Acts of 1774, closed Boston harbour and stripped Massachusetts of self-government. The colonies began to coordinate as never before.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list the four main British taxes or laws that drove the colonies towards revolution &mdash; with the year for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>British attempts to tax the American colonies built towards revolution in steps: Stamp Act 1765, Townshend Duties 1767, Boston Massacre 1770, Boston Tea Party 1773, Coercive Acts 1774. Each step deepened the colonial argument that taxation without representation was unconstitutional.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Declaration of Independence, 1776</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">By April 1775 fighting had broken out at Lexington and Concord. The Second Continental Congress in Philadelphia voted on 4 July 1776 to adopt the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1776 statement, drafted by Thomas Jefferson, in which the thirteen American colonies declared themselves free from British rule on grounds of natural rights and consent of the governed.">Declaration of Independence</dfn>, drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson. Its language drew directly on the English political tradition. &lsquo;All men are created equal&rsquo; and the right to &lsquo;life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness&rsquo; were Lockean ideas already familiar in British radicalism. The argument that governments derive their power from the consent of the governed and could be replaced when they failed echoed the parliamentarians of 1649. The Declaration listed 27 grievances against George III &mdash; in effect, the case for the prosecution.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The War and the World, 1775-1783</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The war was long and unwinnable for Britain. George Washington's Continental Army held together despite defeats. After the American victory at Saratoga in 1777, France entered the war in 1778, followed by Spain in 1779 and the Dutch in 1780 &mdash; turning a colonial dispute into a global conflict that stretched the Royal Navy thin. The decisive defeat came at Yorktown in October 1781, where Lord Cornwallis surrendered an army of 7,000 to a combined American-French force. Britain accepted independence in the Treaty of Paris in September 1783.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Britain had a smaller population than the colonies, an over-stretched navy, and supply lines 3,000 miles long. The French alliance after 1778 was decisive: French money, French ships and French troops at Yorktown ended any realistic chance of British military victory. Politically, Lord North's government, which had pushed the Coercive Acts in 1774, fell in March 1782 once defeat became unavoidable. The loss of America discredited the &lsquo;king's friends&rsquo; faction in Parliament and gave reformers like Christopher Wyvill an opening to revive demands for parliamentary reform at home.</p>
  </div></div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Revolution Bounces Back to Britain</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">The American Revolution did not just remove thirteen colonies from the British empire &mdash; it produced a working model of representative government on the other side of the Atlantic that British radicals could point to for the rest of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Christopher Wyvill's Yorkshire Association launched a petition campaign for parliamentary reform in 1779-83, explicitly drawing on the American example. John Wilkes's earlier movement for press freedom and electoral reform gained renewed energy. Tom Paine, who had moved to Philadelphia in 1774 and written the influential pamphlet <em>Common Sense</em> for the American cause in 1776, returned to attack the British constitution in <em>Rights of Man</em> (1791-92).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: how did the American Revolution influence British reform movements in the late 18th and 19th centuries?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The American Revolution produced a written constitution and a republic that became a permanent reference point for British radicals. The Yorkshire Association (1779-83), John Wilkes, Thomas Paine's <em>Rights of Man</em> (1791-92), and later the Chartists all explicitly cited American precedent. The Revolution's slogan &mdash; no taxation without representation &mdash; became one of the founding arguments of British democratic reform.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Connecting the Pieces: 1215, 1649, 1776</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The American Revolution sits at a hinge point in this thematic study. Magna Carta in 1215 established the principle that even a king was subject to law. Simon de Montfort's Parliament in 1265 introduced the principle of representation. The trial of Charles I in 1649 demonstrated that a monarch could be removed when he broke the law. The American Revolution in 1776 took the logic one step further: a Parliament that misrepresents the people could itself be removed. Each step broadened the social base of who counted as a citizen with rights against the state.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Misconception &mdash; the Revolution was not a revolt of the poor</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">It is tempting to read the American Revolution as a popular rising like 1381 or 1536. It was not. The Patriot leadership came largely from colonial elites &mdash; merchants, lawyers, planters &mdash; who already had political voice in their local assemblies. They were defending privileges they already enjoyed against perceived British infringement. The Declaration's promise of equality was, at the time, restricted: enslaved Africans were not freed, women were not enfranchised, and Native Americans were displaced. The Revolution's significance for British history is that its principles, once written down, could not be confined to the people who wrote them &mdash; later movements (abolition, Chartism, suffragism) used those same principles to demand far more.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">The Factor Thread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">The American Revolution is best read as a victory of <strong>ideas</strong> &mdash; representation, consent, natural rights &mdash; combined with <strong>the economy</strong> (war debt, taxation), <strong>government</strong> (Lord North's miscalculations) and <strong>communication</strong> (pamphlets like <em>Common Sense</em>, the colonial postal network, transatlantic newspapers). It returns the unit to the question first raised in 1215 and 1649: where does political authority actually come from? The American answer &mdash; from the consent of the governed &mdash; reshaped British politics for the next century.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n22">War debt from the Seven Years War (ended 1763) drove British taxation of the colonies; the Stamp Act 1765, Townshend Duties 1767, Boston Tea Party 1773 and Coercive Acts 1774 built towards revolution.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The Declaration of Independence in July 1776 used Lockean ideas already familiar in British radicalism &mdash; consent of the governed, natural rights &mdash; to justify breaking from Britain. French entry in 1778 and the surrender at Yorktown 1781 forced British recognition of independence in 1783.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">The Revolution bounced back to shape British reform: the Yorkshire Association, Wilkes, Paine's <em>Rights of Man</em> and later the Chartists all drew on American precedent; &lsquo;no taxation without representation&rsquo; became a founding argument of British democratic reform.</li>
</ul>
