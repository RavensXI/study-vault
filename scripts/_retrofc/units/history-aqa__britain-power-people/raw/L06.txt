--- DESCRIPTION ---
How a quarrel over money and religion ended with a king on the scaffold and England as a republic.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A King Who Believed He Answered Only to God</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charles I came to the throne in 1625 holding firmly to the doctrine of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that monarchs receive their authority directly from God and cannot be held accountable by Parliament or the people.">Divine Right of Kings</dfn>. He saw Parliament not as a partner in government but as a body that occasionally voted him money. From 1629 he ruled without Parliament for eleven years &mdash; the &lsquo;Personal Rule&rsquo; &mdash; raising revenue through unpopular schemes like ship money, a tax originally imposed only on coastal towns but extended inland. Religious changes under Archbishop William Laud brought ceremony, altar rails and bishops' authority back into English worship, alarming Puritans who saw it as a slide back to Catholicism.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Slide to War, 1640-42</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Personal Rule collapsed when Scottish Covenanters, resisting Laud's prayer book, invaded northern England. Charles needed money for an army, and only Parliament could vote it. The Long Parliament that met in November 1640 attacked the king's ministers (Strafford executed 1641), abolished the prerogative courts, and in December 1641 issued the Grand Remonstrance &mdash; a list of over 200 grievances. In January 1642 Charles tried to arrest five MPs in the Commons chamber, including John Pym. He failed; the Speaker refused to give them up. Within months both sides were raising armies. The First Civil War (1642-46) had begun.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list four causes of the English Revolution covering ideas, religion, money and political crisis.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The English Revolution had four interlocking causes. <strong>Ideas</strong>: Charles I's belief in Divine Right vs Parliament's claim to consent. <strong>Religion</strong>: Laudian reforms vs Puritan reformers. <strong>Money</strong>: ship money and the Personal Rule (1629-40). <strong>Political crisis</strong>: the Grand Remonstrance and the failed arrest of the Five Members in January 1642.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The New Model Army and the Politics of the Camp</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">In 1645 Parliament reorganised its forces into the <dfn class="term" data-def="Parliament's professional army formed in 1645, promoting officers by merit, paying regular wages, and becoming a vehicle for Puritan and political radicalism.">New Model Army</dfn> &mdash; full-time, paid, disciplined, and recruited and promoted by merit rather than aristocratic connection. Sir Thomas Fairfax was its commander; Oliver Cromwell its lieutenant-general of horse. At Naseby in June 1645 the New Model Army crushed Charles's main field army. The First Civil War effectively ended in 1646 with Charles's surrender.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">But the army was now a political force. Soldiers had risked their lives for Parliament and were owed back pay. Many were Puritans with strong religious convictions. From within the ranks emerged a movement called the <dfn class="term" data-def="A radical political movement of the 1640s that argued for manhood suffrage, religious liberty and equality before the law, set out in the Agreement of the People (1647).">Levellers</dfn>, led by John Lilburne. Their 1647 Agreement of the People argued for manhood suffrage (excluding servants and beggars), regular elections, religious liberty and equality before the law &mdash; the most democratic programme yet seen in England.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">In October 1647 senior officers and Leveller agitators met in St Mary's Church, Putney, to debate the future constitution. The exchange between Colonel Thomas Rainsborough and Henry Ireton was the most famous moment in English democratic history before the Chartists. Rainsborough argued, &ldquo;The poorest he that is in England hath a life to live as the greatest he&rdquo; &mdash; and therefore had a right to a vote. Ireton replied that only those with property had a real stake in the country. The debates were never resolved, but their text survived and became a touchstone of British radicalism: even Chartists in the 1830s and 40s explicitly cited Rainsborough.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Trial and Execution of a King, January 1649</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Charles started a Second Civil War in 1648 from his island prison at Carisbrooke, plotting with the Scots. The army, furious, decided he could not be trusted alive. In December 1648 Colonel Pride excluded MPs who wanted to negotiate, leaving a smaller &lsquo;Rump Parliament&rsquo; that voted to put the king on trial. The trial opened on 20 January 1649 in Westminster Hall. Charles refused to plead, denying the court's jurisdiction. He was found guilty of treason. On 30 January 1649, he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall. The English had executed their king.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: why was the trial and execution of Charles I a turning point in the relationship between citizen and state?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Charles I was tried by his own subjects under his own name in Westminster Hall and beheaded on 30 January 1649. For the first time in European history a reigning king had been publicly executed under law by a court claiming to act on the people's authority. Divine Right of Kings, in practice, was finished.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Republic That Wasn't a Democracy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">From 1649 to 1660 England was a republic &mdash; the Commonwealth and, from 1653, the Protectorate under Oliver Cromwell as Lord Protector. Power did not pass to the people. Cromwell suppressed the Levellers (the 1649 Burford mutineers were shot), dissolved the Rump Parliament in 1653 and ruled with the army's backing. He turned down the offer of the crown in 1657 but lived in many ways as a king &mdash; addressed as &lsquo;Your Highness&rsquo;, choosing his successor (his son Richard).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Cromwell died in September 1658. Within two years the regime had collapsed. The Restoration of May 1660 brought Charles II back from exile. The republic was over. But Parliament was now permanently superior. Charles II reigned with Parliament's tolerance, and his brother James II was driven out in 1688 when he tried to assert royal authority again. Divine Right was never seriously claimed in England after the Glorious Revolution of 1688-89.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The misconception trap is to call the Civil War a &lsquo;fight for democracy&rsquo;. Most parliamentarians wanted a constitutional monarchy, not a republic. The Levellers were suppressed by Cromwell, and the Restoration brought back the monarchy. But the long-term significance is real: kings could be removed, Parliament was permanently superior, and a body of democratic ideas (manhood suffrage, religious liberty, equality before the law) had been articulated by the Levellers in the Agreement of the People. Chartists, suffragettes and modern democrats all traced their language back to Putney.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Factor Thread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The English Revolution is the cleanest case study in the unit for the factor of <strong>ideas</strong>: Divine Right against parliamentary authority, Leveller democracy against Ireton's defence of property. <strong>Religion</strong>: Laudian conformity against Puritan reform. <strong>War</strong>: 1642-51 created the New Model Army that gave radical ideas an institutional vehicle. <strong>Government</strong>: Personal Rule and ship money set up the constitutional collapse. The factors converge on the scaffold of 1649 &mdash; one of the central images in this entire 850-year course.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The English Revolution sprang from Charles I's belief in Divine Right, conflict with Parliament over money and religion, the Personal Rule (1629-40), and the failed arrest of the Five Members in 1642.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The New Model Army (1645) gave Parliament victory at Naseby and became a vehicle for political radicalism &mdash; the Levellers, the Putney Debates and the Agreement of the People.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">The trial and execution of Charles I (January 1649) ended Divine Right in practice; the Restoration of 1660 brought back the monarchy but left Parliament permanently superior &mdash; a turning point that seeded later democratic movements.</li>
</ul>
