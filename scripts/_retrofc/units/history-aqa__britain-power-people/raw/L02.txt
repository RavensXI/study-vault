Why barons rebelled against King John, what Magna Carta said in 1215, and the gap between the.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">King John and the Road to Runnymede</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1215, King John of England was loathed. He had inherited a strong throne from his brother Richard the Lionheart in 1199, but had managed to lose the duchy of Normandy to the French king Philip II by 1204, taking Anjou and most of his French inheritance with it. To pay for failed campaigns to recover those lands, John squeezed the barons with <dfn class="term" data-def="A medieval tax paid by knights to a king or lord in place of military service.">scutage</dfn> &mdash; a payment in lieu of military service &mdash; at unprecedented rates. He demanded eleven scutages in seventeen years; his predecessors had averaged one every three years.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">Religion made things worse. John quarrelled with Pope Innocent III over the appointment of Stephen Langton as Archbishop of Canterbury. The Pope put England under an <dfn class="term" data-def="A church punishment that suspended most religious services in a country, used to pressure rulers.">interdict</dfn> from 1208 to 1213, suspending most church services. John eventually surrendered, accepting England as a papal fief in 1213 to pull Innocent onto his side. The financial concession to Rome did not impress the barons.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name three of King John's failures in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>King John's failures stacked up: lost Normandy and Anjou by 1204, taxed barons heavily through scutage, quarrelled with Pope Innocent III, and was decisively defeated by France at the Battle of Bouvines in July 1214. Bouvines triggered the baronial revolt that produced Magna Carta.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Baronial Revolt and Stephen Langton</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">After Bouvines, the rebel barons &mdash; mostly drawn from the north and east of England &mdash; demanded that John confirm the rights they had held under earlier kings. Archbishop Langton played the crucial mediating role. He had read the coronation charter of Henry I (1100), which limited royal powers, and used it as a template. Langton was a Pope&rsquo;s appointee who became a barons&rsquo; ally &mdash; a reminder that the role of the individual depends entirely on the conditions around it.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">In May 1215, the rebels captured London. John was forced to negotiate at Runnymede, a meadow on the Thames between Windsor and Staines. On 15 June 1215, he sealed the document we now call <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1215 charter sealed by King John at Runnymede that limited royal power and protected baronial rights.">Magna Carta</dfn>, the &lsquo;Great Charter&rsquo;, with 63 clauses.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">What Magna Carta Actually Said</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Most of the 63 clauses were technical baronial grievances &mdash; rules about feudal inheritance fees, widows&rsquo; remarriage, and the misuse of royal forests. But three sections mattered for the long term:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n10">The Church was guaranteed its freedoms, including freedom from royal interference in elections.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11">No scutage or <dfn class="term" data-def="A special medieval tax demanded by a king from his subjects on top of regular feudal dues.">aid</dfn> could be raised &lsquo;without the common counsel of the realm&rsquo; &mdash; a council of barons.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12">Clause 39 promised that &lsquo;no free man shall be seized or imprisoned&hellip; except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land.&rsquo; This is the seed of due process.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n13">Standards of weights and measures were also regulated &mdash; an early sign that the king answered to commercial interests, not just barons. Crucially, &lsquo;free men&rsquo; in 1215 did not mean ordinary subjects. Most of the population were unfree villeins; they had no protection from clause 39.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Misconception &mdash; Magna Carta Was Not a People&rsquo;s Charter</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Examiner reports flag this repeatedly: students claim Magna Carta &lsquo;started democracy&rsquo;. It did not. It was a peace treaty between the king and a small armed group of barons, drafted to protect the elite &mdash; nobles, the Church and the City of London. The famous &lsquo;free man&rsquo; clauses applied to roughly 10&ndash;20% of the population at most. The democratic legacy &mdash; rule of law, no taxation without representation &mdash; was constructed by lawyers and politicians centuries later, particularly in the seventeenth century.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Short-Term Failure</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Within ten weeks Magna Carta was a dead letter. John wrote to Innocent III, who annulled the charter on 24 August 1215, calling it &lsquo;shameful, demeaning, illegal and unjust&rsquo;. Civil war resumed. The barons invited Prince Louis of France to take the throne. John died of dysentery in October 1216, leaving the crown to his nine-year-old son Henry III.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Long-Term Survival &mdash; The Reissues</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Magna Carta survived because Henry III&rsquo;s regents reissued it in 1216 and 1217 to win loyal support, and Henry confirmed it again in 1225 in return for a tax. By 1297 it was on the statute roll under Edward I. Each reissue stripped some clauses and confirmed others. The 1297 version remains technically in force today &mdash; clause 39 is still UK law.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The 17th-Century Re-reading</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19">In the 1620s, parliamentary lawyers like Sir Edward Coke held up Magna Carta against Charles I as proof that the king was under the law. Coke argued clause 39 protected every Englishman, not just &lsquo;free men&rsquo;. This re-reading shaped the 1628 Petition of Right and ultimately the trial of Charles I in 1649. The medieval document became the foundation myth of English liberty &mdash; a meaning later than the document itself.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: what did clause 39 say, and why does it still matter today?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Clause 39 promised no free man could be imprisoned except by lawful judgement. In 1215 it protected the elite; by 1628 lawyers used it to limit Charles I; today it is the basis of due process and habeas corpus in UK law. Its significance grew over time, not at the time.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">King John lost Normandy, taxed heavily, fought the Pope and was crushed at Bouvines in 1214 &mdash; baronial revolt followed.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Magna Carta in June 1215 protected the Church, limited royal taxation without consent, and promised due process for free men &mdash; not for most of the population.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The charter was annulled within ten weeks but reissued under Henry III. Its long-term significance was constructed by 17th-century lawyers, who turned it into a foundation of English liberty.</li>
</ul>
