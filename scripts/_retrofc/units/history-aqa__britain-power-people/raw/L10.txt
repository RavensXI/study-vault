From an oath under a Dorset tree to dockers shutting down London — how working people built.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Combination, Repression and Cautious Legality</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">For most of the early industrial revolution, organised labour was illegal. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Two laws (1799 and 1800) that made it a criminal offence for workers to combine together to demand higher wages or shorter hours.">Combination Acts</dfn> of 1799 and 1800 made it a crime for workers to act together to demand better pay or hours. Repealed in 1824-25 partly because magistrates could not enforce them, the Acts left a long shadow: trade unions were technically legal afterwards, but the state remained suspicious, courts hostile, and employers willing to lock out, blacklist or prosecute. The story of the next century is of working people turning a precarious legality into permanent power.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Robert Owen and the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">In 1834 the factory owner and reformer <dfn class="term" data-def="A Welsh factory owner (1771-1858) and early socialist who championed cooperation, model factories at New Lanark, and the GNCTU.">Robert Owen</dfn> launched the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union (GNCTU). Owen wanted a single national union covering every trade &mdash; a million members under one banner, threatening a general strike to force political change. Within months it claimed half a million members. Within a year it had collapsed under employer counter-attack, internal disputes and the cost of supporting locked-out workers. The GNCTU showed both the appetite for national organisation and the limits of trying to build it in one leap.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who were the Tolpuddle Martyrs, what crime they were charged with, and how the public forced their pardon.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In 1834 six Dorset farm workers led by George Loveless formed a friendly society and swore a secret oath to support each other. They were sentenced to seven years&rsquo; transportation to Australia under an obscure 1797 anti-mutiny law. A petition of 800,000 signatures and demonstrations in London forced their pardon by 1836.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Tolpuddle Martyrs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Tolpuddle case shows the state at its most ruthless &mdash; and the public at its most powerful. Wages in Dorset had fallen to seven shillings a week. Loveless and his five colleagues set up a friendly society to bargain for a wage rise. The local landowner, James Frampton, knew unions were legal, so he reached for an old law against secret oath-taking originally aimed at naval mutineers in 1797. The Tolpuddle six were tried at Dorchester Assizes in March 1834 and shipped to Australia. The case backfired catastrophically: a national petition gathered 800,000 signatures, demonstrations of up to 100,000 marched on Whitehall, and by 1836 the government quietly pardoned the men. They returned to England as heroes of the trade union movement.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">New Model Unions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">After Tolpuddle and the collapse of the GNCTU, trade unionism shifted strategy. From the 1850s, skilled workers founded <dfn class="term" data-def="Skilled craft unions of the 1850s and 1860s (engineers, carpenters) that focused on benefits, bargaining and respectability rather than mass agitation.">New Model Unions</dfn> &mdash; the Amalgamated Society of Engineers (1851) and the Amalgamated Society of Carpenters and Joiners (1860) being the largest. These unions charged high subscriptions, offered sickness and unemployment benefits, and worked with employers rather than against them. They were respectable and well-funded; their members were often described in the press as the &lsquo;aristocracy of labour&rsquo;.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">This respectability paid political dividends. The Trade Union Act 1871 gave unions legal protection for their funds. The TUC, founded in Manchester in 1868, gave them a national voice. The 1875 Conspiracy and Protection of Property Act made peaceful picketing legal. Trade unions had moved, in two generations, from criminal conspiracy to recognised institution.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n11">By the 1880s a new generation of organisers wanted to bring unskilled workers &mdash; matchgirls, dockers, gas workers &mdash; into the union movement. These were workers the New Model Unions had ignored: low-paid, easily replaced, and mostly women or migrants. <dfn class="term" data-def="The unionisation of unskilled and low-paid workers in the late 1880s, including the matchgirls&rsquo; strike of 1888 and the London Dock Strike of 1889.">New unionism</dfn> rejected the New Model approach of high subscriptions and benefits, instead offering low fees, mass meetings and the strike as a primary weapon.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Matchgirls and the Dockers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In June 1888 around 1,400 women and girls walked out of the Bryant &amp; May factory in Bow, east London. Conditions were appalling: 14-hour days, fines for talking, white phosphorus that caused &lsquo;phossy jaw&rsquo; (necrosis of the jawbone). The journalist Annie Besant&rsquo;s expos&eacute; in <em>The Link</em> made the strike a national scandal. After three weeks the company conceded, recognising the new union of matchgirls. It was a small dispute by industrial standards, but it proved unskilled women could organise and win.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The London Dock Strike of August 1889 was the breakthrough. Around 100,000 dockers walked out, demanding the &lsquo;dockers&rsquo; tanner&rsquo; (sixpence an hour). Led by Ben Tillett, John Burns and Tom Mann, the strike paralysed the world&rsquo;s largest port for five weeks. Public sympathy was enormous; Australian unions sent &pound;30,000 to support the strikers. The dockers won the tanner, plus union recognition and overtime rates. Within a year, gas workers, transport workers and dockers across Britain had unionised on the new model.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The 1889 dockers&rsquo; victory transformed working-class politics. Total trade union membership doubled in three years. In 1900 the TUC, the Independent Labour Party (founded 1893 by Keir Hardie) and other socialist groups created the Labour Representation Committee, designed to send working-class MPs to Westminster. By the 1906 election the LRC had become the Labour Party, returning 29 MPs. The pattern Chartism had imagined sixty years earlier was finally taking shape: an organised working-class voice inside Parliament.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Close this and write three sentences contrasting Tolpuddle (1834), the New Model Unions (1850s) and the Dock Strike (1889) — one sentence on each, focusing on tactics.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Trade unionism developed through three phases. Tolpuddle (1834) showed the cost of state hostility. The New Model Unions (1850s onwards) won legality and respectability through high subscriptions and benefits. New unionism (1888-89) brought unskilled workers in through mass strikes — and led directly to the Labour Party in 1906.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Factor Thread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Trade unionism brings together several factors at once. <strong>The economy</strong> &mdash; industrial wages, lockouts, the price of bread &mdash; is the constant pressure. <strong>The role of the individual</strong>: Loveless, Owen, Besant, Tillett each step into the gap left by the law. <strong>Government</strong> swings between repression (Combination Acts, transportation) and concession (Trade Union Act 1871, Conspiracy Act 1875). And <strong>communication</strong> &mdash; mass petitioning, exposure journalism, telegraphed solidarity from Australia &mdash; turned local strikes into national events. Compare this with Chartism: trade unions chose economic action where Chartists chose constitutional petitioning, and won concrete gains where Chartism did not.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The Tolpuddle Martyrs (1834) were transported under an obscure 1797 law, but a 800,000-signature petition won their pardon — proving public pressure could check state power.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">New Model Unions (1850s onwards) won legal protection through respectability; the Trade Union Act 1871 and the TUC (1868) gave organised labour a permanent national voice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">New unionism — the matchgirls&rsquo; strike (1888) and the London Dock Strike (1889) — brought unskilled workers in and led directly to the founding of the Labour Party in 1906.</li>
</ul>
