<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Thematic Study Across 850 Years</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">This unit is unusual. Instead of studying one short period in depth, you will leap across nearly nine centuries, from <dfn class="term" data-def="A medieval social and economic system in which land was held in exchange for service or labour, with kings, lords, knights and peasants tied together by obligation.">feudalism</dfn> in the 1170s to twenty-first-century campaigns for racial justice. The thread that connects everything is the relationship between the citizen and the state &mdash; how ordinary people in Britain have challenged authority, and how rulers have responded. By the end you should be able to trace patterns of protest and reform from medieval revolts through to modern social movements.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">In c1170, Britain was a feudal kingdom. The king ruled by divine right, most people were unfree <dfn class="term" data-def="A peasant who was legally tied to a lord's land and could not move or marry without permission.">serfs</dfn>, and there was no Parliament. By 2025, Britain is a parliamentary democracy with universal suffrage, a Human Rights Act and protest protected by law. The unit asks how that journey happened &mdash; and whose pressure forced each step.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the seven AQA factors from memory. Aim for all seven in 60 seconds.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AQA tests seven factors across the unit: war, religion, chance, government, communication, the economy, and ideas (especially equality, democracy and representation). The role of the individual sits alongside these as an eighth strand.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Seven Factors &mdash; Your Spine for the Whole Course</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Every protest, reform and revolt you study fits one or more of these factors. You should learn to spot them quickly, because the 16-mark essay always names one and asks you to weigh it against the others.</p>

<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>War</strong> &mdash; conflict often forces change. The English Civil War (1642&ndash;51) executed a king; the First World War accelerated women&rsquo;s suffrage in 1918.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Religion</strong> &mdash; the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536) defended Catholicism; Quaker networks drove anti-slavery campaigning in the 1780s.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Chance</strong> &mdash; the Black Death (1348&ndash;49) reshaped the labour market and made the Peasants&rsquo; Revolt of 1381 possible.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Government</strong> &mdash; royal overreach (King John, Charles I) and parliamentary repression (Pitt in the 1790s, Thatcher in the 1980s) both trigger resistance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Communication</strong> &mdash; printing presses, Chartist newspapers, radio in 1926 and social media for Black Lives Matter UK have all amplified protest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>The economy</strong> &mdash; the 1381 poll tax, the 1815 Corn Laws and 1980s unemployment all sparked organised protest.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Ideas</strong> &mdash; especially equality, democracy and <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that people should have a voice in choosing the government that rules them, often through elected representatives.">representation</dfn>. The Levellers in 1647, the Americans in 1776 and the suffragettes after 1903 all argued from ideas.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Four Question Types You Will Be Marked On</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Paper 2 Section A always asks the same four questions in the same order. Knowing what each rewards is half the battle.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down the marks for each of the four question types in the right order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four question types: 4-mark &lsquo;two ways similar or different&rsquo;, 8-mark &lsquo;explain the significance&rsquo;, 8-mark &lsquo;explain a development&rsquo;, and 16-mark factors essay. The factors essay is worth as much as the other three combined.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">The 4-mark question wants two clearly distinct points, each with specific supporting detail. Comparing methods or outcomes tends to score higher than comparing causes. The 8-mark <dfn class="term" data-def="A judgement about why an event mattered, both at the time it happened and over the longer term.">significance</dfn> question rewards students who address importance both at the time and over time &mdash; a common trap is to describe what something was, not why it mattered. The 8-mark development question asks for narrative-with-causation, not just a list of events. The 16-mark factors essay must address the named factor first, then weigh it against at least two others, drawing examples from at least two of the four parts of the course.</p>

Both ended when the king broke promises made under pressure: Richard II had ringleaders executed within weeks; Henry VIII used Bigod&rsquo;s rising as an excuse to renege on the Pontefract pardon.&rdquo; Two distinct points, each with specific detail.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Judgement Beats Listing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">The single most common mistake on this paper is listing factors without weighing them. A student who writes &ldquo;the role of individuals mattered, but so did war, religion and the economy&rdquo; is just inventorying causes. The mark scheme rewards students who reach a substantiated overall judgement &mdash; for example, arguing that ideas matter most because they outlast the individuals who carried them. From lesson one, train yourself to take a position and defend it.</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n22">For the 4-mark question, give two clearly different points with specific named examples &mdash; vague comparisons cap at 2 marks. For 8-mark significance, address importance both then and now. For the 16-mark factors essay, name the factor in your opening line, weigh it against at least two others, and reach a judgement &mdash; single-period answers cap in the lower bands. Use specialist vocabulary like &lsquo;franchise&rsquo;, &lsquo;parliamentary supremacy&rsquo;, &lsquo;mass movement&rsquo; precisely &mdash; SPaG marks reward this.</p>
