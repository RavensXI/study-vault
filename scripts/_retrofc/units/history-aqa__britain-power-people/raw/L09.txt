--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Pressure Without the Vote</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Most British people in 1800 could not vote, yet Parliament passed three of the most ambitious reforms in its history between 1807 and 1846: the abolition of the slave trade, the abolition of slavery itself, and the repeal of the Corn Laws. Each was driven by a <dfn class="term" data-def="An organised group that campaigns to change a law or policy without seeking power for itself.">pressure group</dfn> &mdash; an organised body of citizens who used petitions, lectures, newspapers and boycotts to force change. This lesson traces how three of those movements worked, and why they reshaped British politics.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Anti-Slavery: The First Mass Campaign</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">From the 1760s, Quaker meetings in London and Philadelphia argued that <dfn class="term" data-def="The buying, selling and forced labour of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic, in which Britain played a dominant role from the 17th to the early 19th century.">the Atlantic slave trade</dfn> was incompatible with Christian belief. Quakers were excluded from Parliament, so they organised outside it. In 1787 they helped found the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade, with William Wilberforce as its parliamentary face. Within five years, mass petitions, public lectures and a national sugar boycott of 1791 had made abolition one of the central political questions of the day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The voices of the enslaved themselves were central to the campaign. Olaudah Equiano, born in West Africa around 1745 and trafficked through the Caribbean and North America, bought his freedom and settled in Britain. His autobiography, <em>The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano</em> (1789), went through nine editions in his lifetime. Equiano lectured across Britain, putting a human face on a system most British people had never seen.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: the dates of the two abolition Acts, who they freed, and how much was paid in compensation and to whom.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Abolition came in two stages. The Slave Trade Act 1807 banned the buying and selling of enslaved Africans by British ships. The Slavery Abolition Act 1833 banned slavery itself across most of the British Empire &mdash; but paid &pound;20 million in compensation to slave-owners, not the enslaved.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Anti-Corn Law League</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">If anti-slavery was a moral campaign, the <dfn class="term" data-def="A pressure group founded in Manchester in 1838 by Richard Cobden and John Bright to demand the repeal of the Corn Laws and free trade in grain.">Anti-Corn Law League</dfn> was a campaign of economic self-interest dressed in moral language. Founded in Manchester in 1838 by Richard Cobden and John Bright, the League argued that the 1815 Corn Laws kept bread expensive for working people and protected aristocratic landowners at the cost of industrial growth. Backed by Lancashire mill-owners, the League had money the Chartists never had, and it spent it on a new model of campaigning.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">League pamphlets travelled by the new penny post (introduced 1840) and the expanding railway network. Cobden and Bright criss-crossed the country giving lectures. The League ran a candidate-screening operation in elections. By 1845 it employed full-time staff and had a national membership. When the Irish Famine of 1845-46 raised the political cost of high bread prices to breaking point, Sir Robert Peel split his own Conservative Party and pushed through the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. Peel told the Commons it was the League&rsquo;s campaign, more than any single MP, that had moved him.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Factory Reform and Social Reformers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Alongside the great national campaigns ran a slower fight inside Parliament for factory and mining reform. <dfn class="term" data-def="Lord Anthony Ashley Cooper (1801-1885), the Tory MP and social reformer who led parliamentary campaigns to limit working hours for women and children.">Lord Shaftesbury</dfn> &mdash; an evangelical Tory MP &mdash; led the Ten Hours Movement, drawing on testimony from Yorkshire and Lancashire mill towns. The Factory Act 1833 banned children under nine from textile mills and limited working hours. The Mines Act 1842 banned women and children under ten from working underground. The Factory Act 1847 finally won the ten-hour day for women and young people. Shaftesbury combined his religious conviction with parliamentary technique: select committee evidence, careful drafting, and patience over decades.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Edwin Chadwick&rsquo;s <em>Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population</em> (1842) made a similar case for public health, showing that disease in industrial cities cost more in lost labour than sanitation would cost. Public health Acts came later (1848 and 1875), but Chadwick&rsquo;s report changed the terms of debate. The pattern is clear: in the early 19th century, organised information &mdash; evidence, statistics, testimony &mdash; was as powerful a tool for reform as the rights of the citizen.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n14">Both movements organised outside Parliament, both used mass petitioning, both relied on lecturers and pamphlets, both framed their case as a moral one even when economic interests were at stake, and both succeeded by changing public opinion to the point that Parliament could not resist. Both also had charismatic individuals (Wilberforce, Cobden) who became the public face but stood on a much wider organisation. The differences matter for the 4-mark question: anti-slavery used a sugar boycott; the League did not. The League had the railways and penny post; anti-slavery did not. Anti-slavery rested on Quaker networks; the League on industrial wealth.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and write a single sentence linking each of these to the campaign that used them: sugar boycott, penny post, Olaudah Equiano, Edwin Chadwick.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By 1846 three campaigns had proved Parliament could be moved without the vote. Anti-slavery (Quaker networks, petitions, boycott) won abolition in 1807 and 1833. The Anti-Corn Law League (Cobden, Bright, penny post, railways) won repeal in 1846. Factory reform under Shaftesbury used parliamentary inquiry to win the Ten Hours Act of 1847.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Factor Thread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">These campaigns add three factors to the running balance. <strong>Religion</strong> drove the Quaker network and Shaftesbury&rsquo;s evangelical conscience. <strong>Communication</strong> &mdash; pamphlets, petitions, the penny post, the railways &mdash; turned local feeling into national pressure. <strong>The role of the individual</strong> &mdash; Wilberforce, Equiano, Cobden, Shaftesbury &mdash; gave each cause a face, but always inside a wider movement. When you reach the 16-mark factors essay, these campaigns are the strongest evidence that organised pressure can win without armed revolt.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n20">The anti-slavery movement was the first mass British campaign — built on Quaker networks, petitions, the sugar boycott and Equiano&rsquo;s testimony — winning abolition in 1807 and 1833.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The Anti-Corn Law League used new tools (penny post, railways, paid staff) to force repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and pioneered the modern lobby group.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Factory reform under Lord Shaftesbury and Chadwick&rsquo;s sanitary report showed that organised evidence could move Parliament even without the vote.</li>
</ul>
