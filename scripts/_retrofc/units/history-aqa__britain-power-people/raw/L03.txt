--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Henry III and the Sicilian Business</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Magna Carta was supposed to settle the question of royal power. By the 1250s, it was settling nothing. Henry III, son of King John, had reigned since 1216 and inherited his father&rsquo;s habit of overreaching. His most damaging mistake was the &lsquo;Sicilian Business&rsquo; &mdash; in 1254 he agreed with the Pope that his second son Edmund would become king of Sicily, a kingdom Henry would have to conquer first. Henry promised to fund a vast military campaign, then asked his barons to pay for it.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The barons refused. They were already angry that Henry surrounded himself with foreign favourites, especially his French half-brothers from Poitou, who held lucrative offices and lands. They had watched Henry&rsquo;s government fail to enforce justice locally and ignore the spirit of Magna Carta. The Sicilian Business was the breaking point.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what was the Sicilian Business and why did it anger the barons?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1254 Sicilian Business committed Henry III to conquer Sicily for his son Edmund and pay vast sums to the Pope. The barons saw it as a foreign vanity project funded from English pockets, and refused to pay. This refusal, combined with hatred of foreign favourites, broke the relationship between Henry and his nobility.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Provisions of Oxford, 1258</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In April 1258, seven barons stormed into Westminster armed and demanded reform. At a parliament in Oxford that June, they forced Henry to accept the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1258 reform programme that placed Henry III under the supervision of a 15-member council of barons.">Provisions of Oxford</dfn>, sometimes called England&rsquo;s first written constitution. Under the Provisions, a 15-member council of barons supervised the king. A parliament would meet three times a year. Royal sheriffs would answer to local communities, not just to the king.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n7">The most prominent reformer was Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester. Born in France around 1208, he had married Henry&rsquo;s sister Eleanor in 1238 and built a power base in the English Midlands. De Montfort was charismatic, ruthless, and committed to legal reform. He was also &mdash; ironically, given the foreign-favourites complaint &mdash; himself a Frenchman by birth.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Civil War and the Battle of Lewes, 1264</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Henry chafed under the Provisions. In 1261 he asked the Pope to release him from his oath, and reasserted royal authority. The barons split. By 1263 the country was in open civil war, the <dfn class="term" data-def="The civil war of 1264&ndash;67 between Henry III and the reform barons led by Simon de Montfort.">Second Barons&rsquo; War</dfn>. At the Battle of Lewes in May 1264, de Montfort decisively defeated the royal army and captured both King Henry III and his son Prince Edward. For the next fifteen months, de Montfort effectively ruled England.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Parliament of January 1265</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">De Montfort needed broad support against royalist holdouts. In January 1265, he summoned the most innovative <dfn class="term" data-def="A medieval assembly summoned by a king to advise on government, taxation and law.">parliament</dfn> England had ever seen. Alongside the usual barons and bishops, he called two knights from every shire and two <dfn class="term" data-def="Representatives of towns or boroughs summoned to Parliament alongside knights of the shire.">burgesses</dfn> from each major town. For the first time, commoners &mdash; townspeople and lesser landholders &mdash; sat in a national assembly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: who did Simon de Montfort summon to his 1265 Parliament that had never been called before?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1265 Parliament was the first to include knights of the shire and burgesses from towns alongside the barons and bishops. De Montfort summoned them not from democratic principle but because he needed political support. The accidental precedent reshaped the future Parliament permanently.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">De Montfort is sometimes called the &lsquo;father of the House of Commons&rsquo;, but he was no democrat. He summoned commoners because he needed their political and military support against royalist barons who had not joined his side. The knights and burgesses he called were elite property-owners, not ordinary subjects. Voting and serving were restricted to the wealthy. The democratic significance is real but indirect &mdash; the precedent of including commoners survived; the wider franchise took 600 more years.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Battle of Evesham, 1265</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Prince Edward escaped from house arrest in May 1265 and rallied royalist forces. On 4 August 1265, at Evesham in Worcestershire, Edward&rsquo;s army caught de Montfort&rsquo;s smaller force. The earl was killed; his body was mutilated &mdash; head, hands, feet and genitals cut off &mdash; in a deliberate humiliation. The reform movement collapsed; Henry III was restored to full power; many of de Montfort&rsquo;s lands were confiscated.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Long-Term Impact</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">In the short term, the reform movement failed. But Edward I, when he became king in 1272, kept what worked. His &lsquo;Model Parliament&rsquo; of 1295 included two knights from every shire and two burgesses from every borough &mdash; exactly de Montfort&rsquo;s 1265 format. The two-chamber Parliament we know today, with House of Lords and House of Commons, evolved from these summonses. By the fourteenth century, the practice had hardened into custom.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Magna Carta in 1215 established that the king must take counsel before raising taxes. The Provisions of Oxford in 1258 turned that into a permanent supervisory council. The 1265 Parliament expanded who counted as &lsquo;the realm&rsquo; whose counsel mattered. Each step came from baronial revolt against royal overreach &mdash; not from a deliberate plan to build democracy. The institutions were built accidentally, by men solving immediate political problems.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Henry III's foreign favourites and the 1254 Sicilian Business broke trust with the barons; the 1258 Provisions of Oxford forced a 15-member supervisory council on the king.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Simon de Montfort defeated Henry at Lewes in 1264 and ruled in his name; in January 1265 he summoned the first Parliament to include knights of the shire and burgesses.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">De Montfort died at Evesham in August 1265, but Edward I's Model Parliament in 1295 kept the two-knights-and-two-burgesses formula — building the foundations of the modern Commons.</li>
</ul>
