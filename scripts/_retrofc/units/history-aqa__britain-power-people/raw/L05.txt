The largest peacetime revolt in English history and the religious crisis behind 30,000 marchers.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Religious Earthquake of the 1530s</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the early 1530s Henry VIII broke with Rome to secure a divorce from Catherine of Aragon. The 1534 <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1534 law that declared Henry VIII Supreme Head of the Church of England, breaking with the authority of the Pope in Rome.">Act of Supremacy</dfn> declared the king Supreme Head of the Church of England. From 1535 his chief minister Thomas Cromwell sent commissioners around the country to value Church property. By 1536 the smaller monasteries &mdash; those with incomes under &pound;200 a year &mdash; were being closed in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The process from 1536 to 1540 by which Henry VIII closed England's monasteries and sold their lands to the Crown and its supporters.">Dissolution of the Monasteries</dfn>, their lands sold to the Crown and its supporters. To many ordinary northerners this looked less like reform and more like a war on the religion their families had practised for centuries.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Why the North Rose: Four Causes Tangled Together</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The Pilgrimage of Grace had no single cause. Religious grievance was the loudest: monasteries were not just spiritual centres but the social and economic heart of northern villages, providing schools, almshouses, hospitality and employment. The dissolution threatened to destroy a whole local infrastructure. But the rising also drew on economic anxiety &mdash; rumours that Henry would tax baptisms, marriages and burials, on top of bad harvests in 1535 and 1536 that pushed grain prices up. Politically, northerners resented the &lsquo;low-born&rsquo; advisers around the king &mdash; Cromwell above all, who they saw as displacing the traditional nobility. Socially, peasant tenants and town gentry alike were uneasy about rising rents and enclosure on monastic lands now in lay hands.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name four different causes of the Pilgrimage of Grace and give one example for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Pilgrimage of Grace combined religious, economic, political and social grievances. Religion was loudest &mdash; the dissolution of smaller monasteries from 1536 felt like an attack on Catholic identity &mdash; but rumours of new taxes, hostility to Cromwell, and rising rents all fed in. No single cause explains the scale of 30,000 men in the field.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Lincolnshire Rising and the March of 30,000</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The trouble began at Louth in Lincolnshire on 1 October 1536, when a Cromwell commissioner arrived to dissolve the local monastery. Within a week 40,000 men were assembled. Henry sent the Duke of Suffolk with a small royal force and a public pardon; the Lincolnshire rising collapsed by mid-October. But it had already lit a longer fuse across the Pennines.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">In Yorkshire a Yorkshire lawyer named <dfn class="term" data-def="The Yorkshire lawyer who led the main Pilgrimage of Grace in 1536; tricked into trusting Henry VIII's pardon and executed in 1537.">Robert Aske</dfn> took command of what he called the Pilgrimage of Grace &mdash; a deliberately religious word, presenting the rising as a holy procession rather than a rebellion. Around 30,000 marchers, well organised and heavily armed, gathered under the banner of the Five Wounds of Christ. They took York, Pontefract and Hull. They drafted articles demanding the restoration of the monasteries, the removal of Cromwell, the protection of the Catholic Church and a free Parliament held in the north.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Aske chose the word carefully. Calling the movement a &lsquo;pilgrimage&rsquo; framed it as a religious act rather than a treasonous rebellion against the king. The marchers wore badges of the Five Wounds of Christ and sang hymns as they advanced. Aske claimed the rebels had no quarrel with Henry himself &mdash; only with his evil advisers. This framing was politically useful. It allowed gentry and even some clergy to join without admitting open treason, and it put pressure on Henry to listen rather than fight.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Henry's Trap: Pardon, then Reprisal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Henry could not match 30,000 northerners in the field. He had perhaps 8,000 men under the Duke of Norfolk and could not raise more without delay. So Norfolk played for time. At Doncaster in early December 1536 he negotiated. He offered a free pardon, promised that grievances would be discussed in a Parliament held at York, and gave assurances that the monasteries would be reviewed. Aske, trusting the king's word, ordered the marchers home for Christmas.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The promises were never going to hold. In January 1537 a smaller separate rising led by Sir Francis Bigod broke out in the East Riding without Aske's authority. Henry seized the excuse. Bigod's revolt, he argued, proved the northerners could not be trusted. Pardons were withdrawn, martial law was imposed, and around 200 ringleaders were executed across the north. Robert Aske was tricked into returning to London, where Henry feasted him at court before having him arrested, transported back to York and hanged in chains from the city walls in July 1537.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: how did Henry VIII's response to the Pilgrimage of Grace echo Richard II's response to the Peasants' Revolt?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Henry VIII used a free pardon and the promise of a northern Parliament to make the marchers go home. Within months, on the pretext of a smaller follow-up rising, around 200 ringleaders were executed and Aske was hanged at York in July 1537. The pattern of broken royal promise repeats Richard II's revocation of the 1381 charters.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Impact: The North Silenced, the Reformation Continued</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In the short term the rising failed: Aske and the leaders were dead, the dissolution accelerated through the larger monasteries between 1538 and 1540, and a Council of the North was strengthened to keep the region under direct royal control. But the Pilgrimage was the largest peacetime rising in English history, and its scale shocked the Tudor regime into a permanent watchfulness over the north. Later northern risings (the Prayer Book Rebellion of 1549, the Northern Earls of 1569) framed themselves more cautiously and were crushed faster. Royal authority emerged stronger, but its limits had been exposed: a religious cause could move tens of thousands.</p>

    <span>Compare with 1381: pattern repeats</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The shape of the Pilgrimage echoes the Peasants' Revolt 155 years earlier. Both were touched off by a specific grievance &mdash; the poll tax in 1381, the dissolution in 1536. Both produced large-scale risings led by named individuals (Tyler and Ball; Aske). Both involved a face-to-face negotiation in which the Crown bought time with promises. Both ended with those promises broken and the ringleaders executed. The lesson AQA wants you to draw is that royal authority in the medieval and early modern periods was often performative &mdash; the king bargained when he had to, then punished when he could.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The Factor Thread</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">This lesson is dominated by <strong>religion</strong> &mdash; the dissolution of the monasteries and Catholic identity in the north. But several other factors converge. <strong>The economy</strong>: rumours of new taxes and bad harvests; <strong>government</strong>: Cromwell's aggressive style of central administration; <strong>the role of the individual</strong>: Aske's choice of pilgrimage framing and Henry's choice of betrayal. Religion will return as a driver in the seventeenth century when the New Model Army's Puritan radicals push for reform; the role of the individual repeats with Cromwell, the Pankhursts and Doreen Lawrence.</p>
