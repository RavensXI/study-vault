--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Festivals and Commemorations in Islam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Muslims celebrate several important festivals throughout the year. You need to know three: <dfn class="term" data-def="A Muslim festival celebrating the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting.">Eid al-Fitr</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A Muslim festival commemorating the Prophet Ibrahim’s willingness to sacrifice his son for Allah.">Eid al-Adha</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="A day of commemoration on the 10th of Muharram, observed differently by Sunni and Shi’a Muslims.">Ashura</dfn>. Each festival has distinct origins, and Muslims celebrate them in different ways depending on their denomination.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Eid al-Fitr — The Festival of Breaking the Fast</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Eid al-Fitr marks the end of <dfn class="term" data-def="The ninth month of the Islamic calendar during which Muslims fast from dawn to sunset.">Ramadan</dfn>, the holy month of fasting. The word “Eid” means “festival” and “Fitr” means “breaking the fast.” It is a joyful celebration that thanks Allah for the strength to complete the fast and for the revelation of the Qur’an, which took place during Ramadan.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about qur’an states.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Qur’an states: “He wants that you must complete the same number of days, and that you must magnify Allah for having guided you so that you may be grateful to Him” (2:185). This verse is primarily about the Ramadan fasting obligation and its concessions for the ill or travelling, ending with gratitude for divine guidance. Muslims connect the completion of Ramadan — mandated here — with the celebration of Eid al-Fitr.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Preparations begin before the day itself. Muslims send cards, decorate their homes, buy new clothes, and prepare special food and gifts. On the morning of Eid, Muslims rise early, bathe, and eat something sweet — traditionally dates — before attending the Eid prayer. The Prophet Muhammad himself consumed dates before the Eid prayer, and Muslims follow this <dfn class="term" data-def="The practices and traditions of the Prophet Muhammad, used as a model for Muslim life.">Sunnah</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Before the Eid prayer, Muslims give <dfn class="term" data-def="A charitable donation given before Eid prayer, approximately £2–£5, to ensure the poor can also celebrate Eid.">Zakat al-Fitr</dfn> — a small charitable donation (approximately £2–£5) so that poorer Muslims can also celebrate. The Eid prayer takes place at the mosque or in an open area such as a park. After the prayer, the Imam delivers a sermon. Muslims then exchange gifts, visit friends and family, and share a special meal together. It is also Sunnah to take a different route home from the one taken to the prayer. Many Muslims visit the cemetery to remember loved ones and forgive friends or relatives they have fallen out with.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n8">In Muslim-majority countries, Eid al-Fitr is a public holiday. In the UK, British Muslims are often allowed a day off school or work. The festival reinforces the sense of <dfn class="term" data-def="The worldwide community of Muslims, united by shared faith and practice.">ummah</dfn> (community) as Muslims come together to celebrate after a month of shared spiritual discipline.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Eid al-Adha — The Festival of Sacrifice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Eid al-Adha takes place during the month of Dhul Hijjah and coincides with the annual Hajj pilgrimage. It commemorates the Prophet Ibrahim’s willingness to sacrifice his son when commanded by Allah. According to Islamic tradition, Ibrahim told his son of Allah’s command, and his son replied that they must do as Allah demanded. However, at the moment of sacrifice, Allah provided a ram to be killed instead. This demonstrated Ibrahim’s complete submission to the will of Allah.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 3 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Qur’an teaches that Ibrahim agreed to sacrifice his son even though he was tempted by Shaytan (the devil). At three places, the angel Jibril told Ibrahim to “Pelt him,” so Ibrahim threw seven pebbles and Shaytan left. This event is re-enacted during Hajj at the Jamarat stone pillars.</p>
</div>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">The central act of Eid al-Adha is the <dfn class="term" data-def="The ritual sacrifice of an animal (usually a sheep or goat) during Eid al-Adha, remembering Ibrahim’s willingness to sacrifice his son.">Qurbani</dfn> — the sacrifice of an animal, usually a sheep or goat. The meat is divided into three portions: one third for the family, one third for friends and neighbours, and one third distributed to the poor. This ensures that everyone, regardless of wealth, can share in the celebration.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Muslims also attend special Eid prayers at the mosque, listen to a sermon, wear their best clothes, and exchange gifts. For pilgrims on Hajj, this is a particularly solemn moment — men shave their heads and women cut a lock of hair, symbolising the end of the main pilgrimage rituals. The festival lasts for up to three days.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Ashura — A Day of Remembrance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Ashura falls on the 10th day of <dfn class="term" data-def="The first month of the Islamic calendar, during which the festival of Ashura is observed.">Muharram</dfn>, the first month of the Islamic calendar. It is significant for both Sunni and Shi’a Muslims, but its origins and the way it is commemorated differ greatly between the two denominations. This makes Ashura one of the clearest examples of Sunni–Shi’a differences in practice.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about shi’a Muslims.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>For Shi’a Muslims, Ashura commemorates the martyrdom of Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE. For Sunni Muslims, it recalls the day Allah saved Musa (Moses) and the Israelites from slavery in Egypt by parting the Red Sea.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Shi’a origins of Ashura — the Battle of Karbala</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">After the death of the Prophet Muhammad, a dispute arose over who should lead the Muslim community. Shi’a Muslims believed leadership should stay within the Prophet’s family. In 680 CE, Yazid succeeded his father Muawiyah as <dfn class="term" data-def="A political and religious leader of the Muslim community after the death of the Prophet Muhammad.">Caliph</dfn>. Yazid demanded that Husayn, Muhammad’s grandson and son of Imam Ali, pledge allegiance to him. Husayn refused because he believed Yazid was religiously misguided and that the agreement for leadership to remain within the Prophet’s family had been broken.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">The people of Kufa invited Husayn to join them in opposition to Yazid. On his way to Kufa, Yazid’s army forced Husayn, his family, and a small group of supporters to stop at Karbala. They were denied water and food. After about seven days, Husayn and most of his family and companions were killed in battle. Husayn was beheaded. Shi’a Muslims regard Husayn and his followers as <dfn class="term" data-def="People who die for their faith or beliefs, regarded as heroes for standing firm in their convictions.">martyrs</dfn> who chose death rather than compromise their beliefs.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How do Sunni and Shi’a Muslims commemorate Ashura differently?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Shi’a Muslims</strong> treat Ashura as an intense day of mourning and sorrow. Many wear black mourning clothes, participate in processions to mark the martyrdom, and listen to accounts and poems about Husayn’s death, which may lead to weeping. Some Shi’a Muslims make pilgrimage to Husayn’s shrine in Karbala (called Mashad al-Husayn). Some practise self-flagellation or give blood to remember the blood shed by the martyrs. Others pray and visit the mosque, reflecting on how a true Muslim should be steadfast in their faith as Husayn was.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Sunni Muslims</strong> commemorate Ashura primarily through voluntary fasting, following the example of the Prophet Muhammad, who fasted when he learned that the Jews of Medina fasted on this day to remember Allah saving Musa from the Egyptians. Sunni Muslims may also help the less fortunate, repent their sins, ask for forgiveness, and pray at the mosque. In Turkey, some eat a special pudding called Nuh’s pudding.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n21">Ashura is sometimes debated as a term. Some argue it should not be called a “festival” or “celebration” at all, particularly for Shi’a Muslims, because the mood is one of grief and mourning rather than joy. This contrasts sharply with the celebratory atmosphere of Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Nevertheless, it remains one of the most significant dates in the Islamic calendar.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Eid al-Fitr celebrates the end of Ramadan with prayer, charity (Zakat al-Fitr), and communal meals; Eid al-Adha commemorates Ibrahim’s willingness to sacrifice his son and centres on the Qurbani animal sacrifice.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Ashura is observed on the 10th of Muharram — Shi’a Muslims mourn the martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala (680 CE), while Sunni Muslims fast to remember Allah saving Musa from Egypt.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">These festivals show both unity (shared celebration of faith) and diversity (contrasting Sunni and Shi’a practices) within Islam.</li></ul>
