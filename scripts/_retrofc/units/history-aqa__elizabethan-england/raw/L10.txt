--- DESCRIPTION ---
Why poverty surged in Elizabethan England, why it scared the government, and how the Poor Laws responded.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Sharp Rise in Visible Poverty</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Poverty was not new in 1568, but its scale and visibility changed dramatically across Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign. Contemporaries reported large bands of jobless men and women on the roads, swelling crowds of beggars in market towns, and overcrowded slums in London. Modern historians estimate that perhaps half of England&rsquo;s population lived close to the poverty line by 1600, with around a quarter falling below it in years of crisis. The Elizabethan poor were not a single group: they ranged from elderly widows unable to work, to skilled craftsmen squeezed by inflation, to the so-called <dfn class="term" data-def="The Tudor term for jobless wandering beggars considered able-bodied; widely feared as criminals and a threat to public order.">sturdy beggars</dfn> &mdash; able-bodied wanderers feared as criminals.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall four causes of rising Elizabethan poverty in any order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabethan poverty had four main drivers: a population rise from around 3 million in 1551 to around 4 million by 1601, sharp inflation in food prices, the enclosure of common land for sheep farming, and bad harvests &mdash; especially the crisis years of 1586, 1594 and 1596&ndash;97.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Causes of the Increase</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Population growth was the deepest cause. England&rsquo;s population grew by roughly a third between 1551 and 1601 &mdash; from around 3 million to around 4 million &mdash; outpacing food production. More mouths chasing the same harvests pushed prices up. Wages did not keep pace: a craftsman&rsquo;s real wage in 1600 was substantially lower than in 1500. <dfn class="term" data-def="The fencing-off of common or arable land into private fields, often for sheep farming, in Tudor England.">Enclosure</dfn> made things worse for many. Landowners fenced off common fields for sheep, evicting tenants and removing the grazing and gleaning rights that had cushioned poor families. Sheep farming needed fewer workers than arable farming, so jobs disappeared.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Bad harvests were the trigger. The 1586 harvest failed; 1594, 1595 and 1596 produced what contemporaries called &ldquo;the four wet years&rdquo;; 1596&ndash;97 saw genuine famine in parts of the north and Cumbria. The cloth trade, which employed thousands in East Anglia and the West Country, suffered repeated slumps. The dissolution of the monasteries decades earlier had also removed the largest single source of charity for the rural poor &mdash; abbeys had run almshouses and food doles that no longer existed by Elizabeth&rsquo;s reign.</p>

Veterans of the Armada campaign were paid late or not at all and many turned to begging. They are part of the reason the 1593 statute distinguished &lsquo;maimed soldiers&rsquo; as a deserving category, separate from sturdy beggars.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Attitudes to the Poor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Elizabethans drew a hard line between two kinds of poor: the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Tudor term for poor people unable to work because of age, disability or illness, considered deserving of relief.">impotent poor</dfn> &mdash; the elderly, disabled, orphaned, sick, who could not work &mdash; and the able-bodied poor, expected to work or be punished. The impotent poor were treated with sympathy and given parish relief. The able-bodied jobless were treated with suspicion: many people genuinely believed sturdy beggars were lazy criminals, that begging spread plague, and that wandering bands threatened public order. Religious thinking made this worse: Calvinist Protestants tended to see worldly success as a sign of God&rsquo;s favour and poverty as a sign of moral failure.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The fear of disorder mattered more than charity in driving government action. The Privy Council&rsquo;s correspondence in the 1580s and 1590s is full of warnings of riots in market towns when grain prices spiked. The Oxfordshire Rising of 1596 &mdash; a small attempted uprising by a few dozen poor men &mdash; was a real warning of what hunger might unleash.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Poor Laws of 1572, 1576 and 1598</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Parliament responded with three major statutes. The 1572 Vagabonds Act, the harshest, classified jobless beggars as criminals: a first offence brought whipping and ear boring; a second made them felons; a third meant the death penalty. But the same act introduced the first compulsory <dfn class="term" data-def="A local property tax raised by the parish under the Poor Laws to fund relief for the impotent poor and put the able-bodied to work.">poor rate</dfn> &mdash; a property tax raised parish by parish to support the deserving poor. The 1576 Poor Relief Act required every town to provide raw materials (wool, flax, hemp) so that the able-bodied could be put to work, and to set up a House of Correction for those who refused. The 1598 Act for the Relief of the Poor, consolidated into the famous 1601 Poor Law, made the parish the basic unit of welfare for over two centuries afterwards. Each parish elected unpaid Overseers of the Poor to collect the poor rate and distribute relief.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down the year of each Poor Law (1572, 1576, 1598) and one feature of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Elizabethan Poor Laws of 1572, 1576 and 1598 split the poor into deserving (impotent) and undeserving (sturdy beggars), funded relief through a compulsory parish poor rate, set the able-bodied to work, and made the parish &mdash; not central government &mdash; the unit of welfare for the next 250 years.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">How the System Worked in Practice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The 1598 system rested on the parish. Each year, parishioners elected two Overseers of the Poor from the better-off householders. The overseers collected the poor rate, paid pensions to the elderly and disabled, found apprenticeships for poor children, and bought materials for the workhouse. Vagrants caught wandering were whipped and sent back to their parish of birth or last legal settlement &mdash; the principle that one&rsquo;s home parish was responsible for one&rsquo;s welfare. The London authorities went further with their own institutions: Bridewell handled idle vagrants, Bethlem the mentally ill, and Christ&rsquo;s Hospital orphaned children.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How effective were the Poor Laws?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Strengths: the Poor Laws were the first nationally organised system of welfare in Europe; they channelled local charitable energy through the parish; they survived virtually unchanged until 1834. Weaknesses: relief depended on the willingness of parishes to tax themselves &mdash; some did, others did not. The harsh treatment of vagrants did little to address root causes. And the deserving/undeserving distinction obscured the fact that most able-bodied poor were jobless because there was no work, not because they refused to take it. Examiners reward students who weigh both sides.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">Elizabethan poverty rose sharply because of population growth (3 to 4 million by 1601), inflation, enclosure for sheep farming, and harvest failures &mdash; especially in 1586, 1594 and 1596&ndash;97.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Elizabethans split the poor into the impotent (deserving) and the sturdy beggars (undeserving); fear of disorder, not just charity, drove the government to act.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The 1572, 1576 and 1598 Poor Laws made the parish the basic unit of welfare, funded by a compulsory poor rate and run by elected Overseers; the system lasted until the New Poor Law of 1834.</li>
</ul>
