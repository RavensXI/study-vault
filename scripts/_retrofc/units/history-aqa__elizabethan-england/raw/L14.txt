--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Castle Rebuilt Around One Guest</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Kenilworth Castle is your specified site for the 2028 exam. It is a medieval fortress in Warwickshire &mdash; Norman keep, great mere, John of Gaunt&rsquo;s hall &mdash; but the exam is not really about the Middle Ages. It is about what Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, did to the place in the 1570s, and above all about nineteen days in July 1575 when he staged the most lavish entertainment of the reign for Elizabeth I. Read the site properly and it opens up half your course: the court, <dfn class="term" data-def="The queen's summer journeys around England, staying at the houses of courtiers and towns, who bore the enormous cost of hosting her.">royal progresses</dfn>, the marriage question, and the politics of spending money to be loved.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Dudley and the Gift of a Castle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Kenilworth came to Dudley from the queen herself: she granted him the castle in 1563, the year before she made him Earl of Leicester. He was her favourite &mdash; the man she called her &lsquo;eyes&rsquo;, master of the horse, privy councillor, and for decades the most plausible husband she never took (Lesson 3). The gift was typical of how Elizabeth ruled her court: favour made visible in land and stone. What Dudley then spent on the place turned a royal gift into a royal argument &mdash; a castle rebuilt as a machine for entertaining one woman.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: who gave Dudley the castle, when, and what were his three main additions?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Elizabeth granted Kenilworth to Robert Dudley in 1563. For her visits he added a four-storey tower of state apartments &mdash; Leicester&rsquo;s Building &mdash; raised a grand new gatehouse, modernised the medieval keep with great windows, and laid out a privy garden fit for a queen.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Reading the Site: What Leicester Built</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The additions are still legible in the ruins, and each one is evidence. <em>Leicester&rsquo;s Building</em> was a slender four-storey block of glittering state apartments, built so the queen and her ladies could lodge in modern comfort beside the medieval great hall &mdash; a private palace bolted onto a fortress. The <em>gatehouse</em> gave arriving courtiers a fashionable first impression (and survives intact, because later owners made it a house). The Norman <em>keep</em> was kept &mdash; twelfth-century mass declaring the antiquity of the place &mdash; but Leicester punched huge windows into it, dressing ancient strength in Elizabethan glass. And below the walls he laid out the <em>privy garden</em>: terraces, obelisks, an aviary of exotic birds, a marble fountain &mdash; recorded in such detail by an eyewitness that English Heritage rebuilt it in 2009. Around it all lay the <dfn class="term" data-def="The great artificial lake that defended medieval Kenilworth and became the stage for Leicester's water pageants in 1575.">mere</dfn>, the vast lake that had made medieval Kenilworth almost siege-proof and that Leicester turned into a stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Notice what he did <em>not</em> build: nothing military. Like Acton Burnell three centuries earlier, Kenilworth in the 1570s shows defences repurposed as display &mdash; but here the purpose was sharper than status. Every stone was aimed at the queen&rsquo;s opinion.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">The Nineteen Days of 1575</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Elizabeth had visited before &mdash; 1566, 1568, 1572 &mdash; but the visit of 9 to 27 July 1575 outdid everything in the reign: the longest stay she made at any courtier&rsquo;s house. Leicester met her with a floating &lsquo;Lady of the Lake&rsquo; welcoming her to a castle of legend; fireworks were reportedly heard twenty miles away; there was hunting by day and masques by night, bear-baiting, an Italian tumbler, and a swimming pageant in the mere where a performer as Arion, the legendary musician of Greek myth, rode a mechanical dolphin. Contemporaries claimed the entertainment cost Leicester a fortune &mdash; a figure of a thousand pounds a day was bandied about, and even if gossip inflated it, the true cost would have crushed most noble families. We know the detail because two eyewitness accounts survive: a long gossipy letter attributed to Robert Langham, a minor court official, and the poet George Gascoigne&rsquo;s <em>Princely Pleasures</em>.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner"><p>Most historians read 1575 as Leicester&rsquo;s last grand bid for Elizabeth&rsquo;s hand. The pageants said it almost openly &mdash; masques of marriage and of a lady released from an enchanted lake &mdash; and one planned entertainment pressing the theme was, tellingly, never performed; historians suspect the queen declined to hear it. She was forty-one, and hopes of the Leicester match were fading; and she left Kenilworth unmarried, as she left everywhere. For your exam, the visit is the marriage question (Lesson 3) staged in fireworks &mdash; and refused.</p></div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">What the Site Shows About Elizabethan England</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Kenilworth in 1575 is the court system working at full power. It shows how progresses let Elizabeth be seen by her people and fed at her courtiers&rsquo; expense &mdash; power displayed while someone else paid. It shows what favour was worth: a castle, offices and income flowed to Dudley, and he poured them back into loyalty spectacular enough for the whole kingdom to hear about. It shows the marriage question as living politics, played out in allegory before thousands. And it shows the propaganda machinery of the reign &mdash; poets, pageants and print carrying the event far beyond Warwickshire. The gossip, expense and spectacle of those nineteen days are as much a primary source for Elizabeth&rsquo;s rule as any statute.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: which two eyewitness accounts record the 1575 festivities?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Two eyewitness accounts preserve the 1575 visit: the letter attributed to Robert Langham describing the festivities and the garden, and George Gascoigne&rsquo;s <em>Princely Pleasures at Kenilworth</em>. Detail from the Langham letter guided English Heritage&rsquo;s 2009 reconstruction of the Elizabethan garden.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">The Site Afterwards and in the Exam</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Leicester died in 1588, deep in debt. The castle passed on, was <dfn class="term" data-def="Deliberately wrecked to prevent military use; Kenilworth's defences were broken up and the mere drained after the Civil War.">slighted</dfn> after the Civil War in 1650 &mdash; the keep&rsquo;s north side blown out, the mere drained &mdash; and settled into ruin. Today English Heritage keeps the shell, the gatehouse, the rebuilt garden and the empty basin of the mere. In the 16-mark question, chain features to meanings: Leicester&rsquo;s Building &rarr; the cost of hosting the queen; the garden and pageants &rarr; courtship politics; the mere as stage &rarr; medieval strength repurposed as theatre; the gift of 1563 &rarr; how favour worked. Then weigh the site against your contextual knowledge of the court, progresses and the marriage question.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n18">Elizabeth gave Kenilworth to Robert Dudley in 1563; he rebuilt it for her visits — Leicester's Building, the gatehouse, great windows in the Norman keep, and a queen's garden below the walls.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n19">The July 1575 visit — nineteen days, the longest of any courtier stay — staged the Lady of the Lake, fireworks, masques and water pageants on the mere, at a cost gossip put at a thousand pounds a day.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Most historians read 1575 as Leicester's final bid for the queen's hand; she enjoyed the show and refused the argument.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The site opens the wider course: progresses as power display at courtiers' expense, favour paid in castles, and the marriage question performed in allegory — all recorded by Langham and Gascoigne.</li>
</ul>
