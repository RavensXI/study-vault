<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Site Made of Water</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your specified site for 2027 is not a building. The Spanish Armada campaign of 1588 is examined as a historic environment: the Channel and its winds, the ports on each shore, an exposed anchorage at Calais, the sandbanks off the Dutch coast, and the storm-lashed route home around Scotland and Ireland. The 16-mark question will ask how far a study of the Armada supports some statement, and the marks sit exactly where this lesson looks: in how geography shaped the outcome. Lesson 8 covers why Philip launched the Armada; this lesson follows the fleet through the environment that destroyed it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Plan and Its Geographical Flaw</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Philip&rsquo;s plan &mdash; the Enterprise of England &mdash; was not for the Armada to invade on its own. Its 130 ships carried perhaps eighteen thousand soldiers, but the real invasion army was the Duke of Parma&rsquo;s veteran force in the Spanish Netherlands. The Armada&rsquo;s job was to sweep up the Channel, join Parma, and escort his troops across the narrow sea in barges. The flaw was on the map from the start: Spain held no <dfn class="term" data-def="A harbour deep enough for large warships to enter, shelter and resupply in; the Armada had none available between Spain and the Netherlands.">deep-water port</dfn> anywhere along the invasion coast. Parma&rsquo;s embarkation ports at Dunkirk and Nieuwpoort were shallow, fringed by sandbanks, and watched by Dutch <dfn class="term" data-def="Small, shallow-draught Dutch warships able to operate among the coastal sandbanks where large galleons could not go.">flyboats</dfn> that could sail where galleons could not. The Armada therefore had nowhere safe to wait, and the rendezvous depended on a fleet and an army, hundreds of miles apart with no fast communication, arriving in the same place at the same moment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what was the fatal geographical flaw in Philip's plan?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>There was no deep-water port in friendly hands between Spain and Parma&rsquo;s army. The Armada could reach the Netherlands coast &mdash; but it could not wait there safely, and Parma&rsquo;s shallow ports were blockaded by Dutch flyboats his escorts could not clear.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Two Fleets Built for Different Battles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The commander Philip appointed after his best admiral died, the Duke of Medina Sidonia, led a fleet built around big, high-sided ships packed with soldiers, designed to grapple and board &mdash; to turn a sea fight into a land fight on wet decks. The English fleet, under Lord Howard of Effingham with Francis Drake as his deputy, was built on the opposite theory: <dfn class="term" data-def="A new style of English warship — longer, lower and faster than older designs, built to fight with guns at range rather than by boarding.">race-built galleons</dfn>, faster and more weatherly, carrying long-range <dfn class="term" data-def="A long-barrelled cannon firing a lighter ball a greater distance; the characteristic gun of the English fleet in 1588.">culverins</dfn>, crewed by sailors rather than soldiers. Just as important was where each fleet slept: the English operated from their own ports &mdash; Plymouth, then the anchorages up-Channel &mdash; with powder, shot, food and repairs a day away. Every mile the Armada sailed took it further from any harbour that could help it.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Up the Channel: The Crescent and the Weather Gauge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The Armada was sighted off the Lizard on 19 July 1588, and warning beacons carried the news along the coast faster than any rider. For a week the two fleets ran east up the Channel, and the fighting settled into a pattern set by wind and formation. Medina Sidonia sailed in a disciplined crescent, his strongest warships at the tips, transports sheltered inside &mdash; a formation the English could harass but not break. The English, holding the <dfn class="term" data-def="The upwind position in a sailing battle, which lets a fleet choose when to attack and when to stand off.">weather gauge</dfn>, attacked from windward at Plymouth, Portland Bill and the Isle of Wight, staying at culverin range and refusing to close where Spanish soldiers could board. A week of running battle cost the Armada only a few ships &mdash; but it reached Calais with ammunition low, no port to enter, and Parma not ready.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Calais: An Anchorage That Doomed the Fleet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">On 27 July the Armada anchored off Calais &mdash; not a harbour, just an open roadstead, exposed to wind and enemy alike, because there was nowhere else to go. Parma sent word he needed almost a week more, and his barges could not move while the flyboats prowled the banks. The English needed no week. On the night of 28 July they sent eight fireships, stuffed with pitch and gunpowder with guns left loaded to fire as they burned, down the wind into the anchorage. Against ships at anchor in the dark, packed close, the threat was terror itself. The Spanish captains cut their anchor cables to escape &mdash; and the crescent, which gunfire had never broken, ceased to exist in a single night. The cut cables would collect their price later: a fleet without anchors cannot hold itself off a storm coast.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: why did cutting the cables at Calais matter weeks later?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Escaping the fireships, the Spanish captains cut their cables and abandoned their best anchors on the seabed off Calais. Weeks later, on the storm coasts of Scotland and Ireland, ships that could not anchor were driven ashore &mdash; a one-night decision at the site with a months-long consequence.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Gravelines and the Wind That Changed Twice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Off Gravelines on 29 July the English finally fought the battle they wanted: close enough for their guns to hurt, far enough to refuse boarding, against a scattered enemy short of shot. Several Spanish ships were sunk or driven ashore and many more were battered. Then the environment nearly finished the campaign at a stroke: the wind pushed the fleet towards the Zeeland sandbanks, shoaling water with no sea room, and only a shift of wind at the last moment let the Armada claw off into the North Sea. The same shift settled the campaign&rsquo;s direction &mdash; with the English behind them and the wind foul for the Channel, the only way home was north-about, around Scotland and Ireland.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Long Way Home</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The route home killed more ships than the English had. Autumn gales caught a battered fleet on some of the most dangerous coasts in Europe; ships without anchors, with storm-damaged hulls and starving crews, were wrecked in numbers on the Scottish isles and above all on the west coast of Ireland. Perhaps half the fleet never reached Spain, and thousands of men died of drowning, disease and starvation &mdash; far more than died to English gunfire. Victory did not spare the victors either: typhus and dysentery swept the English fleet in the weeks after Gravelines, and sailors who had saved the kingdom died unpaid in the ports they had defended. Elizabeth&rsquo;s famous appearance before the army at Tilbury came while the Armada was already limping north &mdash; the image of victory, minted as the environment completed it.</p>

The missing deep-water port explains why the Armada was at anchor in an open roadstead at Calais; the roadstead explains why fireships worked; the cut cables explain the wrecks on the Irish coast; the sandbanks off Zeeland explain why Gravelines nearly ended everything; the home ports and beacon chains explain English speed and stamina; the north-about route explains why a fleet that lost few ships in battle lost half its strength getting home. <ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The plan died on the map before it died at sea: no deep-water port meant the Armada could not wait for Parma, whose shallow, blockaded ports meant Parma could not come out.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Channel phase was shaped by formation and wind: the crescent resisted a week of English attack, but home ports and the weather gauge let the English fight on their own terms.</li>

</ul>
