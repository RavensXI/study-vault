How Cecil, Walsingham and the Privy Council ran government for the queen day to day.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Ministers Mattered</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Elizabeth set policy, but she could not run a kingdom of four million people on her own. Her power depended on a small group of ministers who managed finance, foreign policy, the law, intelligence and the day-to-day flow of royal correspondence. Three names dominate this story: William Cecil (Lord Burghley), Sir Francis Walsingham, and Cecil&rsquo;s son Robert. Together with the wider <dfn class="term" data-def="The queen's inner advisory body of around twenty senior nobles and officials who met regularly to discuss policy and oversee the running of government.">Privy Council</dfn>, they were the engine room of the regime.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">William Cecil (Lord Burghley)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">William Cecil served Elizabeth from 1558, the day she became queen, until his death in 1598 &mdash; almost the entire reign. He was first her <dfn class="term" data-def="The senior minister responsible for managing the queen's correspondence, foreign policy and the day-to-day running of central government.">Principal Secretary</dfn>, then from 1572 Lord Treasurer (chief finance minister), and was made Lord Burghley in 1571. Cecil&rsquo;s strengths were administration and patience. He kept meticulous notes, drafted memoranda weighing up the pros and cons of every option, and waited for Elizabeth&rsquo;s decision rather than forcing her hand. The historian John Guy has called him &lsquo;the indispensable man&rsquo; of the regime.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Cecil was a moderate Protestant who believed England&rsquo;s safety required peace abroad and stability at home. He pushed for the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, but only after years of building a legal and political case. He resisted ruinously expensive wars but agreed when threats &mdash; such as the Spanish Armada &mdash; could no longer be avoided. He trained his younger son Robert to take over from him, and on Burghley&rsquo;s death in 1598 the transition was almost seamless.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall the two top offices Cecil held under Elizabeth and the years he served.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>William Cecil (Lord Burghley) was Principal Secretary 1558&ndash;1572 and Lord Treasurer 1572&ndash;1598 &mdash; almost the entire reign. He was the administrator who turned Elizabeth&rsquo;s decisions into working policy and prepared his son Robert to succeed him.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Sir Francis Walsingham, Spymaster</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">If Burghley was the manager, Walsingham was the watchman. Appointed Principal Secretary in 1573, he ran what we would now call an intelligence service. Walsingham was a hard Protestant who believed Catholic Europe &mdash; especially Spain &mdash; was an existential threat to Elizabethan England. He recruited informants in English Catholic communities, intercepted letters between exiles abroad and plotters at home, and ran double agents inside hostile courts.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">His most famous coup was the unmasking of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1586 Catholic conspiracy by Anthony Babington and others to assassinate Elizabeth and place Mary, Queen of Scots, on the throne; uncovered by Walsingham's intelligence network.">Babington Plot</dfn> in 1586. Walsingham&rsquo;s agents intercepted ciphered letters between the conspirators and Mary, Queen of Scots, decrypted them, and even forged a postscript to draw out further names. The evidence convicted Mary, leading to her execution at Fotheringhay Castle in February 1587. Walsingham died in 1590, exhausted and in debt; the queen had paid him too little for the work he had done.</p>

His agents posed as Catholic exiles in Paris and Rome, infiltrated seminaries that were training English priests for missions home, and watched the ports for forbidden books. He developed methods for opening, copying and resealing letters without breaking the wax. Code-breakers including Thomas Phelippes worked in his service. None of this had a formal department; it ran through Walsingham&rsquo;s personal household.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Robert Cecil</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Robert Cecil, Burghley&rsquo;s younger son, succeeded his father as the queen&rsquo;s leading minister. Trained from boyhood for the role, he was small, hunchbacked and famously sharp-tongued. Elizabeth nicknamed him her &lsquo;little elf&rsquo;. From the mid-1590s he managed both domestic administration and the secret negotiations that, after Elizabeth&rsquo;s death in 1603, would secure the peaceful succession of James VI of Scotland as James I of England. His clash with Essex, who saw the Cecils as rivals blocking his ambition, would set up the rebellion of 1601.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">The Privy Council</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The Privy Council was the queen&rsquo;s inner advisory body. Around 19&ndash;20 men, all hand-picked, met two or three times a week. They debated foreign policy, military matters, religious enforcement, the granting of monopolies, the management of the harvest and law-and-order issues that came to the Crown. Notes were kept by the Clerk of the Council; decisions were sent out as letters carrying Elizabeth&rsquo;s authority.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Elizabeth used the Council as a sounding board. She did not always attend meetings; instead, ministers debated, summarised the options for her, and waited for her ruling. She often picked apart their conclusions and demanded more information. This style frustrated councillors but kept ultimate authority firmly in her hands. As historian Penry Williams put it, the Council advised; the queen decided.</p>

She is not Mary I (also called Mary Tudor or &lsquo;Bloody Mary&rsquo;), who was Elizabeth&rsquo;s half-sister and queen of England 1553&ndash;1558. Mary I died ten years before this depth study begins. Mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes in Elizabethan exam answers.</p>

  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cecil ran administration and finance; Walsingham ran intelligence and uncovered the Babington Plot of 1586; the Privy Council debated policy and turned the queen&rsquo;s decisions into instructions sent to officials across the kingdom.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">William Cecil served Elizabeth for 40 years, first as Principal Secretary then as Lord Treasurer, providing the steady administration the regime depended on.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Sir Francis Walsingham ran an intelligence network that uncovered the Babington Plot in 1586, leading to the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Privy Council of around 20 men advised the queen and turned her decisions into orders, but final authority always lay with Elizabeth herself.</li>
</ul>
