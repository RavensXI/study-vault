--- DESCRIPTION ---
Streaming, labelling and the self-fulfilling prophecy. Ball, Willis and competing perspectives on school processes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Happens Inside Schools?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Sociologists who focus on the macro level &mdash; class, capitalism, patriarchy &mdash; risk overlooking the daily processes within schools that shape individual outcomes. Interactionist sociologists argue that what happens in classrooms and corridors &mdash; how teachers perceive and label pupils, how pupils are grouped, how peer cultures develop &mdash; has a profound effect on achievement that cannot be explained solely by external factors such as class or home background.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name four processes within schools that sociologists argue affect educational achievement. For each, give a one-sentence explanation of the mechanism.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Processes within schools that affect educational achievement include: <dfn class="term" data-def="The allocation of pupils to fixed ability groups for all or most subjects, usually from the start of secondary school.">streaming</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="The allocation of pupils to different ability groups for individual subjects, allowing movement between groups.">setting</dfn>, mixed ability teaching, <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which teachers and others attach definitions or categories to pupils, which influence how those pupils are treated and how they come to see themselves.">labelling</dfn> and the <dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which a label attached to an individual causes them to behave in ways that confirm the label, so that the prediction becomes true.">self-fulfilling prophecy</dfn>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Streaming and Setting</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Many secondary schools in England allocate pupils to different ability groups. <strong>Streaming</strong> places pupils in a fixed group for all subjects, based on an overall assessment of ability. <strong>Setting</strong> places pupils in different groups for individual subjects, allowing for movement between groups as performance changes. Some schools use <strong>mixed ability teaching</strong>, keeping pupils of all abilities together in the same class.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">From a functionalist perspective, streaming and setting allow teachers to pitch lessons at the appropriate level for each group, potentially improving efficiency. From an interactionist perspective, the process is more problematic. Once pupils are placed in a low stream or set, teachers may have lower expectations of them, offer them less challenging work, and interact with them differently. The pupils themselves may internalise the label &ldquo;low ability&rdquo; and adjust their own aspirations accordingly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Ball on Banding and Teacher Expectations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Stephen Ball&rsquo;s study <em>Beachside Comprehensive</em> (1981) investigated the introduction and effects of <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of ability grouping in which pupils are allocated to broad bands (rather than narrow sets), typically in secondary schools.">banding</dfn> in a fictional comprehensive school (referred to as &ldquo;Beachside&rdquo;). Ball found that the band a pupil was placed in at the start of secondary school significantly shaped their educational trajectory, regardless of their actual ability. Pupils in the lower bands received a less academic curriculum, had lower teacher expectations placed on them, and developed more negative attitudes towards school. Ball&rsquo;s research showed that the act of grouping pupils by ability was not a neutral administrative decision but a process laden with social consequences, particularly for working-class pupils who were disproportionately placed in lower bands.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Labelling Theory and the Self-Fulfilling Prophecy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Labelling theory, associated with interactionist sociologists, argues that the definitions and categories that teachers attach to pupils have real consequences for those pupils&rsquo; futures. When a teacher identifies a pupil as &ldquo;bright&rdquo;, &ldquo;disruptive&rdquo;, &ldquo;lazy&rdquo; or &ldquo;hard-working&rdquo;, this label shapes subsequent interactions: the pupil receives more (or less) encouragement, is called on more (or less) frequently in class, and is treated differently in disciplinary situations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">This process can produce a <strong>self-fulfilling prophecy</strong>: a pupil labelled as &ldquo;high ability&rdquo; begins to internalise that identity and works hard to fulfil expectations; a pupil labelled as &ldquo;low ability&rdquo; or &ldquo;a troublemaker&rdquo; may disengage from school, accept the label and behave accordingly. The original label &mdash; which may have been based on class, ethnicity or gender prejudice rather than genuine ability &mdash; becomes self-confirming. Howard Becker&rsquo;s broader interactionist work on <dfn class="term" data-def="A label that overrides all other aspects of a person's identity, becoming the primary way they are defined by others.">master status</dfn> is relevant here: once a pupil is labelled as &ldquo;a problem&rdquo;, that label can become their master status, colouring every interaction they have with teachers and other pupils.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12">Paul Willis&rsquo;s 1977 ethnographic study <em>Learning to Labour</em> followed twelve working-class boys (whom Willis called &ldquo;the lads&rdquo;) in a West Midlands comprehensive school in the mid-1970s. Willis found that &ldquo;the lads&rdquo; developed a <dfn class="term" data-def="A peer group culture within a school that actively rejects the school's values, rules and academic goals, often developing its own alternative value system.">counter-school culture</dfn>: a system of values that actively opposed the school&rsquo;s emphasis on academic work, obedience and qualifications. The lads saw conformist &ldquo;ear&rsquo;oles&rdquo; (pupils who accepted the school&rsquo;s rules) as passive and weak. The lads valued masculine physical toughness, resistance to authority and the anticipation of manual labour &mdash; the very traits that would lead them into working-class factory jobs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Willis&rsquo;s study is significant for several reasons. First, it challenged Bowles and Gintis by showing that working-class pupils are not passive: they actively resist the school&rsquo;s authority rather than being socialised into compliance. Second, it showed that this very resistance &mdash; which the lads experienced as freedom and rebellion &mdash; ultimately prepared them for working-class jobs just as effectively as conformity did. Third, Willis drew on interactionist ideas about peer culture while framing his analysis within a Marxist concern with class reproduction.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Perspectives on processes within schools</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Functionalist:</strong> Streaming and setting are rational, efficient responses to differences in pupil ability. They allow teachers to target teaching appropriately and ensure that the most able pupils are stretched. The process is seen as broadly meritocratic, matching pupils to future roles based on demonstrated ability.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Interactionist:</strong> Labels attached by teachers shape pupils&rsquo; identities and trajectories through the self-fulfilling prophecy. Banding and streaming are not neutral &mdash; they reflect and reinforce teacher judgements that are often shaped by class, gender and ethnicity (Ball&rsquo;s Beachside research). The focus is on micro-level classroom interactions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Marxist:</strong> Processes within schools are mechanisms of class reproduction. Counter-school cultures (Willis) arise because working-class pupils recognise &mdash; at some level &mdash; that the meritocracy claim is hollow. Streaming channels working-class pupils towards lower expectations and lesser credentials, reproducing the class structure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Feminist:</strong> Labels and expectations are also gendered. Teachers may expect girls to be quieter, more compliant and less mathematically able, and boys to be noisier, more disruptive and more suited to sciences. These gendered expectations shape behaviour and subject choice, reproducing occupational gender segregation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this and distinguish the self-fulfilling prophecy from labelling. Which comes first? Give an example from a school setting.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Labelling comes first: a teacher (or peer) attaches a label to a pupil (&ldquo;trouble-maker&rdquo;, &ldquo;high achiever&rdquo;). The self-fulfilling prophecy describes what happens next: the pupil internalises the label and begins to act in ways that confirm it, so the original prediction comes true regardless of whether it was accurate initially.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Streaming, setting and banding are organisational processes that shape pupils&rsquo; trajectories. Ball&rsquo;s Beachside Comprehensive research showed that band placement &mdash; often shaped by class background &mdash; significantly affected the curriculum pupils received and the expectations placed on them.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Labelling theory (interactionist) argues that teacher labels shape pupil identities; the self-fulfilling prophecy describes how labels cause the behaviour they predict. Becker&rsquo;s concept of master status is relevant: a single label can dominate all interactions.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Willis&rsquo;s Learning to Labour (1977) showed working-class &lsquo;lads&rsquo; actively resisting school authority through a counter-school culture &mdash; challenging the Bowles and Gintis model of passive socialisation while demonstrating that active resistance can ironically reproduce class inequality.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">On 12-mark Discuss questions about in-school processes, the most common error is to describe labelling and streaming as purely functionalist or purely Marxist rather than recognising that interactionists are the perspective most associated with these processes. The spec explicitly names &ldquo;interactionist&rdquo; as one of the four perspectives for section 3.4.4. Make sure you include Ball (Beachside Comprehensive &mdash; banding, teacher expectations) and Willis (Learning to Labour &mdash; counter-school culture, the lads) as your named sociologists, and distinguish between the interactionist focus on micro-level labels and the Marxist concern with macro-level class reproduction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">For 2-mark Define questions on the self-fulfilling prophecy, a common error is to confuse it with labelling. Make clear that the self-fulfilling prophecy is the <em>outcome</em> of labelling &mdash; the process by which the label causes behaviour that confirms it.</p>
