--- DESCRIPTION ---
How insulators charge by friction, electrostatic attraction and repulsion, dangers, and electric fields.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Charging by Friction</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When two <dfn class="term" data-def="A material that does not allow electric charge to flow freely through it; electrons cannot move easily between insulating materials.">insulating</dfn> materials are rubbed together, electrons transfer from one material to the other. The material that gains electrons becomes <dfn class="term" data-def="Having a net negative charge due to gaining extra electrons.">negatively charged</dfn>; the material that loses electrons becomes <dfn class="term" data-def="Having a net positive charge due to losing electrons.">positively charged</dfn>. The total charge is conserved — charge is not created, only redistributed.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">A classic example: rubbing a polythene rod with a cloth causes electrons to transfer from the cloth to the polythene. The rod gains a negative charge; the cloth becomes positive. Rubbing an acetate rod with a cloth has the opposite effect — electrons transfer from the acetate to the cloth, leaving the rod positive.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State which particle transfers when objects are charged by friction, and explain why only electrons transfer (not protons).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Only electrons transfer when charging by friction — protons are locked inside atomic nuclei and cannot move in ordinary chemical processes. The object that gains electrons becomes negative; the object that loses them becomes positive. Total charge before equals total charge after.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Attraction and Repulsion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Charged objects exert forces on one another without touching — this is an electrostatic (non-contact) force. The rule is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.</strong></p>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Two negatively charged rods brought close together will push each other away. A negative rod held near a positive rod will pull each other towards the other. These forces act at a distance because each charged object creates an <dfn class="term" data-def="The region around a charged object in which another charged object experiences a force.">electric field</dfn> around itself.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n9">Charged objects also attract small uncharged objects — for example, a charged comb attracting small pieces of paper. This happens because the charge on the comb induces a redistribution of charge in the paper, attracting the opposite charges on the near side.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Electric Fields</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">An electric field is the region around a charged object in which a force acts on another charged object. Electric fields are represented using <dfn class="term" data-def="Lines drawn to show the direction of the force on a positive test charge placed in an electric field; they go from positive to negative.">electric field lines</dfn>. The rules are:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n12">
  <li>Field lines run from positive to negative (the direction a positive charge would move).</li>
  <li>Field lines never cross each other.</li>
  <li>The closer together the field lines, the stronger the field.</li>
  <li>For a uniform field (between two parallel plates), the lines are parallel and evenly spaced.</li>
</ul>

Around a negative charge, they point inward toward the charge. Between two oppositely charged parallel plates, the field lines are parallel, equally spaced, and perpendicular to the plates — this is a uniform field. The field is stronger close to the plates and weaker at the edges (edge effects).</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Earthing and Electrostatic Hazards</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="Connecting a charged object to the earth using a conductor, allowing charge to flow away and neutralise the object.">Earthing</dfn> removes charge from an object by providing a conducting path to the ground. Since the Earth is very large, it can accept or provide huge numbers of electrons without becoming noticeably charged itself.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Electrostatic charge is a hazard in several situations. Sparks occur when the electric field between a charged object and earth becomes strong enough to ionise the air — the resulting spark can ignite flammable vapours. This is why aircraft are earthed before refuelling: fuel flowing through pipes builds up static charge, and without earthing, a spark could ignite aviation fuel vapour.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Surgeons in operating theatres and workers handling explosive materials wear special anti-static clothing and footwear to prevent charge build-up. Computer components are stored in anti-static bags because even a small static discharge can damage integrated circuits.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name three practical contexts where static electricity is a hazard and state one control measure for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Electrostatic hazards arise wherever charge can accumulate and spark: aircraft refuelling (earth the aircraft before attaching fuel hose), operating theatres (anti-static clothing), grain silos and flour mills (dust clouds are explosive — anti-static earthing). Lightning itself is a large-scale electrostatic discharge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Useful Applications of Static Electricity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Static electricity also has many practical applications:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Electrostatic paint spraying:</strong> The object to be painted (e.g. a car body panel) is given one charge; the paint droplets are given the opposite charge. The paint is attracted to every surface of the object, including awkward recesses, giving an even coat and reducing waste.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Inkjet printers:</strong> Tiny ink droplets are given a charge as they leave the nozzle. Deflector plates with a controlled electric field steer each droplet to exactly the right position on the page.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Photocopiers and laser printers:</strong> A drum is given a static charge pattern representing the image to be printed. Toner (charged powder) is attracted to the charged regions of the drum and then transferred to paper.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Electrostatic precipitators:</strong> Used in power station chimneys, these give particles in smoke a charge so they are attracted to collecting plates, removing them from the exhaust gas and reducing air pollution.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">In a thundercloud, turbulent air movements cause ice crystals and water droplets to collide, transferring charge. The base of the cloud typically accumulates a large negative charge, while the ground below becomes induced into a positive charge. When the potential difference between cloud and ground becomes large enough, the air ionises and an enormous discharge — lightning — occurs. Lightning conductors on tall buildings provide a low-resistance earthing path, protecting the building by allowing the charge to discharge safely into the ground rather than through the structure.</p>

</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Charging by friction transfers electrons only (not protons). The material gaining electrons becomes negative; the material losing electrons becomes positive. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Electric fields surround charged objects. Field lines point from positive to negative; closer lines mean a stronger field. A uniform field exists between parallel plates with opposite charges.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Electrostatic hazards (sparks near fuel, operating theatres, grain dust) are managed by earthing. Applications include electrostatic paint spraying, inkjet printers, photocopiers and electrostatic precipitators.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For static electricity questions, the most common error is writing that 'positive charges move' — in solids, it is always electrons that transfer. Always state 'electrons transfer from X to Y.' For electric field line diagrams, check: lines go from positive to negative; they never cross; closer lines mean stronger field. In application questions (paint spraying, inkjet printers), explain the principle, not just the fact — 'opposite charges attract, so every surface of the object is reached' earns more marks than 'the paint sticks to the car.' For 6-mark extended responses on hazards, structure your answer: state the hazard → explain why charge builds up → explain how the spark is caused → state the control measure.</p>
