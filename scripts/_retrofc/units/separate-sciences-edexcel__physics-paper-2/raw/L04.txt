--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Alternating Current and Direct Current</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electric current in which the direction of flow reverses repeatedly at a set frequency; used in the UK mains supply.">Alternating current</dfn> (a.c.) changes direction repeatedly. The UK mains supply is a.c. at a frequency of 50 Hz, meaning it completes 50 full cycles per second. The peak voltage of UK mains is approximately 325 V, but the declared supply voltage is 230 V — this represents the equivalent d.c. voltage that would deliver the same power.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3"><dfn class="term" data-def="Electric current that flows in one direction only; supplied by batteries and cells.">Direct current</dfn> (d.c.) flows in one direction only. Batteries and cells supply d.c. Electronic devices (phones, laptops) typically require d.c. internally; their chargers convert mains a.c. to low-voltage d.c.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the UK mains voltage, frequency, and type of current. Then state what type batteries supply.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK mains supply: <strong>alternating current (a.c.)</strong>, declared voltage <strong>230 V</strong>, frequency <strong>50 Hz</strong>. Batteries supply <strong>direct current (d.c.)</strong>. The mains frequency of 50 Hz means the current changes direction 100 times per second (two direction changes per cycle).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Three-Pin Plug</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Most UK appliances connect to the mains through a three-pin plug. The three wires have standardised colours introduced with post-2006 wiring regulations:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Live wire (brown)</strong> — carries the alternating potential difference from the supply, alternating between approximately +325 V and −325 V relative to earth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Neutral wire (blue)</strong> — completes the circuit. It is at, or close to, 0 V (earth potential). Current returns to the supply via this wire.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Earth wire (green and yellow)</strong> — a safety wire connected to the metal casing of an appliance. It carries current safely to earth if a fault causes the casing to become live, tripping the fuse or circuit breaker.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10">The live wire is at a high potential difference (∼230 V) relative to earth at all times — even when the appliance switch is off. If you touch it, a large p.d. exists between your body and the ground, driving a current through you. Switches are therefore always placed in the live wire so that when open, the appliance and the neutral/earth wires are isolated from the high-voltage supply.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Fuses and Circuit Breakers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A safety device containing a thin wire that melts when current exceeds a rated value, breaking the circuit and preventing overheating.">fuse</dfn> is connected in series in the live wire. If a fault causes an excessive current to flow, the fuse wire melts, breaking the circuit before the appliance or cable can overheat and cause a fire. Fuses are rated at 3 A, 5 A, or 13 A for most domestic appliances — the fuse rating should be just above the normal operating current.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An automatic switch that opens (trips) when current exceeds a safe level; unlike a fuse it can be reset after tripping.">circuit breaker</dfn> serves the same protective purpose but can be reset by pressing a switch — no replacement is needed. Residual current device (RCD) circuit breakers detect tiny imbalances between live and neutral currents (indicating a leakage to earth) and trip within milliseconds — faster than a fuse melts.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Electrical Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which electrical energy is transferred by a device, measured in watts (W).">Electrical power</dfn> is the rate of energy transfer. The fundamental equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$P = IV$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(P\) is power in watts, \(I\) is current in amperes, and \(V\) is potential difference in volts. Combining with \(V = IR\) gives two further equations:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">$$P = I^2R \quad \text{and} \quad P = \frac{V^2}{R}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">\(P = I^2R\) is particularly useful for calculating power dissipated in a resistor when you know the current; \(P = V^2/R\) when you know the voltage. On a long transmission wire, power is wasted as heat — this dissipation follows \(P_{lost} = I^2R\), showing that reducing current dramatically reduces heat losses.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write all three power equations, name the units for each quantity, and explain which to use when current alone is known.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three power equations: \(P = IV\) (use when both current and voltage are given); \(P = I^2R\) (use when current and resistance are given); \(P = V^2/R\) (use when voltage and resistance are given). Power is always in watts; energy transferred is \(E = Pt\) in joules.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Energy Transferred</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Energy transferred by an electrical device is given by:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">$$E = Pt \quad \text{and also} \quad E = QV$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Where \(E\) is energy in joules, \(P\) is power in watts, \(t\) is time in seconds, \(Q\) is charge in coulombs, and \(V\) is potential difference in volts. The equation \(E = QV\) links directly to the definition of potential difference as energy per unit charge.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">The National Grid and Transformers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The network of cables, pylons and substations that distributes electricity from power stations to homes and businesses across the country.">National Grid</dfn> transmits electricity from power stations to consumers. Because power loss in cables follows \(P = I^2R\), the key strategy is to transmit at very high voltage (and therefore very low current) to minimise energy waste as heat in the cables.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device that uses electromagnetic induction to change the voltage of an alternating current supply; it consists of two coils wound on an iron core.">transformer</dfn> uses electromagnetic induction to change the voltage of an a.c. supply. Transformers work only with a.c. — this is one reason the National Grid uses a.c. rather than d.c.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that increases voltage — the secondary coil has more turns than the primary coil.">step-up transformer</dfn> at the power station increases voltage from around 25 kV to 132 kV, 275 kV, or 400 kV for long-distance transmission. At the end of the transmission line, a series of <dfn class="term" data-def="A transformer that decreases voltage — the secondary coil has fewer turns than the primary coil.">step-down transformers</dfn> reduce the voltage progressively to 33 kV, then 11 kV, then 230 V for domestic use.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain in two sentences why the National Grid transmits at high voltage, and state what type of transformer is used at the power station exit.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>High-voltage transmission keeps current low, which reduces power lost as heat in cables (\(P_{lost} = I^2R\)). A step-up transformer increases voltage at the power station; step-down transformers reduce it before it reaches homes.</p>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Transformers Only Work with A.C.</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34">A transformer works because the changing magnetic field produced by alternating current in the primary coil induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil. A steady (d.c.) current produces a constant magnetic field — no change, no induction, no output voltage. This is why the National Grid must use a.c.: only a.c. allows transformers to step voltage up or down efficiently.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n37">UK mains is a.c. at 230 V, 50 Hz. Plug wires: live (brown), neutral (blue), earth (green/yellow). Fuse in the live wire. Batteries supply d.c.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">Three power equations: \(P = IV\), \(P = I^2R\), \(P = V^2/R\). Energy transferred: \(E = Pt\) and \(E = QV\).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">The National Grid uses step-up transformers at power stations (high voltage, low current) to minimise \(I^2R\) losses during transmission. Step-down transformers reduce voltage to 230 V for domestic use. Transformers only work with a.c.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n35">Plug wiring is tested every year — memorise: brown = live, blue = neutral, green/yellow = earth. The most common error is stating the fuse is in the neutral wire: it is always in the live wire. For National Grid questions, the key explanation is always: high voltage → low current → less power wasted (\(P = I^2R\)). For 6-mark extended responses, link each step explicitly: power station → step-up transformer → high-voltage cables → step-down substation → homes. State the equation and say what each variable does. On power calculations, choose the right equation for the information given — if current and resistance are given, use \(P = I^2R\) directly rather than finding V first.</p>
