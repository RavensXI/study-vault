<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Permanent and Induced Magnets</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A material that produces its own persistent magnetic field with a north and south pole, regardless of external fields.">permanent magnet</dfn> produces its own magnetic field at all times. It always has a north pole and a south pole. Like poles repel each other; unlike poles attract. Common magnetic materials include iron, steel, nickel, and cobalt.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">An <dfn class="term" data-def="A material that becomes magnetic only when placed in an external magnetic field; it loses its magnetism when removed from the field.">induced magnet</dfn> is a material that becomes temporarily magnetic when placed inside a magnetic field. When removed, it quickly loses its magnetism. An induced magnet is always attracted to the permanent magnet that induced it — it is never repelled, because its poles align with the external field.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name the direction of magnetic field lines outside a bar magnet, and state what denser field lines indicate.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A <dfn class="term" data-def="The region around a magnetic material or current-carrying conductor in which a magnetic force acts on other magnetic materials.">magnetic field</dfn> is the space in which a magnetic force acts. Outside a bar magnet, field lines run from the north pole to the south pole. Denser (closer) field lines indicate a stronger field. The Earth has its own magnetic field — a compass needle aligns with this field to point approximately north.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Electromagnetism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When an electric current flows through a wire, it creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A magnetic field produced by an electric current; it surrounds any current-carrying conductor.">magnetic field</dfn> around the wire. The field lines form concentric circles around the wire. Reversing the current reverses the field direction. Increasing the current increases the field strength.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A coil of wire that produces a strong, nearly uniform magnetic field inside it when carrying a current; the external field pattern resembles a bar magnet.">solenoid</dfn> is a coil of wire. When current flows through it, the field inside the solenoid is strong and nearly uniform, with a pattern similar to a bar magnet — one end acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole. The field strength increases if you: increase the current, increase the number of turns per unit length, or insert an iron core.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name four applications of electromagnets and explain what makes them switchable.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An <dfn class="term" data-def="A solenoid with a soft iron core; it produces a strong magnetic field when a current flows and can be switched on or off by controlling the current.">electromagnet</dfn> is a solenoid with a soft iron core. The core concentrates the magnetic field. Switching off the current removes the magnetism — this controllability makes electromagnets useful in scrapyard cranes (lifting/releasing scrap metal), circuit breakers, electric bells, and MRI scanners.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Motor Effect</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">When a current-carrying wire is placed inside a magnetic field, it experiences a force. This is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field, caused by the interaction between the field of the magnet and the field of the current.">motor effect</dfn>. The force is at its maximum when the wire is perpendicular to the magnetic field lines, and zero when the wire is parallel to them.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Reversing either the current direction or the magnetic field direction reverses the force. Reversing both leaves the force in the same direction.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Fleming's Left-Hand Rule</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A memory rule for predicting the direction of force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field: hold the thumb, index finger and middle finger of the left hand mutually at right angles — thumb = thrust (force), index finger = field, middle finger = current.">Fleming's left-hand rule</dfn> gives the direction of the motor-effect force. Hold your left hand with the thumb, index finger, and middle finger at right angles to each other:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
  <li><strong>Index finger</strong> — direction of the magnetic field (north to south).</li>
  <li><strong>Middle finger</strong> — direction of conventional current (positive to negative).</li>
  <li><strong>Thumb</strong> — direction of the force (thrust/motion) on the wire.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Calculating the Motor-Effect Force: F = BIl</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The size of the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field is given by:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">$$F = BIl$$</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Where \(F\) is force in newtons (N), \(B\) is <dfn class="term" data-def="A measure of the strength of a magnetic field, measured in tesla (T); one tesla is the field strength that produces a force of one newton on a one-metre wire carrying a current of one ampere.">magnetic flux density</dfn> in tesla (T), \(I\) is current in amperes (A), and \(l\) is the length of wire within the magnetic field in metres (m). This equation assumes the wire is perpendicular to the field.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Worked example:</strong> A wire of length 0.15 m carries a current of 4 A in a magnetic field of flux density 0.5 T. The wire is perpendicular to the field. Find the force on the wire.<br>\(F = BIl = 0.5 \times 4 \times 0.15 = 0.3\) N</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">The DC Motor</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A simple d.c. motor uses the motor effect to produce rotation. A rectangular coil of wire sits between the poles of a permanent magnet. When current flows through the coil, the motor-effect force pushes one side of the coil up and the other side down. This creates a turning force that rotates the coil.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Split-Ring Commutator — Essential for Continuous Rotation</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">After the coil has rotated 180°, the sides that were being pushed up and down swap positions. Without a correction, the forces would now act in the opposite rotational direction and the coil would stop. A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device in a d.c. motor consisting of a split ring that connects the rotating coil to the external circuit; it reverses the current in the coil every half-turn to maintain continuous rotation.">split-ring commutator</dfn> solves this: as the coil reaches the vertical position, the two halves of the split ring swap which brush contact they touch, automatically reversing the current in the coil. This means the force direction is maintained and the coil continues to spin in the same direction.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">The speed of a d.c. motor can be increased by: increasing the current through the coil, increasing the magnetic flux density (using a stronger magnet), or increasing the number of turns on the coil.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain in two sentences what the split-ring commutator does and why it is essential for continuous rotation.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The split-ring commutator reverses the current through the coil every half-turn. Without it, the motor-effect force would reverse direction after 180° and the coil would oscillate rather than rotate continuously. This makes it the critical component enabling one-direction rotation in a d.c. motor.</p>
</div>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Permanent magnets have a persistent field; induced magnets are temporary (always attracted, never repelled). A current-carrying wire creates a magnetic field; a solenoid with an iron core is an electromagnet.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">The motor effect: a force acts on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field. Fleming's left-hand rule gives the direction. The force F = BIl (tesla × amperes × metres).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">A d.c. motor uses the motor effect; the split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn to maintain continuous rotation in one direction.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n25">Fleming's left-hand rule questions always require you to state which finger represents which quantity — don't assume the examiner knows you know. For F = BIl calculations, check the unit of B is in tesla before substituting, and confirm the wire is stated to be perpendicular to the field (if not, the equation doesn't directly apply). A very common error in motor questions is forgetting the split-ring commutator: many students explain why the coil starts rotating but don't explain why it keeps rotating. Always finish with: 'the commutator reverses the current every half-turn, maintaining the same rotational direction.' For 6-mark extended responses on the motor, a logical sequence earns top marks: current in coil → motor-effect force → rotation → commutator → continuous rotation.</p>
