--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Work Done</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When a force causes an object to move, energy is transferred from one store to another. The amount of energy transferred in this way is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred when a force moves an object through a distance in the direction of that force, measured in joules.">work done</dfn>. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">$$W = F \times d$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Where \(W\) is work done in joules (J), \(F\) is the force in newtons (N), and \(d\) is the distance moved in the direction of the force in metres (m). One joule is the work done when a force of one newton moves an object one metre.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the equation for work done, write the unit of each quantity, and explain why no work is done when a force is perpendicular to motion.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Work done equals energy transferred: \(W = Fd\). If the force is perpendicular to the direction of motion, no work is done — for example, the normal contact force on a horizontally sliding object does no work because it acts vertically.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n6">A common exam application is work done against <dfn class="term" data-def="A force that opposes the relative motion of surfaces in contact, converting kinetic energy into thermal energy.">friction</dfn>. When an object slides across a rough surface, friction transfers energy from the kinetic energy store to the thermal energy store of the surface and the object — both warm up slightly.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n9">All 480 J is transferred to the thermal energy store of the crate and floor — they become slightly warmer. This energy is dissipated and cannot easily be recovered.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Kinetic Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy an object has due to its motion; it depends on both mass and the square of speed.">Kinetic energy</dfn> (KE) is stored in any moving object. The equation is:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">$$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Where \(E_k\) is kinetic energy in joules, \(m\) is mass in kilograms, and \(v\) is speed in metres per second. Notice that doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy — this is why high-speed impacts are so much more destructive than low-speed ones.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Gravitational Potential Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy stored in an object due to its height in a gravitational field; increases as the object is raised higher.">Gravitational potential energy</dfn> (GPE) depends on the mass, height, and gravitational field strength of the location:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">$$E_p = mgh$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Where \(E_p\) is GPE in joules, \(m\) is mass in kg, \(g\) is gravitational field strength (10 N/kg at Earth's surface), and \(h\) is height above a chosen reference point in metres. Note that \(g\) is different on the Moon, Mars, or other planets — always check the value given in a question.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the GPE equation, state the value of g on Earth, and explain what happens to GPE when an object falls.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>On Earth, gravitational field strength \(g = 10\) N/kg. As an object falls, GPE decreases and, if there is no air resistance, KE increases by exactly the same amount — total mechanical energy is conserved.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Conservation of Energy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The principle that energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred between stores or dissipated to the surroundings.">conservation of energy</dfn> principle states that the total energy in a closed system remains constant. In practice, some energy is always dissipated as thermal energy due to friction or air resistance — but the total is never lost, only spread out.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n21">A classic example is a falling object. At the top of its path, the object has maximum GPE and zero KE. As it falls, GPE converts to KE. Just before hitting the ground, nearly all GPE has become KE (ignoring air resistance). This gives us the useful shortcut: \(mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\), which can be used to find the speed of a falling object without knowing the time of fall.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22">A cyclist and bike have a combined mass of 80 kg. They start from rest at the top of a smooth hill 5 m high. What is their speed at the bottom? (g = 10 N/kg)</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23">Loss in GPE = gain in KE (smooth ramp, so no energy lost to friction):<br>\(mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\)<br>\(80 \times 10 \times 5 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 80 \times v^2\)<br>\(4000 = 40v^2\)<br>\(v^2 = 100\)<br>\(v = 10\) m/s</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Note that mass cancels — the speed at the bottom is independent of how heavy the cyclist is.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Efficiency</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><dfn class="term" data-def="The ratio of useful energy output to total energy input, expressed as a decimal or percentage.">Efficiency</dfn> measures how well a device converts energy into a useful form. No real device is 100% efficient — some energy is always wasted, usually as thermal energy or sound.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">$$\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy output}}{\text{total energy input}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Multiply by 100 for a percentage. An electric motor with efficiency 0.75 converts 75% of electrical energy into kinetic energy; the remaining 25% is dissipated as heat in the windings and bearings.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Power</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><dfn class="term" data-def="The rate at which energy is transferred or work is done, measured in watts (W).">Power</dfn> is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">$$P = \frac{E}{t} \quad \text{or equivalently} \quad P = \frac{W}{t}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32">Where \(P\) is power in watts (W), \(E\) (or \(W\)) is energy in joules, and \(t\) is time in seconds. One watt equals one joule per second. A machine with a power of 500 W transfers 500 J every second.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write both power equations — P = E/t and P = Fv — and state when each is most useful.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>There is a second power equation useful when a steady force moves at constant velocity: \(P = Fv\), where \(F\) is force in newtons and \(v\) is speed in m/s. This is derived by combining \(P = W/t\) with \(W = Fd\): since \(d/t = v\), it follows that \(P = Fv\).</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n34">A car engine provides a constant driving force of 1800 N while the car travels at a steady speed of 25 m/s. Calculate the power output of the engine.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35">\(P = Fv = 1800 \times 25 = 45\,000\) W = 45 kW</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36">At steady speed, the driving force equals the resistive forces (air resistance + friction), so this 45 kW is entirely used to overcome resistance.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n38">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Work done = force × distance (\(W = Fd\)); work done equals energy transferred. One joule is one newton-metre.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40">Kinetic energy \(E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\); gravitational potential energy \(E_p = mgh\). When an object falls freely, GPE converts to KE with no net loss.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n41">Power \(P = E/t\) (rate of energy transfer, in watts). The alternative form \(P = Fv\) is used when force and constant speed are given.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n37">For calculate questions on energy and power, always write the equation first, substitute values with units, and give your final answer to 2–3 significant figures with the correct unit. A common error is forgetting to square the speed in \(E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) — the squaring is not optional. For 4-mark explain questions linking work done to energy transfer, pair each step with its consequence: "the force does work on the object <em>because</em> it moves through a distance, so energy transfers from the chemical store of the fuel to the kinetic store of the object." For 6-mark extended responses, reference specific energy stores (kinetic, gravitational potential, thermal) rather than vague terms like 'energy.'</p>
