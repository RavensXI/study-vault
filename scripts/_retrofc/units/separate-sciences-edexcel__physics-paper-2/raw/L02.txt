<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Scalars and Vectors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Physical quantities are classified as either <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has magnitude (size) only, with no direction — for example, speed, mass, or temperature.">scalar</dfn> or <dfn class="term" data-def="A quantity that has both magnitude and direction — for example, force, velocity, or displacement.">vector</dfn>. Scalars have magnitude only; vectors have both magnitude and direction. This distinction matters whenever you add or resolve forces — you must account for direction, not just size.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write four examples of scalar quantities and four examples of vector quantities without looking.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Scalar examples: speed, mass, temperature, distance, energy. Vector examples: force, velocity, acceleration, displacement, momentum. Force is always a vector — direction is essential when drawing and analysing forces.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Free-Body Diagrams</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A diagram that shows all the forces acting on a single object as arrows drawn from the object, with arrow length proportional to force size.">free-body diagram</dfn> shows all forces acting on an object as labelled arrows. Each arrow starts at the object (or its centre of mass) and points in the direction of that force. Arrow length is drawn proportional to force magnitude. Common forces include weight (downward), normal contact force (perpendicular to surface), friction (opposing motion), and tension (along a string or rope).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">If the arrows on a free-body diagram all balance — equal and opposite in every direction — the object is in <dfn class="term" data-def="The state where all forces on an object are balanced, resulting in no change in speed or direction.">equilibrium</dfn>. This means it is either stationary or moving at constant velocity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Turning Effect and Moments</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">A force can cause an object to rotate about a fixed point called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The fixed point about which an object rotates when a turning force is applied.">pivot</dfn> (or fulcrum). The turning effect of a force is called its <dfn class="term" data-def="The turning effect of a force about a pivot, equal to force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force.">moment</dfn>:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">$$M = F \times d$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Where \(M\) is the moment in newton-metres (N m), \(F\) is the force in newtons, and \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force in metres. Perpendicular is critical — if the force is not applied at 90° to the lever arm, only the perpendicular component creates a moment.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. State the principle of moments in one sentence and write the equation for a moment.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <dfn class="term" data-def="For an object in equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about any pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about the same pivot.">principle of moments</dfn> states that for an object in rotational equilibrium, total clockwise moments = total anticlockwise moments about any pivot.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Centre of Mass</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The <dfn class="term" data-def="The single point through which the entire weight of an object appears to act; for a uniform object this is at its geometric centre.">centre of mass</dfn> is the point through which an object's weight effectively acts. For a uniform, symmetrical object — such as a ruler — the centre of mass is at the geometric centre. For irregular objects, it can be found by suspending the object from different points and drawing the plumb-line; the centre of mass is where the lines intersect.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">An object will topple if its centre of mass moves outside its base. Wide, low objects (a racing car) are more stable; tall, narrow objects (a double-decker bus on a slope) are less stable. Lowering the centre of mass or widening the base increases stability.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Levers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A rigid bar that rotates about a fixed pivot; it uses the principle of moments to allow a small effort force to overcome a larger load force.">lever</dfn> is a rigid bar that can rotate about a pivot. It uses the principle of moments to provide a mechanical advantage — a small effort force applied at a large distance from the pivot can balance (or exceed) a large load force applied closer to the pivot.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Real-world levers include: a crowbar (pivot at one end, effort at the other end, load near the pivot), a wheelbarrow (pivot at the wheel, load in the bucket, effort at the handles), and a pair of scissors (the pivot is the central bolt; the effort is the handle, the load is the material being cut).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Gears</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="Toothed wheels that mesh together to transmit turning forces; they can change the speed and force of rotation between a driving wheel and a driven wheel.">Gears</dfn> are toothed wheels that mesh and rotate together. They transmit a turning force (torque) from one axle to another. The key rule is: when a smaller gear drives a larger gear, the larger gear turns more slowly but with a greater turning force — a trade-off between speed and force that mirrors the principle of moments.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The speed ratio between two meshed gears equals the inverse ratio of their numbers of teeth:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26">$$\frac{\text{speed of driven gear}}{\text{speed of driving gear}} = \frac{\text{teeth on driving gear}}{\text{teeth on driven gear}}$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27">A bicycle uses this principle: changing to a larger sprocket (more teeth) on the rear wheel reduces speed but increases the force applied — useful when climbing hills. The pedal gear (chainring) drives the rear sprocket. Adjacent meshed gears always rotate in opposite directions.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n30">The driven gear is larger and therefore rotates more slowly — but it applies three times the torque of the driving gear. The two gears also rotate in opposite directions.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n33">Vectors have magnitude and direction (force, velocity); scalars have magnitude only (speed, mass). Free-body diagrams show all forces on a single object as labelled arrows.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n34">Moment = force × perpendicular distance (M = Fd, in N m). For equilibrium: total clockwise moments = total anticlockwise moments (principle of moments).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n35">Levers use the principle of moments for mechanical advantage. Gears transmit turning forces: a small driving gear meshing a large driven gear reduces speed but increases force, in inverse proportion to tooth count.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">Moments questions are frequently worth 3–4 marks and hinge on one mistake: students use the slant distance rather than the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force. Always check that \(d\) in \(M = Fd\) is the perpendicular distance — if the force is applied at an angle, you must resolve it first. In 6-mark describe-a-method questions about finding the centre of mass, examiners want: suspend object freely from two different points, draw plumb lines, the intersection is the centre of mass. Missing either suspension point or the intersection step loses marks. For gear calculations, write the ratio explicitly before substituting — it prevents sign errors.</p>
