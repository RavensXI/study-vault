--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Electric Current and Charge</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Electric current is the flow of <dfn class="term" data-def="Electrically charged particles (electrons in metals, ions in solutions) that carry current through a conductor.">charge carriers</dfn> through a conductor. In metals, these are electrons moving from the negative terminal to the positive terminal — although by convention, current direction is defined as flowing from positive to negative. In solutions (electrolytes), both positive and negative ions carry the current.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the equation for charge, name the unit of charge, and state what one ampere represents in terms of charge flow per second.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Charge is measured in coulombs (C). The equation linking charge, current and time is: \(Q = It\), where \(Q\) is charge in coulombs, \(I\) is current in amperes (A), and \(t\) is time in seconds. One ampere means one coulomb of charge passes a point every second.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Current is measured with an <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument used to measure electric current; it must be connected in series with the component being measured.">ammeter</dfn> connected in series with the component. Potential difference (voltage) is measured with a <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument used to measure potential difference; it must be connected in parallel across the component being measured.">voltmeter</dfn> connected in parallel.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Potential Difference</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The energy transferred per unit charge between two points in a circuit; also called voltage, measured in volts.">Potential difference</dfn> (p.d.) is the energy transferred per unit of charge that flows between two points. Think of it as the electrical 'push' that drives charge around the circuit. The unit is the volt (V): one volt means one joule of energy is transferred per coulomb of charge.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Resistance and Ohm's Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="The opposition to the flow of current in a circuit, measured in ohms (Ω). Resistance = potential difference ÷ current.">Resistance</dfn> opposes current flow. The relationship between potential difference, current and resistance is given by Ohm's law:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">$$V = I \times R$$</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Where \(V\) is potential difference in volts, \(I\) is current in amperes, and \(R\) is resistance in ohms (Ω). This can be rearranged: \(I = V/R\) (to find current) and \(R = V/I\) (to find resistance).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define an ohmic conductor, state when Ohm's law applies, and write the three rearrangements of V = IR.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>An <dfn class="term" data-def="A conductor through which current is directly proportional to potential difference at constant temperature — it obeys Ohm's law.">ohmic conductor</dfn> has a constant resistance at constant temperature. For ohmic conductors, doubling the voltage exactly doubles the current. Most metal resistors behave ohmically at room temperature.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">I-V Characteristics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">An I-V characteristic graph plots current (y-axis) against potential difference (x-axis). The shape of the graph reveals how resistance behaves. The core practical (Topic 10) requires you to investigate the I-V characteristics of at least a resistor and a filament lamp.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">For a fixed resistor at constant temperature, the I-V graph is a straight line through the origin. This confirms that current is directly proportional to potential difference — the definition of an ohmic conductor. The gradient of the line equals \(1/R\): a steeper line means lower resistance.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The same straight-line graph applies for both positive and negative p.d. values — the resistor works the same way in both directions.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The I-V graph for a <dfn class="term" data-def="A light bulb in which a thin tungsten wire (filament) is heated until it glows; its resistance increases significantly as temperature rises.">filament lamp</dfn> is curved — the gradient decreases as current increases. This is because the tungsten filament heats up as more current flows: higher temperature means more resistance. The graph is symmetrical about the origin (same shape in both directions).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <span>I-V Graph: Diode</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A component that allows current to flow in one direction only; it has very high resistance in the reverse direction.">diode</dfn> conducts very easily in one direction (forward bias) but has an extremely high resistance in the other direction (reverse bias). Its I-V graph shows almost zero current until the forward voltage threshold is reached, then current rises steeply. In reverse bias, current is essentially zero.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">LDRs and Thermistors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Two variable resistors feature prominently in circuit design and exam questions:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A component whose resistance decreases as light intensity increases; used in automatic lighting circuits.">light-dependent resistor (LDR)</dfn> has a resistance that falls as light intensity increases. In bright conditions, resistance is low; in darkness, resistance is high. LDRs are used in automatic street lighting and camera light meters.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A component whose resistance changes with temperature — typically decreasing as temperature increases in NTC thermistors.">thermistor</dfn> (negative temperature coefficient, NTC, type) has a resistance that decreases as temperature increases. They are used in temperature sensors, thermostats, and fire alarms. In a potential-divider circuit, a thermistor can control a relay that switches a heater on or off.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Series and Parallel Circuits</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected end-to-end so that all current passes through each component in turn.">series circuit</dfn>, the same current flows through every component. Voltages add up across components. Total resistance is the sum of individual resistances: \(R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24">In a <dfn class="term" data-def="A circuit in which components are connected on separate branches so that each component receives the full supply voltage.">parallel circuit</dfn>, each branch has the same potential difference across it (equal to the supply). The total current from the supply equals the sum of the branch currents. Adding more branches decreases the total resistance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Without looking, write the three rules for series circuits and the three rules for parallel circuits (current, voltage, resistance).">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Series: current same everywhere; voltages add up; resistances add up. Parallel: voltage same across all branches; currents add up (junction rule); total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance. Household appliances are wired in parallel so each receives the full supply voltage.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">To plot an I-V characteristic, connect the component in series with an ammeter and a variable resistor (rheostat). Connect a voltmeter in parallel with the component. Adjust the variable resistor to vary the current through the component and record corresponding current and voltage values. Plot I (y-axis) against V (x-axis). Repeat for both polarities where relevant (e.g. a resistor — reverse the cell connections).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27">For the filament lamp, allow cooling time between readings, as temperature affects resistance. Record at least six evenly spaced values for a reliable graph. Repeat readings to check for anomalies.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Charge Q = It (coulombs). Current is measured in series with an ammeter; potential difference in parallel with a voltmeter.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Ohm's law: V = IR. An ohmic conductor has a straight-line I-V graph (constant resistance). A filament lamp has a curved I-V graph (resistance increases with temperature). A diode conducts in one direction only.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n32">Series: same current, voltages add, resistances add. Parallel: same voltage, currents add, total resistance less than smallest branch. LDRs and thermistors have resistance that changes with light and temperature respectively.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">The core practical for I-V characteristics comes up regularly in extended questions. Examiners want the method in full: variable resistor to change p.d., ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel — state all three. For the filament lamp graph, explain the curvature: 'as current increases, the filament heats up, which increases resistance, so current does not increase in proportion to p.d.' For the diode, state it conducts in one direction only. In circuit calculations, always identify whether components are in series or parallel before choosing the right equation — many errors come from applying series rules to a parallel branch.</p>
