--- DESCRIPTION ---
Mild steel, stainless steel, tool steel, brass, copper and aluminium — properties and engineering applications.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Two Metal Families</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">All metals used in engineering can be placed into one of two families. The classification rule is simple: does the metal contain iron? <dfn class="term" data-def="Metals that contain iron as their principal component, such as mild steel, stainless steel and tool steel. Ferrous metals are typically magnetic and prone to rusting.">Ferrous metals</dfn> contain iron. <dfn class="term" data-def="Metals that do not contain iron, such as aluminium, brass and copper. Non-ferrous metals do not rust in the same way as ferrous metals.">Non-ferrous metals</dfn> do not. This matters in engineering for three reasons: ferrous metals are generally magnetic (useful for sorting in recycling and for electromagnetic applications); ferrous metals corrode through rusting when exposed to oxygen and moisture; and the two families have quite different density and conductivity profiles, which drives material selection for specific products.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the iron-content rule for distinguishing ferrous from non-ferrous metals. Then name one ferrous and one non-ferrous metal.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The rule: ferrous metals contain iron; non-ferrous metals do not. Ferrous metals are magnetic and can rust. Non-ferrous metals do not contain iron, are not magnetic, and generally do not rust in the same way. A simple magnetism test can separate the two families.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ferrous Metals</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n5">Mild Steel</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="A ferrous metal (iron with a low carbon content) that is strong, tough, weldable and relatively cheap — the most widely used engineering metal.">Mild steel</dfn> is iron with a low carbon content (typically below 0.3% by weight). It is the most widely used engineering metal. Its combination of good tensile strength, toughness and weldability makes it suitable for a very wide range of products. It can be pressed, rolled, bent and welded without cracking under normal workshop conditions. However, mild steel rusts readily when exposed to moisture and must be protected by painting, galvanising (coating with zinc) or another surface treatment. Common uses include car body panels, structural beams, pipes, sheet metal enclosures and bicycle frames.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Stainless Steel</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A ferrous alloy of iron and at least approximately 10.5% chromium that resists corrosion, making it suitable for cutlery, sinks and medical instruments.">Stainless steel</dfn> is an alloy of iron with chromium (and typically nickel) added. The chromium content makes the surface corrosion-resistant &mdash; it forms a thin oxide layer that prevents the iron beneath from rusting. Stainless steel does not need painting to survive in moist environments. It is harder and more expensive to machine than mild steel. Common uses include kitchen sinks, cutlery, surgical instruments, food-processing equipment and the exterior cladding of some buildings.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Tool Steel</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A high-carbon ferrous alloy that can be hardened by heat treatment, used for cutting tools, drills and dies that must retain a sharp edge under repeated use.">Tool steel</dfn> has a higher carbon content than mild steel and can be hardened through heat treatment processes such as quenching (rapid cooling). The result is a very hard material that holds a sharp cutting edge. This makes it suitable for cutting tools, drills, punches, dies and blades. The trade-off is that tool steel is more brittle than mild steel &mdash; it can fracture under impact loading rather than deforming. It is generally not welded in service because welding disrupts the heat-treated microstructure.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Rusting and How to Prevent It</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11">Rusting is an electrochemical process in which iron reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide (rust). Unlike the oxide layer on aluminium or the patina on copper, rust does not form a protective barrier &mdash; it is porous and continues to allow oxygen and water through to attack the metal beneath. Left untreated, rust will eventually weaken a mild steel component significantly. Engineers prevent rusting by painting (blocking contact with air and moisture), galvanising (zinc coating &mdash; zinc corrodes preferentially, protecting the steel beneath), applying plastic coatings, or choosing a different material such as stainless steel or aluminium for applications where rusting would be a serious problem.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Non-Ferrous Metals</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">Brass</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="An alloy of copper and zinc with good machinability, corrosion resistance and an attractive gold-like appearance, used in plumbing fittings and decorative hardware.">Brass</dfn> is an alloy of copper and zinc. The proportions vary by grade, but typical brass is roughly 70% copper and 30% zinc by weight. Brass machines easily, resists corrosion well and has an attractive gold-like appearance. It is used for plumbing fittings (taps, valves, pipe connectors), musical instrument components, electrical connectors and decorative hardware such as door handles. Brass is not as strong as steel for structural applications but is chosen where its corrosion resistance and machinability are more valuable than raw strength.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">Copper</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="A non-ferrous metal with outstanding electrical and thermal conductivity, used for electrical wiring, water pipes and heat exchangers.">Copper</dfn> is notable in engineering for its outstanding electrical conductivity &mdash; it is the standard material for electrical wiring in buildings, appliances and vehicles precisely because it allows current to flow with very low resistance. Copper also has excellent thermal conductivity, making it useful for heat exchangers and water pipes. It is relatively soft and ductile (can be drawn into fine wire or bent into pipe without cracking). Copper corrodes slowly in air, forming a green patina (copper carbonate) that protects the surface beneath. Common uses include electrical cables, water distribution pipes, the coils of electric motors, and printed circuit board tracks.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write one sentence explaining why copper is the standard material for electrical wiring, using the word 'conductivity'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Copper is the standard material for electrical wiring because it has outstanding electrical conductivity &mdash; current flows through it with minimal resistance. It is also ductile enough to be drawn into fine wire and shaped without breaking.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Aluminium</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A lightweight non-ferrous metal with good corrosion resistance and moderate strength, widely used in aircraft, vehicles, food packaging and window frames.">Aluminium</dfn> is the most widely used non-ferrous engineering metal. Its key advantages are low density (making it much lighter than steel for the same volume), good corrosion resistance (it forms a thin protective oxide layer naturally) and adequate strength for many applications, especially when alloyed with elements such as silicon, magnesium or copper. It conducts electricity and heat well (though not as well as copper). Aluminium does not rust &mdash; it oxidises, but the oxide layer is stable and adherent. Common uses include aircraft structures, vehicle body panels and engine components, food and drink cans, window frames, bicycle frames and smartphone casings.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Selecting the Right Metal for the Job</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Material selection requires matching the material&rsquo;s properties to the engineering requirements of the product. A bicycle frame needs high tensile strength (to resist being pulled apart under load) and low density (to reduce weight) &mdash; aluminium alloy meets both. A kitchen sink needs corrosion resistance and ease of cleaning &mdash; stainless steel is the standard answer. Electrical wiring needs high conductivity and ductility &mdash; copper. Cutting tools need hardness and edge retention &mdash; tool steel. Decorative plumbing fittings need corrosion resistance and machinability &mdash; brass. Exam questions often present a product and ask you to justify a specific material choice by linking properties to requirements. Use the pattern: state the property, link it to the product requirement, name the material that provides it.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Ferrous metals contain iron and are typically magnetic; non-ferrous metals do not contain iron and are not magnetic. Ferrous metals rust; non-ferrous metals generally do not.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Ferrous metals on the spec: mild steel (structural, weldable), stainless steel (corrosion-resistant, cutlery/sinks), tool steel (hardenable, cutting tools). Non-ferrous: brass (corrosion-resistant, plumbing), copper (high conductivity, wiring), aluminium (lightweight, aircraft/vehicle panels).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Material selection links a property to a product requirement. State the material, name the relevant property, and explain why that property makes it suitable for the specific application.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n21">Material selection questions nearly always ask you to &lsquo;justify&rsquo; or &lsquo;explain&rsquo; your choice. Naming the metal alone earns very few marks. You must connect a property to the engineering requirement: &lsquo;Stainless steel is chosen for the kitchen sink because its chromium content makes it corrosion-resistant, meaning it will not rust in the wet environment of a kitchen.&rsquo; For the ferrous/non-ferrous distinction, a common exam trap is to describe aluminium as &lsquo;like steel&rsquo; because it is silver-coloured &mdash; always state the iron-content rule to distinguish them clearly. Remember: mild steel, stainless steel and tool steel are ferrous; brass, copper and aluminium are non-ferrous.</p>
