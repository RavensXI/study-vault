--- DESCRIPTION ---
Thermoplastics, thermosetting plastics, smart materials and composites — properties and engineering uses.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Three More Material Families</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After ferrous and non-ferrous metals, the specification introduces three further material families: plastics (divided into two distinct types), smart materials, and composites. Each has a different internal structure that determines how it behaves and what it can be used for. Getting the families right matters in the exam &mdash; a common mistake is to label any modern or technical-sounding material as &lsquo;smart&rsquo;. The definition of a smart material is precise, and you need to apply it correctly.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the one-sentence definition of a smart material. Then name two examples from the specification.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A <dfn class="term" data-def="A material whose property changes reversibly in response to an external stimulus such as temperature, light or stress.">smart material</dfn> is a material whose property changes reversibly in response to an external stimulus. The stimulus triggers the change; remove the stimulus and the material returns to its original state. This reversibility is the key discriminator. Examples: thermochromic pigments (change colour with temperature) and shape-memory alloys such as Nitinol (return to a remembered shape when heated).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Thermoplastics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Plastics that soften when heated and harden on cooling, allowing them to be re-shaped and recycled — examples include acrylic, nylon and HIPS.">Thermoplastics</dfn> are polymers made of long molecular chains. The chains are held together by relatively weak forces rather than permanent chemical bonds between them. When a thermoplastic is heated, the chains become mobile and the material softens. When it cools, the chains lock together again and the material hardens. This heating-softening-cooling-hardening cycle can be repeated many times without permanently degrading the material, which is why thermoplastics can be recycled.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The three thermoplastics on the specification are acrylic (commonly known by trade names but properly called poly(methyl methacrylate)), nylon (a polyamide, tough and low-friction) and high-impact polystyrene (HIPS, impact-resistant and easy to vacuum-form). Acrylic is used for display cases, light diffusers and the rear lights of vehicles; its transparency and optical clarity make it a glass substitute in many applications. Nylon is used for gears, bearings and textile fibres because of its toughness and low coefficient of friction. HIPS is used in packaging, school engineering projects and domestic appliance linings because it is easy to vacuum-form into complex shapes at relatively low cost.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Plastics that are permanently hardened during curing through cross-linking of polymer chains, and cannot be re-melted or recycled — examples include urea formaldehyde and silicone.">Thermosetting plastics</dfn> undergo a different process. During manufacture, the liquid polymer is poured into a mould and heat or a chemical catalyst causes the chains to form permanent cross-links to each other. This produces a rigid, three-dimensional network structure. Because the cross-links are permanent chemical bonds, the material cannot be re-softened by heating &mdash; applying heat will cause it to char or decompose rather than melt. This is why thermosetting plastics cannot be recycled in the same way as thermoplastics.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">The two thermosetting plastics on the specification are urea formaldehyde and silicone (also written &lsquo;silicon&rsquo; in some engineering contexts, though strictly the material is silicone &mdash; a polymer). Urea formaldehyde is a hard, brittle material used for electrical fittings (plug casings, switch housings) and kitchen worktop laminates. It is chosen because it does not soften or distort at the temperatures these applications encounter. Silicone has very different properties: it remains flexible across a wide temperature range and is chemically resistant. It is used for baking moulds, sealants, medical-grade implants and gaskets. Both are permanently set and cannot be re-moulded.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Smart Materials</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Smart materials are defined by their response to a stimulus. Two types are on the specification. The first is <dfn class="term" data-def="Pigments or inks that change colour reversibly when the temperature changes, used in thermometers, mugs and packaging.">thermochromic pigments and inks</dfn>. These contain molecules whose structure rearranges at a critical temperature, causing them to absorb different wavelengths of light and therefore appear a different colour. Below the critical temperature the pigment appears one colour; above it, another. Remove the heat source and the pigment returns to its original colour. Applications include novelty mugs that reveal an image when hot liquid is poured in, baby bath thermometers that change colour to indicate safe water temperature, and packaging that indicates whether a frozen product has been thawed and refrozen (a food safety application).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The second smart material on the specification is <dfn class="term" data-def="A shape-memory alloy of nickel and titanium that returns to a predetermined shape when heated above its transformation temperature.">Nitinol</dfn> (also written as nitinol wire), a shape-memory alloy made from nickel and titanium. It can be deformed at low temperature and will hold the deformed shape. Heat it above its transformation temperature and it returns to the shape it was set to during manufacture. This shape-memory effect is reversible and reliable over many cycles. Engineering applications include stents (medical devices inserted into arteries &mdash; the stent is compressed for insertion, then expands to its set shape at body temperature), eyeglass frames that spring back after bending, and actuators in control systems where a linear movement is needed in response to a temperature change.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain in two sentences what makes Nitinol a 'smart' material. Use the words 'stimulus' and 'reversible'.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Nitinol is a smart material because its shape-change behaviour is triggered by a stimulus (heat) and is reversible (it returns to its set shape every time the temperature rises above the transformation point). Carbon fibre is <strong>not</strong> a smart material &mdash; it is a composite. Smart materials change a property in response to a stimulus; composites combine two materials to create a new set of properties.</p>
</div>

    <span>Composite Materials &mdash; Matrix and Reinforcement</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A material made by combining two or more constituent materials with different properties to produce a material with characteristics neither constituent has alone.">composite material</dfn> combines a reinforcement and a matrix. The reinforcement provides strength or stiffness; the matrix holds the reinforcement in place and transfers loads to it. The resulting material has properties that neither constituent has alone &mdash; this is the engineering rationale for using a composite rather than a single material.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A composite material of carbon fibre strands embedded in a polymer resin matrix, offering a very high strength-to-weight ratio used in aerospace, motorsport and high-performance bicycles.">Carbon fibre reinforced polymer (CFRP)</dfn> consists of woven or laid carbon fibre strands embedded in a polymer resin matrix (typically epoxy resin). Carbon fibres are stiff and strong in tension; the resin matrix holds them in position and protects them from damage. The resulting composite has an exceptionally high strength-to-weight ratio &mdash; significantly stronger than steel for the same mass. It is used in aircraft structures (reducing weight and fuel consumption), Formula One car bodies, high-performance bicycle frames and sports equipment such as tennis rackets and rowing oars. It is expensive and difficult to repair compared with metals, but where weight saving is critical, the cost is justified.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="A composite material of aramid fibres woven into a fabric and set in a resin matrix, known for outstanding impact resistance and used in ballistic protection and cut-resistant gloves.">Kevlar</dfn> (an aramid fibre composite) has a different property profile. Kevlar fibres are extremely strong in tension and highly resistant to impact. A woven Kevlar fabric does not stretch easily under sudden impact loads &mdash; energy is absorbed and distributed across the fibres rather than being concentrated at a point. This makes Kevlar the standard material for ballistic protection vests, helmets, cut-resistant gloves and the reinforcement layer in vehicle tyres. Like CFRP, Kevlar cannot be melted and reshaped &mdash; its composite structure is permanent.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Selecting the Right Polymer or Composite</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Material selection for plastics and composites follows the same logic as for metals: match the material&rsquo;s properties to the engineering requirements. An electrical fitting needs a material that does not soften at operating temperatures and does not conduct electricity &mdash; urea formaldehyde (thermosetting, electrically insulating, heat-stable) is correct. A product that must be recycled at end of life should use a thermoplastic rather than a thermosetting plastic. A product requiring very low weight and high strength &mdash; an aircraft panel, a racing car component &mdash; may justify the high cost of carbon fibre reinforced polymer. A product requiring impact and cut resistance for personal protection &mdash; a ballistic vest &mdash; uses Kevlar.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">For smart material applications, the trigger must match the intended use. Thermochromic pigments are appropriate where temperature indication is needed at the point of use. Nitinol is appropriate where a reliable, reversible mechanical movement is needed in response to a specific temperature change. Neither thermochromic pigments nor Nitinol are structural materials &mdash; they are functional materials responding to stimuli, not load-bearing elements of a structure.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Thermoplastics (acrylic, nylon, HIPS) can be recycled &mdash; polymer chains re-soften on heating. Thermosetting plastics (urea formaldehyde, silicone) cannot &mdash; cross-linked structure is permanent.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Smart materials change a property reversibly in response to a stimulus: thermochromic pigments change colour with temperature; Nitinol (shape-memory alloy) returns to a set shape when heated. Carbon fibre is a composite, not a smart material.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Composites combine reinforcement and matrix to create properties neither constituent has alone. Carbon fibre reinforced polymer offers a high strength-to-weight ratio; Kevlar offers outstanding impact and cut resistance.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n19">The most common error on materials questions is calling carbon fibre a smart material. Always define smart materials first: &lsquo;A smart material changes a property reversibly in response to a stimulus.&rsquo; Carbon fibre does not change its properties in response to a stimulus &mdash; it is a composite of carbon fibres in a resin matrix. For thermoplastic vs thermosetting questions, the discriminator is always the cross-linked structure: &lsquo;Thermosetting plastics cannot be recycled because heating does not re-soften them &mdash; their cross-linked bonds are permanent.&rsquo; For 4-mark and 6-mark questions on material choice, always give the material name, the relevant property, and the product requirement it meets.</p>
