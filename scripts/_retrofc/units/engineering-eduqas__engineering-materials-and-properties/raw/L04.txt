--- DESCRIPTION ---
Destructive and non-destructive tests for tensile strength, hardness, toughness and conductivity.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Test Materials?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Before an engineer specifies a material for a product, they need evidence that it will perform as required. Material testing provides that evidence. Some tests are carried out during the design phase, to check that a chosen material meets the specification. Others are used in production <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of testing materials or products during production to ensure they meet the required specification before use.">quality control</dfn>, to check that every batch of material supplied to the factory has the properties expected. The type of test chosen depends on what property needs to be measured &mdash; and whether the sample being tested can be sacrificed.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write one sentence each to distinguish destructive testing and non-destructive testing, focusing on what happens to the test sample.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="Testing that permanently damages or destroys the sample in the process of measuring a material property.">Destructive testing</dfn> (DT) damages or destroys the sample to obtain a measurement. It cannot be used on a finished product that is going into service. <dfn class="term" data-def="Testing that measures material properties without permanently damaging the sample, so the part can still be used.">Non-destructive testing</dfn> (NDT) measures properties without permanently harming the sample &mdash; the part can still be used after testing.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Destructive Testing: Tensile Test</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A destructive test in which a sample is pulled apart by an increasing tensile force until it fractures, measuring the material's tensile strength.">tensile test</dfn> measures tensile strength. The sample is machined into a standardised &lsquo;dog-bone&rsquo; shape so that the test section has a known cross-sectional area. It is gripped in a tensile testing machine and a gradually increasing pulling force is applied until the sample fractures. The maximum force sustained before fracture, divided by the original cross-sectional area, gives the tensile strength. The sample is destroyed in the process.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The tensile test also reveals ductility. As the force increases, the sample stretches. The engineer measures how much the sample elongates before it fractures: a ductile material will neck (form a visible waist) and stretch significantly; a brittle material will fracture suddenly with very little stretching. Plotting force against extension produces a stress&ndash;strain curve, which shows the elastic limit, yield point and ultimate tensile strength of the material.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Destructive Testing: Hardness Test</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="A destructive test in which a hard indenter is pressed into a material surface under a known force, and the size of the indent is used to calculate hardness.">Hardness testing</dfn> uses an indenter &mdash; a hard ball or diamond-tipped cone &mdash; pressed into the surface of the sample under a controlled force. The size of the indent left in the surface is measured. A smaller indent means a harder material. Common hardness scales include the Brinell test (steel ball) and the Vickers test (diamond pyramid). Although the indenter leaves a mark in the surface, the sample is generally not destroyed, so hardness testing is sometimes described as semi-destructive. For exam purposes, the specification lists it under destructive testing, so classify it accordingly.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Destructive Testing: Toughness Test</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9b">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A destructive impact test in which a notched sample is struck by a swinging pendulum, and the energy absorbed is used to measure toughness.">Charpy impact test</dfn> measures toughness. A notched sample is placed horizontally on two supports and struck by a heavy pendulum. (Note: in the related Izod test the specimen is clamped vertically at one end — this is the key difference between the two methods.) The height the pendulum swings to after impact tells the engineer how much energy the material absorbed &mdash; a tough material absorbs more energy and the pendulum does not swing as high on the follow-through. The sample is fractured in the test. This test is used to check that structural steels maintain their toughness at low temperatures, since some metals become brittle in cold conditions.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Malleability test:</strong> A sample is repeatedly hammered or rolled while being observed for cracking. A malleable material can be worked without cracking; a brittle material will crack early in the test. The test is destructive because the sample is deformed in the process.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Ductility test (cup and cone / wire draw):</strong> The tensile test already provides ductility data through percentage elongation measurements. A separate bend test &mdash; bending a sample through a set angle &mdash; also checks ductility: a ductile sample bends without cracking, a brittle one fractures.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Elasticity test:</strong> A spring or sample is loaded to a known force and the extension is measured. The load is then removed. If the sample returns to its original length, it behaved elastically within the test range. If the sample remains permanently deformed, it was loaded beyond its elastic limit. The test can be conducted without fracturing the sample if it is kept below the elastic limit.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Conductivity Testing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Electrical conductivity is tested by passing a known current through a sample of defined dimensions and measuring the voltage across it. Using Ohm&rsquo;s law (V = IR), the resistance can be calculated, which is the inverse of conductivity. A multimeter is a common workshop tool used to measure resistance in materials and components. This test does not destroy the sample, so it is a non-destructive test.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Non-Destructive Testing: The Core Principle</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">NDT methods leave the test sample intact and usable. This matters enormously in engineering practice because many parts &mdash; the welds on a bridge, the fuselage skin of an aircraft, the gas pipe below a city street &mdash; cannot be sacrificed for a destructive test. NDT allows engineers to check for internal flaws, cracks or weaknesses without cutting the part open. The most important NDT methods at this level are visual inspection and ultrasonic testing.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Worked Example: Choosing DT or NDT</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Scenario:</strong> A manufacturer produces a batch of steel cable for a new suspension bridge. They want to verify that the cable meets its tensile strength specification before installation. After installation, the bridge maintenance team wants to check the cables annually for internal corrosion and micro-cracks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Before installation &mdash; destructive tensile test:</strong> A sample of cable from the batch is pulled to fracture in a tensile testing machine. This confirms the tensile strength meets the specification. The tested sample is destroyed, but because it is taken from the production batch rather than from the actual bridge cable, this is acceptable. The tested sample cannot be used in the bridge.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>After installation &mdash; non-destructive ultrasonic test:</strong> Sound waves are passed through the installed cable. Any crack or void inside the cable reflects the sound waves back differently from solid metal. The maintenance team can detect internal flaws without cutting the cable out of the bridge. The cable remains in service throughout the test.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Key principle:</strong> Use DT to characterise material properties before the product goes into service. Use NDT to inspect finished parts and products that must remain intact and in service.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: write down two reasons why an engineer would choose NDT over DT for inspecting a weld on a gas pipeline.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The fundamental reason to choose NDT over DT is that the in-service part cannot be sacrificed. A weld in a live gas pipeline cannot be cut out for a destructive test; an ultrasonic scan checks for cracks non-destructively. Top-band exam answers connect the choice of test to whether the product can be sacrificed.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">Destructive testing (tensile test, impact test, hardness test) permanently damages or destroys the sample but gives precise measurements of properties such as tensile strength, toughness and hardness.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">Non-destructive testing leaves the sample intact and is used on in-service parts that cannot be sacrificed &mdash; such as welds, pipelines and structural cables.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n26">Choosing between DT and NDT depends on whether the part being tested can be sacrificed and whether the test is part of quality control on a new batch or inspection of an existing product.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p>A common exam mistake is to say destructive testing &lsquo;breaks the sample&rsquo; and leave it there. You must also say why this matters for the product &mdash; namely, that the tested sample cannot go into service. For &lsquo;explain&rsquo; questions on NDT, state that the part remains intact and can continue to be used after testing.</p>
