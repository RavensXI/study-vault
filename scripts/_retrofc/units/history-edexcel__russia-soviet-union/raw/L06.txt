--- DESCRIPTION ---
Humiliating peace with Germany, Trotsky’s new Red Army, and a civil war that scarred Russia.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Peace That Saved the Revolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By the time Russian and German negotiators sat down at Brest-Litovsk in December 1917, the Russian army had stopped fighting in any meaningful sense. Soldiers were walking home; trains were being commandeered to take them; officers had lost the authority to issue orders that would be obeyed. Lenin&rsquo;s Decree on Peace had promised an immediate armistice. The Germans answered with terms that nobody in Russia wanted to sign. The peace that followed humiliated the new Soviet state, split the Bolshevik leadership and lit the fuse of a civil war that would kill millions. Within months Trotsky was building a new Red Army from scratch, the Whites were marching on Moscow and Petrograd, and foreign troops were landing on Russian soil.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: list four reasons the Reds won the Civil War, with one supporting detail for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Reds won the Civil War of 1918&ndash;21 because they held the central railway hub of Russia (interior lines), they had unified leadership under Lenin and Sovnarkom while the Whites fought as separate armies, Trotsky built a 5-million-strong Red Army with conscription and ex-Tsarist officers, and Bolshevik propaganda framed the Reds as defenders of revolution and Russia against monarchist returnees and foreign invaders.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, March 1918</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The German terms were brutal. Russia would lose Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, Finland and Ukraine &mdash; roughly a quarter of its population, a quarter of its industry and almost half of its grain-producing land. Trotsky, who led the first Soviet delegation, tried a strategy he called &lsquo;no war, no peace&rsquo; &mdash; refusing to sign while ordering full demobilisation. The Germans responded by simply marching deeper into Russia in February 1918. With nothing to stop them, Lenin overruled Trotsky and the &lsquo;Left Communists&rsquo; in the Central Committee. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The peace treaty of 3 March 1918 between the new Soviet government and the Central Powers, ending Russian participation in the First World War on heavy territorial terms.">Treaty of Brest-Litovsk</dfn> was signed on 3 March 1918.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Reactions were sharply divided. War-weary peasants and workers in the central provinces were relieved that the slaughter was over. The Left Socialist Revolutionaries, who had joined Sovnarkom in coalition, walked out in protest and within weeks had launched an uprising in Moscow. Inside the Bolshevik Party itself, Bukharin&rsquo;s &lsquo;Left Communists&rsquo; argued that signing was a betrayal of revolutionary internationalism. Lenin&rsquo;s answer was pragmatic: a breathing space at any price was worth more than a doomed revolutionary war. By November 1918 Germany had collapsed and Sovnarkom annulled the treaty. The territorial losses, however, were the seed of the conflict that followed.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n7">Students often write that the treaty was universally hated. It was not. Peasant villages that had lost sons at the front saw an end to the war as the most basic Bolshevik promise being kept. Sovnarkom kept producing pamphlets explaining that the breathing space would be used to consolidate revolutionary Russia. The treaty was a foundational grievance for the Whites and for foreign-intervention governments &mdash; but at home Lenin&rsquo;s gamble that the country would tolerate the loss of Ukraine and the Baltics in exchange for peace was largely vindicated. Strong answers separate elite Bolshevik anger from war-weary popular relief.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Whites and the Outbreak of Civil War</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The opposition to Bolshevik rule was loose and incoherent. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Loose coalition of monarchists, liberals, ex-Tsarist generals, Kadets and Socialist Revolutionaries who fought the Bolsheviks in the Civil War of 1918&ndash;21.">Whites</dfn> were not a single army but a label for several armies fighting in different theatres. General Anton Denikin led the Volunteer Army in the south. Admiral Alexander Kolchak set up a government at Omsk in Siberia and was recognised by other White leaders as &lsquo;Supreme Ruler&rsquo;. General Nikolai Yudenich attacked Petrograd from Estonia. General Pyotr Wrangel took over the southern army after Denikin and finally evacuated his troops from Crimea in November 1920.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">The catalyst for full-scale war was the revolt of the Czech Legion in May 1918. Czech and Slovak prisoners of war from the Austro-Hungarian army, organised into a 40,000-strong unit on Bolshevik soil and trying to leave Russia by way of Vladivostok, fell out with local Soviet authorities and turned their rifles on the Bolsheviks. Within weeks they had seized stations along the Trans-Siberian Railway and helped a Socialist Revolutionary government, the Komuch, take Samara. The Bolsheviks now faced multiple fronts at once.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Trotsky and the Red Army</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">In March 1918 Lenin moved Trotsky from foreign affairs to war. As Commissar for War, Trotsky inherited an army that did not exist. The old Tsarist forces had dissolved; the Red Guards were factory militias of a few thousand. By the end of 1920 Trotsky had built a Red Army of around 5 million. He did it by reintroducing conscription, recruiting roughly 50,000 ex-Tsarist officers as &lsquo;military specialists&rsquo; under the watch of <dfn class="term" data-def="Bolshevik party officials attached to Red Army units to enforce political loyalty and oversee former Tsarist officers; their counter-signature was needed on military orders.">political commissars</dfn>, and reimposing harsh military discipline including the death penalty for desertion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Trotsky&rsquo;s armoured train was the symbol of the war effort. From it he travelled the fronts almost without stopping, rushed reserves to threatened sectors, addressed wavering units in person and shot deserters and corrupt commanders. Strong students note that this drive was matched by Lenin&rsquo;s political grip in Moscow and the Cheka&rsquo;s terror in the rear. Trotsky did not win the Civil War alone, but the Red Army he built was the indispensable instrument of victory.</p>



<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how big was the Red Army by 1920, who supervised the ex-Tsarist officers, and what penalty did Trotsky impose for desertion?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By the end of 1920 Trotsky had built the Red Army to roughly 5 million troops. He used conscription, recruited around 50,000 ex-Tsarist officers as &lsquo;military specialists&rsquo; under the watch of political commissars, and reimposed harsh discipline including the death penalty for desertion.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Foreign Intervention</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">From the spring of 1918 onwards British, French, American and Japanese forces landed at Russian ports. The British went into Murmansk and Archangel in the north and the Caucasus in the south. The French landed in Odessa. The Americans deployed troops in Vladivostok and the Russian north. Japan put around 70,000 men into the Russian Far East. The official aim was first to keep Allied war supplies out of German hands, and after November 1918 to strangle Bolshevism in its cradle. The reality was indecisive. Allied governments were exhausted by the world war and unable to commit large forces. By 1920 most foreign troops had withdrawn.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Bolshevik propaganda used the intervention as a gift. The Reds presented themselves as the defenders of Russia against foreign invaders propping up monarchist generals. Strong answers point out that the intervention may have prolonged the war but did relatively little militarily; politically it helped the Bolsheviks more than it hurt them.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Peasants disliked both sides. White armies seized grain and conscripted men just as harshly as the Reds did, and on the territory the Whites recovered they often returned land to the gentry &mdash; a fatal mistake in a country where the peasantry had just gained land under the Bolshevik decree. The Whites lost the Civil War mainly for structural reasons. They could not coordinate Denikin in the south, Kolchak in Siberia and Yudenich in the Baltic; they had no common political programme; they relied on monarchist symbols that alienated socialists; and the Reds held the central railway hub of Russia, while the Whites had to march in from the periphery. Strong answers separate &lsquo;the Whites were unpopular&rsquo; from the harder, more accurate point about coordination and geography.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Why the Reds Won</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Strong answers explain Red victory by combining four sets of reasons. First, geography: the Reds held Moscow, Petrograd and the central railway hub, giving them <dfn class="term" data-def="A military advantage in which one side, holding the central position, can move forces between fronts faster than its scattered enemies; held by the Reds in the Russian Civil War.">interior lines</dfn> and short supply routes. Second, leadership: Lenin and Sovnarkom gave the Reds a single political authority while the Whites had no equivalent. Third, the Red Army: Trotsky&rsquo;s mass conscript force, ex-Tsarist officers under commissar oversight, and ruthless discipline. Fourth, propaganda: the Reds offered land, peace and bread, and presented their opponents as monarchists and foreign agents. The Whites&rsquo; weaknesses &mdash; division, scattered fronts, the return of land to gentry &mdash; finished the case. By November 1920 Wrangel had evacuated Crimea, and the war was effectively over.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918) cost Russia a quarter of its population, a quarter of its industry and almost half of its grain land &mdash; but Lenin treated the loss as the breathing space the new state needed to survive.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n24">The Civil War of 1918&ndash;21 was fought between a unified Red regime in the centre and a coalition of separate White armies on the periphery, with limited foreign intervention from Britain, France, the United States and Japan.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n25">The Reds won because they held the central railway hub, had unified leadership under Lenin and Sovnarkom, built a 5-million-strong Red Army under Trotsky, and used propaganda to brand the Whites as monarchists and foreign agents.</li>
</ul>
