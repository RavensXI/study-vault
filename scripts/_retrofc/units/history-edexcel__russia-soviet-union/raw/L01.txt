--- DESCRIPTION ---
How war, peasant misery and city strikes brought the Romanov Empire to the brink in early 1917.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">An Empire Stretched to Breaking</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In early 1917 the Russian Empire ruled a sixth of the world&rsquo;s land surface and around 165 million people across more than a hundred languages. Tsar Nicholas II governed it as an <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government in which a single ruler holds absolute, unchecked power.">autocracy</dfn>: he claimed to rule by divine right, with no constitution worth the paper it was printed on and a Duma he had clipped four times since 1905. Beneath the imperial flag the country was poorer, hungrier and angrier than it looked in the shop windows of central Petrograd. By February 1917 three crises had collided &mdash; war, peasant land hunger and city strikes &mdash; and the regime had no answer to any of them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name the three pressures squeezing Russia in early 1917, with one specific fact for each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>By early 1917 three pressures were squeezing Russia at once: military disaster on the Eastern Front, peasant land hunger going back to the 1861 Emancipation, and a strike wave in Petrograd driven by food and fuel shortages. None of them was new &mdash; but together they were unmanageable.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">A Peasant Country with Industrial Cities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Around 80% of Russians were peasants. The 1861 Emancipation had freed the serfs but left them with redemption payments, communal &lsquo;mir&rsquo; land tenure and small, scattered strips. Land hunger was the dominant grievance in the countryside &mdash; peasants wanted the estates of the gentry, the Church and the Crown, and they wanted them now. At the same time, Sergei Witte&rsquo;s industrialisation drive from the 1890s had built huge factories in Petrograd, Moscow, the Donbas and Baku. The Putilov works in Petrograd alone employed about 30,000 workers in early 1917. This urban working class, though only a small slice of the population, was concentrated in exactly the places where revolutions are made.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">It is easy to caricature 1917 Russia as one vast village. The picture is sharper if you hold the two facts together: a peasant country with a Western-scale industrial belt grafted on. The Bolsheviks were strongest in the second part; the first part decided whether the cities ate.</p>

    <span>Misconception: &ldquo;Russia in 1917 was just a peasant economy&rdquo;</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7">Students often write that Russia was a backward agricultural country and stop there. Witte&rsquo;s industrialisation made Russia the fourth largest industrial economy in the world by 1914 by some measures. The factories were enormous, often foreign-owned and packed into a few districts of Petrograd, Moscow and the south. That concentration mattered: when the Putilov workers went out on 18 February 1917, half of working Petrograd followed them within days. A scattered peasantry could not have brought down a tsar &mdash; a concentrated urban working class could.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The First World War: A Disaster the Tsar Could Not Outrun</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Russia entered the war in August 1914 to a wave of patriotic crowds. The wave broke fast. At Tannenberg (August 1914) and the Masurian Lakes (September 1914) two Russian armies were destroyed by smaller German forces; in the 1915 Galician retreat Russia lost Poland and much of the western borderlands. By 1917 about 1.7 million Russian soldiers had been killed and another 5 million wounded or captured. Industry was retooled for shells, but the railways could not keep up: troop trains, food trains and grain trains all queued for the same lines, and food rotted in sidings while Petrograd queued for bread.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10">In September 1915, against the advice of his ministers, Nicholas II left for the front to take personal command at <dfn class="term" data-def="The headquarters of the Russian high command during the First World War, located at Mogilev from August 1915.">Stavka</dfn>, the army headquarters near Mogilev. The decision tied every defeat to him personally. It also took him out of the capital just as the home front was unravelling.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down: which decision in September 1915 chained every Russian defeat to Nicholas II personally, and why was it a mistake?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In September 1915 Nicholas II took personal command of the army at Stavka, near Mogilev. The defeats of 1915&ndash;17 now belonged to him in name as well as fact, and his absence from Petrograd left the home government in the hands of Tsarina Alexandra and her advisers.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Alexandra, Rasputin and the Collapse of Public Trust</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">With Nicholas at the front, his German-born wife <dfn class="term" data-def="German-born wife of Nicholas II; ran the home government from 1915 and was widely distrusted because of her reliance on Rasputin.">Tsarina Alexandra</dfn> ran the home government. She replaced ministers at speed &mdash; four prime ministers, five interior ministers and three war ministers in just over a year &mdash; and leaned heavily on Grigori Rasputin, a self-styled Siberian holy man who had won her trust by appearing to ease the haemophilia of her son Alexei. Rumours that Rasputin was the Tsarina&rsquo;s lover, that they were German agents, that Rasputin was choosing ministers, swept Petrograd. Most were untrue. The damage was the rumour, not the reality.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">In December 1916 a group of aristocrats led by Prince Yusupov murdered Rasputin and dumped his body in the Neva. Killing him solved nothing &mdash; Alexandra&rsquo;s grip on policy continued, and the Romanov family had publicly admitted that the dynasty&rsquo;s closest circle thought the home government had become indefensible.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Strong answers separate Rasputin&rsquo;s actual influence from his political effect. His real power over policy was modest &mdash; he flattered the Tsarina, recommended a few cronies and was overruled often. But the perception that a peasant mystic was sleeping with the Tsarina and choosing ministers destroyed what was left of the dynasty&rsquo;s respectability. By late 1916 even the Duma president Mikhail Rodzianko was warning Nicholas that the regime was finished. The lesson for the exam: in revolutions, public belief about a fact can matter more than the fact itself.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Petrograd in the Winter of 1916&ndash;17</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">By January 1917 Petrograd was running on fumes. Bread rations, when they arrived, were down to about 280 grams a day. Coal trains were stuck in the south; temperatures fell below &minus;30&deg;C; the bakeries closed because of the fuel shortage as much as the flour shortage. Strikes that had been about wages in 1915 were now about food. The Okhrana &mdash; the tsarist secret police &mdash; reported to ministers in January 1917 that the city was on the edge of an explosion.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">When historians reach for a single short answer to &ldquo;why did the Tsar fall?&rdquo;, they tend to argue it was not the principle of <dfn class="term" data-def="A system of government in which a single ruler holds absolute, unchecked power.">autocracy</dfn> that broke Nicholas. It was that he had managed military disaster, bread queues and the Rasputin scandal at the same time, while disappearing to a war headquarters that kept losing battles. By February 1917 the question was no longer whether something would crack, but where.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Russia in 1917 was both a peasant country and an industrial one &mdash; and revolutions are made where the factories are concentrated, not where the villages are scattered.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The First World War tied military disaster directly to Nicholas II after he took command at Stavka in September 1915, leaving the home government to Alexandra and her advisers.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Rasputin&rsquo;s real power was modest, but the rumours around him destroyed the Romanov dynasty&rsquo;s public legitimacy &mdash; killing him in December 1916 came too late and changed nothing.</li>
</ul>
