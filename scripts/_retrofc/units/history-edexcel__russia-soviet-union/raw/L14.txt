<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Society in Motion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">By 1941 the Soviet Union was a country whose social map had been redrawn in fifteen years. Twenty-nine million peasants had moved into the cities to work in the new factories of the First and Second Five-Year Plans. The countryside had been collectivised, dekulakised and partly starved. Women were doing factory and tractor work that would have been unimaginable in 1917. The state had begun deporting whole nationalities. The experience of Soviet life in 1941 depended sharply on who you were: a Stakhanovite shock-worker in the Donbas lived a different life from a kolkhoz peasant in Ukraine or a Soviet Korean being put on a train for Central Asia.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Towns: Kommunalka Flats, Rationing and the Internal Passport</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The headline of urban life in the 1930s was housing shortage. Rapid industrialisation drew millions into cities that had not been rebuilt for them; the typical Soviet family lived in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A Soviet communal flat in which several unrelated families shared a kitchen, bathroom and corridor; their own room held a bed, a wardrobe and a table; the dominant form of Moscow and Leningrad housing through the 1930s and beyond.">kommunalka</dfn> &mdash; a communal flat in which two or three families shared a kitchen, bathroom and corridor. Food was rationed from 1929 to 1935. Bread was the staple; meat and butter were luxuries; queues for everything were normal. From December 1932 every Soviet citizen aged sixteen or over needed an <dfn class="term" data-def="The 1932 Soviet identity document required for travel between regions and for residence in cities; rural collective-farm members were initially denied passports, tying them to the kolkhoz; used to control internal migration and arrest &lsquo;passportless&rsquo; persons.">internal passport</dfn> with a residence stamp; collective-farm peasants were initially denied passports altogether, which tied them to the kolkhoz like serfs. Factories used piece rates, fines for absenteeism, and from 1932 a labour book recording every job change. The 1940 labour law made unauthorised absence from work a criminal offence punishable by prison.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: in what year were internal passports introduced, who was denied them, and what was the penalty for unauthorised absence from work in 1940?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Internal passports were introduced in December 1932; collective-farm peasants were initially denied them, tying them to the kolkhoz. Bread rationing ran from 1929 to 1935. The 1940 labour law made unauthorised absence from work a criminal offence punishable by prison, alongside piece rates, labour books and fines for lateness.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">The Stakhanovites and the Bureaucratic Elite</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Soviet life was not uniformly miserable. From August 1935, when the Donbas miner Alexei Stakhanov reportedly cut 102 tonnes of coal in a single shift &mdash; fourteen times the norm &mdash; the regime promoted the <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet shock-workers held up as records-breakers from August 1935; rewarded with bonuses, larger flats, holidays and consumer goods, and used as a vehicle for raising production norms across industry.">Stakhanovites</dfn> as a model. Top performers were rewarded with cash bonuses, larger flats with their own kitchens, holiday vouchers to Black Sea sanatoria, and access to closed shops stocked with goods unavailable to ordinary workers. Party officials, NKVD officers and senior managers shared this privileged tier. The historian Sheila Fitzpatrick has described 1930s Soviet society as one of sharp inequality dressed in egalitarian language. The same shop-window that displayed shock-worker rewards also fixed production norms higher across whole sectors, so the unintended cost of the Stakhanovite movement was that ordinary workers had to run faster to meet the new targets.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">The Countryside: Kolkhoz Life and the Private Plot</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">In the countryside the dominant institution was the <dfn class="term" data-def="A Soviet collective farm in which peasants pooled their land, livestock and labour and were paid a share of the harvest in &lsquo;labour-day&rsquo; units; supplemented by small private plots that supplied much of urban meat and dairy.">kolkhoz</dfn>. Peasants pooled land, livestock and labour and were paid a share of what was left after the state had taken its quota, valued in &lsquo;labour-day&rsquo; units that were often worthless. Their lifeline was the small private plot, half a hectare or less, on which they could grow vegetables and keep a cow or pigs &mdash; by the late 1930s these private plots produced the majority of urban meat, dairy and eggs despite being a tiny fraction of cultivated land. State farms (sovkhozy) were less common but better paid, with workers on a wage. Rural living standards remained well below urban levels; the houses were wooden, electricity was rare, and the famine of 1932&ndash;33 had killed several million people in Ukraine, Kazakhstan and the North Caucasus. From 1935 collective-farm peasants were required to deliver compulsory grain quotas; failure was punishable as &lsquo;sabotage&rsquo;.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n10">Students often write that everyone in 1930s USSR was equally poor and oppressed. The picture is more textured. Stakhanovites, party bureaucrats, NKVD officers and skilled workers in defence industries lived a recognisably middle-class life with bonuses, larger flats and access to closed shops. Newly literate peasant migrants who had reached the cities and learned a trade saw real upward mobility. The historian Sheila Fitzpatrick has called the 1930s a period of &lsquo;Big Deal&rsquo; social bargaining for those who proved useful to the regime. Strong answers explain that experience varied by class, region and ethnicity, and avoid &lsquo;everyone suffered equally&rsquo; framing.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Women: Industry, Education and the 1936 Family Code</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Soviet women entered industry on a scale unmatched in any other major economy. Between 1928 and 1940 around ten million women joined the urban workforce. Women trained as engineers, doctors and tractor drivers; by 1940 around three quarters of Soviet doctors were women. Education for girls was free and compulsory through to 14, and Soviet propaganda celebrated the model peasant girl who became a tractor driver or a Hero of Socialist Labour. Yet from 1936 the regime sharply rolled back the early Bolshevik record on the family. The 1936 Family Code banned abortion (which had been legalised in 1920 by Alexandra Kollontai&rsquo;s Commissariat for Social Welfare), made divorce far more expensive and bureaucratic, restored awards for mothers of large families and criminalised male non-payment of child support. Stalin called the family &lsquo;the basic cell of socialist society&rsquo;. The retrenchment was both ideological &mdash; positioning the regime as morally conservative on the eve of war &mdash; and demographic: the famines, the Terror, the urban housing crisis and falling birth rates had all reduced the population.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: name three changes the 1936 Family Code made to women&rsquo;s legal position, and one early Bolshevik gain it reversed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The 1936 Family Code banned abortion (legalised 1920), made divorce expensive and bureaucratic, restored awards for mothers of large families, and criminalised male non-payment of child support. The early-1920s Bolshevik record under Alexandra Kollontai &mdash; legal abortion, easy divorce, civil marriage &mdash; was sharply reversed. By 1940 around three quarters of Soviet doctors were women, but their position at home had been re-traditionalised.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Persecution of Ethnic Minorities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">In August&ndash;October 1937 the NKVD deported around 172,000 Soviet Koreans from the Pacific Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, on the grounds that some might spy for Imperial Japan. Around 40,000 are estimated to have died of disease, hunger and cold during or shortly after the journey. Through 1937&ndash;38 the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="NKVD operations of 1937&ndash;38 targeting Soviet citizens of Polish, German, Latvian, Estonian, Finnish, Greek and Korean ethnic background; produced a third of all Yezhovshchina executions despite these groups being a small fraction of the Soviet population.">national operations</dfn>&rsquo; targeted Soviet Poles, Germans, Latvians, Estonians, Finns, Greeks and Bulgarians; around 350,000 people were arrested and roughly two thirds were shot. After the German invasion in June 1941, Stalin ordered the deportation of around 440,000 Volga Germans &mdash; an autonomous community since the eighteenth century &mdash; to Siberia and Kazakhstan. The wartime deportations of Crimean Tatars, Chechens, Ingush and Kalmyks would follow in 1943&ndash;44, but the Soviet pattern of preventive ethnic deportation began in 1937, well before Operation Barbarossa.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Students often present ethnic deportation as a 1941&ndash;44 wartime measure. The Soviet Korean deportation of 1937 came four years before Barbarossa, and the &lsquo;national operations&rsquo; of 1937&ndash;38 against Poles, Germans and other diaspora communities were running alongside the Yezhovshchina. Lynne Viola and Lynne Lawrence&rsquo;s work on the special-settlement archives shows preventive ethnic targeting was a 1930s pattern that the war intensified. Strong answers separate pre-war ethnic persecution from wartime deportations and avoid the simpler &lsquo;Stalin reacted to the German invasion&rsquo; framing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">The USSR in June 1941</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">By June 1941, on the eve of Operation Barbarossa, Soviet life had been remade. An urban industrial workforce had been built; women were a majority of doctors and a large minority of engineers; literacy had risen sharply; the Communist Party reached into every workplace through the Komsomol and the Stakhanovite movement. The cost was real and unevenly distributed. Peasants were tied to the kolkhoz with passport controls; workers faced criminal sanctions for absenteeism; women had gained access to industry and lost ground at home; entire ethnic communities had been deported. Stakhanovites and party bureaucrats lived above the rationed economy in a world of bonuses and closed shops. The regime that the German army was about to attack was not the egalitarian society its propaganda described, but it was a Soviet society fundamentally different from the one Lenin had inherited in 1917.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">Urban Soviets in the 1930s lived in kommunalka flats with shared kitchens, queued for rationed bread, and were tied to their cities by internal passports introduced in 1932; the 1940 labour law made unauthorised absence from work a criminal offence.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">Soviet women entered industry and the professions on a scale unmatched elsewhere &mdash; around three quarters of doctors by 1940 &mdash; but the 1936 Family Code banned abortion, made divorce harder, and reversed the early Bolshevik record set under Alexandra Kollontai.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n23">Ethnic persecution was not just a wartime measure: the Soviet Koreans were deported in 1937, the &lsquo;national operations&rsquo; of 1937&ndash;38 targeted Poles, Germans and other diaspora communities, and around 440,000 Volga Germans were deported following the German invasion in 1941.</li>
</ul>
