Magnitogorsk, Stakhanov and turning the USSR into a heavy-industrial power in a decade.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Stalin Chose Rapid Industrialisation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In November 1927 Stalin warned the Party Congress that &lsquo;we are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or they crush us.&rsquo; The argument rested on four reasons. First, defence: a peasant economy could not arm itself against the industrial powers of Europe and Japan. Second, autarky: an isolated socialist state needed to make at home what capitalist countries would not sell on credit. Third, ideology: socialism, in Marxist theory, required an industrial working class, and the USSR in 1928 was overwhelmingly rural. Fourth, succession: by tearing up the New Economic Policy, Stalin defined himself against Bukharin&rsquo;s Right Opposition and seized the political initiative inside the party. The result was the First Five-Year Plan, launched in October 1928 with targets backdated to begin from 1 October 1928 and a one-year acceleration of the deadline announced in 1930.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: name Stalin&rsquo;s four reasons for industrialisation, the dates of each Five-Year Plan, and the priority sector of each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Three Five-Year Plans framed Soviet industrialisation: the First (1928&ndash;32) prioritised heavy industry &mdash; coal, iron, steel, oil; the Second (1933&ndash;37) promised consumer goods but delivered defence and rearmament; the Third (1938&ndash;41) focused on armaments and was cut short by Operation Barbarossa in June 1941. Targets were set by <dfn class="term" data-def="The Soviet State Planning Commission, founded in 1921; under Stalin from 1928 it set production targets for industry across each Five-Year Plan.">Gosplan</dfn>, the State Planning Commission, and enforced through the People&rsquo;s Commissariats and the secret police.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Gosplan and the Planning System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Soviet planning was not what it claimed to be. Gosplan in Moscow set headline output figures for each sector. The Commissariats translated those into factory quotas. Local managers, on pain of arrest, had to fulfil &mdash; and ideally over-fulfil &mdash; the quota by the deadline. Inputs (coal, steel, labour, machinery) were allocated centrally rather than purchased on a market. Prices on planning targets were political, not economic. Managers responded by hoarding raw materials, padding workforce numbers, ignoring quality and faking returns. Targets in tonnes encouraged heavy, low-quality output: shoes the wrong sizes, nails the wrong weights, cement that did not set. Output figures published in <em>Pravda</em> as &lsquo;over-fulfilment&rsquo; were inflated as a matter of political requirement, not as a neutral observation.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6">Students often quote Soviet headline figures &mdash; coal up four-fold, steel up four-fold &mdash; as if they were neutral measurements. They were not. Output was measured in inconsistent units across sectors, padded by managers desperate to fulfil quotas, and political over-fulfilment was a survival tactic during the Terror. The historian R. W. Davies, working with archival data, has shown that some sectors fell well short of plan and that consumer goods consistently lagged. Strong answers acknowledge that Soviet industry genuinely grew at remarkable speed &mdash; but treat the published figures as evidence of state ambition, not as exact fact.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">The First Five-Year Plan, 1928&ndash;32</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The First Plan poured everything into heavy industry. Coal, iron, steel, oil, electricity and machine tools were the priority; consumer goods, housing and food were starved. The flagship project was <dfn class="term" data-def="A new steel city in the southern Urals, built from scratch from 1929 around the Magnetic Mountain iron-ore deposit; flagship project of the First Five-Year Plan.">Magnitogorsk</dfn> in the southern Urals, a steel city built from scratch around the iron ore of the Magnetic Mountain. The Dnieper hydroelectric dam was completed in 1932, then the largest in Europe. The Stalingrad and Kharkov tractor factories, the Moscow Metro (begun 1932), and the White Sea&ndash;Baltic Canal &mdash; the infamous Belomor Canal, dug largely by Gulag prisoners between 1931 and 1933 with around 12,000&ndash;25,000 dead &mdash; were among the headline projects. Coal output rose from around 36 million tonnes in 1928 to roughly 64 million in 1932; pig iron, steel and electricity all grew sharply. By 1932 the Plan was declared completed in four years and three months.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Workers paid for the speed. Real wages fell by perhaps half between 1928 and 1932 as food prices rose and consumer goods disappeared from the shops. Internal passports were introduced in December 1932 to stop workers leaving badly-supplied factories. Labour discipline laws criminalised lateness and absenteeism: from 1932 a single day&rsquo;s unauthorised absence could cost a worker their job, ration card and accommodation. Foreign engineers &mdash; perhaps 10,000 of them, mostly American &mdash; were hired to install machinery, train Soviet workers and run the new plants until Soviet engineers could replace them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Second Five-Year Plan, 1933&ndash;37</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The Second Plan was originally branded the &lsquo;<dfn class="term" data-def="The Soviet propaganda slogan for the Second Five-Year Plan (1933&ndash;37) promising better-quality output and consumer goods after the headlong drive of the First Plan; promise that was largely abandoned as defence budgets rose from 1935.">Three Goods&rsquo; Plan</dfn>: better food, clothing and housing for Soviet citizens after the headlong drive of the First Plan. From 1933 to 1935 there was indeed some recovery in living standards: ration cards were abolished in 1935 and bread came back into the shops. From 1935, however, the international situation darkened with the rise of Nazi Germany. Defence spending rose from around 4 per cent of state expenditure in 1933 to over 16 per cent by 1937. The promised consumer goods were quietly dropped in favour of tanks, aircraft and shells. New industrial regions were developed in the Urals, Siberia and Kazakhstan, deliberately east of the Ural mountains so that German bombers could not reach them. The Moscow Metro opened its first line in 1935, hailed as a propaganda triumph by Lazar Kaganovich.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">The Stakhanovite Movement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">In August 1935 a Donbas miner called Alexei Stakhanov reportedly cut 102 tonnes of coal in a single six-hour shift &mdash; fourteen times the normal quota. Soviet propaganda turned him into a national hero. Workers who beat their norms were named <dfn class="term" data-def="Soviet workers from 1935 onwards held up as model record-breakers; their published feats were used to raise official labour norms across whole industries.">Stakhanovites</dfn>, given medals, bonuses, holidays in the Crimea and articles in <em>Pravda</em>. The movement was quickly used as a political lever: production norms were raised across whole industries on the basis of what the &lsquo;hero&rsquo; had achieved. Stakhanov&rsquo;s record itself had been carefully stage-managed &mdash; a team of helpers cleared the rubble, fitted the props and moved the coal &mdash; but the propaganda effect was real. Stakhanovites were resented on the shop floor by ordinary workers whose output expectations rose without a corresponding rise in pay.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Students often write that the Five-Year Plans raised living standards. The reality was more uneven. Real wages fell sharply during the First Plan; bread was rationed until 1935; housing in the new industrial cities was overcrowded and primitive. Working-class families gained free education, free healthcare, free childcare and the chance of upward mobility into engineering or managerial posts &mdash; the historian Sheila Fitzpatrick has stressed this generational mobility as a real social gain. But take-home pay only began to recover slowly after 1935, and food consumption per head was still below 1928 levels into the late 1930s. Strong answers separate the headline industrial growth from the lived experience of Soviet workers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">The Third Plan and the Verdict</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">The Third Five-Year Plan opened in 1938 with rearmament its overriding priority. Defence spending consumed nearly a third of the state budget by 1940. Civilian projects were squeezed; the Terror had decimated the engineering and managerial cadres just as production quotas rose; output of consumer goods stagnated. The Plan was cut short by Operation Barbarossa in June 1941. By 1941 the USSR was the third largest industrial economy in the world, behind only the United States and Germany. Coal, steel, oil, machine tools and electricity output all dwarfed the 1928 figures. The whole new industrial base east of the Urals was indispensable to the Soviet war effort after 1941. The cost was a generation of suppressed living standards, hundreds of thousands of Gulag deaths in showpiece projects, and a planning system whose distortions endured into the 1980s.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Close this and write from memory: which Plan built Magnitogorsk, what the Second Plan promised but did not deliver, and what year Stakhanov set his record.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The First Plan (1928&ndash;32) built Magnitogorsk, the Dnieper Dam and the Belomor Canal &mdash; coal output roughly doubled and pig iron output rose sharply, with Gulag labour central to the showpiece projects. The Second Plan (1933&ndash;37) promised the &lsquo;Three Goods&rsquo; (food, clothing and housing) but defence spending overrode them after 1935; it also opened the Moscow Metro (1935) and developed industry in the Urals and Siberia. Alexei Stakhanov&rsquo;s record shift was August 1935; it was used to raise norms across whole industries.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n20">Stalin&rsquo;s four reasons for rapid industrialisation were defence against capitalist powers, autarky, the ideological need for an industrial working class, and victory over Bukharin&rsquo;s Right Opposition. The First Five-Year Plan launched in October 1928 with targets set by Gosplan and enforced through the Commissariats and the secret police.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n21">The First Plan (1928&ndash;32) built Magnitogorsk, the Dnieper Dam and the Belomor Canal, doubled coal output and quadrupled pig iron output on official figures &mdash; but real wages fell, Gulag labour built the showpieces, internal passports and labour-discipline laws bound workers to their factories, and 10,000 foreign engineers trained Soviet replacements.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n22">The Second Plan (1933&ndash;37) promised consumer goods but delivered rearmament after 1935; the Stakhanovite movement from August 1935 turned a stage-managed coal record into a national propaganda lever to raise norms. The Third Plan (1938&ndash;41) was cut short by the German invasion, but the new industrial base east of the Urals carried the Soviet war effort.</li>
</ul>
