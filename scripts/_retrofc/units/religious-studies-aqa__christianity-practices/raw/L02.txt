--- DESCRIPTION ---
The meaning of sacrament, infant and believers' baptism, and Holy Communion.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is a Sacrament?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A holy ritual through which believers receive God's grace, regarded as an outward sign of an inward spiritual blessing.">sacrament</dfn> is a sacred ritual through which Christians believe they receive God’s grace. The word comes from the Latin ‘sacramentum’, meaning a solemn oath. Sacraments are outward, visible signs of an inward, spiritual blessing. Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians recognise seven sacraments, while most Protestant churches recognise only two — <dfn class="term" data-def="A Christian ceremony using water as a sign of purification and admission into the Church.">baptism</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A Christian service in which bread and wine are shared in memory of Jesus's Last Supper with his disciples.">Holy Communion</dfn>. These two were specifically commanded by Jesus in the Gospels.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="From memory, state how many sacraments Catholics and Protestants recognise, and name the two that both share.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>A sacrament is an outward sign of inward grace. Roman Catholics recognise seven sacraments, while most Protestants recognise only two — baptism and Holy Communion — because only these were directly instituted by Jesus.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Infant Baptism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A ceremony in which a baby is baptised with water, welcomed into the Church, and cleansed from original sin.">Infant baptism</dfn> is practised by Roman Catholic, Anglican and Methodist churches. During the ceremony, a priest or minister pours water over the baby’s head three times in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Parents and godparents make promises on the child’s behalf to reject evil and turn to Christ.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">For Catholics, infant baptism removes <dfn class="term" data-def="The sinful state that all humans are born into as a result of Adam and Eve's disobedience in the Garden of Eden.">original sin</dfn> — the sinful state inherited from Adam and Eve. The baby receives a white garment symbolising purity and a candle symbolising the light of Christ. Other Christians see it as a way of welcoming the child into the Church community and dedicating them to God. Jesus himself said, “Let the little children come to me, and do not hinder them” (Mark 10:14).</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Baptism of adults or older children who are old enough to declare their own faith, usually by full immersion in water.">Believers’ baptism</dfn> is practised by Baptist and Pentecostal churches. It involves the full immersion of a person who is old enough to make their own declaration of faith. The person is submerged completely under water and then raised up — symbolising dying to their old sinful life and being raised to new life in Christ, mirroring Jesus’s death and resurrection.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Baptists argue that babies cannot understand the commitment being made. They point to the example of Jesus, who was baptised as an adult by John the Baptist in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13–17). Believers’ baptism is a personal choice and a public declaration of faith, making the commitment more meaningful.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Different Beliefs About Baptism</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Christians hold different views on which form of baptism is correct. Those who practise infant baptism believe that God’s grace should be available to everyone from birth and that parents can make promises on a child’s behalf. Those who practise believers’ baptism argue that faith requires a conscious, personal decision and that Jesus was baptised as an adult. Some Christians, such as the Salvation Army and Quakers, do not practise baptism at all — they believe the inward spiritual change matters more than the outward ritual.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Without looking, list three differences between infant baptism and believers' baptism.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Infant baptism and believers’ baptism share the same purpose — entry into the Christian faith — but differ in method and timing. Infant baptism uses pouring of water and relies on parents’ promises; believers’ baptism uses full immersion and requires a personal declaration of faith.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Holy Communion / The Eucharist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A Christian service in which bread and wine are shared to remember Jesus's sacrifice at the Last Supper.">Holy Communion</dfn> (also called the <dfn class="term" data-def="From the Greek for 'thanksgiving', another name for the service of Holy Communion.">Eucharist</dfn>, the Lord’s Supper or the Mass) remembers the <dfn class="term" data-def="The final meal Jesus shared with his disciples before his crucifixion, at which he broke bread and shared wine.">Last Supper</dfn>, the final meal Jesus shared with his disciples before his crucifixion. At this meal, Jesus took bread and said, “This is my body given for you; do this in remembrance of me,” and took wine saying, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:19–20).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Different Celebrations of Holy Communion</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">In the <strong>Roman Catholic Church</strong>, the Mass is the central act of worship. Catholics believe in <dfn class="term" data-def="The Catholic belief that during the Mass, the bread and wine literally become the body and blood of Jesus Christ.">transubstantiation</dfn> — that the bread and wine literally become the body and blood of Christ during the service. Only a priest can celebrate Mass, and the service follows a strict liturgical structure.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">In <strong>Orthodox churches</strong>, the Divine Liturgy is a highly elaborate and symbolic celebration. The bread and wine are also believed to become the body and blood of Christ, though the Orthodox Church does not use the term transubstantiation.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">In <strong>Protestant churches</strong>, Holy Communion is generally seen as a symbolic act of remembrance rather than a literal transformation. Anglicans vary in their interpretation — some lean towards the Catholic view, others see it as symbolic. Methodists and Baptists see the bread and wine as symbols of Jesus’s body and blood, taken in remembrance of his sacrifice.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The <strong>Salvation Army</strong> and <strong>Quakers</strong> do not celebrate Holy Communion. They believe that the spiritual reality of Christ’s presence does not depend on physical elements and that every meal can be sacred.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why Is Holy Communion Important?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Holy Communion is important to Christians for several reasons. It obeys Jesus’s direct command to “do this in remembrance of me.” It brings Christians closer to God and to one another through a shared sacred meal. It reminds believers of Jesus’s sacrifice on the cross and the promise of salvation. For Catholics, receiving the Eucharist is considered essential for spiritual nourishment — Jesus said, “Unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you” (John 6:53).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this and define transubstantiation. Then explain how the Protestant view differs.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The biggest difference in Holy Communion is between Catholics, who believe the bread and wine literally become the body and blood of Christ (transubstantiation), and most Protestants, who see it as a symbolic remembrance of Jesus’s sacrifice at the Last Supper.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n21">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n22">A sacrament is an outward sign of inward grace — Catholics recognise seven, most Protestants recognise two (baptism and Holy Communion).</li><li data-narration-id="n23">Infant baptism (water poured, parents’ promises, removes original sin) contrasts with believers’ baptism (full immersion, personal declaration of faith, mirrors Jesus’s baptism).</li><li data-narration-id="n24">Holy Communion remembers Jesus’s Last Supper — Catholics believe in transubstantiation (literal change), while most Protestants see it as symbolic remembrance.</li></ul>
