<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Arranging Sounds in a DAW</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Once sounds have been created, they need to be arranged into a complete soundtrack that fits the visual media. This is done inside a <dfn class="term" data-def="Digital Audio Workstation — software such as Logic Pro, Ableton Live, or Pro Tools used for recording, editing, mixing, and mastering audio.">DAW</dfn> (Digital Audio Workstation). The arrangement stage is where individual sounds — Foley, ambience, dialogue, underscore, and spot effects — are placed, timed, and balanced against each other.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">DAW Arrangement Tools</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Modern DAWs provide a powerful set of tools for arranging sounds to picture. You must know the following arrangement tools:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Six Essential DAW Arrangement Tools</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Video file import</strong> — the DAW can import a video file so the sound designer sees the visuals directly in the timeline. This allows precise synchronisation of audio to picture.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Audio import</strong> — pre-recorded audio files (field recordings, effects library sounds, dialogue takes) are imported into the project and placed on tracks in the timeline.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>MIDI import</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="Musical Instrument Digital Interface — a protocol that allows electronic instruments and computers to exchange performance data such as note, velocity, and duration.">MIDI</dfn> data from external sources can be imported and assigned to virtual instruments within the DAW, allowing synthesised sounds to be arranged alongside audio.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Audio and MIDI editing</strong> — clips can be cut, moved, copied, trimmed, faded, time-stretched, and pitch-shifted. MIDI notes can be quantised (snapped to a grid), transposed, or velocity-edited.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Layering</strong> — multiple sounds are stacked on separate tracks to build a rich, complex soundtrack. For example, a storm scene might layer wind ambience, rain Foley, thunder spot effects, and a tense underscore across four or more tracks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Automation</strong> — <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of recording parameter changes over time within a DAW, such as gradual volume fades or filter sweeps, so they play back automatically.">Automation</dfn> records parameter changes over time — for example, gradually fading the ambience volume down as dialogue begins, or sweeping a filter across a synthesised pad. Automation lanes are drawn directly onto tracks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Markers</strong> — reference points placed on the timeline to label important moments (e.g. “Car crash — 01:23”, “Dialogue starts — 02:05”). Markers help the sound designer navigate a long project and ensure key events are hit precisely.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list the six DAW arrangement tools from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The six DAW arrangement tools you must know are: <strong>video file import</strong>, <strong>audio/MIDI import</strong>, <strong>audio/MIDI editing</strong>, <strong>layering</strong>, <strong>automation</strong>, and <strong>markers</strong>. Each plays a specific role in building a synchronised soundtrack.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Tempo Changes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="The speed of a piece of music, measured in beats per minute (BPM).">Tempo</dfn> changes are used when the pace of the music needs to match changes in the visual action. A chase scene might require an accelerating tempo, while a reflective moment might need a gradual slowing down. In a DAW, tempo changes can be programmed into the tempo map, and all MIDI tracks automatically follow the new tempo.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Sound Arrangement Concepts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Arranging sounds for media is not just about timing — it involves creative decisions about how sounds work together to support the visual story.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Texture</strong> refers to how many layers of sound are present and how they interact. A thin texture (one or two sounds) creates a sense of isolation or stillness. A thick texture (many layered sounds) creates richness or chaos.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Mood</strong> is the emotional feeling the soundtrack creates. Major keys and warm timbres suggest happiness; minor keys and dissonant harmonies suggest tension or sadness. The sound designer chooses and arranges sounds to match the intended mood of each scene.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Style</strong> refers to the genre or aesthetic of the soundtrack. A period drama might use orchestral underscore, while a modern thriller might use electronic textures and processed effects.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Impact</strong> is the power or force of a sound moment. High-impact sounds (a loud explosion, a sudden musical hit) grab the audience’s attention. Low-impact sounds (a quiet ambient wash) sit in the background. Contrast between high and low impact creates drama.</p>



<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Diegetic vs Non-Diegetic Sound</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">One of the most important concepts in sound arrangement for media is the distinction between <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound that originates from within the world of the story — characters can hear it. Examples include dialogue, a radio playing, or a car horn.">diegetic</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Sound that exists outside the world of the story — only the audience hears it. Examples include underscore, voice-over narration, and dramatic sound effects.">non-diegetic</dfn> sound.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Diegetic sound</strong> comes from within the world of the story. The characters can hear it. Examples include dialogue between characters, a radio playing music in a room, traffic outside a window, or a phone ringing. Diegetic sound helps establish the reality of the scene.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Non-diegetic sound</strong> exists outside the story world. Only the audience hears it. Examples include underscore music, voice-over narration, and dramatic sound effects added for impact. Non-diegetic sound guides the audience’s emotions without the characters being aware of it.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: give two examples of diegetic and two examples of non-diegetic sound.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Diegetic sound</strong> = characters can hear it (dialogue, a TV in the room, footsteps). <strong>Non-diegetic sound</strong> = only the audience hears it (underscore, voice-over, dramatic effects). Some sounds can shift between the two — for example, music playing on a character’s headphones (diegetic) that transitions into a full orchestral arrangement (non-diegetic).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Exporting the Final Audio</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Once the arrangement is complete, the final step is exporting. The sound designer must choose the correct <dfn class="term" data-def="The file type and encoding used to save audio or video, each with different quality, compression, and compatibility characteristics.">format</dfn> for the intended use:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Export Formats Explained</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>MP4</strong> — a compressed video format widely used for web and mobile delivery. Audio is embedded within the video file.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>MOV</strong> — Apple’s video format, commonly used in professional video editing workflows. Supports high-quality codecs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>MP3</strong> — a compressed audio format. Smaller file size but some quality is lost due to lossy compression. Suitable for podcasts, web audio, and drafts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>WAV</strong> — an uncompressed audio format. Full quality with no data loss, but large file sizes. Industry standard for professional delivery.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>AIF</strong> (AIFF) — Apple’s uncompressed audio format. Equivalent quality to WAV, used mainly on Mac systems.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n33">The final process is the <strong>render of audio to video</strong> — combining the finished audio mix with the video file into a single exported media file. The DAW or video editing software bounces (exports) the audio and embeds it into the video container (MP4 or MOV).</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n34" data-revision-tip="Close this and name the five export formats and state which are compressed and which are uncompressed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Compressed formats:</strong> MP3 (audio, lossy), MP4 (video, compressed), MOV (video, compressed). <strong>Uncompressed formats:</strong> WAV (audio, full quality), AIF (audio, full quality). Always use WAV or AIF for professional delivery; MP3 for web or draft use.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n37">DAW arrangement tools include video import, audio/MIDI import and editing, layering, automation, and markers — all essential for synchronising sound to picture.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Diegetic sound comes from within the story world (characters hear it); non-diegetic sound is only heard by the audience (underscore, voice-over).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Export formats include compressed (MP3, MP4, MOV) and uncompressed (WAV, AIF) — the choice depends on the intended delivery platform and quality requirements.</li></ul>
