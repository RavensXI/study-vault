<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Truth About Anita</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">As Meena matures through her illness and Nanima’s visit, she begins to see Anita clearly for the first time. The glamour fades. Anita’s boldness, which once seemed so thrilling, is revealed as desperation. Her cruelty, which Meena had tolerated as the price of friendship, is exposed as a pattern of manipulation and control. Most damagingly, Anita’s association with Sam Lowbridge and her willingness to participate in racist behaviour forces Meena to confront an unbearable truth: the person she admired most does not see her as an equal.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Syal structures this revelation carefully. It is not a single dramatic moment but a gradual accumulation of evidence. Meena pieces together incidents that she previously overlooked or excused — Anita’s casual racist comments, her willingness to humiliate Meena for the approval of others, her fundamental inability to see Meena as anything more than a convenient follower. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A gradual recognition or understanding of something previously hidden or denied, often accompanied by emotional pain.">dawning realisation</dfn> is all the more powerful because Meena has invested so much in the friendship.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: is Meena's rejection of Anita sudden or gradual? Explain what enables her to reinterpret past events.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena’s disillusionment with Anita is a gradual process, not a sudden revelation. Syal shows how Meena’s growing maturity allows her to reinterpret events she previously accepted, recognising the racism and manipulation that were always present in the friendship.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Meena’s Rejection of Anita</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">The moment Meena finally rejects Anita is one of the novel’s most significant scenes. After years of following, accommodating, and suppressing her own identity, Meena walks away. This is not an act of anger but of <dfn class="term" data-def="The quality of being true to one's own personality, spirit, or character, regardless of external pressures.">self-assertion</dfn> — a quiet but decisive choice to stop seeking validation from someone who will never truly offer it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Syal presents this rejection as the climax of Meena’s coming-of-age journey. By rejecting Anita, Meena is not rejecting Tollington or English culture — she is rejecting the idea that belonging requires the erasure of her own identity. She chooses to define herself on her own terms rather than through Anita’s approval. The line “If you can’t fit in, you might as well stand out” captures this shift: Meena stops trying to disappear into the crowd and starts valuing what makes her different.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">While Meena moves forward, Anita remains trapped. She does not have the family support, the education, or the self-awareness to escape Tollington’s limitations. Syal does not condemn Anita — instead, she presents her with a kind of sorrowful understanding. Anita is a product of her environment: the neglect, the poverty, the lack of aspiration that defines the Rutter family. Her fate underlines the novel’s argument that class and deprivation are as destructive as racism.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">The contrast between Meena’s trajectory and Anita’s is one of the novel’s most poignant elements. Both girls started from positions of disadvantage, but Meena has something Anita does not: a loving family, a rich cultural heritage, and the intellectual ability to imagine a different future.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Scholarship and Leaving Tollington</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The novel ends with Meena winning a <dfn class="term" data-def="A financial award given to a student based on academic achievement, enabling them to attend a school they might not otherwise afford.">scholarship</dfn> to a prestigious school and leaving Tollington. This departure is both a triumph and a loss. Meena is escaping the limitations of the village, but she is also leaving behind the only community she has ever known. The ending is bittersweet: Meena is moving towards a future full of possibility, but she is doing so by leaving a place that, for all its flaws, shaped who she is.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Syal uses the departure to resolve the novel’s central question about belonging. Meena does not have to choose between being Indian and being English — she can be both, and she can find belonging in new places, on her own terms. The scholarship represents the meritocratic promise of education: that talent and hard work can overcome the barriers of race and class.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: how does the novel end for Meena — what does she accept, and what does the scholarship symbolise?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena’s departure from Tollington resolves the novel’s central tension. She does not reject either culture but learns to embrace both. The scholarship symbolises the power of education to transcend the limitations of race and class, while the act of leaving represents growth, not abandonment.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Growing Up: The Novel’s Resolution</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">As a <dfn class="term" data-def="A coming-of-age novel that follows a young person's moral and psychological growth from childhood into maturity.">Bildungsroman</dfn>, the novel traces a clear arc: from innocence (the early comic chapters) through experience (the racism, the betrayal, the illness) to maturity (the rejection of Anita and the scholarship). Meena ends the novel as a fundamentally different person from the girl who stole sweets from Mr Ormerod’s shop. She has learned that belonging cannot be earned by pretending to be someone you are not.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The ending is optimistic but not naïve. Syal does not pretend that racism has been defeated or that Meena’s future will be easy. The world outside Tollington will present its own challenges. But Meena now has something she lacked at the start: a secure sense of who she is. She knows her heritage, she values her family, and she no longer needs Anita’s approval to feel that she belongs.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The retrospective narration — the adult Meena looking back — gives the ending an additional layer of meaning. We are hearing this story from someone who has survived and thrived. The act of writing the novel (Syal writing her own story) is itself an assertion of identity and belonging: “This is also my home.”</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Meena’s rejection of Anita represents her rejection of the need to belong on someone else’s terms — a pivotal moment in her coming of age.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The scholarship and departure from Tollington resolve the identity conflict: Meena embraces both her Indian heritage and her English upbringing.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The ending is optimistic but realistic — Syal does not pretend racism is defeated, but shows that a secure sense of identity enables Meena to face the future with confidence.</li></ul>
