<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Tollington: Meena’s World</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">The novel opens with nine-year-old Meena Kumar living in Tollington, a small working-class village in the Black Country. Tollington is a place where everyone knows everyone, gossip travels fast, and nothing much happens. For Meena, it is both home and prison — she loves the freedom of the yard and the fields, but she is acutely aware that she is different from the other children. She is the only Indian child in the village, and her family are the only non-white residents.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Syal paints Tollington with affection and sharp observation. The village is populated by memorable characters — the <dfn class="term" data-def="A busybody or nosy person who watches and comments on everything that happens in the neighbourhood.">nosy neighbours</dfn>, the struggling families, the men who drink at the pub while the women hold everything together. It is a community defined by its routines, its hierarchies, and its unspoken rules about who belongs and who does not.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name three features of Tollington that make it a microcosm of 1960s working-class England.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Tollington functions as a microcosm of 1960s working-class England. Its insularity, economic decline, and social hierarchies reflect the wider national tensions around immigration, class, and identity that Syal explores through Meena’s eyes.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Kumar Family</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Meena’s parents, known as <dfn class="term" data-def="Punjabi terms for mother and father, used by Meena to address her parents throughout the novel.">Mama and Papa</dfn>, are educated, dignified, and deeply committed to maintaining their Punjabi heritage. Papa works at a factory despite being overqualified, reflecting the reality that many South Asian immigrants faced — professional qualifications from India were often not recognised in Britain. Mama is warm, creative, and culturally proud, filling their home with Indian cooking, music, and traditions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The Kumar home is a world apart from the rest of Tollington. Inside, there is colour, warmth, spice, and laughter. Outside, there is the grey, fading English village. This contrast between the domestic interior and the external world is one of Syal’s most important structural devices. Meena moves constantly between these two worlds, never fully at ease in either.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Although the Kumars are the only Indian family in Tollington, they are part of a wider network of South Asian families who gather regularly for social events. The “<dfn class="term" data-def="A group of Indian family friends who are not biological relatives but are given the honorary title of 'Auntie' and 'Uncle', reflecting the communal nature of South Asian culture.">Aunties</dfn>” — family friends given honorary familial titles — represent the cultural safety net that sustains Meena’s parents. Their gatherings are full of food, stories, and shared memories of India. For the adults, these events are a lifeline. For Meena, they are sometimes an embarrassment — she sees them through English eyes and cringes at how “different” they seem.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This tension is comic but also poignant. Meena does not yet understand that what she finds embarrassing is actually a source of strength and resilience. Syal uses the Aunties to show the richness of the culture that Meena takes for granted.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Meena as Narrator</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Meena is a vivid, unreliable, and deeply engaging narrator. She is imaginative, impulsive, and prone to exaggeration — “I’m not lying, I’m just telling a story.” This line, delivered early in the novel, immediately establishes Meena as someone who reshapes reality to suit herself. She is a natural storyteller, but her stories are also a coping mechanism — a way to make her life more exciting and to bridge the gap between who she is and who she wants to be.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<p data-narration-id="n12">Syal uses Meena’s child perspective to create dramatic irony. Meena often misreads situations — she does not understand the racism directed at her family, or the dysfunction in Anita’s home life — but the adult reader can see what the child cannot. This <dfn class="term" data-def="When the audience or reader understands more about a situation than the characters involved, creating tension or humour.">dramatic irony</dfn> is central to the novel’s emotional power.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: what two perspectives does Meena's retrospective narration create, and what effect does this have?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena’s retrospective narration creates a dual perspective: the child who lives the events and the adult who understands them. The gap between these two voices produces the novel’s distinctive blend of comedy and emotional depth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Meena’s Desire to Fit In</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">From the opening pages, Meena’s deepest wish is to belong. She watches the white children of Tollington with envy and longing: “I wanted to be them. I wanted their life.” She sees their freedom, their confidence, their easy belonging to the village — and she wants it desperately. This desire drives her towards Anita Rutter, who represents everything Meena thinks she lacks: boldness, rebellion, and an unquestioned place in the community.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Early Comic Episodes</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">The early chapters are full of comic set pieces that establish Meena’s character. She steals sweets from Mr Ormerod’s shop and lies about it. She tells outrageous stories to get attention. She navigates the social minefield of the village with a mixture of bravado and desperation. These episodes are funny, but they also reveal Meena’s insecurity — her need to perform, to be noticed, to be accepted.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Syal’s comic writing serves a serious purpose. The humour draws the reader into Meena’s world and makes her sympathetic, so that when the darker themes of racism and betrayal emerge later, the emotional impact is all the greater. The early comedy also reflects the innocence of childhood — a state that the novel will gradually strip away.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n18">The opening section of the novel establishes the key dynamics that will drive the story: Meena’s position between two cultures, her hunger for belonging, and the small, enclosed world of Tollington where every action has consequences.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Tollington is a microcosm of 1960s working-class England — insular, declining, and defined by unspoken social rules about who belongs.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Meena’s dual narrative voice (child living events, adult understanding them) creates the novel’s distinctive blend of humour and emotional depth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Meena’s overwhelming desire to fit in drives her towards Anita and sets up the central conflict between belonging and identity.</li></ul>
