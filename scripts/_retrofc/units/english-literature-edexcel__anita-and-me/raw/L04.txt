<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Racism Comes Into the Open</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">In the first half of the novel, the racism Meena encounters is mostly subtle — whispered comments, awkward silences, the feeling of being watched. But as the story progresses, Syal strips away this veneer of politeness. The racism becomes explicit, public, and threatening. The turning point is Sam Lowbridge’s speech at the village fete, where he declares “We are the British” and openly attacks the presence of immigrants in Tollington.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This moment is devastating for Meena because it shatters her illusion of belonging. She has spent the novel trying to fit in, trying to be accepted as one of Tollington’s children. Sam’s speech makes it brutally clear that, in the eyes of people like him, she will never belong. The phrase “We are the British” deliberately excludes Meena and her family — it draws a line between “us” and “them” based entirely on race.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what does Sam Lowbridge say at the village fete, whose rhetoric does it echo, and how does it affect Meena?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Sam Lowbridge’s declaration “We are the British” at the village fete is the novel’s most explicit moment of racism. It echoes Enoch Powell’s <dfn class="term" data-def="Language or ideas designed to provoke fear, anger, or hatred, often used by politicians to stir up prejudice against minority groups.">inflammatory rhetoric</dfn> and forces Meena to confront the reality that her race makes her an outsider in the community she calls home.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Mines Closing</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Tollington’s economic decline is a crucial backdrop to the rising racial tension. The coal mines and factories that sustained the Black Country for generations are closing, and the community feels abandoned. Jobs are disappearing, prospects are shrinking, and there is a pervasive sense of <dfn class="term" data-def="A feeling of bitterness or anger caused by a sense of being treated unfairly, often directed at those perceived as taking something that belongs to others.">resentment</dfn> that needs a target.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Syal is careful to show that racism does not exist in a vacuum. Characters like Sam Lowbridge are products of economic deprivation — their anger at losing their livelihoods gets redirected towards immigrant families who are seen as competition for scarce resources. This does not excuse racism, but it explains the social conditions that allow it to flourish. The novel asks the reader to understand how structural inequality breeds prejudice.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><dfn class="term" data-def="Blaming a person or group for problems they did not cause, usually to deflect attention from the real source of the issue.">Scapegoating</dfn> is a key concept in the novel. When communities face hardship, they often look for someone to blame. In Tollington, immigrant families become the scapegoat for problems caused by deindustrialisation and government neglect. Sam’s anger is real — his community is genuinely suffering — but it is misdirected at the Kumars rather than at the economic forces that are actually destroying Tollington.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This pattern — economic hardship breeding racial resentment — was widespread across industrial England in the 1960s and 1970s. Syal uses Tollington as a case study to show how ordinary people can be radicalised by poverty and political rhetoric. The closing of the mines is not just a plot detail; it is the engine driving the novel’s racial conflict.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Meena’s Illness</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">A major turning point in the novel is Meena’s leg injury, which confines her to bed for an extended period. This enforced stillness is transformative. For the first time, Meena is removed from the social pressures of Tollington — from Anita, from the need to perform, from the constant effort of fitting in. The illness creates a <dfn class="term" data-def="A pause or break from normal activity that allows a character to reflect, grow, or gain new perspective.">liminal space</dfn> in which Meena can think, reflect, and begin to see her life more clearly.</p>

<!-- DIAGRAM -->

<p data-narration-id="n12">During her recovery, Meena starts to question the friendship with Anita and to recognise the racism she has been ignoring or minimising. The illness functions as a metaphor for the painful process of growing up — the shedding of childhood innocence and the development of a more mature, honest understanding of the world.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain: what happens during Meena's illness that changes her, and what does she begin to see differently?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Meena’s illness is both a literal event and a metaphor. It removes her from Anita’s influence, forces reflection, and marks the beginning of her emotional and psychological maturation. After the illness, Meena begins to see Tollington — and herself — more honestly.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Nanima’s Visit</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">The arrival of <dfn class="term" data-def="Meena's maternal grandmother, who visits from India and represents the deep cultural and familial roots that Meena has been neglecting.">Nanima</dfn> (Meena’s maternal grandmother) during Meena’s illness is one of the novel’s most emotionally significant moments. Nanima does not speak English, but she communicates through touch, food, stories, and the sheer force of her presence. She represents everything Meena has been running from — her Indian heritage, her family history, the Punjabi language, the deep roots of a culture that stretches back generations.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Nanima’s visit coincides with Meena’s period of reflection, and the combination is powerful. For the first time, Meena begins to see her heritage not as an embarrassment but as a source of strength and identity. Nanima’s stories about Partition — the traumatic division of India and Pakistan in 1947 — give Meena a sense of her family’s history and resilience that she has never had before.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n17">The middle section of the novel sees Meena in full <dfn class="term" data-def="A period of uncertainty and confusion about one's sense of self, values, or place in the world.">identity crisis</dfn>. She has tried to be English and been rejected. She has neglected her Indian heritage and now feels disconnected from it. She is caught between Sam’s “We are the British” (which excludes her) and her family’s quiet insistence that “This is also my home” (which claims belonging on different terms). Meena confesses: “I did not have a name for what I felt.”</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">This line captures the essence of the novel’s exploration of cultural duality. Meena’s confusion is not simply about choosing between two cultures — it is about finding a way to be both. Nanima’s visit begins to show her that this is possible: that she can be Indian and English, that belonging does not require the erasure of one identity for the sake of another.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Sam Lowbridge’s declaration “We are the British” is the novel’s turning point, forcing Meena to confront the racism she has been ignoring and shattering her illusion of belonging.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Meena’s illness creates a structural pause that allows reflection and marks the beginning of her maturation.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Nanima’s visit reconnects Meena with her Indian heritage, beginning the process of resolving her identity crisis by showing that belonging does not require cultural erasure.</li></ul>
