--- DESCRIPTION ---
Obesity, CHD, bone diseases, dental decay, anaemia and type 2 diabetes linked to poor diet.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Overview</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">A poor diet does not just affect your weight — it can cause serious, long-term health conditions that affect the heart, bones, teeth, blood and the body's ability to manage blood sugar. This lesson focuses on six diet-related health risks: <dfn class="term" data-def="A medical condition where a person has a BMI of 30 or above, caused by consuming more energy than the body uses over a sustained period.">obesity</dfn>, coronary heart disease (CHD), bone diseases (rickets and osteoporosis), dental decay, iron deficiency <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition in which the blood does not contain enough healthy red blood cells or haemoglobin, often caused by a lack of iron in the diet.">anaemia</dfn> and type 2 diabetes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">You need to understand the <strong>cause</strong> of each condition, the <strong>dietary factors</strong> that contribute to it, and the <strong>groups most at risk</strong>. Link specific nutrients to specific conditions with precision.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Obesity</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Obesity occurs when a person's <dfn class="term" data-def="Body Mass Index — a measure calculated by dividing a person's weight in kilograms by the square of their height in metres. A BMI of 30 or above is classified as obese.">BMI</dfn> reaches 30 or above. It is caused by a sustained <dfn class="term" data-def="A state where the energy consumed from food and drink exceeds the energy the body uses through BMR and physical activity, leading to weight gain over time.">energy imbalance</dfn> — when energy intake from food consistently exceeds the energy the body uses through basal metabolic rate and physical activity. The excess energy is stored as body fat.</p>




<p data-narration-id="n6">Obesity significantly increases the risk of several serious conditions:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong> — excess body fat makes cells resistant to insulin</li>
<li><strong>Coronary heart disease (CHD)</strong> — obesity raises blood cholesterol and blood pressure</li>
<li><strong>Certain cancers</strong> — including bowel, breast and kidney cancer</li>
<li><strong>Joint problems</strong> — excess weight puts strain on weight-bearing joints such as knees and hips, increasing the risk of osteoarthritis</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does BMI stand for? List each part from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Obesity is defined as a <strong>BMI of 30 or above</strong>. It is caused by <strong>energy in exceeding energy out</strong> over a sustained period. It increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, CHD, certain cancers and joint problems. Prevention focuses on balancing energy intake with physical activity.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Coronary Heart Disease (CHD)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the coronary arteries that supply blood to the heart become narrowed or blocked by a build-up of fatty deposits (atheroma), reducing blood flow and oxygen supply to the heart muscle.">Coronary heart disease</dfn> occurs when fatty deposits build up inside the coronary arteries — the blood vessels that supply the heart muscle with oxygen. Over time, these deposits narrow the arteries, restricting blood flow. If an artery becomes completely blocked, it causes a heart attack.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Two dietary factors are particularly important:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>High Saturated Fat and Cholesterol</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">A diet high in <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of fat found mainly in animal products and some processed foods. Eating too much raises blood cholesterol and increases the risk of heart disease.">saturated fat</dfn> raises blood <dfn class="term" data-def="A waxy, fat-like substance found in the blood. High levels of LDL cholesterol contribute to the build-up of fatty deposits in artery walls, increasing the risk of heart disease.">cholesterol</dfn> levels. When there is too much cholesterol in the blood, it is deposited on the walls of arteries, forming fatty plaques. These plaques narrow the arteries and reduce blood flow to the heart. Saturated fat is found in butter, cheese, fatty cuts of meat, cakes, biscuits and palm oil.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13">To reduce the risk: replace saturated fats with unsaturated fats (such as olive oil and rapeseed oil), eat oily fish twice a week, and increase intake of soluble fibre from oats, beans and lentils — which helps to lower cholesterol.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>High Salt and Blood Pressure</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n14">A diet high in salt causes the body to retain water, which increases blood volume and raises <dfn class="term" data-def="The force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries. Consistently high blood pressure (hypertension) damages artery walls and increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.">blood pressure</dfn>. High blood pressure (hypertension) damages the walls of arteries and makes it easier for fatty deposits to accumulate. Adults should consume no more than <strong>6g of salt per day</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">To reduce salt intake: use herbs and spices for flavour instead of salt, avoid processed foods (which contain hidden salt), and check food labels for sodium content. Around 75% of the salt people eat comes from processed foods rather than salt added at the table.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does CHD stand for? List each part from memory.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — CHD Pathway</div>
<p>High saturated fat → raised blood cholesterol → fatty deposits in artery walls → narrowed arteries → reduced blood flow → heart attack. High salt → water retention → raised blood pressure → damaged artery walls → increased risk of CHD and stroke.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Bone Diseases: Rickets and Osteoporosis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Two important bone conditions are linked to dietary deficiencies:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Rickets</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="A childhood condition caused by a deficiency of vitamin D and/or calcium, leading to soft, weak bones that can bend and become deformed.">Rickets</dfn> is a childhood condition caused by a lack of <strong>vitamin D</strong> and/or <strong>calcium</strong>. Without enough of these nutrients, bones cannot mineralise properly and remain soft and weak. This can cause bowed legs, bone pain and poor growth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20">Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium from food. The body makes vitamin D when skin is exposed to sunlight, but dietary sources are also important — especially oily fish, eggs, fortified cereals and fortified margarine. Children who spend little time outdoors, or who have darker skin (which produces less vitamin D from sunlight), are at greater risk.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Osteoporosis</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where bones become fragile and brittle due to loss of bone density, most common in elderly people, particularly post-menopausal women.">Osteoporosis</dfn> is a condition that mainly affects elderly people, particularly post-menopausal women. Bones gradually lose density and become fragile and brittle, making fractures much more likely — especially in the hip, wrist and spine.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">A lifetime of insufficient calcium and vitamin D intake contributes to osteoporosis. Calcium-rich foods include milk, cheese, yoghurt, canned fish with bones (sardines), and green leafy vegetables (broccoli, kale). Weight-bearing exercise also helps maintain bone density.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Dental Decay</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><dfn class="term" data-def="The destruction of tooth enamel caused by acids produced when bacteria in the mouth feed on sugars from food and drink.">Dental decay</dfn> (also called dental caries or tooth decay) occurs when bacteria in the mouth feed on sugars from food and drink. As the bacteria break down the sugars, they produce acid. This acid attacks the hard outer layer of the tooth — the <strong>enamel</strong> — gradually dissolving it. Over time, this creates holes (cavities) in the teeth.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25">The more frequently sugary foods and drinks are consumed, the more often the teeth are under acid attack. Sticky, sugary foods are particularly harmful because they cling to the teeth for longer. The risk increases with the <strong>frequency</strong> of sugar intake, not just the total amount.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Describe the process for pathway of dental decay step by step.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The pathway of dental decay: <strong>sugars in food → bacteria feed on sugars → acid produced → enamel dissolves → cavities form</strong>. It is the <strong>frequency</strong> of sugar consumption that matters most, not just the quantity. Limiting sugary snacks between meals reduces the number of acid attacks on teeth.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Iron Deficiency Anaemia</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">Iron deficiency anaemia occurs when the body does not have enough <strong>iron</strong> to produce adequate amounts of <dfn class="term" data-def="The protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Iron is an essential component of haemoglobin.">haemoglobin</dfn> — the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen around the body. Without enough haemoglobin, cells do not receive sufficient oxygen, leading to tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness and difficulty concentrating.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Who Is Most at Risk?</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Teenage girls</strong> are at particular risk because they lose iron through menstruation. If their diet does not replace this lost iron, anaemia can develop. Girls aged 11–18 need <strong>14.8mg of iron per day</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Vegetarians and vegans</strong> are also at increased risk because the most easily absorbed form of iron (haem iron) comes from animal sources such as red meat, liver and kidney. Plant-based iron (non-haem iron) from spinach, lentils, beans and fortified cereals is less readily absorbed by the body.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Vitamin C aids iron absorption.</strong> Eating vitamin C-rich foods (such as oranges, peppers or tomatoes) at the same meal as iron-rich foods significantly increases the amount of non-haem iron the body absorbs. This is especially important for vegetarians and vegans.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you name all 2 forms from memory?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Iron Absorption</div>
<p>There are two forms of dietary iron: <strong>haem iron</strong> (from animal sources — easily absorbed) and <strong>non-haem iron</strong> (from plant sources — less easily absorbed). Consuming <strong>vitamin C</strong> alongside non-haem iron significantly improves absorption. Tea and coffee can <em>reduce</em> iron absorption.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Type 2 Diabetes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34"><dfn class="term" data-def="A long-term condition where the body either does not produce enough insulin or the body's cells do not respond properly to insulin, leading to high blood sugar levels.">Type 2 diabetes</dfn> is a condition where the body's cells stop responding properly to <dfn class="term" data-def="A hormone produced by the pancreas that controls blood sugar levels by allowing glucose to enter cells for energy.">insulin</dfn> — the hormone that controls blood sugar levels. This is called insulin resistance. As a result, glucose remains in the blood instead of entering cells for energy, causing blood sugar levels to rise dangerously.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n35">Type 2 diabetes is strongly linked to:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n36">
<li><strong>Obesity</strong> — excess body fat, particularly around the abdomen, increases insulin resistance</li>
<li><strong>A diet high in sugar and refined carbohydrates</strong> — causes repeated blood sugar spikes that strain the body's insulin response over time</li>
<li><strong>Physical inactivity</strong> — exercise helps cells use glucose more effectively</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n37">Type 2 diabetes can be managed and sometimes reversed through dietary changes, weight loss and regular exercise. Key dietary strategies include reducing sugar and refined carbohydrate intake, eating more wholegrain foods (which release energy slowly), increasing fibre intake, and maintaining a healthy weight through energy balance.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n38" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define type 2 diabetes in your own words.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Type 2 diabetes is caused by <strong>insulin resistance</strong> — the body's cells stop responding to insulin, so blood sugar stays high. It is strongly linked to <strong>obesity</strong>, <strong>high sugar diets</strong> and <strong>inactivity</strong>. Management includes diet modification, weight loss and exercise.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Obesity</strong> (BMI ≥30) is caused by energy in exceeding energy out, increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes, CHD, cancers and joint problems. <strong>CHD</strong> results from high saturated fat (raising cholesterol) and high salt (raising blood pressure).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42"><strong>Rickets</strong> (children, vitamin D/calcium deficiency, soft bones) and <strong>osteoporosis</strong> (elderly, brittle bones) are both prevented by adequate calcium and vitamin D. <strong>Dental decay</strong> is caused by frequent sugar consumption feeding bacteria that produce enamel-destroying acid.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n43"><strong>Iron deficiency anaemia</strong> results from insufficient iron for haemoglobin production — teenage girls and vegetarians are most at risk, and vitamin C aids absorption. <strong>Type 2 diabetes</strong> is linked to obesity and high sugar intake, causing insulin resistance.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n39">When answering questions on diet-related health risks, always use the correct <strong>pathway</strong> — for example, “high saturated fat → raised blood cholesterol → fatty deposits in arteries → narrowed arteries → CHD.” Examiners reward precise chains of cause and effect, not vague statements like “eating too much fat is bad for you.” If asked to ‘evaluate’ or ‘discuss,’ consider both the dietary cause <em>and</em> how it can be managed or prevented. Always name specific nutrients, not just food groups.</p>
