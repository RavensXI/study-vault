<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Introduction to Fats and Carbohydrates</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Fats and carbohydrates are two of the three <dfn class="term" data-def="Nutrients needed in large amounts by the body — protein, carbohydrates, and fat.">macronutrients</dfn>. Together with protein, they provide the energy your body needs to function. Both topics frequently appear in exam questions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding the different types of fats and carbohydrates, where they come from, and what they do in the body is essential for answering both short and extended-response questions confidently.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">What Are Fats?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A macronutrient that provides the most concentrated source of energy at 9 kcal per gram. Also called lipids.">Fat</dfn> is the most energy-dense macronutrient, providing <strong>9 kcal per gram</strong> — more than double the energy of protein or carbohydrate (both 4 kcal/g). Fats are also called <dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific term for fats and oils — fats are solid at room temperature, oils are liquid.">lipids</dfn>. The key difference between fats and oils is their state at room temperature: fats are solid (e.g. butter, lard) and oils are liquid (e.g. olive oil, sunflower oil).</p>




<p data-narration-id="n6">Government guidelines recommend that <strong>no more than 35% of total energy</strong> should come from fat, and <strong>saturated fat should be less than 11%</strong> of total energy intake.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What percentage is given for fat provides 9 kcal per gram?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Fat provides <strong>9 kcal per gram</strong> — more than twice the energy of protein or carbohydrate. Government advice: total fat <35% of energy, saturated fat <11%.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Types of Fat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Fats are classified by their chemical structure — specifically, whether or not their carbon chains contain <dfn class="term" data-def="A covalent bond where two atoms share two pairs of electrons — in fatty acids, double bonds between carbon atoms determine whether a fat is saturated or unsaturated.">double bonds</dfn> between carbon atoms. This determines whether a fat is saturated or unsaturated.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Saturated Fat</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of fat with no double bonds between carbon atoms in its fatty acid chains — solid at room temperature and linked to raised cholesterol.">Saturated fat</dfn> has <strong>no double bonds</strong> between the carbon atoms in its fatty acid chains. The carbon chain is fully “saturated” with hydrogen atoms. Saturated fats are typically <strong>solid at room temperature</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Sources:</strong> Fatty meat, butter, lard, hard cheese, cream, cakes, biscuits, pastries, and coconut oil.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Health link:</strong> Eating too much saturated fat raises levels of LDL (“bad”) cholesterol in the blood, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD). This is why the government recommends keeping saturated fat below 11% of total energy intake.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Unsaturated Fat — Monounsaturated and Polyunsaturated</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of fat with one double bond between carbon atoms in its fatty acid chain — liquid at room temperature, found in olive oil and avocados.">Monounsaturated fat</dfn> has <strong>one double bond</strong> in its fatty acid chain. It is liquid at room temperature but may solidify when chilled. <strong>Sources:</strong> Olive oil, rapeseed oil, avocados, almonds, and peanuts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A type of fat with two or more double bonds between carbon atoms in its fatty acid chains — liquid at room temperature, includes omega-3 and omega-6.">Polyunsaturated fat</dfn> has <strong>two or more double bonds</strong> in its fatty acid chains. It is always liquid at room temperature. <strong>Sources:</strong> Sunflower oil, corn oil, seeds, walnuts, and oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Health link:</strong> Unsaturated fats can help lower LDL cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart disease when they replace saturated fats in the diet. Polyunsaturated fats include essential fatty acids such as <strong>omega-3</strong> (found in oily fish) and <strong>omega-6</strong> (found in vegetable oils), which the body cannot make itself.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does LDL stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Saturated vs Unsaturated</div>
  <p><strong>Saturated:</strong> No double bonds, solid at room temperature, raises LDL cholesterol. <strong>Monounsaturated:</strong> One double bond, liquid at room temperature. <strong>Polyunsaturated:</strong> Two or more double bonds, liquid at room temperature, includes omega-3 and omega-6.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Functions of Fat</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Fat performs several important roles in the body. You need to know these for the exam because questions often ask you to explain <em>why</em> fat is needed, not just what it is.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Energy:</strong> Fat is the body’s most concentrated energy source (9 kcal/g). It provides a long-lasting energy reserve. <strong>Insulation:</strong> A layer of fat beneath the skin (subcutaneous fat) helps maintain body temperature by reducing heat loss. <strong>Protection:</strong> Fat cushions and protects vital organs such as the kidneys, heart, and liver from physical damage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Fat-soluble vitamins:</strong> Vitamins A, D, E, and K are <dfn class="term" data-def="Vitamins that dissolve in fat and require dietary fat for absorption — vitamins A, D, E, and K.">fat-soluble vitamins</dfn>. This means the body needs dietary fat to absorb and transport them. Without enough fat in the diet, a person could become deficient in these vitamins even if they eat foods that contain them.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">What Are Carbohydrates?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="A macronutrient that is the body’s preferred source of energy, providing 4 kcal per gram. Includes sugars, starch, and fibre.">Carbohydrate</dfn> is the body’s preferred and most readily available source of energy, providing <strong>4 kcal per gram</strong>. Government guidelines recommend that approximately <strong>50% of total energy</strong> should come from carbohydrate, with <strong>free sugars making up no more than 5%</strong> of total energy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Carbohydrates are classified into three groups based on their chemical structure: <dfn class="term" data-def="The simplest form of carbohydrate — single sugar units such as glucose, fructose, and galactose.">monosaccharides</dfn> (simple sugars), <dfn class="term" data-def="A carbohydrate made of two monosaccharide units joined together — examples include sucrose, lactose, and maltose.">disaccharides</dfn> (double sugars), and <dfn class="term" data-def="Complex carbohydrates made of many monosaccharide units joined in long chains — examples include starch, glycogen, and dietary fibre.">polysaccharides</dfn> (complex carbohydrates).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Monosaccharides — Simple Sugars</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates — single sugar molecules that cannot be broken down further. The three you need to know are:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Glucose:</strong> Found in fruits, vegetables, and honey. It is the body’s main energy source and circulates in the blood. <strong>Fructose:</strong> Found naturally in fruits and honey — it is the sweetest natural sugar. <strong>Galactose:</strong> Found in milk and dairy products. It combines with glucose to form lactose.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Disaccharides — Double Sugars</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Disaccharides are formed when two monosaccharides join together. The three you need to know are:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Sucrose</strong> (glucose + fructose): Table sugar — found in sugar cane, sugar beet, and added to many processed foods. <strong>Lactose</strong> (glucose + galactose): Milk sugar — found naturally in milk and dairy products. Some people are <dfn class="term" data-def="The inability to digest lactose (milk sugar) due to a lack of the enzyme lactase, causing bloating, cramps, and diarrhoea.">lactose intolerant</dfn>, meaning they lack the enzyme to digest it. <strong>Maltose</strong> (glucose + glucose): Malt sugar — found in malted foods, cereals, and produced during digestion of starch.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Polysaccharides — Complex Carbohydrates</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28">Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharide units. They take longer to digest, which means they release energy <strong>slowly and steadily</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Starch:</strong> The main energy-providing polysaccharide in the diet. Found in potatoes, bread, pasta, rice, cereals, and root vegetables. <strong>Glycogen:</strong> The storage form of glucose in the body, held in the liver and muscles. It is broken down to release glucose when the body needs energy quickly (e.g. during exercise). <strong>Dietary fibre (NSP):</strong> Found in plant cell walls. Not digested by the body but plays a vital role in keeping the digestive system healthy.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n30" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about monosaccharides: Glucose.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Sugar Classification</div>
  <p><strong>Monosaccharides:</strong> Glucose, fructose, galactose (single sugars). <strong>Disaccharides:</strong> Sucrose, lactose, maltose (double sugars). <strong>Polysaccharides:</strong> Starch, glycogen, dietary fibre (complex, slow-release energy).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n31">Dietary Fibre</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><dfn class="term" data-def="The indigestible part of plant foods that aids digestion, helps prevent constipation, and may reduce the risk of bowel cancer and heart disease. Also called non-starch polysaccharide (NSP).">Dietary fibre</dfn> (also called non-starch polysaccharide or NSP) is a type of polysaccharide that the body cannot digest. Despite this, it plays a crucial role in health. The government recommends adults eat <strong>30 g of fibre per day</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Functions of fibre:</strong> It adds bulk to food, making you feel full (which helps prevent overeating). It absorbs water and helps move food through the digestive system, preventing constipation. A high-fibre diet is linked to a reduced risk of bowel cancer, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Sources:</strong> Wholegrain bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta, oats, pulses (lentils, beans, chickpeas), fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds. Choosing <dfn class="term" data-def="Foods made from grain that retains all three parts of the seed — the bran, germ, and endosperm — providing more fibre and nutrients than refined grains.">wholegrain</dfn> versions of bread, rice, and pasta is one of the easiest ways to increase fibre intake.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n35">Free Sugars</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n36"><dfn class="term" data-def="Sugars added to food or drink by the manufacturer, cook, or consumer, plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and fruit juices. Does not include sugars in whole fruit or milk.">Free sugars</dfn> are sugars added to food and drink by the manufacturer, cook, or consumer, plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, and unsweetened fruit juices. They do <strong>not</strong> include sugars found naturally in whole fruit, vegetables, or milk.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37">The government recommends that free sugars should make up <strong>no more than 5% of total energy intake</strong>. Excess free sugar consumption is linked to tooth decay, obesity, and type 2 diabetes. This is why food labels must now show “of which sugars” to help consumers make informed choices.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n38" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 2 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Fibre and Free Sugars</div>
  <p>Adults should aim for <strong>30 g of fibre per day</strong> (from wholegrain, fruit, vegetables, and pulses) and limit <strong>free sugars to less than 5%</strong> of total energy. These two targets work together — choosing wholegrain and whole fruit over refined and sweetened alternatives achieves both.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n39">Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n40"><strong>Fat deficiency:</strong> Too little fat can lead to poor absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), dry skin, poor temperature regulation, and low energy levels. <strong>Fat excess:</strong> Too much fat, especially saturated fat, leads to weight gain, obesity, raised cholesterol, and increased risk of coronary heart disease and type 2 diabetes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Carbohydrate deficiency:</strong> Without enough carbohydrate, the body uses protein and fat for energy instead, which can cause tiredness, poor concentration, ketosis, and muscle wasting. <strong>Carbohydrate excess:</strong> Excess carbohydrate (especially free sugars) is converted to fat and stored, leading to weight gain, obesity, tooth decay, and increased risk of type 2 diabetes.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n43">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n44">Fats provide 9 kcal/g and come in three types: <strong>saturated</strong> (no double bonds, raises cholesterol), <strong>monounsaturated</strong> (one double bond), and <strong>polyunsaturated</strong> (two or more double bonds, includes omega-3 and omega-6).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n45">Carbohydrates are classified as <strong>monosaccharides</strong> (glucose, fructose, galactose), <strong>disaccharides</strong> (sucrose, lactose, maltose), and <strong>polysaccharides</strong> (starch, glycogen, fibre) — complex carbs release energy slowly.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n46">Government targets: fat <35% of energy (saturated <11%), carbohydrate ~50% of energy (free sugars <5%), fibre 30 g/day from wholegrain sources.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n42">In AQA Food Technology questions about fats or carbohydrates, always be specific about the <strong>type</strong> (e.g. saturated vs polyunsaturated, monosaccharide vs polysaccharide). Naming specific food sources and linking them to health effects will push your answer into the higher mark bands. For “discuss” questions, consider both positive roles (energy, insulation, vitamins) and negative effects of excess (CHD, obesity, tooth decay) to show balance.</p>
