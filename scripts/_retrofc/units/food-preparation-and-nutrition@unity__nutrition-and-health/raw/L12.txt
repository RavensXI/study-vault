--- DESCRIPTION ---
Where food comes from, how it is processed and fortified, and the environmental challenges of food production.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Where Does Our Food Come From?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Every ingredient has a journey from <dfn class="term" data-def="The origin or source of a food ingredient, including where and how it was grown, reared, or caught.">food provenance</dfn> to your plate. Understanding where food comes from — and how it is produced — is essential for making informed, sustainable choices. Food is grown (crops, fruit, vegetables), reared (livestock for meat, eggs, dairy), or caught (fish, shellfish).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The way food is produced varies widely. <dfn class="term" data-def="A method of farming that avoids synthetic pesticides, fertilisers, and GM organisms, relying instead on natural methods and crop rotation.">Organic farming</dfn> avoids synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, relying on crop rotation, composting, and natural pest control. It is seen as better for the environment and animal welfare, but produces lower yields and costs more. <dfn class="term" data-def="A farming system using synthetic chemicals and intensive methods to maximise output. Also known as intensive farming.">Conventional farming</dfn> uses chemical inputs to maximise yield, keeping prices lower but raising concerns about chemical residues and environmental damage.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and list three advantages of organic farming and three advantages of conventional farming from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Organic farming</strong>: no synthetic pesticides or fertilisers, better for biodiversity, higher animal welfare standards, but lower yields and higher prices. <strong>Conventional farming</strong>: higher yields, cheaper food, more widely available, but concerns about chemical residues, soil degradation, and lower animal welfare in intensive systems.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="A farming system in which animals are given access to outdoor space and the freedom to exhibit natural behaviours.">Free-range farming</dfn> gives animals access to outdoor space and the freedom to exhibit natural behaviours. <dfn class="term" data-def="A farming method in which large numbers of animals are kept in confined spaces to maximise production. Also called factory farming.">Intensive farming</dfn> (factory farming) confines large numbers of animals in small spaces to maximise output. Free-range products cost more but are considered more ethical. <dfn class="term" data-def="Fishing practices that maintain fish populations and ecosystems for the future, avoiding overfishing and minimising environmental damage.">Sustainable fishing</dfn> aims to maintain fish populations by avoiding overfishing, using methods like line-caught fishing rather than large-scale trawling, and respecting quotas.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Local, Seasonal, and GM Foods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Local food</strong> is produced and sold within a short distance. Advantages: fresher, supports local economy, lower food miles, fewer preservatives needed. Disadvantages: limited variety (especially in winter), may cost more, smaller-scale production.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Seasonal food</strong> is grown and harvested at its natural time of year in a particular region. Advantages: better flavour, cheaper (greater supply), lower environmental impact. Disadvantages: limited availability, consumers accustomed to year-round supply may resist.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><dfn class="term" data-def="Food produced from organisms whose DNA has been altered using genetic engineering techniques to introduce desirable traits.">Genetically modified (GM) food</dfn> has had its DNA altered to introduce desirable traits, such as pest resistance, higher yields, or improved nutritional content. Advantages: can increase food production, reduce pesticide use, improve nutritional value (e.g. golden rice). Disadvantages: concerns about long-term health effects, cross-pollination with wild plants, ethical objections, labelling requirements in the EU/UK.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Environmental Impact of Food Production</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="The distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is consumed.">Food miles</dfn> measure the distance food travels from production to consumption. High food miles mean more transport emissions, contributing to a larger <dfn class="term" data-def="The total amount of greenhouse gases produced by transporting, processing, and storing food.">carbon footprint</dfn>. However, food miles alone are not the full picture — a product grown in a heated UK greenhouse may have a higher carbon footprint than the same product grown outdoors in a warmer country and shipped by sea.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Packaging waste</strong> is a major environmental issue. Excessive plastic packaging ends up in landfill or pollutes oceans. Solutions include using recyclable or biodegradable packaging, buying loose produce, and reusing containers. <strong>Food waste</strong> occurs at every stage of the supply chain — from farms (cosmetically imperfect produce discarded) to households (buying too much, poor storage). Reducing food waste saves money and reduces greenhouse gas emissions from decomposing food in landfill.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Close this and write down four ways consumers can reduce the environmental impact of food from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Consumers can reduce environmental impact by: buying <strong>local and seasonal</strong> food (lower food miles), reducing <strong>packaging waste</strong> (choosing recyclable options), minimising <strong>food waste</strong> (meal planning, proper storage), and choosing <strong>sustainably sourced</strong> products (MSC-certified fish, Fairtrade goods).</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Food Security and Sustainability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><dfn class="term" data-def="The state in which all people have access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs.">Food security</dfn> means all people having access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food at all times. Globally, food security is threatened by: a <strong>growing population</strong> (expected to reach 9.7 billion by 2050), <strong>climate change</strong> (droughts, flooding, unpredictable seasons), <strong>insufficient agricultural land</strong> (urbanisation reduces farmland), and <strong>water scarcity</strong>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><dfn class="term" data-def="Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, particularly in relation to food production.">Sustainability</dfn> in food production means meeting current needs without compromising future generations. Strategies include: reducing food waste, sustainable farming practices, developing drought-resistant crops, and improving food distribution systems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="An international certification system that ensures producers in developing countries receive a fair price for their goods, along with better working conditions.">Fairtrade</dfn> certification ensures that producers in developing countries receive a fair price for their goods, along with improved working conditions and community investment. Fairtrade products include coffee, tea, chocolate, bananas, and sugar. Buying Fairtrade supports sustainable livelihoods in poorer communities.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Primary and Secondary Processing</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="The first stage of converting raw food into a form that can be eaten or used in cooking, such as milling wheat into flour or pasteurising milk.">Primary processing</dfn> is the first stage of turning raw food into something usable. Examples include: <strong>milling wheat into flour</strong> (cleaning, grinding, sieving), and <strong>heat-treating milk</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Milk undergoes different levels of heat treatment: <strong>pasteurised</strong> (heated to 72°C for 15 seconds — kills most harmful bacteria, short shelf life), <strong>UHT</strong> (ultra-high temperature, 135°C for 1–4 seconds — long shelf life, can be stored at room temperature), <strong>sterilised</strong> (heated above 100°C for longer — very long shelf life but altered flavour), and <strong>micro-filtered</strong> (passed through fine filters to remove bacteria — fresher taste than UHT).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of turning primary-processed ingredients into finished food products, such as flour into bread or milk into cheese.">Secondary processing</dfn> turns primary-processed ingredients into finished products. Examples: flour → bread, pasta, cakes; milk → cheese, yoghurt, butter; fruit → jams, juices; meat → sausages, pies. This adds value and extends shelf life.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Processing can cause <strong>vitamin loss</strong>. Heat destroys vitamins B and C. Drying and milling remove nutrients from the outer layers of grains. This is why some products are <strong>fortified</strong> — nutrients are added back in.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Fortification and Food Additives</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><dfn class="term" data-def="The addition of nutrients to a food product that were not originally present, or that were lost during processing, to improve its nutritional value.">Fortification</dfn> is the addition of nutrients to food to improve its nutritional value. UK law requires white flour to be fortified with <strong>thiamin</strong> (vitamin B1), <strong>niacin</strong> (vitamin B3), <strong>calcium</strong>, and <strong>iron</strong>. Many breakfast cereals are fortified with <strong>folic acid</strong> (to help prevent neural tube defects) and <strong>iron</strong>. Margarine and spreads must contain added <strong>vitamins A and D</strong>. Some spreads are enriched with plant stanols/sterols to help lower <strong>cholesterol</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><dfn class="term" data-def="Substances added to food during processing to improve its colour, flavour, texture, or shelf life. They must be listed on the label and approved for safety.">Food additives</dfn> are substances added to food during processing. There are four main types:</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Colourings</strong> — make food look more appealing (e.g. adding colour to sweets or soft drinks). Some artificial colourings have been linked to hyperactivity in children. <strong>Flavourings</strong> — enhance or add taste (e.g. vanilla flavouring in ice cream). Can be natural or artificial. <strong>Preservatives</strong> — extend shelf life by slowing bacterial growth or oxidation (e.g. salt, sugar, vinegar, sulphites). <strong>Emulsifiers and stabilisers</strong> — prevent mixtures from separating and maintain texture (e.g. lecithin in chocolate, xanthan gum in sauces).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Additives have pros (longer shelf life, improved safety, consistent quality, lower cost) and cons (some may cause allergic reactions, concerns about artificial ingredients, may mask poor-quality food). All approved additives in the UK are given an <strong>E-number</strong> and must be listed on food labels.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: name four nutrients that UK law requires white flour to be fortified with, and explain why fortification is needed.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>UK law requires white flour to be fortified with <strong>thiamin, niacin, calcium, and iron</strong> because milling removes nutrient-rich outer layers of the grain. Margarine must contain vitamins A and D. Cereals are often fortified with folic acid and iron. Fortification helps prevent nutritional deficiencies in the population.</p>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n27" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the four types of food additive, and give one example and one concern for each?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The four types of food additive are: <strong>colourings</strong> (linked to hyperactivity), <strong>flavourings</strong> (natural or artificial), <strong>preservatives</strong> (extend shelf life, e.g. salt, sulphites), and <strong>emulsifiers/stabilisers</strong> (prevent separation, e.g. lecithin). All must have E-numbers and be listed on labels.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n29">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n30">Food provenance covers where and how food is grown, reared, or caught — organic, free-range, conventional, and sustainable methods all have different environmental, ethical, and economic trade-offs.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n31">Environmental impact is measured through food miles, carbon footprint, packaging waste, and food waste — consumers can reduce their impact by buying local, seasonal, and sustainably sourced products.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n32">Primary processing (milling, pasteurisation) and secondary processing (flour to bread, milk to cheese) add value and extend shelf life, while fortification and additives improve nutritional content and product quality.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n28">In AQA Food Preparation and Nutrition, questions about food provenance often ask you to <strong>evaluate</strong> or <strong>discuss</strong>. This means you must give <strong>both sides</strong> — advantages and disadvantages. For example, if asked about GM foods, write about higher yields and pest resistance (advantages) <em>and</em> long-term health concerns and ethical objections (disadvantages). Always use <strong>specific examples</strong> rather than vague statements: “golden rice has been genetically modified to contain beta-carotene to address vitamin A deficiency” is much stronger than “GM food can be healthier.” For fortification, learn the specific nutrients required by UK law.</p>
