<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Energy?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Your body needs <dfn class="term" data-def="The capacity to do work. In nutrition, energy is measured in kilocalories (kcal) or kilojoules (kJ) and comes from the macronutrients in food.">energy</dfn> for everything it does — from breathing and pumping blood to walking and exercising. This energy comes from the food and drink you consume. The amount of energy a person needs depends on their age, gender, body size, muscle mass, and how active they are.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of energy the body uses at complete rest to maintain basic life functions such as breathing, circulation, and cell repair.">Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)</dfn> is the amount of energy your body needs just to stay alive when you are completely at rest. It covers essential functions like breathing, keeping your heart beating, maintaining body temperature, and repairing cells. BMR accounts for the largest portion of your daily energy use — typically 60–75% of total energy expenditure.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Factors Affecting BMR</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Age:</strong> BMR decreases as you get older because muscle mass naturally declines with age. Younger people have a higher BMR because their bodies are still growing and repairing cells rapidly.</p>




    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Gender:</strong> Males generally have a higher BMR than females because they tend to have more muscle mass. Muscle tissue is metabolically active — it burns energy even at rest.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Body size:</strong> A larger body has more cells to maintain, so taller or heavier people typically have a higher BMR than smaller people.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Muscle mass:</strong> The more muscle you have, the higher your BMR. This is why athletes and people who exercise regularly burn more energy at rest than sedentary individuals.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does BMR stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>BMR is the energy needed for basic functions at complete rest. It is affected by four main factors: <strong>age</strong>, <strong>gender</strong>, <strong>body size</strong>, and <strong>muscle mass</strong>. A younger, larger, more muscular male will generally have the highest BMR.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Physical Activity Level (PAL)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="A number that represents how physically active a person is. It is multiplied by BMR to calculate total daily energy needs.">Physical Activity Level (PAL)</dfn> is a value that reflects how active a person is during the day. A sedentary office worker has a low PAL (around 1.4), while an athlete or manual labourer has a high PAL (around 2.0 or more). The more active you are, the higher your PAL.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Typical PAL Values</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Sedentary (PAL 1.4):</strong> Little or no exercise, desk-based work. Examples include office workers or students who do not take part in sport.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Moderately active (PAL 1.6–1.7):</strong> Some regular exercise or a job that involves standing and walking. Examples include teachers or shop workers who also exercise a few times a week.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Very active (PAL 1.9–2.0+):</strong> Daily vigorous exercise or a physically demanding job. Examples include professional athletes, builders, or farmers.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Calculating Total Energy Needs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">To work out how much energy a person needs each day, you multiply their BMR by their PAL:</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does BMR stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Total daily energy requirement = BMR × PAL</strong></p>
  <p>For example, if a woman has a BMR of 1,400 kcal and a PAL of 1.6 (moderately active), her total daily energy need is 1,400 × 1.6 = <strong>2,240 kcal per day</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Energy Balance</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="The relationship between energy consumed from food and drink (energy in) and energy used by the body for activity and body functions (energy out).">Energy balance</dfn> is the relationship between the energy you take in from food and the energy your body uses. When energy in equals energy out, your weight stays stable. When they are not equal, your weight changes.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Energy Balance Scenarios</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Energy in = Energy out (balanced):</strong> Weight stays the same. The person is eating exactly as much energy as they are using. This is the ideal for maintaining a healthy weight.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Energy in > Energy out (surplus):</strong> The excess energy is stored as body fat, leading to weight gain. Over time, this can contribute to obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and some cancers.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Energy in < Energy out (deficit):</strong> The body uses its stored fat for energy, leading to weight loss. If prolonged, this can cause <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the body does not receive enough nutrients to function properly, resulting from under-eating or a diet lacking essential nutrients.">malnutrition</dfn>, fatigue, a weakened immune system, and in children it can slow growth and development.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Energy Values of Macronutrients</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Different <dfn class="term" data-def="Nutrients needed in large amounts by the body — protein, carbohydrates, and fats — each providing energy measured in kilocalories per gram.">macronutrients</dfn> provide different amounts of energy per gram. Knowing these values is essential for <dfn class="term" data-def="The process of calculating the nutrient and energy content of a food product or meal using food composition tables or software.">nutritional analysis</dfn> and for exam calculations.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n25">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">4 kcal/g</div>
    <h4>Protein</h4><p>Provides 4 kilocalories per gram. Protein is primarily used for growth and repair, but the body can use it for energy when carbohydrate and fat stores are low.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">4 kcal/g</div>
    <h4>Carbohydrate</h4><p>Provides 4 kilocalories per gram. Carbohydrate is the body’s preferred source of energy, especially for the brain and during exercise.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">9 kcal/g</div>
    <h4>Fat</h4><p>Provides 9 kilocalories per gram — more than double the energy of protein or carbohydrate. Fat is a concentrated energy source and is stored in the body for use when needed.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">7 kcal/g</div>
    <h4>Alcohol</h4><p>Provides 7 kilocalories per gram. Alcohol is not a nutrient and has no nutritional benefit, but it does contribute to energy intake.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n26" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the formula or equation from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Energy values you must know: <strong>protein = 4 kcal/g</strong>, <strong>carbohydrate = 4 kcal/g</strong>, <strong>fat = 9 kcal/g</strong>, <strong>alcohol = 7 kcal/g</strong>. Fat provides more than twice the energy of protein or carbohydrate gram for gram.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Recommended Macronutrient Proportions</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The UK government publishes <dfn class="term" data-def="Government guidelines that state how much of each nutrient a person should consume to maintain good health, including reference intakes for energy and individual nutrients.">dietary recommendations</dfn> for how much of each macronutrient should make up a person’s total energy intake. Following these helps reduce the risk of diet-related diseases.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Macronutrient Targets</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Protein (~15% of total energy):</strong> Supports growth, repair, and maintenance of body tissues. Found in meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, and nuts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Fat (no more than 35% of total energy):</strong> Provides essential fatty acids and carries fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K). Within this, <dfn class="term" data-def="Fats found mainly in animal products and processed foods. Eating too much raises LDL cholesterol and increases the risk of cardiovascular disease.">saturated fat</dfn> should be limited to no more than 11% of total energy to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Carbohydrate (~50% of total energy):</strong> The body’s preferred energy source. Of this 50%, around 45% should come from starchy carbohydrates (bread, pasta, rice, potatoes). <dfn class="term" data-def="Sugars added to foods by manufacturers, cooks, or consumers, plus sugars found naturally in honey, syrups, and fruit juices. The government recommends no more than 5% of energy from free sugars.">Free sugars</dfn> should make up no more than 5% of total energy — about 30g per day for adults.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Nutritional Analysis</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">Nutritional analysis is the process of working out the nutrient and energy content of a food product or meal. It helps food manufacturers, chefs, and individuals understand what they are eating and whether a dish meets dietary guidelines.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Methods of Nutritional Analysis</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Food composition tables:</strong> Published tables such as McCance and Widdowson’s <em>The Composition of Foods</em> list the nutrient content of hundreds of foods per 100g. You look up each ingredient, calculate the amounts used in your recipe, and add them together. This manual method is accurate but time-consuming.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Nutritional analysis software:</strong> Programs like <em>Nutritics</em> or <em>MyFitnessPal</em> allow you to enter a recipe and instantly see a full nutritional breakdown. The software compares results against <dfn class="term" data-def="The daily amount of a nutrient that is enough to meet the needs of most people in a particular group. Used as a benchmark when assessing nutritional adequacy.">reference intakes</dfn> and can highlight where a dish is too high in fat, sugar, or salt, or too low in fibre or vitamins.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>Food labels:</strong> Packaged foods display nutritional information per 100g and per portion. Traffic light labels use red, amber, and green to show whether levels of fat, saturated fat, sugar, and salt are high, medium, or low.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n37">Using Nutritional Analysis to Plan and Modify Dishes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n38">In the exam, you may be asked how nutritional analysis can help you plan, make, or modify a dish. The key idea is that once you know the nutritional profile of a recipe, you can adjust ingredients to make it healthier or to meet a specific dietary need.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Examples of Modifying Dishes</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n39"><strong>Reducing fat:</strong> Swap butter for a lower-fat spread, grill instead of fry, use semi-skimmed milk instead of whole milk, or trim visible fat from meat.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n40"><strong>Reducing sugar:</strong> Use fruit purée to sweeten a cake instead of sugar, reduce the amount of sugar in a recipe by one-third, or choose tinned fruit in juice rather than syrup.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n41"><strong>Increasing fibre:</strong> Use wholemeal flour instead of white flour, add vegetables or pulses to a sauce or stew, or serve brown rice instead of white rice.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n42"><strong>Increasing vitamins and minerals:</strong> Add extra vegetables, choose fortified cereals, or include a side salad to boost micronutrient content.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n43" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What percentage is given for the exam, always justify?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the exam, always <strong>justify modifications using nutritional data</strong>. For example: “The analysis showed the dish was high in saturated fat (22g per serving), so I swapped butter for olive oil to reduce it to 8g, bringing the fat content below the 35% guideline.”</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n45">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n46">Total daily energy needs are calculated by multiplying BMR (energy at rest) by PAL (activity level). Key factors affecting BMR are age, gender, body size, and muscle mass.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n47">Energy balance means energy in equals energy out — a surplus causes weight gain, a deficit causes weight loss. Recommended proportions: protein ~15%, fat ≤35%, carbohydrate ~50% (free sugars max 5%).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n48">Nutritional analysis using food tables or software allows you to assess and modify dishes to meet dietary guidelines and specific nutritional needs.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n44">When answering questions on energy needs, always state the formula: <strong>Total energy = BMR × PAL</strong> and show your working with correct units (kcal). If asked to explain why two people have different energy needs, compare their age, gender, body size, and activity level — give a <em>reason</em> for each factor, not just a list. For nutritional analysis questions, name a specific method (food tables or software) and explain how the results would be used to improve a dish. Strong answers show precise nutritional data, not vague suggestions like “make it healthier.”</p>
