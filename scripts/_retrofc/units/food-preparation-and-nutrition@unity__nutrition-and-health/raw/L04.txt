The six key minerals, their functions and food sources, plus why hydration matters.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Minerals in the Diet</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Minerals are <dfn class="term" data-def="Nutrients needed in small amounts that regulate body processes and maintain structures like bones and teeth.">micronutrients</dfn> that your body needs in small amounts. Unlike vitamins, minerals are inorganic — they come from the soil and water rather than from living things. For your AQA exam, you need to know six minerals: calcium, iron, sodium, fluoride, iodine, and phosphorus.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Calcium</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Calcium is the most abundant mineral in the body. It is essential for strong bones and teeth, but it also plays a role in muscle contraction, blood clotting, and nerve function. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Reference Nutrient Intake — the amount of a nutrient that is enough to meet the needs of 97.5% of the population.">RNI</dfn> for adults is 700 mg per day.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Good sources include milk, cheese, yoghurt, green leafy vegetables (such as broccoli and kale), canned fish with bones (like sardines), and fortified foods such as fortified bread and breakfast cereals.</p>





<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain the cause or effect related to calcium deficiency causes rickets.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Calcium deficiency causes <strong>rickets</strong> in children (soft, weak bones that bend) and <strong>osteoporosis</strong> in adults (brittle bones that fracture easily). Excess calcium can lead to kidney stones.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n7">Iron</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n8">Iron is needed to make <dfn class="term" data-def="The protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body.">haemoglobin</dfn>, the protein in red blood cells that transports oxygen around the body. Without enough iron, your cells do not get the oxygen they need, and you feel tired and weak.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9">There are two types of dietary iron. <strong>Haem iron</strong> comes from animal sources such as red meat and liver — the body absorbs it easily. <strong>Non-haem iron</strong> comes from plant sources such as dark green leafy vegetables, beans, nuts, and fortified cereals — it is harder to absorb. Eating vitamin C alongside non-haem iron (for example, a glass of orange juice with a fortified cereal) significantly improves absorption.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Deficiency:</strong> Iron deficiency causes <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition where the blood lacks enough healthy red blood cells to carry adequate oxygen, causing tiredness, pale skin, and breathlessness.">anaemia</dfn>. Symptoms include tiredness, pale skin, and breathlessness. It is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies in the UK, particularly among teenage girls and women of childbearing age.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Excess:</strong> Too much iron can cause constipation, nausea, and in severe cases, liver damage. Iron supplements should only be taken on medical advice.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Sodium</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Sodium is essential for maintaining fluid balance in the body. It also helps transmit nerve impulses and supports muscle contraction. Most people get sodium from salt (sodium chloride), processed foods, bread, and cheese.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about adults should consume no more than 6 g of.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Adults should consume no more than <strong>6 g of salt per day</strong> (about one teaspoon). Excess sodium raises blood pressure, increasing the risk of stroke and heart disease. Deficiency is rare but can cause muscle cramps.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Fluoride, Iodine & Phosphorus</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Fluoride</strong> strengthens tooth enamel and helps prevent <dfn class="term" data-def="Tooth decay caused by acids produced when bacteria in the mouth break down sugars.">dental caries</dfn> (tooth decay). Sources include fluoridated water, tea, and toothpaste. Many areas in the UK add fluoride to the water supply specifically to reduce tooth decay.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>Iodine</strong> is needed for the production of thyroid hormones, which control your <dfn class="term" data-def="The chemical processes in the body that convert food into energy and build or repair tissues.">metabolism</dfn>. Good sources are seafood, milk, and dairy products. A deficiency causes <strong>goitre</strong> — a visible swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Phosphorus</strong> works alongside calcium to build and maintain strong bones and teeth. It also helps the body release energy from food. Sources include meat, fish, dairy, and cereals. Because it is found in so many foods, deficiency is extremely rare.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Iron</strong> — haemoglobin (oxygen transport). Sources: red meat, liver, beans, fortified cereals. Deficiency: anaemia.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Sodium</strong> — fluid balance, nerve impulses, muscle contraction. Sources: salt, processed foods. Excess: high blood pressure. Max 6 g salt/day.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Fluoride</strong> — tooth enamel, prevents dental caries. Sources: fluoridated water, tea, toothpaste.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Iodine</strong> — thyroid hormones, metabolism. Sources: seafood, milk, dairy. Deficiency: goitre.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Phosphorus</strong> — bones/teeth (with calcium), energy release. Sources: meat, fish, dairy, cereals.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Water & Hydration</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Water is not a mineral, but it is essential for life. Every cell, tissue, and organ in your body needs water to function properly. The specification requires you to understand the functions of water, how it is lost, and how much you need each day.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Functions of Water in the Body</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Waste elimination:</strong> Water helps the kidneys flush out waste products through urine.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Temperature regulation:</strong> When you are hot, your body produces sweat. As sweat evaporates from the skin, it cools you down.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Digestion:</strong> Water is needed to break down food and absorb nutrients in the digestive system.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30"><strong>Nutrient transport:</strong> Blood, which is mostly water, carries nutrients and oxygen to cells and removes waste.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Chemical reactions:</strong> Many chemical processes in the body, including <dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction that uses water to break down larger molecules into smaller ones during digestion.">hydrolysis</dfn>, require water to take place.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n32">Water is lost continuously through urine, sweat, and breathing. The government recommends drinking <strong>6–8 glasses of fluid per day</strong>. Water, lower-fat milk, and sugar-free drinks all count towards this. About 20% of your daily water intake comes from food — soups, yoghurts, fruit, and vegetables all contain significant amounts of water. The remaining 80% should come from drinks.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n33" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain the cause or effect related to fruit juice and smoothies count as.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Fruit juice and smoothies count as a maximum of <strong>150 ml per day combined</strong>, no matter how much you drink, because of their high sugar content. Drinking too much water can cause <dfn class="term" data-def="A dangerous condition where sodium levels in the blood drop too low, usually from drinking excessive amounts of water.">hyponatraemia</dfn> — dangerously low sodium levels in the blood.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n34">Extra fluids are needed during hot weather, physical activity, illness (especially fever, vomiting, or diarrhoea), and pregnancy or breastfeeding. <dfn class="term" data-def="The state of not having enough water in the body to carry out normal functions, causing symptoms like headache, fatigue, and dark urine.">Dehydration</dfn> causes headaches, fatigue, poor concentration, and dark-coloured urine. In severe cases it can be life-threatening.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n36">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n37">Six minerals to know: calcium (bones), iron (haemoglobin), sodium (fluid balance), fluoride (teeth), iodine (thyroid), and phosphorus (bones & energy) — each with specific sources, deficiency diseases, and excess risks.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Haem iron from animal sources is absorbed more easily than non-haem iron from plants; vitamin C improves non-haem iron absorption.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n39">Aim for 6–8 glasses of fluid per day. Water supports waste removal, temperature control, digestion, nutrient transport, and chemical reactions in the body.</li></ul>
