--- DESCRIPTION ---
Fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins, their sources, functions, and deficiency diseases.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Are Vitamins?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Organic compounds needed in small amounts for essential body functions such as immune health, energy release, and cell repair.">Vitamins</dfn> are <dfn class="term" data-def="Nutrients the body needs in very small amounts — vitamins and minerals.">micronutrients</dfn> — the body only needs them in tiny amounts, but without them things go seriously wrong. Unlike macronutrients, vitamins do not provide energy directly, but many are involved in the chemical reactions that release energy from food, as well as controlling vital processes like bone growth, wound healing, and protecting cells from damage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Vitamins are split into two groups based on how the body absorbs and stores them: <dfn class="term" data-def="Vitamins that dissolve in fat and can be stored in the liver and body fat — vitamins A, D, E, and K.">fat-soluble vitamins</dfn> (A, D, E, K) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Vitamins that dissolve in water and cannot be stored in the body, so they must be consumed regularly — B-group and C.">water-soluble vitamins</dfn> (B-group and C). This distinction matters because it affects how the body absorbs them, how they can be lost during cooking, and what happens if you consume too much.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain the cause or effect related to fat-soluble vitamins (A.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Fat-soluble vitamins</strong> (A, D, E, K) dissolve in fat and are stored in the liver and body fat. Because they are stored, it is possible to consume too much over time. <strong>Water-soluble vitamins</strong> (B-group and C) dissolve in water and cannot be stored — excess is excreted in urine, so they must be consumed regularly.</p>




</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Fat-Soluble Vitamins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Fat-soluble vitamins require dietary fat to be absorbed in the small intestine. They are transported in the blood attached to fat molecules and stored in the liver and adipose tissue. Because they are stored, a daily intake is not strictly necessary — but long-term deficiency causes serious health problems.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Vitamin A (Retinol)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="A fat-soluble vitamin essential for vision (especially in dim light), immune function, and healthy skin. Found as retinol in animal foods and as beta-carotene in orange and green vegetables.">Vitamin A</dfn> has three main functions: it is needed for <strong>normal vision</strong>, particularly seeing in dim light; it supports the <strong>immune system</strong> by helping the body fight infections; and it keeps <strong>skin and mucous membranes</strong> healthy, acting as a barrier against bacteria.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sources:</strong> Liver, oily fish, dairy products (butter, cheese, whole milk), eggs, and orange-coloured vegetables such as carrots, sweet potatoes, and butternut squash. The orange colour comes from <dfn class="term" data-def="A plant pigment found in orange and dark green vegetables that the body converts into vitamin A.">beta-carotene</dfn>, which the body converts into vitamin A. Dark green leafy vegetables like spinach also contain beta-carotene.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Deficiency:</strong> A lack of vitamin A causes <strong>night blindness</strong> — difficulty seeing in dim or dark conditions. In severe cases, it can lead to complete blindness. Deficiency also weakens the immune system and makes skin dry and rough.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Vitamin D (Cholecalciferol)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="A fat-soluble vitamin essential for calcium absorption and strong bones. Made by the skin when exposed to sunlight; also found in oily fish, eggs, and fortified foods.">Vitamin D</dfn> is sometimes called the “sunshine vitamin” because the body can make it when the skin is exposed to <strong>sunlight</strong> (specifically ultraviolet B radiation). Its main function is to help the body <strong>absorb calcium</strong> from food, which is essential for building and maintaining strong <strong>bones and teeth</strong>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Sources:</strong> Oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), eggs, red meat, and fortified foods such as breakfast cereals and margarine. In the UK, sunlight is only strong enough to make vitamin D between April and September, so the government recommends a 10 microgram supplement during autumn and winter.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Deficiency:</strong> Without enough vitamin D, the body cannot absorb calcium properly. In children, this causes <dfn class="term" data-def="A bone disease in children caused by vitamin D deficiency, leading to soft, weak bones that can bow under the body’s weight.">rickets</dfn> — soft, weak bones that bow under the body’s weight. In adults, the equivalent condition is called <dfn class="term" data-def="A condition in adults caused by vitamin D deficiency, resulting in weak, painful bones and muscle weakness.">osteomalacia</dfn>, which causes bone pain and muscle weakness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Vitamin E</strong> is a powerful <dfn class="term" data-def="A substance that protects cells from damage caused by free radicals — unstable molecules produced during normal metabolism and by environmental factors such as pollution.">antioxidant</dfn>. It protects cell membranes from damage caused by <dfn class="term" data-def="Unstable molecules that can damage cells and DNA, contributing to ageing and diseases such as cancer and heart disease.">free radicals</dfn>. Sources include vegetable oils (sunflower, olive), nuts (almonds, hazelnuts), seeds, and avocados.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Vitamin K</strong> is essential for normal <strong>blood clotting</strong>. Without it, even small cuts would bleed excessively. It is also involved in bone health. Sources include green leafy vegetables (broccoli, spinach, kale, cabbage), vegetable oils, and some fermented foods.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does ADEK stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Remember <strong>ADEK</strong> for the four fat-soluble vitamins. <strong>A</strong> = vision + immunity. <strong>D</strong> = calcium absorption + bones. <strong>E</strong> = antioxidant. <strong>K</strong> = blood clotting. They all need dietary fat to be absorbed.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Water-Soluble Vitamins: B-Group</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A group of water-soluble vitamins involved in energy release, red blood cell production, nervous system function, and cell division.">B-group vitamins</dfn> are a family of water-soluble vitamins that work together in the body. They cannot be stored, so they must be consumed regularly through the diet. Several B vitamins are involved in releasing energy from carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.</p>

<div class="timeline" data-narration-id="n18">
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">B1</div>
    <h4>Thiamin</h4><p>Helps release energy from carbohydrates. Found in wholegrains, pork, nuts, and fortified cereals. Deficiency causes beriberi (nerve and heart damage).</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">B2</div>
    <h4>Riboflavin</h4><p>Helps release energy from food and supports healthy skin and eyes. Found in milk, eggs, fortified cereals, and mushrooms.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">B3</div>
    <h4>Niacin</h4><p>Helps release energy from food and supports the nervous system and skin. Found in meat, fish, wheat flour, and eggs.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">B9</div>
    <h4>Folic Acid (Folate)</h4><p>Essential for cell division and the formation of DNA. Critical during early pregnancy to prevent <dfn class="term" data-def="Birth defects affecting the brain and spinal cord, such as spina bifida, linked to insufficient folic acid intake in early pregnancy.">neural tube defects</dfn> (e.g. spina bifida). Found in green leafy vegetables, pulses (chickpeas, lentils), fortified bread, and oranges.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="timeline-event">
    <div class="timeline-date">B12</div>
    <h4>Cobalamin</h4><p>Needed for making red blood cells and maintaining the nervous system. Found only in animal sources: meat, fish, dairy, and eggs. <strong>Vegans are at risk of deficiency</strong> and should take supplements or eat fortified foods.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n19" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define vitamin B12 in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>Vitamin B12</strong> is found <em>only</em> in animal sources. This means vegans must get it from fortified foods (plant milks, nutritional yeast, breakfast cereals) or supplements. A deficiency causes a type of anaemia (megaloblastic anaemia) where red blood cells are too large and do not function properly.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Water-Soluble Vitamins: Vitamin C</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="A water-soluble vitamin essential for the formation of connective tissue (collagen), wound healing, iron absorption, and antioxidant protection.">Vitamin C</dfn> (ascorbic acid) has several important functions. It is needed for the formation of <dfn class="term" data-def="A structural protein found in skin, tendons, ligaments, and blood vessels — the most abundant protein in the body.">collagen</dfn>, a protein that forms connective tissue in the skin, blood vessels, tendons, and ligaments. This means vitamin C is essential for <strong>wound healing</strong> — without it, wounds take much longer to repair.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Vitamin C also helps the body <strong>absorb iron</strong> from plant sources (non-haem iron), so eating vitamin C-rich foods alongside iron-rich foods improves iron uptake. On top of this, vitamin C is a powerful <strong>antioxidant</strong>, protecting cells from free radical damage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Sources:</strong> Citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, grapefruits), peppers (especially red and green), broccoli, strawberries, blackcurrants, kiwi fruit, tomatoes, and potatoes.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Deficiency:</strong> A severe lack of vitamin C causes <dfn class="term" data-def="A disease caused by prolonged vitamin C deficiency, resulting in bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor wound healing, and bruising.">scurvy</dfn> — symptoms include bleeding gums, loose teeth, bruising easily, poor wound healing, and joint pain. Historically, scurvy was common among sailors on long voyages with no access to fresh fruit or vegetables.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Cooking & Water-Soluble Vitamin Loss</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Because B vitamins and vitamin C dissolve in water, they can easily be lost during food preparation and cooking. This is an important practical point for the exam — you may be asked how to retain vitamins when cooking vegetables.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>How Water-Soluble Vitamins Are Lost & How to Minimise Loss</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>How they are lost:</strong> Boiling vegetables in large amounts of water causes vitamins to leach out into the cooking water, which is then thrown away. Overcooking destroys vitamins through prolonged exposure to heat. Chopping vegetables into very small pieces increases the surface area exposed to water and air, speeding up vitamin loss. Keeping food warm for long periods also reduces vitamin content.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>How to minimise loss:</strong> Steam vegetables instead of boiling. Use as little water as possible if boiling. Cook for the shortest time necessary. Keep vegetables in large pieces until just before cooking. Serve immediately rather than keeping warm. Use the cooking water for sauces or gravy to retain any leached vitamins.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n29">Antioxidant Vitamins: A, C, and E</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n30">Vitamins A, C, and E all have <strong>antioxidant properties</strong>. Antioxidants protect the body’s cells from damage caused by free radicals — unstable molecules produced during normal metabolism and by environmental factors such as pollution, cigarette smoke, and UV radiation.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Free radicals can damage cell membranes and DNA, contributing to ageing and increasing the risk of diseases such as cancer, heart disease, and cataracts. A diet rich in antioxidant vitamins — from colourful fruits, vegetables, nuts, and seeds — helps neutralise free radicals and reduce this damage. This is why the government recommends eating at least <strong>five portions of fruit and vegetables per day</strong>.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does ACE stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three antioxidant vitamins are <strong>A, C, and E</strong> (remember: “ACE”). They protect cells from free radical damage. A diet rich in colourful fruits and vegetables provides all three. In the exam, link antioxidants to reduced risk of cancer, heart disease, and premature ageing.</p>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n34">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n35">Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) need dietary fat for absorption and are stored in the body — key deficiency diseases include night blindness (A), rickets and osteomalacia (D).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Water-soluble vitamins (B-group and C) cannot be stored and are easily lost through boiling and overcooking — B12 is only in animal sources (vegans at risk), and vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Vitamins A, C, and E are antioxidants (“ACE”) that protect cells from free radical damage, reducing the risk of cancer, heart disease, and premature ageing.</li></ul>
