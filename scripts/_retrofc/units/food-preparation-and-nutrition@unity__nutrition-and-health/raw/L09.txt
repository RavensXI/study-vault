--- DESCRIPTION ---
Personal, kitchen, storage, and cooking hygiene rules that prevent contamination and food poisoning.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Food Hygiene Matters</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Good <dfn class="term" data-def="The practices and procedures that ensure food is handled, prepared, stored, and cooked safely to prevent contamination and foodborne illness.">food hygiene</dfn> prevents contamination and food poisoning. Every year in the UK, around one million people suffer from foodborne illness — most cases are caused by poor hygiene during preparation, storage, or cooking. For the exam , you need to know the hygiene rules that apply to people, kitchens, storage, and cooking temperatures.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Food hygiene covers four main areas: <strong>personal hygiene</strong> (what the food handler does), <strong>kitchen hygiene</strong> (how the workspace is kept clean), <strong>food storage</strong> (how food is kept at safe temperatures), and <strong>cooking and temperature control</strong> (how food is heated, held, cooled, and reheated safely).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Personal Hygiene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Anyone handling food must follow strict personal hygiene rules to prevent bacteria from their body, clothing, or habits from contaminating the food.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Wash hands thoroughly</strong> — use hot water and antibacterial soap for at least 20 seconds before handling food, after touching raw food, after using the toilet, after sneezing or coughing, and after handling waste. Proper <dfn class="term" data-def="The thorough cleaning of hands with hot water and soap to remove bacteria and prevent the transfer of micro-organisms to food.">handwashing</dfn> is the single most important step in preventing cross-contamination.</li>
<li><strong>Tie hair back</strong> — long hair must be tied back and ideally covered with a hairnet or hat to prevent loose hairs falling into food (physical contamination).</li>
<li><strong>Wear a clean apron</strong> — protects food from bacteria on outdoor clothing and protects the handler from splashes. Aprons should be changed if they become soiled.</li>
<li><strong>Use blue plasters</strong> — any cuts or sores on the hands must be covered with a <dfn class="term" data-def="A bright blue waterproof dressing used in food preparation areas because it is easy to spot if it falls into food and does not match any natural food colour.">blue plaster</dfn>. Blue is used because no natural food is blue, so a fallen plaster is easy to spot. In commercial kitchens, these plasters often contain a metal strip so they can be detected by metal detectors on production lines.</li>
<li><strong>No sneezing or coughing over food</strong> — <em>Staphylococcus aureus</em> lives in the nose and throat. Sneezing or coughing near food can spray thousands of bacteria onto it. If you need to sneeze, turn away from the food and wash your hands afterwards.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the 48-hour rule</strong> — food handlers should not prepare food for others for at least <dfn class="term" data-def="The guideline that anyone who has had vomiting or diarrhoea must wait at least 48 hours after symptoms stop before handling food, to avoid spreading infection.">48 hours</dfn> after the last episode of vomiting or diarrhoea. This prevents the spread of norovirus and other foodborne infections to others.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define the 48-hour rule in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The <strong>48-hour rule</strong> means a food handler must wait 48 hours after symptoms of vomiting or diarrhoea have stopped before returning to work. This is a legal requirement under food safety regulations and applies in schools, restaurants, and food factories.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Kitchen Hygiene</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">A clean, well-organised kitchen reduces the risk of <dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of harmful bacteria or other contaminants from one food (usually raw) to another (usually ready-to-eat), typically via hands, utensils, or surfaces.">cross-contamination</dfn>. Good kitchen hygiene is about preventing bacteria from building up on surfaces, equipment, and cloths.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Kitchen Hygiene Rules</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Clean as you go:</strong> Wipe down surfaces, wash up equipment, and put away ingredients as you work rather than leaving everything until the end. This prevents bacteria from multiplying on dirty surfaces and reduces the risk of cross-contamination between tasks.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Use antibacterial spray or sanitiser:</strong> Work surfaces must be cleaned with an <dfn class="term" data-def="A cleaning product that kills or inhibits the growth of bacteria on surfaces, used to reduce the risk of contamination in food preparation areas.">antibacterial</dfn> cleaning product before and after food preparation, especially after contact with raw meat. Standard washing-up liquid removes visible dirt but does not kill bacteria — an antibacterial product is needed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Colour-coded chopping boards:</strong> Different coloured boards are used for different food types to prevent cross-contamination. This system is a legal requirement in commercial kitchens and strongly recommended in school and home kitchens.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Wash cloths and tea towels at 60°C:</strong> Kitchen cloths and tea towels harbour bacteria quickly. They must be washed at <strong>60°C or above</strong> to kill harmful bacteria. Ideally, use disposable cloths or replace reusable ones daily.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Use foot-operated bins:</strong> Bins with foot-operated lids prevent food handlers from touching the bin lid with their hands, which would transfer bacteria back to the food. Bins should be emptied regularly and lined with a bin bag.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the formula or equation from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Colour-Coded Chopping Boards</div>
  <p>The standard colour-coding system is: <strong style="color:#dc2626;">Red</strong> = raw meat, <strong style="color:#2563eb;">Blue</strong> = raw fish, <strong style="color:#d97706;">Yellow</strong> = cooked meat, <strong style="color:#16a34a;">Green</strong> = salad & vegetables, <strong>White</strong> = bakery & dairy, <strong style="color:#92400e;">Brown</strong> = raw vegetables & fruit. Using the correct board for each food type prevents bacteria from raw foods being transferred to ready-to-eat foods.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Food Storage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Correct storage keeps food out of the <dfn class="term" data-def="The temperature range between 5°C and 63°C in which bacteria multiply most rapidly, also known as the danger zone.">danger zone</dfn> (5–63°C) and prevents cross-contamination. There are three main types of storage: refrigeration, freezing, and dry (ambient) storage.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Storage Rules in Detail</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Refrigerator (0–5°C):</strong> The fridge must be kept between 0°C and 5°C. Store <strong>raw meat on the bottom shelf</strong> so that juices cannot drip down onto ready-to-eat food below. Ready-to-eat food, dairy, and cooked leftovers go on upper shelves. Cover all food to prevent drying out and to reduce the risk of cross-contamination. Check the fridge temperature regularly with a thermometer.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Freezer (−18°C or below):</strong> A freezer should operate at <strong>−18°C or below</strong>. Freezing does not kill bacteria — it stops them multiplying by removing available water. Once food is thawed, bacteria become active again, which is why frozen food must be <strong>defrosted thoroughly</strong> (ideally in the fridge) and cooked immediately. Food should never be refrozen once thawed unless it has been cooked first.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Dry (ambient) storage — FIFO:</strong> Dry stores hold tinned, packaged, and dried goods at room temperature. They must be clean, cool, dry, and well-ventilated. Stock should be rotated using the <dfn class="term" data-def="First In, First Out — a stock rotation method where the oldest items are used first to prevent food from passing its date and going to waste.">FIFO</dfn> (First In, First Out) system: place new deliveries at the back and bring older stock to the front so the oldest items are used first. This prevents food going past its date.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Cooking & Temperature Control</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Temperature control is the most effective way to make food safe. Bacteria are killed at high temperatures, and their growth is slowed or stopped at very low temperatures. For the exam, you must know the critical temperatures and rules.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n23">
<li><strong>Core temperature of 75°C for 2 minutes:</strong> When cooking, the centre (core) of the food must reach at least <strong>75°C for at least 2 minutes</strong> to kill harmful bacteria such as <em>Salmonella</em> and <em>E. coli</em>. Use a <dfn class="term" data-def="A digital thermometer with a metal probe that is inserted into the centre of food to measure its internal (core) temperature accurately.">temperature probe</dfn> pushed into the thickest part of the food to check.</li>
<li><strong>Hot holding at 63°C:</strong> Food displayed on a buffet or carvery must be kept at <strong>63°C or above</strong> to stay out of the danger zone. If it drops below this, it must be discarded or rapidly reheated.</li>
<li><strong>Cool within 90 minutes:</strong> After cooking, if food is not being served immediately, it must be cooled to below 5°C <strong>within 90 minutes</strong>. This minimises the time food spends in the danger zone. Use shallow containers, blast chillers, or an ice bath to cool food quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Reheat only once:</strong> Food should be <strong>reheated only once</strong> to a core temperature of at least <strong>75°C</strong>. Each time food is cooled and reheated, it passes through the danger zone, giving bacteria a chance to multiply. Repeated reheating increases the risk of food poisoning.</li>
<li><strong>Use a temperature probe:</strong> A temperature probe is the only reliable way to check that food has reached a safe core temperature. The probe must be cleaned and sanitised between uses to avoid cross-contamination. Insert it into the thickest or densest part of the food.</li>
</ul>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write out the formula or equation from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Critical Temperatures</div>
  <p><strong>−18°C</strong> = freezer (bacteria dormant). <strong>0–5°C</strong> = fridge (bacteria growth very slow). <strong>5–63°C</strong> = danger zone (bacteria multiply rapidly). <strong>63°C</strong> = hot holding minimum. <strong>75°C for 2 minutes</strong> = safe core cooking temperature. <strong>100°C</strong> = boiling point of water. Learn these numbers — they appear in almost every food hygiene exam question.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n25">Date Markings on Food</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n26">Food labels carry date markings that tell consumers and food handlers when food is safe to eat. The specification requires you to understand the difference between three types of date.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A date marking on food labels that indicates the last date on which the food is safe to eat — after this date, the food must be discarded even if it looks and smells fine.">Use-by date</dfn>:</strong> This is a <strong>safety</strong> date. It appears on perishable, high-risk foods such as meat, fish, dairy, and ready meals. After the use-by date, the food may contain harmful levels of bacteria even if it looks and smells normal. It is <strong>illegal</strong> for shops to sell food past its use-by date, and food handlers must discard it.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="A date marking on food labels that indicates the date until which the food will be at its best quality — the food is still safe to eat after this date but may not taste or look as good.">Best-before date</dfn>:</strong> This is a <strong>quality</strong> date. It appears on longer-lasting foods such as canned goods, dried pasta, biscuits, and frozen food. After the best-before date, the food is still safe to eat but may not be at its best in terms of taste, texture, or appearance. Shops can legally sell food past its best-before date.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Display-until date:</strong> This is a <strong>shop-only</strong> date used by retailers for stock control. It tells shop staff when to remove products from the shelves. It has no legal food safety meaning for consumers and is often removed or not printed on smaller products.</p>

Illegal to sell past this date. <strong>Best-before</strong> = quality. Found on longer-lasting foods. Safe to eat after this date but quality may decline. Legal to sell past this date. <strong>Display-until</strong> = stock control only. Used by shops to manage shelf displays. No legal meaning for consumers.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n32">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n33">Personal hygiene rules — handwashing, tied hair, clean apron, blue plasters, no sneezing over food, and the 48-hour rule — prevent bacteria from food handlers reaching food.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n34">Kitchen hygiene relies on cleaning as you go, using antibacterial products, colour-coded chopping boards (red = raw meat, blue = fish, yellow = cooked meat, green = veg, white = bakery, brown = fruit), washing cloths at 60°C, and using foot-operated bins.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n35">Critical temperatures: fridge 0–5°C (raw meat on bottom), freezer −18°C, core cooking 75°C for 2 minutes, hot holding 63°C, cool within 90 minutes, reheat once only. Use-by = safety, best-before = quality, display-until = shop only.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n31">When AQA asks you to “explain” a food hygiene practice, always link the <strong>action</strong> to the <strong>reason</strong>. For example: “Raw meat should be stored on the bottom shelf of the fridge <em>because</em> if juices drip onto ready-to-eat food below, bacteria such as <em>Salmonella</em> could cause cross-contamination and food poisoning.” Naming a specific bacterium and a specific food lifts your answer from Level 1 to Level 2. For 8-mark “discuss” questions on hygiene, organise your answer by area (personal, kitchen, storage, cooking) rather than writing a long unstructured list.</p>
