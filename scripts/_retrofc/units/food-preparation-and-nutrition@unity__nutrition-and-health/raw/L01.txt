<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Protein?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A macronutrient made up of chains of amino acids, essential for growth, repair, and maintenance of body tissues.">Protein</dfn> is one of the three <dfn class="term" data-def="Nutrients needed in large amounts by the body — protein, carbohydrates, and fat.">macronutrients</dfn>. It is found in every cell of the body and is essential for growth, repair, and maintenance of tissues such as muscle, skin, hair, and nails. Protein also plays a role in making enzymes, hormones, and antibodies that keep the body functioning properly.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Protein is made up of building blocks called <dfn class="term" data-def="Small organic molecules that link together in chains to form proteins — there are 20 found in food.">amino acids</dfn>. There are 20 amino acids found in protein. The body can make some of these itself, but eight must come from the food we eat. These are called <dfn class="term" data-def="The eight amino acids the body cannot make and must obtain from food: isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine.">essential amino acids</dfn> (EAAs): isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Explain the cause or effect related to young children.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In young children, additional amino acids such as histidine and tyrosine are sometimes considered “conditionally essential” because their bodies cannot yet produce them in sufficient quantities.</p>




</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Functions of Protein</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Protein carries out several vital functions in the body. Understanding these is important for the exam because questions often ask you to explain why protein is needed.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Growth:</strong> Children, teenagers, and pregnant women need protein to build new cells and tissues as the body grows. <strong>Repair:</strong> When tissues are damaged — through injury, surgery, or everyday wear — protein provides the raw materials to rebuild them. <strong>Maintenance:</strong> Even in adults who are not growing, protein is needed to replace cells that die naturally, such as skin cells and red blood cells.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Secondary functions:</strong> Protein is also used to make enzymes (which speed up chemical reactions in the body), hormones (which carry chemical messages), and antibodies (which fight infection). In an emergency, when carbohydrate and fat stores are depleted, the body can use protein as a source of energy — providing 4 kcal per gram.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">HBV and LBV Proteins</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Not all protein sources are equal. The exam board distinguishes between two categories based on amino acid content.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="High biological value protein — contains all eight essential amino acids in sufficient quantities. Found in animal sources and soya.">HBV (high biological value) proteins</dfn> contain all eight essential amino acids. Most animal sources are HBV: meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese, and yoghurt. Soya is the only common plant source that is also HBV, which makes it particularly important for vegetarians and vegans.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="Low biological value protein — missing one or more essential amino acids. Found in most plant sources such as pulses, nuts, seeds, and cereals.">LBV (low biological value) proteins</dfn> are missing one or more essential amino acids. Most plant sources are LBV: pulses (beans, lentils, chickpeas), nuts, seeds, cereals (wheat, rice, oats), and vegetables. This does not mean LBV proteins are bad — it means people who rely on them need to combine different sources to get all eight EAAs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does HBV stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><strong>HBV = all 8 EAAs</strong> (animal sources + soya). <strong>LBV = missing 1 or more EAAs</strong> (most plant sources). Remember: soya is the exception — it is a plant source but HBV.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Protein Complementation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Combining two or more LBV protein sources in the same meal so that together they provide all eight essential amino acids.">Protein complementation</dfn> is the practice of combining two or more LBV protein sources at the same meal so that together they provide all eight essential amino acids. The amino acids missing from one food are supplied by the other.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">This is especially important for vegetarians and vegans who do not eat animal products. Classic examples include beans on toast (pulses + cereal), rice and lentils (cereal + pulse), and hummus with pitta bread (chickpeas + wheat). As long as a variety of plant proteins are eaten across the day, the body gets everything it needs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. What does LBV stand for? List each part from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Protein complementation does not mean LBV proteins must be eaten at exactly the same time. Eating a variety of plant proteins across the day will provide all eight EAAs. However, the exam often asks for examples of complementary combinations eaten together.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Protein Alternatives</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The specification requires you to know about protein alternatives — products developed to replace or supplement traditional meat and dairy sources. These are increasingly important as more people adopt vegetarian, vegan, or flexitarian diets.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Protein Alternatives</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>TVP (textured vegetable protein):</strong> Made from soya flour that has been processed into a chewy, meat-like texture. It absorbs flavours well and is used in dishes such as chilli, bolognese, and stir-fries. TVP is HBV because it comes from soya.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Tofu:</strong> Made by curdling soya milk and pressing the curds into blocks. It comes in firm and silken varieties. Firm tofu can be sliced and stir-fried; silken tofu works in smoothies and desserts. Tofu is HBV and a good source of calcium when set with calcium salts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Mycoprotein (Quorn™):</strong> Made from a <dfn class="term" data-def="A protein derived from the fungus Fusarium venenatum, grown in fermenters and used as a meat substitute — sold under the brand name Quorn.">mycoprotein</dfn> — a type of fungus called Fusarium venenatum. It is grown in large fermenters using glucose syrup. Mycoprotein is high in protein and fibre, low in saturated fat, and has a texture similar to chicken. It is HBV.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Soya products:</strong> Soya beans are processed into many forms: soya milk, soya mince, soya yoghurt, tempeh (fermented soya), and edamame beans. All are HBV. Soya is one of the 14 major food allergens, so it must be declared on food labels.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n27">Dietary Reference Values</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><dfn class="term" data-def="A set of guidelines published by the government showing the amounts of nutrients needed by different groups of people.">Dietary reference values</dfn> (DRVs) are guidelines that show how much of a nutrient different groups of people need. For protein, the <dfn class="term" data-def="Reference nutrient intake — the amount of a nutrient that is enough for 97.5% of the population.">reference nutrient intake</dfn> (RNI) for adults is 0.75 g per kilogram of bodyweight per day. So a person weighing 70 kg needs approximately 52.5 g of protein daily.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n29">Some groups need more protein than the standard RNI: growing children and teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women, people recovering from illness or surgery, and athletes building muscle. The <dfn class="term" data-def="Reference intakes — daily guidelines shown on food labels indicating the typical amount of energy and nutrients an average adult needs.">reference intakes</dfn> (RIs) shown on food labels use a standard value of 50 g protein per day for an average adult.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">Deficiency and Excess</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Deficiency:</strong> A lack of protein can lead to poor growth in children, slow wound healing, muscle wasting, weakened immunity, and a condition called kwashiorkor (swollen abdomen, thinning hair, skin lesions) — most commonly seen in developing countries where food is scarce. In the UK, protein deficiency is rare but can occur in people with very restricted diets or eating disorders.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Excess:</strong> Eating more protein than the body needs is not stored as protein. Excess amino acids are broken down by the liver in a process called deamination. The nitrogen part is converted to urea and excreted by the kidneys. Over long periods, very high protein intakes may put strain on the kidneys and liver. Excess protein can also be converted to fat and stored, contributing to weight gain.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Exam Focus: Deficiency vs Excess Summary</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Too little protein:</strong> Poor growth, slow healing, muscle wasting, weak immune system, kwashiorkor. <strong>Too much protein:</strong> Strain on kidneys and liver, excess converted to fat (weight gain), deamination produces urea. A balanced diet provides enough protein without excess.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n36">Protein is made of amino acids — 8 are essential (must come from food). HBV sources (animal + soya) contain all 8; LBV sources (most plants) are missing one or more.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Protein complementation means combining LBV sources (e.g. beans on toast) to get all essential amino acids — vital for vegetarians and vegans.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Know the four protein alternatives for the exam: TVP, tofu, mycoprotein (Quorn), and soya products — plus the effects of both deficiency and excess.</li></ul>
