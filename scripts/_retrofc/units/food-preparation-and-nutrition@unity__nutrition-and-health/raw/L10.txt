<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Influences Food Choice?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">People do not simply eat whatever is available — their food choices are shaped by a wide range of factors. For the exam , you need to understand why individuals and groups choose certain foods and avoid others. These factors fall into several categories: personal preferences, practical considerations, religion, medical needs, and ethical beliefs.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The main factors influencing food choice are: <strong>enjoyment</strong>, <strong>culture and tradition</strong>, <strong>season and availability</strong>, <strong>cost</strong>, <strong>time of day</strong>, <strong>activity level</strong>, <strong>religion</strong>, <strong>medical conditions</strong>, <strong>ethical beliefs</strong>, and <strong>marketing</strong>. The exam may ask you to explain how any of these factors affect what people eat.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Personal & Practical Factors</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><strong>Enjoyment</strong> is the most basic driver of food choice — people eat foods they find pleasurable. This is closely linked to <dfn class="term" data-def="The evaluation of food using the five senses: appearance (sight), aroma (smell), taste, texture (touch), and sound.">sensory analysis</dfn>, which considers how food appeals to our senses: <strong>appearance</strong> (colour, shape, presentation), <strong>aroma</strong> (smell), <strong>taste</strong> (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami), <strong>texture</strong> (crunchy, smooth, chewy), and <strong>sound</strong> (the crunch of a crisp, the sizzle of a stir-fry).</p>




<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>Culture and tradition</strong> play a huge role. A traditional British Sunday roast, a Chinese New Year banquet, or an Indian Diwali celebration all involve specific foods tied to cultural identity. Many people eat the foods they grew up with because of family habits and traditions.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Season and availability</strong> affect what is fresh, local, and affordable. Strawberries are cheaper and tastier in summer; root vegetables like parsnips are at their best in winter. Seasonal eating also reduces <dfn class="term" data-def="The distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is consumed, measured to assess the environmental impact of transportation.">food miles</dfn> and environmental impact.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Cost</strong> is a major factor for many families. Processed and convenience foods are often cheaper per calorie than fresh fruit and vegetables, which is one reason why low-income households may have less varied diets. Special offers and budget brands influence choices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Time of day</strong> and <strong>activity level</strong> also matter. A builder doing heavy physical work needs more energy-dense food than an office worker. Breakfast foods differ from evening meals. Athletes may eat high-carbohydrate meals before training to maximise glycogen stores.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Marketing and advertising</strong> have a powerful influence, especially on young people. Packaging design, celebrity endorsements, social media campaigns, and product placement can all shape what consumers buy. This is why there are restrictions on advertising high fat, salt, and sugar (HFSS) foods to children.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Religious Dietary Requirements</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Many religions have specific rules about what followers can and cannot eat. Food preparers must understand these requirements to cater for diverse communities.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Religious Dietary Laws</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Islam (Halal)</strong> — Muslims eat only <dfn class="term" data-def="Food that is permitted under Islamic law, including meat from animals slaughtered according to specific religious requirements with a prayer recited at the time of slaughter.">halal</dfn> food. Pork and its by-products are strictly forbidden (haram). Animals must be slaughtered according to Islamic law, with a prayer recited and the blood drained. Alcohol is also prohibited.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Judaism (Kosher)</strong> — Jewish dietary laws (<dfn class="term" data-def="Food that meets the requirements of Jewish dietary law (kashrut), including the separation of meat and dairy, avoidance of pork and shellfish, and specific slaughter methods.">kosher</dfn>) forbid pork and shellfish. Meat and dairy products must never be prepared, cooked, or eaten together — separate utensils, pans, and even sinks are used. Animals must be slaughtered by a trained person (shochet) with a single cut.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Hinduism</strong> — many Hindus are vegetarian because of the principle of <em>ahimsa</em> (non-violence). The cow is considered sacred, so beef is never eaten. Those who do eat meat avoid beef and may also avoid pork. Fasting is common on certain holy days.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Sikhism</strong> — some Sikhs are vegetarian, while others eat meat but avoid beef and may avoid pork. Food served in the Gurdwara (Sikh temple) is always vegetarian so that everyone can eat together regardless of their personal dietary choice. This communal meal is called <em>langar</em>.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Medical Dietary Needs</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Some people must follow specific diets for medical reasons. Getting these wrong can cause serious illness or even death.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Medical Conditions Affecting Diet</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="An autoimmune condition where the lining of the small intestine is damaged by gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye, causing symptoms such as bloating, diarrhoea, and malabsorption of nutrients.">Coeliac disease</dfn></strong> — an autoimmune condition triggered by <strong>gluten</strong>, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. People with coeliac disease must follow a strict gluten-free diet. Even small traces of gluten damage the lining of the small intestine, leading to bloating, diarrhoea, weight loss, and poor absorption of nutrients. Gluten-free alternatives include rice, potatoes, corn, and oats (if certified gluten-free).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="The inability to properly digest lactose, a sugar found in milk and dairy products, causing symptoms such as bloating, cramps, and diarrhoea.">Lactose intolerance</dfn></strong> — the body cannot digest <strong>lactose</strong>, the natural sugar in milk and dairy products. Symptoms include bloating, stomach cramps, and diarrhoea. Lactose-free milk, plant-based milks (soya, oat, almond), and hard cheeses (which are lower in lactose) are suitable alternatives.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Nut allergy</strong> — one of the most dangerous food allergies. Even tiny amounts of tree nuts or peanuts can trigger <dfn class="term" data-def="A life-threatening allergic reaction that causes swelling of the throat, a drop in blood pressure, and difficulty breathing, requiring immediate treatment with an adrenaline auto-injector.">anaphylaxis</dfn> — a life-threatening reaction causing swelling of the throat, breathing difficulties, and a drop in blood pressure. People with nut allergies carry an adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen). Food labels must clearly declare nuts as one of the 14 major allergens.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Can you recall all 3 items listed here?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The three medical conditions you must know for AQA are <strong>coeliac disease</strong> (no gluten), <strong>lactose intolerance</strong> (no lactose/dairy), and <strong>nut allergy</strong> (can cause anaphylaxis). For each one, know the cause, the symptoms, and suitable food alternatives.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Ethical & Environmental Food Choices</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">An increasing number of consumers make food choices based on ethical beliefs about animal welfare, the environment, and fair treatment of workers.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n25">
<li><strong>Vegetarian</strong> — does not eat meat or fish. Eats dairy products and eggs. Motivations include animal welfare, health, and environmental concerns.</li>
<li><strong>Vegan</strong> — does not eat any animal products at all (no meat, fish, dairy, eggs, or honey). Vegans often also avoid non-food animal products such as leather.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Food produced using farming methods that avoid artificial chemicals, pesticides, and genetically modified organisms, certified by organisations such as the Soil Association.">Organic</dfn></strong> — produced without artificial pesticides, fertilisers, or genetically modified organisms (GMOs). Organic food is certified by bodies like the Soil Association. It is typically more expensive.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="An international certification ensuring that producers in developing countries receive a fair price for their products, along with better working conditions and community investment.">Fairtrade</dfn></strong> — products guarantee farmers in developing countries receive a fair price. Common Fairtrade items include bananas, coffee, chocolate, and tea.</li>
<li><strong>Free-range</strong> — animals have access to outdoor space, which improves animal welfare. Free-range eggs and chicken are common examples.</li>
<li><strong>Food miles</strong> — the distance food travels from producer to consumer. Locally sourced food has fewer food miles, reducing carbon emissions from transport. Seasonal eating also reduces the need for long-distance imports.</li>
<li><strong>Sustainability</strong> — choosing foods produced in ways that protect the environment for future generations, such as sustainably caught fish (MSC certified) or food with minimal packaging.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Food Labelling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27">Food labelling is governed by UK and EU law. Labels give consumers the information they need to make safe and informed choices. There are <strong>mandatory</strong> requirements — things that must appear on every pre-packed food label by law.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Mandatory Label Information</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n28">By law, every pre-packed food must display:</p>
    <ul data-narration-id="n29">
    <li><strong>Name of the food</strong> — must accurately describe the product (e.g. “strawberry flavour yoghurt” if it contains no real strawberries)</li>
    <li><strong>Ingredients list</strong> — listed in <strong>descending order of weight</strong>, so the first ingredient is the one present in the largest amount</li>
    <li><strong>Allergen declaration</strong> — the <strong>14 major allergens</strong> must be highlighted in <strong>bold</strong>, italics, or CAPITALS within the ingredients list</li>
    <li><strong>Net quantity</strong> — the weight (grams/kilograms) or volume (millilitres/litres) of the product</li>
    <li><strong>Date marking</strong> — “use by” dates for safety (must not be eaten after this date) and “best before” dates for quality (safe but may not be at best quality)</li>
    <li><strong>Storage conditions</strong> — how to store the product (e.g. “keep refrigerated below 5°C”)</li>
    <li><strong>Manufacturer/packer name and address</strong> — so consumers know who made the product</li>
    <li><strong>Country of origin</strong> — required for certain foods such as beef, fish, honey, olive oil, and fresh fruit/vegetables</li>
    <li><strong>Nutrition information</strong> — a panel showing energy (kJ/kcal), fat, saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt per 100g and per serving</li>
    <li><strong>Cooking/preparation instructions</strong> — where necessary for safe consumption</li>
    </ul>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">The 14 Major Allergens</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31">UK law requires that <strong>14 specific allergens</strong> are clearly declared on food labels whenever they are used as ingredients. These allergens must be emphasised (usually in <strong>bold</strong>) in the ingredients list so that people with allergies can quickly identify them.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — The 14 Allergens</div>
  <p>The 14 major allergens are: <strong>celery</strong>, <strong>cereals containing gluten</strong> (wheat, barley, rye, oats), <strong>crustaceans</strong> (prawns, crab, lobster), <strong>eggs</strong>, <strong>fish</strong>, <strong>lupin</strong>, <strong>milk</strong>, <strong>molluscs</strong> (mussels, squid, snails), <strong>mustard</strong>, <strong>tree nuts</strong> (almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, cashews, pecans, brazils, pistachios, macadamias), <strong>peanuts</strong>, <strong>sesame</strong>, <strong>soya</strong>, and <strong>sulphur dioxide/sulphites</strong>. You must be able to list these For the exam .</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n33">Traffic Light Labelling & Nutrition Panels</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n34">The <dfn class="term" data-def="A voluntary front-of-pack colour-coded labelling system used in the UK that shows whether a food is high (red), medium (amber), or low (green) in fat, saturated fat, sugar, and salt.">traffic light system</dfn> is a voluntary front-of-pack labelling scheme used in the UK. It uses <strong>red</strong>, <strong>amber</strong>, and <strong>green</strong> colour coding to show at a glance whether a food is high, medium, or low in four key nutrients: <strong>fat</strong>, <strong>saturated fat</strong>, <strong>sugar</strong>, and <strong>salt</strong>.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n35">
<li><strong>Red</strong> — high in this nutrient. Eat these foods less often or in small amounts.</li>
<li><strong>Amber</strong> — medium. An acceptable choice most of the time.</li>
<li><strong>Green</strong> — low in this nutrient. A healthier choice.</li>
</ul>
<p data-narration-id="n36">The mandatory <strong>nutrition panel</strong> on the back of pack shows values <strong>per 100g</strong> (for comparison between products) and <strong>per serving</strong> (to show what you actually get in a portion). It includes energy (in kJ and kcal), fat, saturated fat, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, and salt.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n37"><dfn class="term" data-def="The amount of a nutrient that an average adult (based on a 2000 kcal diet for women) needs each day, shown on food labels as a percentage to help consumers judge how much of their daily allowance one serving provides.">Reference Intakes</dfn> (RIs) are guidelines showing the maximum amount of energy and nutrients an average adult should consume daily. They are based on a <strong>2,000 kcal diet</strong> for an average woman. Labels often show each nutrient as a percentage of the RI, helping consumers judge how a product fits into their overall daily diet.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n38" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Recall the specific examples given for health claims on food packaging.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact — Health Claims</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="Statements on food packaging claiming a health benefit, such as 'helps maintain normal blood cholesterol', which must be authorised and scientifically proven before they can appear on labels.">Health claims</dfn> on food packaging (e.g. “helps maintain normal blood cholesterol”) must be <strong>authorised and scientifically proven</strong> before they can appear. Manufacturers cannot make unproven health claims. Nutrition claims like “low fat” or “high fibre” also have legally defined thresholds — a product labelled “low fat” must contain no more than 3g of fat per 100g.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n40">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n41">Food choice is influenced by enjoyment, culture, cost, season, religion (Halal, Kosher, Hindu, Sikh rules), medical needs (coeliac disease, lactose intolerance, nut allergy), and ethical beliefs (vegetarian, vegan, organic, Fairtrade, food miles).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n42">Food labels must include the food name, ingredients in descending weight order, the 14 allergens in bold, dates, storage, nutrition information, and manufacturer details — the traffic light system (red/amber/green for fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt) is voluntary.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n43">Reference Intakes are based on a 2,000 kcal daily diet and health claims must be authorised and scientifically proven before they can appear on packaging.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n39">When the exam asks you to <em>explain</em> a factor influencing food choice, always give a <strong>specific example</strong> linked to a <strong>reason</strong>. Don’t just write “religion affects food choice” — say “a Muslim would choose halal chicken <strong>because</strong> Islamic law requires animals to be slaughtered with a prayer recited and the blood drained.” For labelling questions, know the difference between mandatory and voluntary information, and remember that allergens must be in <strong>bold</strong> in the ingredients list. If asked about the traffic light system, explain what each colour means and name the four nutrients it covers: fat, saturated fat, sugar, and salt.</p>
