--- DESCRIPTION ---
How bacteria, moulds and yeasts spoil food, and the causes of contamination and food poisoning.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Food Spoilage?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process by which food deteriorates in quality, becoming unfit or unpleasant to eat.">Food spoilage</dfn> is the process by which food deteriorates and becomes unfit to eat. It is caused by the growth of <dfn class="term" data-def="Single-celled micro-organisms that multiply rapidly in warm, moist conditions and can cause food poisoning.">bacteria</dfn>, <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of fungus that grows on food surfaces, producing visible fuzzy patches and releasing spores into the air.">moulds</dfn>, and <dfn class="term" data-def="Single-celled fungi used in baking and brewing, but which can also cause food spoilage by fermenting sugars.">yeasts</dfn>. These are all types of <dfn class="term" data-def="Tiny living organisms, including bacteria, moulds and yeasts, that are too small to see without a microscope.">micro-organisms</dfn> — they are too small to see without a microscope, but their effects on food are very visible.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Bacteria are the most dangerous because they can cause food poisoning. Moulds grow on the surface of food and produce visible fuzzy patches. Yeasts ferment sugars in food, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol, which changes the taste and smell.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Define not all micro-organisms in your own words.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Not all micro-organisms are harmful. Some bacteria are used to make yoghurt and cheese. Yeast is essential for bread-making. Moulds give blue cheese its flavour. The difference is whether their growth is controlled or uncontrolled.</p>




</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Signs of Food Spoilage</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">You can detect spoiled food using your senses. The main signs are:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Colour changes</strong> — meat may turn grey or green, bread may develop blue or white spots, and fruit may turn brown. <strong>Texture changes</strong> — food becomes slimy, soft, or mushy when bacteria break down its structure. <strong>Smell</strong> — spoiled food often produces a sour, rancid, or “off” odour as micro-organisms release waste products. <strong>Taste</strong> — food tastes sour, bitter, or fermented (though you should never taste food you suspect is spoiled). <strong>Visible mould</strong> — fuzzy patches of white, green, blue, or black growth appear on the surface.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n8" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write down what you recall about pathogenic bacteria do not cause visible.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p><dfn class="term" data-def="Harmful bacteria that cause illness but do not change the appearance, smell, or taste of food.">Pathogenic bacteria</dfn> do <strong>not</strong> cause visible signs of spoilage. Food can look, smell, and taste perfectly normal but still contain dangerous levels of harmful bacteria. This is why food safety rules matter even when food appears fine.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Conditions for Bacterial Growth</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Bacteria need specific conditions to multiply. If any of these conditions are removed, bacterial growth slows down or stops. You can remember them using the mnemonic <strong>FATTOM</strong>: Food, Acidity, Time, Temperature, Oxygen, Moisture.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Six Conditions for Bacterial Growth (FATTOM)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Food</strong> — bacteria need nutrients to grow. High-protein foods like meat, dairy, and eggs are ideal. These are called <dfn class="term" data-def="Foods that are ready to eat, high in protein, and moist, making them ideal for bacterial growth. Examples include cooked meat, dairy products, and cooked rice.">high-risk foods</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Acidity (pH)</strong> — most bacteria prefer neutral pH (around 7). Acidic conditions (low pH) slow growth, which is why vinegar and lemon juice are used as preservatives.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Time</strong> — in ideal conditions, bacteria can divide every 10–20 minutes. A single bacterium can become millions within just a few hours. This is called <dfn class="term" data-def="The rapid increase in bacterial numbers that occurs when conditions are favourable, with cells dividing every 10 to 20 minutes.">binary fission</dfn>.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Temperature</strong> — bacteria multiply fastest between 5°C and 63°C. This range is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="The temperature range between 5 and 63 degrees Celsius in which bacteria multiply most rapidly.">danger zone</dfn>. Below 5°C, growth slows dramatically. Above 63°C, most bacteria are killed. Food must be kept out of the danger zone as much as possible.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Oxygen</strong> — most food-poisoning bacteria are aerobic (they need oxygen). However, some are anaerobic and can grow without oxygen, such as <em>Clostridium perfringens</em> in vacuum-packed foods.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Moisture</strong> — bacteria need water to grow. Dried foods like pasta, rice, and flour have very low moisture content, so bacteria cannot multiply. This is why drying is an effective preservation method.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Enzymic Browning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="A chemical reaction that occurs when enzymes in cut fruit and vegetables are exposed to oxygen, causing the surface to turn brown.">Enzymic browning</dfn> is a type of food spoilage caused by enzymes, not micro-organisms. When fruit or vegetables are cut, enzymes inside the cells react with oxygen in the air. This causes the surface to turn brown — think of a sliced apple left on a worktop.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">You can prevent enzymic browning in three ways: <strong>add acid</strong> (lemon juice lowers the pH and slows the enzyme reaction), <strong>blanching</strong> (briefly plunging food into boiling water destroys the enzymes), or <strong>excluding air</strong> (submerging food in water or wrapping tightly prevents oxygen reaching the surface).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n20">Types of Contamination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><dfn class="term" data-def="The presence of unwanted or harmful substances in food that make it unsafe to eat.">Food contamination</dfn> means that something unwanted or harmful has entered the food. There are four types, and the exam may ask you to give examples of each.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Four Types of Food Contamination</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Microbiological contamination</strong> — caused by bacteria, viruses, moulds, or parasites entering food. This is the most common cause of food poisoning. Examples include <em>Salmonella</em> from raw chicken and <em>E. coli</em> from undercooked beef.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Chemical contamination</strong> — caused by cleaning products, pesticides, or other chemicals coming into contact with food. For example, storing food in containers previously used for chemicals, or not rinsing surfaces after using antibacterial spray.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Physical contamination</strong> — caused by foreign objects entering food, such as hair, glass, plasters, packaging, insects, or pieces of equipment. This can cause choking, broken teeth, or cuts.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Allergenic contamination</strong> — caused by <dfn class="term" data-def="Substances found in food that trigger an immune response in sensitive individuals, such as nuts, milk, eggs, or gluten.">allergens</dfn> being accidentally transferred into food. For example, using the same knife to cut a nut cake and then a plain cake. The 14 major allergens must be declared by law in the UK.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n26">Cross-Contamination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><dfn class="term" data-def="The transfer of harmful bacteria or other contaminants from one food, surface, or piece of equipment to another.">Cross-contamination</dfn> is the transfer of harmful bacteria from one food or surface to another. It is one of the most common causes of food poisoning in the home and in commercial kitchens.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28">The main routes of cross-contamination are: <strong>hands</strong> — touching raw meat then handling ready-to-eat food without washing hands; <strong>chopping boards</strong> — using the same board for raw chicken and salad; <strong>utensils</strong> — using the same knife for raw and cooked food; <strong>cloths</strong> — wiping surfaces with a dirty cloth spreads bacteria; and <strong>dripping</strong> — raw meat juices dripping onto ready-to-eat food below in the fridge.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. List the exam technique advice given here.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>In the exam, always store raw meat on the <strong>bottom shelf</strong> of the fridge to prevent juices dripping onto other foods. Use colour-coded chopping boards: red for raw meat, green for salad and vegetables, blue for raw fish, yellow for cooked meat.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">High-Risk Foods</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31">High-risk foods are ready-to-eat foods that are high in protein and moisture. Because they will not be cooked again before eating, any bacteria present will not be destroyed. Examples include: <strong>cooked meat</strong> and poultry, <strong>dairy products</strong> (milk, cream, soft cheese), <strong>cooked rice</strong> (<em>Bacillus cereus</em> produces toxins in reheated rice), <strong>shellfish</strong>, <strong>eggs</strong> and egg products (mayonnaise, custard).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n32">Key Pathogenic Bacteria</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n33">You need to know six key <dfn class="term" data-def="Disease-causing bacteria that can multiply in food and produce toxins, leading to food poisoning.">pathogens</dfn> for the exam, including where they are found and how to prevent them.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Six Key Pathogens</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong><em>Salmonella</em></strong> — found in raw poultry, eggs, and raw meat. Causes vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, and abdominal cramps. Prevented by thorough cooking (core temperature 75°C) and avoiding raw egg in uncooked dishes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong><em>E. coli</em> (O157)</strong> — found in undercooked beef, raw milk, and contaminated water. Can cause severe bloody diarrhoea and kidney failure. Prevented by cooking minced beef thoroughly and avoiding cross-contamination.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong><em>Campylobacter</em></strong> — the most common cause of food poisoning in the UK. Found on raw poultry. Causes diarrhoea (often bloody), cramping, and fever. Prevented by not washing raw chicken (which splashes bacteria) and cooking poultry to 75°C.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong><em>Staphylococcus aureus</em></strong> — carried on the skin, in the nose, and in infected cuts of food handlers. Transferred to food by coughing, sneezing, or touching food with unwashed hands. Prevented by good personal hygiene, covering cuts with blue waterproof plasters, and not handling food when ill.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38"><strong><em>Listeria</em></strong> — found in soft cheeses (Brie, Camembert), pâté, and ready-to-eat salads. Unlike most bacteria, <em>Listeria</em> can grow at fridge temperatures (below 5°C). Particularly dangerous for pregnant women. Prevented by avoiding high-risk chilled foods and checking use-by dates.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39"><strong><em>Clostridium perfringens</em></strong> — found in soil, dust, and the intestines of animals. Causes illness when food (especially meat and poultry) is cooked, cooled too slowly, and then reheated. The bacterium forms heat-resistant spores. Prevented by cooling food quickly (within 90 minutes) and reheating to at least 75°C.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n40">Symptoms of Food Poisoning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n41"><dfn class="term" data-def="An illness caused by eating food contaminated with pathogenic bacteria, viruses, or their toxins.">Food poisoning</dfn> symptoms typically appear 1–72 hours after eating contaminated food. The most common symptoms are: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, and fever. In severe cases, food poisoning can cause dehydration, kidney failure, and even death.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n42">Vulnerable Groups</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n43">Some people are more vulnerable to food poisoning because their immune systems are weaker. These <dfn class="term" data-def="Groups of people who are more susceptible to food poisoning due to weaker immune systems, including young children, the elderly, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals.">at-risk groups</dfn> include: <strong>young children</strong> (underdeveloped immune system), <strong>elderly people</strong> (declining immune function), <strong>pregnant women</strong> (<em>Listeria</em> and <em>Salmonella</em> are particularly dangerous and can harm the unborn baby), and <strong>immunocompromised individuals</strong> (people with conditions like HIV/AIDS or those undergoing chemotherapy).</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Why These Groups Are More at Risk</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n44">Young children have immune systems that are still developing, so they cannot fight off infections as effectively as adults. Elderly people experience natural decline in immune function, and they may also be dehydrated or malnourished, which makes recovery harder. Pregnant women’s immune systems are naturally suppressed to protect the baby, which makes them more susceptible to infections like <em>Listeria</em>. Immunocompromised individuals have weakened immune defences due to illness or medical treatment, so even small amounts of bacteria can cause serious illness.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n46">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n47">Bacteria multiply rapidly in the danger zone (5–63°C) and need food, moisture, warmth, time, suitable pH, and oxygen to grow — removing any condition slows or stops growth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n48">There are four types of contamination (microbiological, chemical, physical, allergenic) and cross-contamination spreads bacteria via hands, boards, utensils, cloths, and dripping.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n49">Know the six key pathogens by name, food source, and prevention method — and remember that vulnerable groups (children, elderly, pregnant, immunocompromised) face the greatest risk.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n45">When answering questions on food spoilage and contamination, always be <strong>specific</strong>. Do not just say “bacteria grow in warm conditions” — state the exact danger zone temperature range (5–63°C). Name specific pathogens and link them to specific foods. For example, “<em>Campylobacter</em> is found on raw poultry” is far stronger than “bacteria can be found on chicken.” In longer-answer questions, always explain <em>how</em> contamination can be prevented, not just what causes it.</p>
