How recording, electronic instruments and consumer formats have evolved over time.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Evolution of Music Technology</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Music technology has transformed dramatically over the past century. From early analogue tape machines to modern <dfn class="term" data-def="Digital Audio Workstation — software used to record, edit and produce music on a computer.">DAWs</dfn>, each development has changed how music is recorded, produced and consumed. This lesson covers the key milestones you need to know for your exam.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Recording Technology</h2>
<h3 data-narration-id="n4">Analogue Recording</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Early recording used <dfn class="term" data-def="A recording medium where sound is stored as a continuous magnetic signal on tape.">magnetic tape</dfn>. Sound was captured as a continuous analogue signal on tape reels. The key development was <dfn class="term" data-def="A recording technique that allows multiple separate audio tracks to be recorded independently on the same tape.">multitrack recording</dfn>, which allowed engineers to record different instruments on separate tracks and mix them later.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Multitrack tape machines evolved through several formats: 2-track (stereo), 4-track, 8-track, 16-track and eventually 24-track. Each increase in track count gave producers more flexibility. The Beatles’ <em>Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band</em> (1967) was famously recorded on 4-track, while later albums used 24-track machines for far greater complexity.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list the five multitrack tape formats in order from fewest to most tracks.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Analogue multitrack tape formats progressed through 2-track, 4-track, 8-track, 16-track and 24-track, giving producers increasing control over individual instruments.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n8">Digital Recording</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n9">Digital recording converts sound into binary data. Key digital recording formats include:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n10"><strong>DASH</strong> (Digital Audio Stationary Head) — an early digital tape format used in professional studios.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n11"><strong>ADAT</strong> (Alesis Digital Audio Tape) — made digital multitrack recording affordable for home studios by recording 8 tracks onto VHS tape.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Hard disc recording</strong> — stored audio directly on computer hard drives, allowing non-linear editing (you could jump to any point instantly).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n13"><strong>DAW</strong> — Digital Audio Workstations combined recording, editing and mixing into a single software environment on a computer.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Electronic Instruments</h2>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Synthesisers, Samplers & Drum Machines</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An electronic instrument that generates sound using oscillators, filters and envelopes.">synthesiser</dfn> generates sound electronically. Analogue synthesisers use voltage-controlled oscillators to create waveforms, while digital synthesisers use mathematical algorithms. Both can shape sound using filters and envelopes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n16">A <dfn class="term" data-def="An instrument that records and plays back short audio recordings (samples) which can be triggered and manipulated.">sampler</dfn> records and plays back short audio clips called samples. Digital samplers store samples in memory and allow them to be triggered, pitched and layered. The <dfn class="term" data-def="An early analogue tape-based sampler that used pre-recorded tape strips to produce realistic instrument sounds.">Mellotron</dfn> was an early analogue tape-based sampler — it played pre-recorded tape strips of real instruments when keys were pressed.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="An electronic instrument that produces percussive sounds, either from synthesis or stored samples.">Drum machines</dfn> produce percussive sounds electronically. Early drum machines used analogue synthesis (like the Roland TR-808), while later models used digital samples of real drums. They allowed producers to program rhythms without a live drummer.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sequencers, Workstations & MIDI</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18">A <dfn class="term" data-def="A device or software that records and plays back a sequence of musical events (notes, rhythms, controls) in order.">sequencer</dfn> records and plays back musical events in order. Hardware sequencers store note data and trigger sounds automatically, allowing complex arrangements to be programmed step by step.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="An all-in-one electronic instrument combining a synthesiser, sampler, sequencer and effects in a single unit.">Keyboard workstations</dfn> combine a synthesiser, sampler, sequencer and effects into one unit. They allow musicians to compose, arrange and produce entire tracks from a single instrument.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Musical Instrument Digital Interface — a protocol that allows electronic instruments and computers to communicate note, control and timing data.">MIDI</dfn> (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) was introduced in 1983. It allows electronic instruments and computers to communicate by sending note, control and timing data — not audio. MIDI revolutionised music production by letting multiple devices play in sync.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Hardware Effects</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">Before software plug-ins, studios used dedicated hardware units to process sound:</p>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Spring reverb</strong> and <strong>plate reverb</strong> — physical devices that created reverberation by vibrating metal springs or plates.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Tape delay</strong> — used tape loops to create echo effects by recording and replaying audio with a time gap.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Dynamic processors</strong> — compressors, limiters and gates that controlled volume levels.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Compact effects pedals</strong> — small floor units used by guitarists for distortion, chorus, delay and other effects.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Digital multi-effects</strong> — units that combined several effects in one device.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Pitch correction</strong> — hardware that adjusted vocal or instrument pitch in real time (e.g. Auto-Tune).</li>
<li data-narration-id="n29"><strong>Looping devices</strong> — pedals that record and replay audio phrases for live layering.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-narration-id="n30">Consumer Formats</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31">The way people listen to music has changed as much as the way it is made. Consumer formats have evolved from physical media to digital files and streaming.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n32" data-revision-tip="Write from memory: list the seven consumer formats in chronological order.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Consumer formats in approximate chronological order: vinyl, cassette, CD, minidisc, MP3, music video, streaming. Each format changed how music was distributed and consumed.</p>
</div>

<p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Vinyl</strong> records were the first mass consumer format. <strong>Cassettes</strong> made music portable. <strong>CDs</strong> offered digital clarity. <strong>Minidisc</strong> combined digital quality with recordability but was short-lived. <strong>MP3</strong> files allowed music to be compressed and shared online. <strong>Music videos</strong> became a promotional format in their own right. Today, <strong>streaming</strong> services deliver music instantly over the internet, changing how artists earn revenue.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n35">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n36">Analogue recording used magnetic tape in multitrack formats (2, 4, 8, 16, 24 tracks). Digital recording progressed through DASH, ADAT, hard disc and DAWs.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n37">Electronic instruments include synthesisers, samplers, drum machines, sequencers, keyboard workstations and MIDI controllers.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n38">Consumer formats evolved from vinyl and cassette through CD, minidisc and MP3 to streaming.</li>
</ul>

For extended responses, show how technologies build on each other: analogue tape led to digital tape, which led to hard disc recording, which led to DAWs.</p>
