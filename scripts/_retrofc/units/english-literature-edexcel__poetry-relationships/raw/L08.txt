<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Exam Day Preparation</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">This final lesson brings together everything you have learned across the collection. Your anthology comparison question will test your ability to select, compare and analyse two poems under timed conditions. The challenge is not just knowledge — it is making fast, confident decisions about which poem to choose and what to write about. This lesson gives you the tools to do that: a decision-making framework, a complete quotation bank for all 15 poems, and strategies for writing under pressure.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">The Question Format</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Anthology comparison questions typically follow a similar pattern: you are asked to compare how poets present a given theme or aspect of relationships in a named poem and one other poem from your studied collection. The named poem is usually printed in full on the question paper. You choose the second poem from memory. You are assessed on <dfn class="term" data-def="Assessment Objective 2: the ability to analyse language, form and structure used by a writer to create meanings and effects.">AO2</dfn> (language, form and structure) and <dfn class="term" data-def="Assessment Objective 3: understanding the relationships between texts and the contexts in which they were written.">AO3</dfn> (context). Check your exam board’s specimen paper for the exact wording, mark allocations and recommended timings.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain the main point in your own words from memory, then check yourself.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>You do not need to prepare all 15 poems equally. Choose 4–5 poems that you know deeply — these become your “go-to” choices for any question. A well-analysed poem you know thoroughly will always outscore a poem you half-remember. Quality of analysis beats breadth of knowledge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Choosing Your Comparison Poem: A Decision Framework</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">When you see the named poem and the theme, ask yourself three questions:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>1. Which of my prepared poems connects to this theme?</strong> If the theme is “loss,” your options include “Neutral Tones,” “A Complaint,” “One Flesh,” “La Belle Dame Sans Merci.” If it is “power,” consider “My Last Duchess,” “Valentine,” “The Manhunt.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>2. Can I identify both similarities AND differences?</strong> The best comparisons have both. A poem that is simply “the same” as the named poem gives you less to say than one with interesting contrasts in method, tone or context.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>3. Can I discuss form and structure, not just language?</strong> If your only point is about imagery, you are missing AO2 marks. Choose a poem where you can compare form (sonnet vs free verse), structure (cyclical vs linear) or voice (dramatic monologue vs first person).</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Quotation Bank: All 15 Poems</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">Below are 3–4 key quotations per poem. For your chosen “go-to” poems, memorise all of these. For the others, know at least one. Each quotation is paired with its main <dfn class="term" data-def="The specific language, form or structural feature that the quotation best demonstrates for analytical purposes.">analytical focus</dfn>.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Romantic Poems: Keats, Baillie, Byron, Wordsworth</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>La Belle Dame Sans Merci (Keats):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">• “Full beautiful — a faery’s child” — supernatural, idealised beauty (AO2: imagery). • “La Belle Dame sans Merci / Thee hath in thrall” — love as enslavement (AO2: metaphor). • “Alone and palely loitering” — devastation after love’s loss (AO2: pathetic fallacy). • “And no birds sing” — cyclical refrain, nature emptied (AO2: structure).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>A Child to his Sick Grandfather (Baillie):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n22">• “Grand-dad, they say you’re old and frail” — innocent directness cataloguing decline (AO2: voice). • “I’ll lead you kindly by the hand” — child’s tender, inadequate comfort (AO2: voice). • Rhyming couplets — nursery-rhyme quality contrasts with death (AO2: form).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>She Walks in Beauty (Byron):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n24">• “She walks in beauty, like the night / Of cloudless climes and starry skies” — beauty compared to darkness (AO2: simile). • “One shade the more, one ray the less” — perfect balance (AO2: antithesis). • “A mind at peace with all below, / A heart whose love is innocent” — inner beauty (AO2: structure — external to internal).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>A Complaint (Wordsworth):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">• “A fountain at my fond heart’s door” — love as visible abundance (AO2: extended metaphor). • “A well of love — it may be deep” — dashes create doubt (AO2: punctuation). • Three-stanza structure: past, present, reflection (AO2: structure).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Neutral Tones (Hardy):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n28">• “The sun was white, as though chidden of God” — drained, punished landscape (AO2: pathetic fallacy). • “Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove / Over tedious riddles” — boredom and indifference (AO2: simile). • “And a pond edged with greyish leaves” — cyclical return to the scene (AO2: structure).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n29"><strong>My Last Duchess (Browning):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n30">• “That’s my last Duchess painted on the wall” — possessive pronoun, ownership (AO2: word choice). • “I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together” — euphemism for murder (AO2: understatement). • “Looking as if she were alive” — casually menacing (AO2: dramatic irony). • Single-stanza dramatic monologue (AO2: form).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n31"><strong>Sonnet 43 (Barrett Browning):</strong></p>
    <p data-narration-id="n32">• “How do I love thee? Let me count the ways” — quantifying the unquantifiable (AO2: rhetorical question). • “I love thee to the depth and breadth and height / My soul can reach” — spatial metaphor for limitless love (AO2: imagery). • “I shall but love thee better after death” — love transcending mortality (AO2: volta). • Petrarchan sonnet form — female poet subverting male tradition (AO3: context).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>1st Date She/He (Cope):</strong> • Dual perspective — both sides of dating anxiety (AO2: structure). • Colloquial inner monologue (AO2: voice).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Valentine (Duffy):</strong> • “Not a red rose or a satin heart” — rejecting clichés (AO2: opening). • “It will blind you with tears” — love causes pain (AO2: metaphor). • “Its scent will cling to your fingers, / cling to your knife” — darkening to violence (AO2: tone shift).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>One Flesh (Jennings):</strong> • “Lying apart now, each in a separate bed” — physical and emotional distance (AO2: imagery). • “Tossed up like flotsam from a former passion” — wreckage of love (AO2: simile). • “Strangely apart, yet strangely close together” — paradox (AO2: language). • “Whose fire from which I came, has now grown cold?” — unsettling final question (AO2: volta).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n36"><strong>i wanna be yours (Cooper Clarke):</strong> • “I wanna be your vacuum cleaner / breathing in your dust” — domestic devotion (AO2: metaphor). • “I will not run out” — sincere constancy (AO2: tone shift). • Anaphora of “I wanna be your” — hypnotic repetition (AO2: structure).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n37"><strong>Love’s Dog (Hatfield):</strong> • “What I love about love is its / What I hate about love is its” — love as paradox (AO2: structure). • Oxymorons throughout (AO2: language).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n38"><strong>Nettles (Scannell):</strong> • “A regiment of spite” — military semantic field (AO2: imagery). • “Tall as soldiers” — nettles regrow, protection is futile (AO2: metaphor). • Single stanza, blank verse (AO2: form).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n39"><strong>The Manhunt (Armitage):</strong> • “The damaged, porcelain collar-bone” — fragile beauty (AO2: juxtaposition). • “The frozen river which ran through his face” — scar as landscape (AO2: metaphor). • “Only then would he let me” — gradual access (AO2: structure). • Inward journey: external wounds to hidden PTSD (AO2: structure).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n40"><strong>My Father Would Not Show Us (de Kok):</strong> • “Would not show us,” “would not let us see” — repeated negatives (AO2: language). • Free verse with irregular lines (AO2: form). • The body as concealed text (AO2: imagery).</p>
  </div></div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n41" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain both sides of the contrast in one sentence each from memory.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The strongest students walk into the exam with 3–4 pre-planned comparison pairings. For example: “If they ask about loss, I’ll compare with Neutral Tones. If they ask about power, I’ll compare with My Last Duchess. If they ask about family, I’ll compare with Nettles.” Adapt these to the specific question wording, but having a starting point saves valuable planning time.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n42">Writing Under Pressure: Final Checklist</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n43"><strong>Before writing:</strong> Read the question twice. Identify the theme. Choose your poem. Plan 3 points (each covering both poems). <strong>While writing:</strong> Start every paragraph with a comparative point. Quote from both poems (the named poem from the paper, your chosen poem from memory). Analyse <em>how</em> (AO2), not just <em>what</em>. Weave in context (AO3). <strong>After writing:</strong> Check you have discussed both poems in every paragraph. Check you have mentioned form or structure at least once. Check for spelling and punctuation.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n45">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n46">Prepare 4–5 “go-to” poems deeply rather than trying to memorise all 15 equally. Know form, key technique, context and 4–5 short quotations for each.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n47">Use the decision framework: which of my poems fits this theme? Can I find similarities AND differences? Can I discuss form/structure as well as language?</li>
<li data-narration-id="n48">Plan before writing, compare throughout every paragraph, and weave AO3 context into your analysis rather than adding it as a separate paragraph.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n44">If you forget a quotation during the exam, <strong>don’t panic</strong>. You can paraphrase the line and still earn marks for analysis: “Keats describes the knight as being held in enslavement by the beautiful lady, suggesting that love has removed his freedom and agency.” A well-analysed paraphrase beats an unanalysed quotation. The examiner rewards <dfn class="term" data-def="Close, detailed analysis of specific language choices and their effects on meaning.">close analysis</dfn> of meaning, not just memorised words.</p>
