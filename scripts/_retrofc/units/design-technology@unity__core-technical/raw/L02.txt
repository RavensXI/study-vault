<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Energy Generation & Storage</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2">Every product that uses power needs an energy source. As a designer, you must understand how energy is generated and stored so you can select the right power system for your product. This lesson covers <dfn class="term" data-def="Energy sources formed over millions of years from the remains of ancient organisms, including coal, oil and natural gas.">fossil fuels</dfn>, nuclear power, renewable energy and energy storage systems.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n3">Fossil Fuels</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n4">Fossil fuels — coal, oil and natural gas — are burned in power stations to heat water, producing steam that turns turbines connected to generators. They currently supply a large share of the UK’s electricity. Coal produces the most carbon dioxide per unit of energy, while natural gas is the cleanest of the three.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: List three arguments for and three arguments against using fossil fuels.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Arguments for fossil fuels: reliable, high energy output, existing infrastructure. Arguments against: finite resources that will run out, release greenhouse gases causing climate change, extraction damages habitats.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Nuclear Power</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n7"><dfn class="term" data-def="Energy released by splitting atoms of radioactive elements such as uranium in a controlled chain reaction.">Nuclear power</dfn> generates electricity through <dfn class="term" data-def="A process in which the nucleus of an atom is split into two smaller nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy.">nuclear fission</dfn> — splitting atoms of uranium to release heat, which produces steam to drive turbines. A small amount of fuel generates a huge amount of energy, and nuclear power stations produce no carbon dioxide during operation.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n8">However, nuclear power produces radioactive waste that remains dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored securely. Building and decommissioning nuclear power stations is extremely expensive, and accidents (though rare) can have catastrophic consequences.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Renewable Energy</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n10"><dfn class="term" data-def="Energy from sources that are naturally replenished and will not run out, such as wind, solar and tidal power.">Renewable energy</dfn> comes from sources that will not run out. The main types are:</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Types of Renewable Energy</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Wind power</strong> uses turbines to convert kinetic energy from the wind into electricity. Wind farms can be onshore or offshore. They produce no emissions during operation but depend on weather conditions and can affect wildlife.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><strong>Solar power</strong> uses photovoltaic cells to convert sunlight into electricity, or solar thermal panels to heat water. Solar panels are silent and low-maintenance but only generate power during daylight and are less effective in cloudy climates.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Tidal power</strong> harnesses the movement of tides using barrages or underwater turbines. Tides are predictable, making this a reliable source, but installation costs are high and it can affect marine ecosystems.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Hydroelectric power</strong> uses flowing or falling water (usually from a dam) to turn turbines. It is highly reliable and can respond quickly to demand, but large dams flood valleys and displace communities.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Biomass</strong> generates energy by burning organic materials such as wood, crops or waste. It is considered carbon neutral because the plants absorbed CO₂ while growing, but it still produces air pollution and requires large areas of land for fuel crops.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n16" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: Name the five types of renewable energy and give one advantage of each.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The five main renewable energy sources are wind, solar, tidal, hydroelectric and biomass. All produce little or no greenhouse gas emissions during operation, but each has limitations related to cost, reliability or environmental impact.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Energy Storage Systems</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Energy must often be stored for later use. <dfn class="term" data-def="A storage system where water is pumped uphill to a reservoir when demand is low, then released downhill through turbines to generate electricity when demand is high.">Kinetic pumped storage</dfn> systems pump water uphill to a reservoir when electricity demand is low (often at night). When demand rises, the water is released downhill through turbines to generate electricity almost instantly. Dinorwig in Wales is a well-known example.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n19"><dfn class="term" data-def="Batteries that use a chemical reaction to produce electrical energy; they cannot be recharged and are discarded after use.">Alkaline batteries</dfn> are single-use batteries found in everyday products like torches and remote controls. <dfn class="term" data-def="Batteries that can be charged, discharged and charged again many times, such as lithium-ion batteries in phones and laptops.">Rechargeable batteries</dfn> (such as lithium-ion) can be charged and discharged hundreds of times, making them essential for portable electronics, electric vehicles and power tools.</p>

<div class="collapsible">
<button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
<span>Choosing the Right Energy Source</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n20">When selecting an energy source for a product, designers consider: the power requirement (how much energy is needed), portability (does the product move?), environmental impact, cost, availability and reliability. A portable speaker needs a rechargeable battery; a factory needs mains electricity from the grid. The choice of energy source is always a compromise between these factors.</p>
</div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li data-narration-id="n23">Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) are reliable but finite and produce greenhouse gases; nuclear power is low-carbon but creates radioactive waste.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n24">Renewable sources (wind, solar, tidal, hydroelectric, biomass) produce little or no emissions but each has limitations around cost, reliability or environmental impact.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">Energy storage options include kinetic pumped storage, alkaline batteries and rechargeable (lithium-ion) batteries — the choice depends on the product’s power needs, portability and environmental considerations.</li>
</ul>
