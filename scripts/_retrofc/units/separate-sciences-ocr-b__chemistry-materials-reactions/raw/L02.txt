--- DESCRIPTION ---
Splitting compounds with electricity, in molten salts and solutions.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is Electrolysis?</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="The process of using a direct electric current to break down an ionic compound (electrolyte) into its elements. The compound must be molten or dissolved so that its ions are free to move.">Electrolysis</dfn> uses electricity to decompose an ionic compound into its elements. The compound being broken down is called the <dfn class="term" data-def="An ionic compound that can conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, because its ions are free to move.">electrolyte</dfn>. For electrolysis to work, the ions must be free to move — so the electrolyte must either be molten or dissolved in water (aqueous).</p>

<p data-narration-id="n3">The electrolyte is placed between two <dfn class="term" data-def="Solid electrical conductors placed in an electrolyte. The cathode is negative and the anode is positive.">electrodes</dfn> connected to a direct current supply. The <dfn class="term" data-def="The negative electrode in electrolysis. Positive ions (cations) are attracted to it and gain electrons here.">cathode</dfn> is the negative electrode; the <dfn class="term" data-def="The positive electrode in electrolysis. Negative ions (anions) are attracted to it and lose electrons here.">anode</dfn> is the positive electrode.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Name which electrode is positive and which is negative. Then state which type of ion goes to which electrode and what happens at each.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Cathode = negative electrode → attracts positive ions (cations) → cations gain electrons here (reduction). Anode = positive electrode → attracts negative ions (anions) → anions lose electrons here (oxidation). Memory aid: PANIC — Positive Anode, Negative Is Cathode.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Electrolysis of Molten Ionic Compounds</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n6">When a pure ionic compound is melted, its ions become free to move. Passing a current through molten lead bromide (\(PbBr_2\)), for example, produces lead at the cathode and bromine at the anode. In general:</p>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n7"><strong>At the cathode:</strong> metal cations are attracted, gain electrons and are deposited as atoms of the metal.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n8"><strong>At the anode:</strong> non-metal anions are attracted, lose electrons and are released as atoms or molecules of the non-metal.</li>
</ul>

It shows only the species involved at that electrode and the electrons gained or lost.">half-equation</dfn> shows what happens at one electrode. Electrons are included, represented as \(e^-\).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10">For molten lead bromide:<br>Cathode: \(Pb^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow Pb\) (reduction — gain of electrons)<br>Anode: \(2Br^- \rightarrow Br_2 + 2e^-\) (oxidation — loss of electrons)</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Electrolysis of Aqueous Solutions</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n13">When an ionic compound is dissolved in water, there are additional \(H^+\) and \(OH^-\) ions from the water itself. The electrode product therefore depends on which ions are preferentially discharged:</p>

<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>At the cathode:</strong> if the metal is more reactive than hydrogen, hydrogen gas is produced instead of the metal (water's \(H^+\) ions are discharged). If the metal is less reactive than hydrogen (e.g. copper), the metal is deposited.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>At the anode:</strong> if a <dfn class="term" data-def="An ion formed from a halogen atom (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻). In electrolysis, halide ions present at high concentration are discharged at the anode in preference to hydroxide ions.">halide ion</dfn> (\(Cl^-\), \(Br^-\)) is present at significant concentration, the halogen is produced (e.g. chlorine gas from sodium chloride solution). If no halide is present, oxygen is produced from the discharge of hydroxide ions (\(OH^-\)).</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n16">Question: predict the products at each electrode when copper sulfate (\(CuSO_4\)) solution is electrolysed with inert carbon electrodes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n17">Step 1: Ions present — \(Cu^{2+}\), \(SO_4^{2-}\), \(H^+\) (from water), \(OH^-\) (from water).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n18">Step 2: At cathode — \(Cu^{2+}\) or \(H^+\). Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so \(Cu^{2+}\) is preferentially discharged. Cathode product: copper metal.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19">Step 3: At anode — \(SO_4^{2-}\) or \(OH^-\). No halide present, so \(OH^-\) is discharged. Anode product: oxygen gas.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n20">Observations: a reddish-brown coating of copper on the cathode; colourless gas (oxygen) at the anode; the blue colour of the solution fades as \(Cu^{2+}\) ions are removed.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Extraction of Aluminium by Electrolysis</h2>

<p data-narration-id="n22">Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, yet it is expensive to produce because it sits above carbon in the reactivity series. It cannot be extracted by reduction with carbon, so electrolysis of molten <dfn class="term" data-def="Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃), the ore from which aluminium is extracted by electrolysis. It is dissolved in molten cryolite to lower its melting point and reduce energy costs.">aluminium oxide</dfn> (\(Al_2O_3\)) is used instead.</p>

<p data-narration-id="n23">Aluminium oxide has a very high melting point, so dissolving it in molten <dfn class="term" data-def="A mineral (sodium aluminium fluoride, Na₃AlF₆) used as a solvent for aluminium oxide in electrolysis. It reduces the melting point significantly, lowering energy costs.">cryolite</dfn> (sodium aluminium fluoride) lowers the operating temperature dramatically, reducing energy costs. The electrolysis is carried out in large steel cells lined with graphite, which acts as the cathode. Carbon anodes dip into the molten mixture from above.</p>

<div class="higher-only">
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n24" data-revision-tip="Cover this box. Write the half-equations at the cathode and anode during aluminium extraction, and explain why carbon anodes need to be replaced regularly.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Aluminium extraction: Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite. Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (aluminium deposited). Anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (oxygen released). Carbon anodes burn away because oxygen reacts with them at high temperature — they must be replaced regularly, adding to costs.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>The Aluminium Misconception — Why Not Just Use Carbon?</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Aluminium is above carbon in the reactivity series, which means carbon is not reactive enough to reduce aluminium oxide. Even at very high temperatures, the reaction \(Al_2O_3 + C \rightarrow Al + CO_2\) does not proceed as written, because the stronger bond in aluminium oxide is not broken by carbon.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n26">Additionally, there is a common misconception that aluminium 'rusts'. In fact, aluminium reacts quickly with oxygen in the air to form a thin, tightly bonded aluminium oxide layer. Unlike iron rust, this layer is impermeable and protective — it prevents any further oxidation. This self-sealing property is why aluminium appears unreactive in everyday life despite being quite high in the reactivity series.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n28">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Electrolysis uses a direct current to decompose an ionic electrolyte. Cations move to the cathode (negative electrode) and are reduced; anions move to the anode (positive electrode) and are oxidised.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">In aqueous solutions, hydrogen or the metal is produced at the cathode depending on reactivity; halogen or oxygen is produced at the anode depending on whether halide ions are present.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n31">Aluminium is extracted by electrolysis of aluminium oxide dissolved in cryolite. Carbon anodes gradually burn away because oxygen reacts with them and must be replaced regularly.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n27">For electrolysis questions, always start by identifying all the ions present in the electrolyte (including H⁺ and OH⁻ from water in aqueous solutions). Do not say aluminium 'rusts' in any extended-response — use 'forms a protective oxide layer' instead.</p>
