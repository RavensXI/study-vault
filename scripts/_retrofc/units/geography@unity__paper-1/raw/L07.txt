--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">UK Extreme Weather</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Although the UK has a moderate <dfn class="term" data-def="A mild climate without extreme heat or cold, typical of the UK.">temperate climate</dfn>, it still experiences extreme weather events. These are weather conditions that are significantly different from the usual patterns, including heatwaves, cold snaps, heavy rainfall, thunderstorms, drought and heavy snowfall. Evidence suggests that extreme weather events are becoming more common in the UK.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Air Masses and UK Weather</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">The UK’s weather is influenced by <dfn class="term" data-def="A large body of air with similar temperature and moisture characteristics, determined by where the air has come from.">air masses</dfn> — large bodies of air that bring different weather depending on where they come from:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n5">
<li><strong>South-west (Atlantic)</strong> — the prevailing wind direction. Warm, moist air from the Atlantic Ocean brings <strong>low pressure, wind and heavy rain</strong>. This is the most common cause of UK flooding.</li>
<li><strong>North</strong> — cold air from the Arctic brings <strong>snow and freezing temperatures</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>South</strong> — warm air from Africa and southern Europe brings <strong>heatwaves and drought</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>East</strong> — dry continental air from mainland Europe brings <strong>very cold winters</strong> (cold snaps) and <strong>hot, dry summers</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n6" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the TWO atmospheric cells that cause the UK's low pressure!">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The UK sits near the boundary of the Hadley and Ferrel cells, where warm air rises creating <strong>low pressure</strong>. This is why the UK gets so much rain and unsettled weather, especially on the western coast which faces the prevailing south-westerly winds from the Atlantic.</p>
</div>

<h3 data-narration-id="n7">Types of UK Extreme Weather</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n8">The main types of extreme weather experienced in the UK are:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n9">
<li><strong>Heatwaves</strong> — unusually high temperatures above 30°C, causing health risks especially for the elderly.</li>
<li><strong>Cold snaps</strong> — unusually low temperatures below freezing, causing disruption to transport and schools.</li>
<li><strong>Flooding</strong> — extreme amounts of rainfall that rivers cannot cope with.</li>
<li><strong>Thunderstorms</strong> — heavy rain, lightning and sometimes hail.</li>
<li><strong>Drought</strong> — prolonged periods without rain, causing water shortages.</li>
<li><strong>Heavy snowfall</strong> — caused when warm air meets freezing cold air.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Case Study: Cockermouth Floods (November 2009)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">In November 2009, the town of <dfn class="term" data-def="A market town in the Allerdale district of Cumbria, north-west England, devastated by flooding in November 2009.">Cockermouth</dfn> in Cumbria, north-west England, experienced severe flooding. It was one of the worst flood events in UK history.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>Causes of the Cockermouth Flood</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n12">A combination of factors caused the flood:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li>A warm <strong>air mass crossing the Atlantic</strong> picked up large amounts of moisture from the ocean.</li>
<li>This air was forced upwards over the <strong>hills of Cumbria</strong>, where it cooled and condensed, producing <dfn class="term" data-def="Rainfall caused when air is forced to rise over hills or mountains, cooling and condensing as it gains altitude.">relief rainfall</dfn>.</li>
<li>Up to <strong>314 mm of rain</strong> fell in just 24 hours on 18th November 2009 — a UK record at the time.</li>
<li>The ground was already <dfn class="term" data-def="When soil is completely full of water and cannot absorb any more.">saturated</dfn> from weeks of previous heavy rain, and the underlying rock was <dfn class="term" data-def="Rock or soil that does not allow water to pass through it.">impermeable</dfn>, so water ran straight off the hillsides into the rivers.</li>
<li>The rivers Derwent and Cocker filled rapidly as water ran down the steep mountain slopes. The two rivers meet at a <dfn class="term" data-def="The point where two rivers meet, which can increase flood risk as water volume doubles.">confluence</dfn> in Cockermouth, doubling the volume of water.</li>
<li>The rivers were already high from previous rainfall and quickly <strong>burst their banks</strong>, flooding the town.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div></div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n14">Social Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n15">
<li><strong>1 police officer killed</strong> — PC Bill Barker died when a bridge collapsed as he was directing traffic away from it.</li>
<li><strong>1,300 homes flooded</strong> — residents had to be housed in hotels and B&amp;Bs, often far from their community and workplace.</li>
<li>Residents suffered significant <strong>emotional distress</strong> from losing possessions, homes and community connections.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n16">Economic Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n17">
<li>Total insurance cost of <strong>£275 million</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>£100 million</strong> cost to the local economy from damaged businesses and lost trade.</li>
<li><strong>16 bridges damaged</strong> (4 completely destroyed) and <strong>25 roads closed</strong>, forcing diversions of up to <strong>40 miles</strong> for people trying to get to work.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n18">Environmental Effects</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n19">
<li><strong>River banks</strong> were heavily eroded by the fast-moving floodwater.</li>
<li><strong>Sewage contamination</strong> polluted the floodwater, posing a health risk — anything touched by the water had to be destroyed.</li>
<li><strong>Trees were destroyed</strong> and severe <dfn class="term" data-def="The movement of rock, soil and debris down a slope, often triggered by heavy rain saturating the ground.">landslides</dfn> occurred along the River Derwent.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Immediate Responses</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li><strong>200 people were airlifted</strong> from rooftops by RAF helicopters.</li>
<li>Others were rescued by <strong>boats</strong> from flooded streets.</li>
<li>Evacuees were taken to <strong>temporary shelters</strong>.</li>
<li>Buildings were <strong>assessed for structural safety</strong> before residents could return.</li>
<li>The flooding happened so rapidly that many people had <strong>no time to respond</strong> and needed rescuing.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Long-Term Responses</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">The total damage cost was <strong>£276.5 million</strong>, broken down as: businesses and local economy (£129.2m), property (£98.3m), roads and bridges (£34m), and health and welfare (£12.9m). The government provided £100 million, with the rest covered by insurance.</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n24">
<li>The main bridge over the River Derwent was <strong>closed for two months</strong> for safety checks and repairs.</li>
<li>Town centre roads were <strong>resurfaced</strong> after being broken up by floodwaters.</li>
<li>The rivers were <dfn class="term" data-def="The removal of sediment, debris and material from the bed of a river to deepen it and increase its capacity.">dredged</dfn> to remove sediment and debris, increasing the river’s capacity to reduce future flooding.</li>
<li><strong>Flood walls</strong> were built to keep water within the river channel.</li>
<li>New <strong>embankments</strong> (earth banks) were installed as a greener alternative to concrete walls.</li>
<li><strong>Flood gates and barriers</strong> were installed that could be closed quickly when flooding threatened.</li>
<li>The <dfn class="term" data-def="The UK government body responsible for flood risk management, environmental protection and pollution control.">Environment Agency</dfn> improved its flood warning system, using social media (Twitter, Facebook) to provide real-time advice.</li>
<li>The total flood defence scheme cost <strong>£4.5 million</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n25" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what FOUR things needed replacing due to flood contamination?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Many residents did not return to their homes for <strong>over a year</strong> because of the extent of the damage. Flood-contaminated homes needed completely new flooring, furniture, wiring and plaster before they were safe to live in again.</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The UK experiences a range of extreme weather events driven by different air masses. The 2009 Cockermouth floods were caused by record rainfall on saturated ground in a hilly area with a river confluence, and the effects were severe across social, economic and environmental categories.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Air masses</strong> from different directions bring different extreme weather to the UK — the prevailing south-westerly Atlantic air mass brings the most rain.</li>
<li><strong>Cockermouth 2009</strong>: 314 mm of rain in 24 hours, 1,300 homes flooded, £275 million insurance cost, 1 death.</li>
<li><strong>Responses</strong> included RAF helicopter rescues, river dredging, flood walls, embankments and a £4.5 million flood defence scheme.</li>
</ul>
