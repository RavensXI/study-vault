--- DESCRIPTION ---
Where hot deserts are found, their climate and soil characteristics, and how plants and animals have adapted to survive extreme heat and limited water.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Distribution of Hot Deserts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A biome that receives less than 250 mm of precipitation per year, with extremely dry conditions.">Hot deserts</dfn> are found in subtropical areas, typically between 20° and 30° north and south of the equator. They are located on every continent except Europe. Major hot deserts include the Sahara (Africa), the Arabian Desert (Middle East), the Thar Desert (India/Pakistan), the Sonoran Desert (North America) and the Great Sandy Desert (Australia).</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n3">Why Are Deserts So Dry?</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n4">Deserts are located in the high-pressure belt of the <dfn class="term" data-def="A large-scale pattern of atmospheric circulation where warm air rises at the equator and sinks at around 30 degrees latitude.">Hadley Cell</dfn>. Warm air rises at the equator, loses its moisture as heavy rainfall over tropical rainforests, and then descends as dry, cool air at around 30° latitude. Because the air is descending and not rising, few clouds form and there is very little rainfall. The lack of cloud cover also explains the extreme temperature range — intense sunshine during the day but rapid heat loss at night.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n5" data-revision-tip="Close this and state the rainfall amount (mm) &amp; day/night temperature range in hot deserts.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Hot deserts receive less than 250 mm of rainfall per year. Daytime temperatures can exceed 45°C, but at night they can drop below 10°C. This large <dfn class="term" data-def="The difference between daytime and night-time temperatures.">diurnal temperature range</dfn> is caused by the lack of cloud cover, which allows heat to escape rapidly after sunset.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n6">Characteristics of Hot Deserts</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Hot deserts have distinctive physical characteristics:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n8">
<li><strong>Climate</strong> — extremely hot during the day, cold at night, with very low and unreliable rainfall (under 250 mm per year).</li>
<li><strong>Landscape</strong> — stony and sandy surfaces. Only about 25% of desert landscapes are sand dunes; most are rocky or gravelly plains.</li>
<li><strong>Vegetation</strong> — sparse, with plants widely spaced to reduce competition for water.</li>
<li><strong>Biodiversity</strong> — low compared to other biomes, because the extreme conditions make survival very difficult.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n9">Desert Soil</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Desert soil is very different from the brown earth of deciduous woodlands or the latosol of tropical rainforests. Rainfall is too low for effective decomposition by bacteria (which need water), so very little <dfn class="term" data-def="Dark, organic material formed from the decay of dead plant and animal matter.">humus</dfn> develops. The soil is dry, sandy and loose, with very few nutrients. This poor soil, combined with the extreme heat and lack of water, limits plant growth and keeps biodiversity low.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Plant Adaptations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Desert plants have developed remarkable adaptations to survive with minimal water and extreme heat:</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n13">The Cactus</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n14">
<li><strong>Spines instead of leaves</strong> — spines have a tiny surface area, which drastically reduces water loss compared to broad leaves. They also help catch condensation and protect the plant from animals seeking water.</li>
<li><strong>Fleshy stems</strong> — store large amounts of water. The cactus swells quickly when water is available.</li>
<li><strong>Thick, waxy skin</strong> — creates a waterproof coating that reduces water loss through evaporation.</li>
<li><strong>Long and shallow roots</strong> — a mix of deep roots to access underground water sources and shallow, widespread roots to catch rain near the surface.</li>
</ul>
<h3 data-narration-id="n15">The Quiver Tree</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n16">
<li><strong>Self-amputation</strong> — the quiver tree can cut off its own branches and seal the wound to reduce water loss during severe drought.</li>
<li><strong>Smooth, impermeable bark</strong> — prevents water from escaping through the trunk.</li>
<li><strong>White powder coating</strong> — reflects the heat of the sun, keeping the tree cool.</li>
<li><strong>Thick-skinned leaves</strong> — leaves have a thick rind and very few pores, minimising water loss.</li>
<li><strong>Spongy tissue</strong> — a bloated, spongy core stores water inside the trunk.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n17" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name a plant adaptation beyond spines &amp; the second plant you could use in an exam question.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In exam questions about plant adaptations, you will often need to discuss the <strong>figure</strong> (usually a cactus photograph) and then add <strong>your own knowledge</strong> of a second plant (the quiver tree is an excellent choice). Always explain <strong>how</strong> the adaptation helps the plant survive — for example, “spines reduce surface area, which minimises water loss through evaporation.”</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n18">Animal Adaptations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Desert animals have also developed clever adaptations to cope with extreme heat, lack of water and limited food:</p>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>The Camel</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<ul data-narration-id="n20">
<li><strong>Humps</strong> — store fat (not water!), which can be broken down into energy and water when food is scarce.</li>
<li><strong>Rarely sweats</strong> — conserves water for long periods even in extreme heat.</li>
<li><strong>Large, tough lips</strong> — can pick at dry and thorny desert vegetation without injury.</li>
<li><strong>Broad, flat feet</strong> — spread the camel’s weight to stop it sinking into sandy soil. Leathery soles protect against the hot sand.</li>
<li><strong>Slit-like nostrils and two rows of eyelashes</strong> — protect against windblown sand.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div></div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">
<span>The Fennec Fox</span>
<svg class="collapsible-icon" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" viewbox="0 0 24 24"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"></polyline></svg>
</button>
<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<ul data-narration-id="n21">
<li><strong>Large, bat-like ears</strong> — radiate body heat, helping the fox stay cool in the desert heat.</li>
<li><strong>Thick fur on feet</strong> — protects against the hot ground when walking on sand.</li>
<li><strong>Long, thick hair</strong> — insulates the fox during cold nights and protects from the hot sun during the day.</li>
<li><strong>Light-coloured fur</strong> — reflects sunlight and keeps the body cool.</li>
<li><strong><dfn class="term" data-def="Active during the night rather than the day, helping animals avoid the intense daytime heat.">Nocturnal</dfn> behaviour</strong> — the fennec fox is active at night, avoiding the worst of the daytime heat.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n22" data-revision-tip="From memory, describe the contents of camel humps and what happens to it, connecting it to a desert challenge.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>A common exam mistake is saying that camel humps contain water. They contain <strong>fat</strong>, which can be broken down into water and energy when needed. Always be precise about adaptations and link them to the specific desert challenge they address (heat, water scarcity, or sandy terrain).</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Hot deserts are found between 20° and 30° north and south of the equator, in the high-pressure belt of the Hadley Cell. They have extremely low rainfall, extreme temperatures and poor, sandy soils. Both plants and animals have developed remarkable adaptations to survive these harsh conditions.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Distribution</strong> — hot deserts lie in subtropical zones (20–30°N/S) and are dry because descending air in the Hadley Cell prevents cloud formation.</li>
<li><strong>Plant adaptations</strong> — cacti use spines, waxy skin and water-storing stems; quiver trees self-amputate branches and reflect heat with white powder.</li>
<li><strong>Animal adaptations</strong> — camels store fat in humps and rarely sweat; fennec foxes have large ears to lose heat and are nocturnal to avoid daytime temperatures.</li>
</ul>

<p>When asked about adaptations in the hot desert, the question often provides a <strong>figure</strong> (photograph) of one organism. Describe adaptations visible in the figure first, then add your <strong>own knowledge</strong> of a different organism. For example, if the figure shows a cactus, describe its spines, waxy skin and fleshy stems, then discuss the quiver tree’s self-amputation and reflective coating. For animals, pair the camel (figure) with the fennec fox (own knowledge). Always explain <strong>how</strong> each adaptation helps the organism survive the specific desert conditions.</p>
