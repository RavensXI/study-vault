--- DESCRIPTION ---
The physical characteristics of tropical rainforests, how plants and animals have adapted to the hot, wet conditions, and why biodiversity is so high.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Distribution of Tropical Rainforests</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="A dense forest found near the equator with consistently high temperatures and rainfall throughout the year.">Tropical rainforests</dfn> are found in a belt around the equator, between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (roughly 5°N to 5°S). They are located across South America (particularly Brazil), Central Africa, South-East Asia (including Indonesia) and parts of Australia.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Tropical rainforests cover only about 6% of the Earth’s land surface, yet they contain between 50% and 90% of all the world’s plant and animal species. This extraordinary <dfn class="term" data-def="The variety of plant and animal life in an area or ecosystem.">biodiversity</dfn> makes them one of the most important biomes on the planet.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Climate of the Tropical Rainforest</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Because tropical rainforests sit on or near the equator, they have a distinctive <dfn class="term" data-def="A climate with consistently high temperatures and heavy rainfall throughout the year, found near the equator.">equatorial climate</dfn>:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n6">
<li><strong>Temperature</strong> — consistently hot throughout the year, typically between 25°C and 30°C. There are no real seasons.</li>
<li><strong>Rainfall</strong> — extremely high, usually between 2,000 mm and 3,000 mm per year. Most afternoons experience a heavy downpour.</li>
<li><strong>Growing season</strong> — 12 months. The constant heat and moisture mean plants can grow all year round.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name 3 factors creating the TRF's high biodiversity.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The combination of high temperatures, high rainfall and a 12-month growing season creates the conditions for the highest biodiversity of any biome on Earth. The TRF has more species of plants and animals than any other ecosystem.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n8">Layered Structure of the Rainforest</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n9">The tropical rainforest has a distinctive layered structure as trees compete for sunlight. The amount of light reaching each layer decreases from top to bottom:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n10">
<li><strong>Emergent layer</strong> (above 40 m) — the very tallest trees that poke above the main canopy to capture maximum sunlight and rainwater. Examples include mahogany trees. Birds and insects are common here.</li>
<li><strong>Canopy</strong> (around 30 m) — a continuous layer of treetops that blocks out most of the sunlight from below. It contains the greatest variety of plant species and is home to birds and monkeys.</li>
<li><strong>Under-canopy</strong> (10–20 m) — a darker layer with fewer trees, but many <dfn class="term" data-def="Woody climbing plants that use trees to reach sunlight in the rainforest canopy.">lianas</dfn> (climbing vines) that hang from taller trees, reaching up towards the light.</li>
<li><strong>Shrub layer</strong> (5–10 m) — small trees and plants that wait patiently for a gap in the canopy (caused by a tree falling) to let in enough light to grow.</li>
<li><strong>Forest floor</strong> (0–5 m) — very little light reaches here (only about 2%). The ground is covered in fallen leaves, rotting branches and a network of shallow roots. Ferns and mosses are common.</li>
</ul>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Soil and Nutrient Cycling</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Despite the lush vegetation, rainforest soil is surprisingly poor. The soil type is called <dfn class="term" data-def="A nutrient-poor, reddish tropical soil where nutrients are quickly leached away by heavy rainfall.">latosol</dfn> — a thin, infertile layer lacking in nutrients. This seems contradictory, but the rapid nutrient cycle explains it:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n13">
<li>The hot, moist conditions mean leaf litter decomposes very quickly — much faster than in temperate woodlands.</li>
<li>Decomposed material creates a thin layer of <dfn class="term" data-def="Dark, organic material that forms in soil when plant and animal matter decays.">humus</dfn>, full of nutrients.</li>
<li>These nutrients are rapidly reabsorbed by plant roots before they can build up in the soil.</li>
<li>Heavy rainfall causes <dfn class="term" data-def="The removal of nutrients from soil by water draining through it.">leaching</dfn> — washing away any remaining nutrients before they become part of the soil.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Without looking, what happens to soil fertility &amp; erosion when TRF trees are removed?">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>If the vegetation is removed (through deforestation), the soil quickly becomes infertile. Without trees to absorb nutrients, rapid leaching strips the humus layer. Without roots to hold the soil together, soil erosion accelerates.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Plant Adaptations</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Plants in the tropical rainforest have developed remarkable adaptations to survive the hot, wet conditions and compete for light:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n17">
<li><strong>Drip tip leaves</strong> — many leaves are large and pointed with waxy surfaces. This allows water to run off quickly, preventing damage to the leaves and stopping bacteria and fungi from growing on them.</li>
<li><strong>Buttress roots</strong> — huge, wide roots at the base of emergent trees. They are needed to support trees that grow incredibly tall (over 50 m) in their competition for sunlight. The roots spread outward rather than growing deep, because nutrients are near the surface.</li>
<li><strong>Smooth bark</strong> — unlike UK trees, which have thick bark to prevent moisture loss, rainforest trees have thin, smooth bark. This makes it harder for <dfn class="term" data-def="Plants that grow on other plants to reach sunlight, without taking nutrients from the host.">epiphytes</dfn> (plants like ferns and orchids that grow on tree surfaces) to take hold and steal light and nutrients.</li>
<li><strong>Lianas</strong> — woody climbing vines that use tall trees as support to reach sunlight in the canopy.</li>
</ul>

<p data-narration-id="n18">Animals have also adapted to the conditions in the TRF. Specific animals live in different layers of the forest:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n19">
<li><strong>Toucan</strong> (canopy) — has a long, lightweight beak designed to reach, pick and break apart tropical fruit. Four toes on each foot (two facing forward, two back) give excellent grip on branches. Excellent eyesight helps spot predators from a distance.</li>
<li><strong>Green anaconda</strong> (under-canopy) — green scales and mottled patterns provide effective <dfn class="term" data-def="Colours or patterns on an animal that help it blend in with its surroundings.">camouflage</dfn> in the dense vegetation. Eyes and nostrils sit on top of its head, allowing it to lie underwater while ambushing prey. An extremely wide jaw allows it to swallow large prey.</li>
<li><strong>Leaf-cutter ants</strong> (forest floor) — live in colonies of over 1 million. They have razor-sharp jaws that vibrate up to a thousand times per second to cut leaves. They carry leaf pieces back to their nests where the leaves decompose and grow fungus, which the ants eat.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="Close this and name 2 plant adaptations in TRFs &amp; the conditions they address.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>In the exam, you may be asked to explain how <strong>either</strong> plants or animals have adapted to the conditions in the TRF. Always link the adaptation to the specific condition it helps the organism survive — for example, drip tip leaves are an adaptation to <strong>high rainfall</strong>, while buttress roots are an adaptation to <strong>competition for light</strong> (trees grow very tall and need support).</p>
</div>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>Tropical rainforests are found near the equator in hot, wet conditions. They have a distinctive layered structure, surprisingly poor latosol soil with a rapid nutrient cycle, and extraordinary biodiversity. Both plants and animals have developed remarkable adaptations to thrive in this environment.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Climate</strong> — consistently hot (25–30°C) with 2,000–3,000 mm of rainfall and a 12-month growing season.</li>
<li><strong>Soil</strong> — latosol is thin and infertile because nutrients are rapidly cycled through decomposition and reabsorbed by plants before they accumulate.</li>
<li><strong>Adaptations</strong> — plants have drip tip leaves, buttress roots and smooth bark; animals use camouflage, specialised body parts and behaviours suited to their specific layer of the forest.</li>
</ul>
