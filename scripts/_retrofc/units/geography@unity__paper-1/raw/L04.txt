--- DESCRIPTION ---
Prediction, protection and planning — how scientists monitor hazards and how communities prepare to reduce the impact of earthquakes and volcanoes.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---

<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Three Ps</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">People cannot stop tectonic hazards from happening, but they can reduce the risks they pose. The three main strategies for reducing the impact of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions are known as the <dfn class="term" data-def="The three strategies for reducing the impact of tectonic hazards: Prediction (monitoring for warning signs), Protection (building resistant structures) and Planning (preparing emergency responses).">Three Ps</dfn>: <strong>Prediction</strong>, <strong>Protection</strong> and <strong>Planning</strong>. The wealthier a country is, the more it can invest in all three.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain how Prediction, Protection, and Planning work together for hazard management.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The Three Ps work together. Prediction gives people warning, protection reduces the damage when a hazard strikes, and planning ensures people know what to do and help arrives quickly. No single strategy is enough on its own — an effective approach uses all three.</p>
</div>

<img alt="Comparison of Mount Pinatubo 1991 and Eyjafjallajökull 2010 eruptions" src="https://pub-aeb94e100e5a48f4a133be5bf206aecb.r2.dev/geography/paper-1/diagram_volcano_comparison.jpg"/>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Prediction (Monitoring)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5"><dfn class="term" data-def="Using scientific equipment and techniques to monitor the Earth and try to forecast when a tectonic hazard will occur.">Prediction</dfn> involves using scientific equipment and techniques to monitor the Earth’s movements and detect warning signs before a major tectonic event. The aim is to give people enough warning to evacuate or take protective action. Scientists ideally want at least <strong>two days’ warning</strong> before a major hazard, but this is extremely difficult to achieve with current technology.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n6">Methods of Prediction</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n7">
<li><strong>Seismometers</strong> — instruments placed in the ground that detect vibrations and small earthquakes. A series of small tremors can sometimes indicate that a larger earthquake is building. <dfn class="term" data-def="A scientific instrument that detects and records vibrations in the Earth’s crust, used to monitor earthquake activity.">Seismometers</dfn> are the most widely used monitoring tool.</li>
<li><strong>Ground radar and satellites</strong> — used to detect tiny movements in the Earth’s surface, changes in ground level, and shifts in tectonic plates. GPS technology can track plate movement to within millimetres.</li>
<li><strong>Gas monitoring</strong> — rising levels of gases such as <dfn class="term" data-def="A radioactive gas that can escape from the ground before an earthquake due to stress on rock.">radon</dfn> and sulphur dioxide from the ground can signal increased volcanic or seismic activity beneath the surface.</li>
<li><strong>Changes in magnetism</strong> — shifts in the Earth’s magnetic field near fault lines can sometimes precede earthquakes.</li>
<li><strong>Animal behaviour</strong> — some scientists study unusual animal behaviour (such as animals fleeing an area) as a potential early warning sign, though this remains unproven and unreliable.</li>
<li><strong>Studying past events</strong> — scientists analyse patterns from previous earthquakes along known fault lines to estimate the likelihood and timing of future events.</li>
</ul>

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n8">Despite all these methods, <strong>scientists cannot yet predict exactly when or where an earthquake will happen</strong>. Current techniques are unreliable and at best can narrow the window to within about two years. For volcanoes, prediction is somewhat more successful — rising magma causes measurable changes in ground shape, gas emissions and seismic activity that can give days or weeks of warning. Even so, false alarms are common and evacuating millions of people based on uncertain data is extremely difficult. Scientists are constantly improving their knowledge, but reliable earthquake prediction remains one of the biggest challenges in geoscience.</p>
</div>
</div></div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n9" data-revision-tip="Without looking, name the locations scientists monitor for earthquakes and explain the key challenge.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Scientists know <em>where</em> earthquakes are likely to happen (along plate boundaries and known fault lines) and can focus monitoring on these areas. The challenge is knowing <em>when</em> the next major earthquake will strike.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Protection</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11"><dfn class="term" data-def="Designing and constructing buildings and infrastructure to withstand the shaking of an earthquake.">Protection</dfn> involves constructing buildings and infrastructure that can withstand the forces of an earthquake, making them safer to live and work in. Most people killed or injured during an earthquake are hit by or trapped in buildings that collapse. If buildings can be made stronger, the death toll drops dramatically.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n12">Earthquake-Resistant Building Features</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><dfn class="term" data-def="Buildings designed with special features to absorb and resist the shaking forces of an earthquake.">Earthquake-resistant buildings</dfn> use a range of engineering techniques to absorb the energy of an earthquake rather than cracking and collapsing:</p>
<ul data-narration-id="n14">
<li><strong>Deep foundations</strong> — driven deep into solid rock to anchor the building securely.</li>
<li><strong>Rubber shock absorbers</strong> — placed between the foundations and the building to absorb vibrations, allowing the structure to move with the shaking rather than resisting it rigidly.</li>
<li><strong>Steel frames</strong> — flexible steel structures that bend without breaking, unlike rigid concrete which cracks.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-bracing</strong> — diagonal steel beams in the walls that strengthen the structure against sideways forces.</li>
<li><strong>Shatter-proof glass</strong> — windows designed to flex rather than shatter, reducing the risk of injury from flying glass.</li>
<li><strong>Fire-resistant materials</strong> — reduce the risk of fires caused by broken gas pipes and electrical cables.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and explain why buildings are earthquake-resistant, not earthquake-proof, and their purpose.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>Buildings can be made earthquake-<em>resistant</em>, but they cannot yet be made earthquake-<em>proof</em>. Even the best-designed structures can be damaged by an extremely powerful earthquake. The goal is to keep the building standing long enough for people to evacuate safely.</p>
</div>
<div class="collapsible">
<button aria-expanded="false" class="collapsible-toggle">

<div class="collapsible-content">
<div class="collapsible-inner">
<p data-narration-id="n16">Earthquake-resistant buildings are expensive to design and construct. Wealthy countries like Japan and New Zealand can afford strict building codes and modern construction methods. In poorer countries like Haiti and Nepal, many buildings are made from cheap, unreinforced materials such as mud brick or basic concrete that crumble instantly during an earthquake. This is a major reason why death tolls are so much higher in LICs — the buildings themselves become the biggest danger.</p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Planning</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="Preparing people, communities and governments for a tectonic hazard through emergency plans, drills and stockpiled supplies.">Planning</dfn> involves preparing people and communities for what to do before, during and after a tectonic event. Good planning saves lives because people react faster and more calmly when they have practised what to do.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Elements of Planning</h3>
<ul data-narration-id="n20">
<li><strong>Evacuation plans</strong> — clear routes and procedures for moving people away from danger zones. These are especially important for volcanic eruptions, where ash clouds, <dfn class="term" data-def="A fast-moving flow of hot gas and volcanic matter that travels down the side of a volcano.">pyroclastic flows</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="A destructive mudflow of volcanic debris and water that rushes down the slopes of a volcano.">lahars</dfn> can threaten entire communities.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency drills</strong> — regular practice so that people know exactly what to do when an earthquake strikes. In Japan, children practise earthquake drills at school from a young age.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency supplies</strong> — households in earthquake-prone areas are encouraged to keep supplies of water, food, torches, first aid kits and batteries ready at all times.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency shelters</strong> — pre-identified buildings, schools or open areas where evacuated people can gather safely and receive food, water and medical help.</li>
<li><strong>Emergency response plans</strong> — governments and emergency services create detailed plans for search and rescue, medical treatment, distributing aid, and maintaining law and order. These plans are rehearsed regularly.</li>
<li><strong>Specialist search and rescue teams</strong> — wealthy countries invest in highly trained teams with specialist equipment such as sniffer dogs, thermal cameras and heavy lifting gear.</li>
<li><strong>National emergency response plans</strong> — central government plans that coordinate the response across the whole country, ensuring help reaches the right places quickly.</li>
</ul>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n21" data-revision-tip="From memory, compare HICs and LICs in terms of the Three Ps and deaths from tectonic hazards.">
<div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
<p>The wealthier a country is, the more money it can invest in all Three Ps. This is why HICs like Japan and New Zealand suffer far fewer deaths from tectonic hazards than LICs like Haiti and Nepal, even though they experience earthquakes of similar or greater magnitude.</p>
</div>
<h3 data-narration-id="n22">Seismometer Warnings</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Even though scientists cannot predict earthquakes far in advance, modern seismometer networks can detect the first seismic waves from an earthquake within seconds. This can give people a brief warning — sometimes just 10–60 seconds — to take protective action such as turning off gas supplies, getting out of buildings, or sheltering under sturdy furniture. Japan’s earthquake early warning system sends automatic alerts to mobile phones across affected regions, giving millions of people a few vital seconds to prepare.</p>
<h3 data-narration-id="n24">Education and Community Preparedness</h3>
<p data-narration-id="n25">Education is one of the most cost-effective forms of planning. Teaching people how to respond — “drop, cover and hold on” during an earthquake, or knowing the signs of an approaching tsunami — can save lives even where expensive technology is not available. Community preparedness programmes in countries like Chile and the Philippines have reduced death tolls by ensuring that even poorer communities know what to do.</p>


--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---

<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>The Three Ps — Prediction, Protection and Planning — are the main strategies for reducing the impact of tectonic hazards. While none can prevent earthquakes or eruptions, together they significantly reduce deaths, speed up responses and help communities recover faster.</p>
<h3>Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prediction</strong> uses seismometers, satellites, gas monitoring and studying past events, but scientists still cannot predict exactly when an earthquake will strike.</li>
<li><strong>Protection</strong> involves earthquake-resistant building features like deep foundations, rubber shock absorbers and steel frames — but these are expensive and not available in poorer countries.</li>
<li><strong>Planning</strong> through evacuation plans, emergency drills, stockpiled supplies and trained rescue teams saves lives by ensuring people know what to do when a hazard strikes.</li>
</ul>
