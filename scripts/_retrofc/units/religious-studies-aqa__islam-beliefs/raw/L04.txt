--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Al-Qadr — Predestination</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><dfn class="term" data-def="Predestination in Islam — the belief that Allah has already determined everything that will happen, past, present and future.">Al-Qadr</dfn> is the sixth article of Sunni faith. It means predestination — the belief that Allah already knows and has determined everything that will happen. Nothing occurs by accident or chance. Surah 9:51 states: “Only what God has decreed will happen to us. He is our Master.” This includes every event in a person’s life, from birth to death.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Muslims believe Allah has complete knowledge of the past, present and future. Before a child is born, an angel records four things about their life: their provision (livelihood), their lifespan, their deeds, and whether they will be happy or unhappy. This is written in the “Preserved Tablet” (Lawh al-Mahfuz), which contains the record of all that has been and all that will be.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Without looking, explain the tension between predestination and free will in Islam, covering both sides.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Al-Qadr does not mean humans are puppets. Muslims believe Allah knows in advance what choices a person will make, but the person still makes those choices freely. It is this freedom that makes humans accountable on the Day of Judgement.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Predestination and Human Freedom</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">One of the most complex issues in Islamic theology is how predestination relates to <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability of humans to make their own choices and decisions, for which they will be held accountable by Allah.">human freedom</dfn>. If Allah has already decided everything, can humans truly be free? And if they are not free, how can they be held responsible for their actions on the Day of Judgement?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Most Muslims hold that both beliefs are true simultaneously. Allah’s knowledge is absolute — He knows what every person will do before they do it. But this foreknowledge does not force people to act in a particular way. Humans have genuine freedom of choice (ikhtiyar) and are responsible for their decisions. It is like a teacher who knows a student will fail an exam — the teacher’s knowledge did not cause the failure; the student’s lack of revision did.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Sunni and Shi’a Views on Predestination</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Sunni view:</strong> Al-Qadr is one of the Six Articles of Faith. Sunni Muslims generally emphasise that everything happens according to Allah’s will. Even suffering and hardship are part of His plan. This belief gives comfort in difficult times — if something bad happens, a Muslim can trust it is part of Allah’s greater purpose, even if they cannot understand it.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Shi’a view:</strong> Shi’a Muslims place greater emphasis on <dfn class="term" data-def="The Shi’a belief in the justice of Allah — that God is perfectly fair and never acts unjustly.">Adalat</dfn> (divine justice). They argue that Allah’s justice means He would not predetermine someone to sin and then punish them for it — that would be unjust. Shi’a theology therefore gives more weight to human free will, arguing that people genuinely choose their actions and are justly accountable for them.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Belief in Al-Qadr influences Muslims in practical ways. It encourages <dfn class="term" data-def="Patience and steadfastness in the face of difficulty — a highly valued quality in Islam.">Sabr</dfn> (patience) during hardship, because Muslims trust that suffering is part of Allah’s plan. It also encourages gratitude in good times, since all blessings come from Allah. However, it does not encourage passivity — Muslims are still expected to work hard, make good choices and strive to follow Allah’s guidance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n11">The Day of Judgement (Yawm al-Din)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Muslims believe that this life is a test, and that every person will face judgement before Allah at the end of time. The Day of Judgement is described vividly in the Qur’an as a terrifying, earth-shaking event. Surah 99:1–2 states: “When the earth is shaken with its earthquake, and the earth discharges its burdens.”</p>



<p data-narration-id="n13">On this day, the angel <dfn class="term" data-def="The angel who will blow the trumpet to announce the Day of Judgement and the resurrection of all people.">Israfil</dfn> will blow a trumpet, and all living things will die. Then a second blast will resurrect every person who has ever lived. Each person will stand before Allah and their book of deeds — recorded by the angels throughout their life — will be opened. Those who receive their book in the right hand will enter paradise; those who receive it in the left hand will face punishment.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Key Qur’anic Teachings on Judgement</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Surah 99:7–8 states: “Whoever does an atom’s weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom’s weight of evil will see it.” This teaches that no action — however small — escapes Allah’s notice. Every good deed and every sin will be accounted for.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">Surah 23:102–103 describes the scales of judgement: “Those whose scales are heavy [with good deeds] will be the successful ones. But those whose scales are light will have lost their souls; in hell they will abide.” Good deeds are weighed against bad deeds, and the balance determines a person’s fate.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">Akhirah — Life After Death</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><dfn class="term" data-def="The Islamic belief in life after death — the eternal existence that follows the Day of Judgement.">Akhirah</dfn> means life after death. Muslims believe the afterlife is eternal and that this earthly life is merely a temporary test. What a person does in this life determines their fate in the next. This belief underpins Muslim morality — every action carries consequences that extend beyond death.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Heaven (Jannah) and Hell (Jahannam)</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>Jannah (Paradise):</strong> The Qur’an describes Jannah as a place of eternal peace, pleasure and closeness to Allah. Surah 47:15 describes rivers of water, milk, wine and honey, with fruits of every kind and forgiveness from Allah. Jannah has multiple levels, with the highest reserved for the most righteous. It is the ultimate reward for those who lived according to Allah’s will.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Jahannam (Hell):</strong> Jahannam is a place of torment and punishment for those who rejected Allah or lived sinfully without repentance. The Qur’an describes fire, boiling water and suffering. Surah 4:56 describes how the skin of those in hell will be replaced so they continue to feel the punishment. Most Muslims believe Jahannam can be temporary for sinful Muslims — Allah may forgive them and eventually admit them to paradise — but it is eternal for those who committed Shirk (associating partners with Allah).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n20" data-revision-tip="From memory, explain three ways belief in Akhirah influences how Muslims live their daily lives.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Belief in Akhirah means Muslims see this life as a preparation for eternity. Every deed is recorded and will be judged. This motivates Muslims to pray, give charity, avoid sin and follow the Qur’an and Sunnah — because the stakes are not just for this life but for the life to come.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n21">Human Responsibility and Accountability</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n22">The concepts of predestination, judgement and the afterlife work together to create a strong framework of personal accountability. Muslims believe every person is responsible for their own actions. No one can carry another’s sins (Surah 17:15). On the Day of Judgement, there are no excuses — the angels’ records are complete and Allah’s judgement is perfect.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n23">This accountability influences how Muslims live. It encourages honesty, generosity, compassion and obedience to Allah. It discourages dishonesty, cruelty and arrogance, because Muslims know that even the smallest action — an “atom’s weight” — will be seen and judged. The belief in resurrection means there is no escape from this accountability, even in death.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Barzakh — Life in the Grave</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n24">Muslims believe that after death, the soul enters <dfn class="term" data-def="The barrier or waiting period between death and the Day of Judgement, during which the soul remains in the grave.">Barzakh</dfn> — a state of waiting between death and the Day of Judgement. In the grave, two angels (Munkar and Nakir) question the deceased about their faith: “Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?” A righteous person will answer correctly and experience comfort; a sinful person will struggle and experience suffering. This period lasts until the Day of Resurrection.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n26">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n27">Al-Qadr (predestination) means Allah knows everything in advance, but humans still have free will and are accountable for their choices. Shi’a Muslims emphasise Adalat (justice) to explain why free will is genuine.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">On the Day of Judgement, every person will be resurrected and judged by Allah. Their book of deeds will determine whether they enter Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell).</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Akhirah (life after death) is central to Muslim morality. This life is seen as a test, and every action — no matter how small — carries eternal consequences.</li>
</ul>
