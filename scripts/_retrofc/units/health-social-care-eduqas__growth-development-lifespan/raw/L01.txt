Five life stages, exact age boundaries, and key PIES milestones for each.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Why Life Stages Matter</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Health and social care professionals do not treat all individuals the same way. They use a lifespan framework &mdash; dividing human life into five distinct stages, each with its own expected pattern of growth and development. Understanding these stages helps practitioners identify what is typical for a person&rsquo;s age and what might indicate a problem. When a child or adult does not reach an expected milestone, a practitioner needs to know what &ldquo;on track&rdquo; looks like before they can help.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Development is described across four domains, remembered using the acronym <dfn class="term" data-def="A framework dividing development into four domains: Physical, Intellectual, Emotional, and Social. Used by health and social care professionals to assess growth across the lifespan.">PIES</dfn> &mdash; Physical, Intellectual, Emotional, and Social. These four threads run through every life stage, changing in pace and character as an individual moves from infancy to later adulthood.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and write the exact age boundaries for all five life stages from memory. Check each one against the spec wording.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The five life stages and their age boundaries are: <strong>infancy</strong> (0&ndash;2 years), <strong>childhood</strong> (3&ndash;10 years), <strong>adolescence</strong> (11&ndash;19 years), <strong>adulthood</strong> (20&ndash;64 years), and <strong>later adulthood</strong> (65 years and over). Exam questions that ask for age boundaries only credit these exact ranges.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Stage 1: Infancy (0&ndash;2 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><dfn class="term" data-def="The first life stage, spanning 0 to 2 years, characterised by rapid physical growth and the formation of the first emotional bonds.">Infancy</dfn> is the period of the most rapid development in the entire lifespan. A newborn baby is entirely dependent on their caregivers but begins building skills at an extraordinary pace. Health visitors and midwives work closely with families at this stage to monitor that development is on track.</p>

    <span>PIES Milestones in Infancy</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Physical:</strong> Infants are born with primitive reflexes such as the rooting reflex and the grasp reflex. By around 12 months, most infants can sit unaided and begin to pull themselves upright. By 18&ndash;24 months many take their first independent steps. Weight typically triples in the first year.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Infants learn through their senses and through exploring objects. Language begins with babbling around 6 months, progresses to single recognisable words by around 12 months, and simple two-word combinations by 18&ndash;24 months.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Emotional:</strong> An infant&rsquo;s most significant emotional development is forming an attachment bond with a primary caregiver &mdash; the foundation for emotional security later in life. Distress when separated from a caregiver (separation anxiety) begins around 6&ndash;8 months.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Social:</strong> Infants begin making eye contact within weeks of birth and develop social smiling by around 6 weeks. They learn to interact with familiar faces and are soothed by familiar voices. Most social interaction at this stage is with immediate family.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n11">Stage 2: Childhood (3&ndash;10 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n12"><dfn class="term" data-def="The second life stage, from 3 to 10 years, during which physical growth steadies, language develops rapidly, and children begin formal education and peer socialisation.">Childhood</dfn> is a period of steady physical growth alongside rapid intellectual and social development. Children in early years settings, primary schools, and community clubs are acquiring skills that will shape the rest of their development. A nursery key-worker or primary school support assistant plays an important role in observing milestones at this stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n13"><strong>Physical:</strong> Gross motor skills (running, jumping, climbing) and fine motor skills (drawing, writing, cutting) continue to develop. By around age 5 most children can dress themselves and use scissors accurately. Height and weight increase steadily but more slowly than in infancy.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Language expands rapidly &mdash; vocabulary grows from around 200 words at age 2 to several thousand by age 7. Children learn to read, count, and solve simple problems. They move from egocentric thinking toward understanding others&rsquo; perspectives (theory of mind). Concentration spans increase as formal schooling begins.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Children develop a clearer sense of their own identity and begin to compare themselves to peers. Self-esteem can be influenced significantly by praise or criticism from adults. Fear of failure and pride in achievement both emerge in this stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16"><strong>Social:</strong> Play becomes increasingly cooperative rather than parallel. Friendships form on the basis of shared interests. Primary socialisation (learning values and norms from family) begins to be supplemented by secondary socialisation through school and peer groups.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Stage 3: Adolescence (11&ndash;19 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><dfn class="term" data-def="The third life stage, from 11 to 19 years, marked by puberty, rapid physical change, identity formation, and increasing independence.">Adolescence</dfn> spans the transition from childhood to adulthood and is characterised by dramatic physical changes (puberty) and significant shifts in how a person sees themselves. Adolescents may interact with GPs, school nurses, and youth workers during this stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Physical:</strong> Puberty triggers growth spurts, development of secondary sex characteristics, and changes in body composition. In girls this typically begins between ages 10&ndash;11; in boys slightly later at 11&ndash;13. By the end of adolescence physical growth is largely complete.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Abstract thinking and the ability to consider hypothetical situations develop. Young people can weigh up consequences, form opinions, and think critically. This supports academic achievement at GCSE and A Level and development of personal values.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Identity formation is the central emotional task of adolescence &mdash; young people explore who they are in relation to their peers, family, culture, and aspirations. Mood fluctuations are common as hormones and social pressures combine. Self-esteem is strongly linked to peer relationships.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n22"><strong>Social:</strong> Peer groups become more influential than family in shaping behaviour. Adolescents begin to form their first intimate relationships outside the family unit. The balance between seeking independence and still depending on caregivers is a defining social tension of this stage.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n23">Stage 4: Adulthood (20&ndash;64 Years)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n24">Adulthood is the longest life stage. It spans a huge range of experiences &mdash; from a 20-year-old starting their first job to a 60-year-old approaching retirement. Physical development peaks in early adulthood and then gradually begins to decline. GP practices, occupational health services, and family support workers all interact with adults across this stage.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Physical:</strong> Physical strength and fitness peak in the mid-twenties. From the thirties onwards gradual changes occur &mdash; metabolism slows, muscle mass reduces, and skin begins to show signs of ageing. For women, the menopause typically occurs between ages 45 and 55, ending the reproductive years.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n26"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Adults apply accumulated knowledge to complex real-world problems. Career development, managing finances, and raising children all require higher-order reasoning. Intellectual performance generally remains high throughout adulthood, though the speed of processing information may slow slightly from middle age.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n27"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Adults form long-term intimate partnerships and close friendships. Managing the emotional demands of work, parenting, and caring responsibilities is a key feature of this stage. Life events such as bereavement, redundancy, or divorce can significantly disrupt emotional wellbeing.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n28"><strong>Social:</strong> Social networks are typically at their widest in early and middle adulthood &mdash; work colleagues, community involvement, and family ties create a rich social world. Parenting and caregiving roles bring new social responsibilities.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n29" data-revision-tip="Close this and recall: which physical change marks the end of a woman's reproductive years, and in which life stage does it typically occur?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Physical development peaks in early adulthood (mid-twenties) and gradually declines from that point. The menopause, marking the end of female reproductive capacity, typically occurs during adulthood (ages 45&ndash;55) &mdash; not in later adulthood as students often assume.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n30">Stage 5: Later Adulthood (65 Years and Over)</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n31">Later adulthood begins at 65 and continues until death. The experiences of people in this stage vary enormously &mdash; a healthy 68-year-old runner and a 90-year-old with advanced dementia are both in &ldquo;later adulthood&rdquo; by the spec&rsquo;s age classification. Geriatric wards, hospices, day centres, and residential care homes are all settings where practitioners support individuals at this stage.</p>

  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>PIES Milestones in Later Adulthood</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n32"><strong>Physical:</strong> Bone density decreases (increasing fracture risk), muscle strength reduces, and sensory abilities such as sight and hearing may decline. Immune function weakens. These changes increase the likelihood of long-term health conditions.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n33"><strong>Intellectual:</strong> Many older adults remain cognitively active and continue acquiring new skills. However, processing speed typically slows, and some individuals experience memory loss &mdash; in its more severe forms this may indicate dementia, which increases in prevalence with age.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n34"><strong>Emotional:</strong> Retirement can be a positive transition (freedom, leisure) or a difficult one (loss of purpose, identity). Bereavement becomes more frequent as friends and partners die. Maintaining a positive sense of self despite physical decline is a key emotional challenge.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n35"><strong>Social:</strong> Social networks may shrink as mobility reduces and peers die. Loneliness and isolation are significant risks. However, many older adults maintain strong family ties and remain active in community groups, faith communities, and volunteering.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n37">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n38">There are five life stages with fixed age boundaries: infancy (0&ndash;2), childhood (3&ndash;10), adolescence (11&ndash;19), adulthood (20&ndash;64), and later adulthood (65+). Only these boundaries are credited in mark schemes.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n39">Development across each stage is described using the PIES framework &mdash; Physical, Intellectual, Emotional, and Social &mdash; and different stages bring different headline changes in each domain.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n40">Practitioners use knowledge of life-stage milestones to identify when an individual may need support &mdash; making this foundational knowledge for anyone working in health and social care.</li>
</ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n36">Questions that ask you to &lsquo;identify&rsquo; or &lsquo;state&rsquo; a life stage expect an exact answer from the spec&rsquo;s named list &mdash; do not paraphrase or combine stages. If a question gives you an age (e.g. &ldquo;a 12-year-old&rdquo;), convert it to the correct stage name (adolescence) and its exact age range (11&ndash;19) before writing anything else. For PIES questions, practise writing one bullet per domain rather than paragraphs &mdash; this prevents mixing up domains and losing marks for an unclear response. The command word &lsquo;Describe&rsquo; on a 2-mark PIES question expects one specific feature plus a qualifying detail, not a general statement.</p>
