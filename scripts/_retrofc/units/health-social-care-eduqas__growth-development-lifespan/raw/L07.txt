Formal care, informal carers, advocacy and financial support — help when milestones aren't met.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">When Milestones Aren&rsquo;t Reached</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Not every individual moves through the five life stages meeting every expected developmental milestone. A child may show delayed language development; a teenager may experience social withdrawal after a traumatic event; an adult may lose physical or cognitive functioning following illness or injury; an older person may become dependent as their mobility and sensory abilities decline. When milestones are not reached, individuals and their families need support &mdash; and the health and social care sector provides this through two main channels: <dfn class="term" data-def="Support provided by trained, paid professionals working within recognised organisations, agencies, or statutory services.">formal care</dfn> and <dfn class="term" data-def="Support provided by unpaid individuals, typically family members, friends, neighbours, or volunteers, who are not professional carers.">informal care</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding both types of support &mdash; and how they complement each other &mdash; is central to health and social care practice. A holistic care plan for an individual who has not reached expected milestones will typically draw on both formal and informal sources, alongside advocacy, financial assistance, and specialist equipment.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Formal Care: Professional Roles</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">Formal care is delivered by trained professionals within statutory services (funded and organised by the state, such as the NHS and local authority social services) or voluntary and independent sector organisations. The spec names several key professional roles involved in supporting individuals who have not reached milestones.</p>
<div class="collapsible">
  <button class="collapsible-toggle" aria-expanded="false">
    <span>Named Formal Care Professionals</span>
    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>GP (General Practitioner):</strong> Often the first point of contact when a developmental concern arises. A GP may refer a child with delayed motor skills to a paediatrician, or an adult showing signs of cognitive decline to a memory clinic. GPs coordinate access to other services and provide ongoing monitoring.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Nurse:</strong> Including health visitors (who monitor infant and child development in the community), school nurses (who screen for physical and emotional difficulties in school-age children), and district nurses (who support adults and older people with health needs in their own homes).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Social worker:</strong> Assesses the needs of individuals and families, coordinates care packages, and ensures safeguarding requirements are met. Social workers are key professionals when life factors such as abuse, neglect, or poverty are affecting developmental outcomes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Care worker:</strong> Provides direct personal care in residential homes, day centres, or the individual&rsquo;s home &mdash; assisting with daily living tasks that the individual can no longer manage independently.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Allied health professionals (AHPs):</strong> A group of specialist practitioners whose expertise targets specific developmental needs. These include: <strong>speech and language therapists</strong> (supporting children with delayed language or adults with communication difficulties after a stroke), <strong>occupational therapists</strong> (helping individuals adapt their daily activities and environment when physical or cognitive ability has declined), and <strong>physiotherapists</strong> (supporting physical rehabilitation and mobility).</p>
  </div></div>
</div>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n11" data-revision-tip="Cover this and name at least four formal care professionals from the spec, stating what developmental need each one primarily addresses. Check your list against the lesson.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Formal care professionals named in the spec include: GP, nurse (health visitor, school nurse, district nurse), social worker, care worker, speech and language therapist, occupational therapist, and physiotherapist. In exam answers, always link the professional to the specific developmental need &mdash; don&rsquo;t just list names.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Settings Where Formal Support Is Delivered</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Formal support to individuals who have not reached milestones is delivered across a range of settings. A child with developmental delay might be supported in a children&rsquo;s centre or specialist nursery placement. A teenager with significant mental health difficulties might attend a community mental health team (CAMHS). An adult with acquired disability might access support from a rehabilitation ward in a hospital. An older person with advancing dementia might move to a residential care home or access specialist day centre provision.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n14">Mental health services deserve particular mention. For individuals whose milestone delays include emotional and social development &mdash; including those affected by trauma, abuse, anxiety, or depression &mdash; mental health support provides <dfn class="term" data-def="Therapeutic or clinical support focused on improving psychological well-being, managing mental health conditions, and building emotional resilience.">mental health care</dfn> that addresses the emotional and social dimensions of development alongside any physical needs.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Informal Care: Family, Friends, Neighbours and Volunteers</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Informal care is provided by unpaid individuals who are not trained as professional carers. It is overwhelmingly provided by family members &mdash; parents caring for a child with a learning disability, adult children caring for a parent with dementia, a partner managing medication for a spouse with a chronic condition. Friends, neighbours, and community volunteers also contribute informally.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17">Informal care is significant in scale and impact. Research consistently shows that informal carers provide hundreds of millions of hours of care in the UK annually &mdash; care that would be unaffordable to replace with formal services. However, informal caring is demanding: carers are at elevated risk of their own physical and emotional burnout, financial pressure, and social isolation. Recognising this, formal services offer <strong>carer support</strong> &mdash; respite care (temporary relief from caring responsibilities), carers&rsquo; assessments, support groups, and access to carers&rsquo; allowance.</p>
<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n18" data-revision-tip="Close this and explain from memory: what is the key difference between formal and informal care, and give one example of each in the context of a child with developmental delay.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Formal care = trained professionals within statutory or voluntary organisations (e.g. a speech and language therapist supporting a child with language delay). Informal care = unpaid family members, friends, or volunteers (e.g. a parent practising speech exercises at home between therapy sessions). The two types work best when they complement each other within a coordinated care plan.</p>
</div>
<h2 data-narration-id="n19">Advocacy</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n20"><dfn class="term" data-def="Speaking up for, or supporting someone to speak up for, their own rights, needs, and preferences in relation to their care — particularly when they may not be able to do so effectively themselves.">Advocacy</dfn> is a form of support that ensures an individual&rsquo;s voice is heard in decisions about their own care. This is especially important for those who face barriers to self-expression &mdash; people with learning disabilities, individuals with severe mental health conditions, children, or older adults with dementia. An advocate may be a trained professional (a formal advocate from a specialist organisation such as MIND or MENCAP), a family member, or a volunteer.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n21">Advocacy supports PIES development by protecting the individual&rsquo;s right to have their needs accurately represented. An older person moving into residential care may not feel able to raise concerns about their care &mdash; an advocate ensures those concerns reach the relevant practitioner or care manager. For a child with a communication difficulty, an advocate helps ensure their educational needs are properly assessed and met.</p>
<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Training, Equipment, Financial Support, and Support Groups</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23">Several additional forms of support help individuals who have not reached expected milestones. <strong>Training and development</strong> may be provided to the individual (learning new skills adapted to their current abilities) or to their informal carers (e.g. a parent trained in Makaton signing to communicate with a child with a language disorder). <strong>Equipment and resources</strong> &mdash; including mobility aids such as walking frames, hearing loops, specialist keyboards for people with fine motor difficulties, and adapted cutlery for individuals with reduced grip strength &mdash; help individuals maintain independence in their daily lives.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Financial support</strong> is available through state benefits and grants. These include Disability Living Allowance or Personal Independence Payment (for individuals with disabilities), Attendance Allowance (for older people who need help), and Carers&rsquo; Allowance (for informal carers). Without financial support, the cost of specialist equipment, therapies, and adapted housing falls entirely on the individual and their family, creating an economic barrier to development.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n25">Support groups bring together individuals who share a common condition, experience, or challenge. They provide <strong>social support</strong> (reducing isolation), <strong>information sharing</strong> (members learn from each other&rsquo;s experiences), and <strong>emotional support</strong> (being understood by people with similar experiences improves emotional wellbeing). Examples include carers&rsquo; support groups, bereavement support groups, and groups organised around specific conditions (dementia caf&eacute;s, cerebral palsy family groups). They sit at the intersection of formal and informal support &mdash; often run by voluntary sector organisations but drawing on the informal bonds between members.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul>
  <li data-narration-id="n28">Formal care is delivered by trained professionals &mdash; GPs, nurses, social workers, care workers, and allied health professionals (speech and language therapists, occupational therapists, physiotherapists) &mdash; across statutory, voluntary, and independent sector settings.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n29">Informal care is provided by unpaid family members, friends, neighbours and volunteers. It is essential to the UK&rsquo;s care system and works best when coordinated with formal care and supported by respite, carers&rsquo; assessments, and carers&rsquo; allowance.</li>
  <li data-narration-id="n30">Additional forms of support &mdash; advocacy, training, specialist equipment, financial support, and support groups &mdash; each address a different dimension of need when individuals have not reached expected developmental milestones.</li>
</ul>
