<h2 data-narration-id="n1">A Novel About What It Means to Be Human</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2"><em>Never Let Me Go</em> was published in 2005 by <dfn class="term" data-def="A British-Japanese novelist (born 1954) known for restrained prose and exploration of memory, loss, and self-deception. Won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2017.">Kazuo Ishiguro</dfn>. It tells the story of three friends — Kathy, Tommy, and Ruth — who grow up at a seemingly idyllic English boarding school called Hailsham. The novel gradually reveals that they are <dfn class="term" data-def="Genetically identical copies of human beings, created in the novel solely to supply organs for transplant. The clones are raised as children but have no legal rights or future beyond donation.">clones</dfn>, created to donate their vital organs to ‘normal’ humans. Ishiguro never uses shock tactics. Instead, he presents the horror quietly, through the voice of Kathy H., a 31-year-old <dfn class="term" data-def="A clone who looks after other clones during their donation operations. Kathy has been a carer for almost twelve years when the novel opens.">carer</dfn> reflecting on her childhood and the people she has lost.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">For the Eduqas exam, you will face a two-part question on <em>Never Let Me Go</em>: part (a) asks you to analyse a printed extract (20 marks), and part (b) asks you to write about the wider text (20 marks). Both assess AO1 (response to text), AO2 (language, form, structure), and AO4 (context). This first lesson provides the essential background knowledge you need.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what are the three Assessment Objectives tested in the Eduqas NLMG question, and what does each assess?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The Eduqas Component 2, Section A question is worth 40 marks total: (a) 20 marks on a printed extract and (b) 20 marks on the wider novel. AO1 (response to text), AO2 (language, form, structure), and AO4 (context) are all assessed across both parts.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The Science and the Ethics</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ishiguro wrote the novel during intense public debate about <dfn class="term" data-def="The scientific process of creating a genetically identical copy of an organism. Dolly the Sheep, cloned in 1996, provoked widespread ethical debate about human cloning.">cloning</dfn> and bioethics. Dolly the Sheep was cloned in 1996, and by the early 2000s, stem cell research and organ transplantation raised urgent questions about where science should stop. The novel imagines a Britain where these questions were answered by creating a class of disposable human beings. Crucially, Ishiguro sets the story in the late 1990s — roughly the present day when he wrote it — suggesting this is not a far-off future but a version of our own world.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">The novel does not dwell on the science. We never learn exactly how the clones are created. Ishiguro deliberately withholds these details because the novel is not about cloning as a technological problem — it is about what we choose not to see. The ‘normal’ humans benefit from organ <dfn class="term" data-def="The euphemistic term used in the novel for organ harvesting operations. Clones typically undergo up to four donations before they ‘complete’ (die).">donations</dfn> but avoid thinking about where the organs come from. Miss Emily later admits: “How can you ask a world that has come to regard cancer as curable … to put away that cure?” This rhetorical question implicates the reader in the system’s logic.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">In the novel’s world, clones follow a fixed path: childhood at an institution like Hailsham, a period as a carer (looking after other clones who are donating), and finally their own donations. After the third or fourth donation, they “complete” — a <dfn class="term" data-def="A mild or indirect word substituted for one considered too harsh or blunt. The novel uses ‘donations’, ‘completions’, and ‘carers’ to sanitise a system of forced organ removal and early death.">euphemism</dfn> for death. The entire system depends on the clones’ passive acceptance. Nobody escapes. Nobody fights. The absence of rebellion is perhaps the most disturbing aspect of the novel, and Ishiguro uses it to explore how people can be conditioned to accept even the most unjust fate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">This passivity mirrors real-world situations where oppressed groups internalise the values of the society that exploits them. Ishiguro has said the novel is “about how we all find strategies to avoid thinking about death” — the clones are simply a more literal version of what every human being does.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Genre: Dystopia Without Rebellion</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Most <dfn class="term" data-def="A genre of fiction set in an imagined society characterised by suffering, oppression, or injustice. Classic examples include Orwell’s 1984 and Atwood’s The Handmaid’s Tale.">dystopian</dfn> novels feature a protagonist who recognises injustice and fights against it. Orwell’s <em>1984</em> has Winston Smith’s doomed rebellion; Atwood’s <em>The Handmaid’s Tale</em> has Offred’s secret resistance. Ishiguro subverts this entirely. Kathy, Tommy, and Ruth never rebel. They never try to escape. They accept their fate with a quiet resignation that many readers find more horrifying than any act of violence.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">The novel also draws on the <dfn class="term" data-def="A type of novel set in a residential school, often exploring themes of friendship, hierarchy, and the loss of innocence.">boarding school genre</dfn>. Hailsham has dormitories, art lessons, sports pavilions, and playground hierarchies. But these comforting, familiar elements make the underlying reality more sinister. The conventions of an English boarding school story — midnight feasts, friendships, petty rivalries — gradually become a mask for institutional cruelty. Ishiguro uses the reader’s genre expectations against them: you expect a comforting coming-of-age story and instead receive a meditation on mortality.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n13">Ishiguro’s prose is deliberately understated. Kathy narrates in a conversational, almost casual tone, even when describing the most devastating events. When she describes Tommy’s final donation, there is no dramatic outcry — only the quiet statement that she “lost” him. This restraint forces the reader to supply the emotional response that the narrator withholds. It also reflects how the clones have been conditioned: they lack the emotional vocabulary to express outrage because they have never been taught that outrage is appropriate.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Ishiguro once described his approach as “the surface calm, and the turmoil underneath.” The gap between what Kathy says and what the reader feels creates a persistent sense of unease. In the exam, you should analyse how this understated language produces a more powerful emotional effect than dramatic writing would.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n15" data-revision-tip="Close this and list three ways Ishiguro’s novel differs from a typical dystopian story like 1984 or The Handmaid’s Tale.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ishiguro subverts the dystopian genre by removing rebellion entirely. The clones never fight, escape, or protest. Their passive acceptance is what makes the novel so disturbing — it asks the reader why <em>we</em> accept injustice in our own world. Kathy reflects: “What I’m not sure about is if our lives have been so different from the lives of the people we save.”</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">“We Were Told and Not Told”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">One of the novel’s most important phrases appears early: “We were told and not told.” Kathy reflects that the guardians at Hailsham gave the students fragments of truth about their purpose but never stated it plainly. The children absorbed these fragments without fully understanding them. Ishiguro uses this idea structurally — the reader, too, is “told and not told.” The novel drops clues from the opening pages, but the full picture emerges gradually. This technique of <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrative technique where important information is hinted at but not fully revealed until later, creating suspense and forcing the reader to piece together the truth.">delayed revelation</dfn> mirrors how the clones themselves come to understand their fate: slowly, reluctantly, and too late to change anything.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">This phrase also captures Ishiguro’s view of how societies manage uncomfortable truths. We know about factory farming, climate change, and global inequality — but we choose not to think about them fully. The novel suggests that being “told and not told” is a universal human condition, not just a feature of Hailsham.</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n20">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n21">Ishiguro wrote <em>Never Let Me Go</em> during debates about cloning and bioethics, but the novel is ultimately about denial, mortality, and what it means to be human.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">The novel subverts the dystopian genre: there is no rebellion, no escape — only quiet acceptance, which forces the reader to confront their own passivity.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n23">“We were told and not told” describes both the characters’ experience and the reader’s: Ishiguro uses delayed revelation to mirror how we avoid uncomfortable truths.</li></ul>
