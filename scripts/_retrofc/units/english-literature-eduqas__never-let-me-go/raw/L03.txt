<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The Cottages: Freedom Without Purpose</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">After leaving Hailsham, Kathy, Tommy, and Ruth move to the <dfn class="term" data-def="A rundown former farmstead where young clones live after leaving Hailsham and before becoming carers or beginning donations. It represents a transitional space between childhood protection and adult reality.">Cottages</dfn> — a halfway house where young clones live before becoming carers and then donors. Ishiguro’s description emphasises their bleakness: “the buildings were old and full of draughts.” Unlike Hailsham’s curated environment, the Cottages offer a version of freedom. The students can visit nearby towns, read magazines, and form relationships. But this freedom is hollow — they have no money, no family, no career prospects, and no future beyond the donation programme. Their ‘freedom’ only highlights how trapped they truly are.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">The Cottages also mark the transition from innocence to awareness. At Hailsham, the clones were sheltered. At the Cottages, they begin to encounter the outside world — and to realise how different they are from ‘normal’ humans. This growing awareness does not lead to rebellion but to a deeper, more painful form of acceptance.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Ruth’s Mimicry: Borrowed Identities</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">At the Cottages, the clones encounter <dfn class="term" data-def="Older clone students who have already spent time at the Cottages and developed social habits copied from television and magazines rather than from genuine personal experience.">veterans</dfn> — older students who imitate behaviours they have seen on television: casual couple gestures, slang, magazine reading. Ruth begins copying these affectations, particularly a gesture where couples tap each other on the arm while laughing. Kathy notices: “it wasn’t something she’d done before.” Ruth also borrows conversational phrases from a television show, using them as if they were her own.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Ishiguro uses this <dfn class="term" data-def="The imitation of other people’s behaviour, speech, or mannerisms. In the novel, the clones copy behaviour from television and veterans because they have no authentic cultural identity of their own.">mimicry</dfn> to explore identity. The clones have been denied family, history, and social context. Without authentic roots, they construct selves from borrowed fragments of ‘normal’ life. Ruth’s mimicry is both poignant and disturbing: she is trying to become a person the world will never allow her to be. When Kathy confronts her about the copied gestures, Ruth reacts defensively — the fragility of her constructed identity cannot survive scrutiny.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n7" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain: why do the clones copy behaviour from television, and what does this reveal about their sense of identity?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>The clones copy behaviours from television and veterans because they have no authentic cultural identity. Denied family, history, and social belonging, they construct selves from borrowed fragments. Ishiguro shows that identity depends on belonging — and the clones belong nowhere.</p>
</div>

    <p data-narration-id="n8">At the Cottages, Tommy develops a theory about why the guardians placed such emphasis on art at Hailsham. He believes that if he can create truly good artwork, it will prove he has a soul, and this will help him and Kathy obtain a <dfn class="term" data-def="A rumoured postponement of donations, supposedly available to couples who can prove they are truly in love. It is revealed at the end of the novel to be a myth.">deferral</dfn> — a postponement of their donations. He begins drawing small, intricate animals: “tiny, meticulously detailed creatures.” These drawings are both hopeful and heartbreaking. Tommy is literally trying to draw his way to survival, investing enormous care in art that the system will never value.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Tommy’s theory is half-right: the Gallery <em>was</em> about proving clones have souls. But the deferral does not exist. Ishiguro uses Tommy’s misunderstanding to show how the clones create their own myths of escape — stories that give them hope precisely because the truth is unbearable.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">The Search for Ruth’s “Possible”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The most emotionally charged Cottages episode is the search for Ruth’s <dfn class="term" data-def="The original human from whom a clone was copied. Finding one’s ‘possible’ would offer a glimpse of who you ‘really’ are — the person whose genetic material you share.">possible</dfn> — the original human she was cloned from. A veteran claims to have spotted a woman in an office in Norfolk who looks like Ruth. The group travels there, and the search ends in failure: the woman looks nothing like Ruth. Ruth is devastated and lashes out: “We’re modelled from trash. Junkies, prostitutes, winos, tramps.”</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">This moment is crucial. Ruth’s outburst reveals the clones’ deepest fear: that their originals are not respectable people but society’s outcasts. The word “trash” carries enormous weight — it is how society views the clones themselves, and Ruth has <dfn class="term" data-def="When a marginalised group absorbs and believes the negative views that the dominant society holds about them.">internalised</dfn> this judgment. The search for the possible is really a search for human worth: if your original is ‘somebody’, then perhaps you are somebody too. Its failure strips away even that hope.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: what does Ruth’s reaction to the failed ‘possible’ search reveal about internalised oppression?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Ruth’s outburst — “We’re modelled from trash” — shows internalised oppression: the clones have absorbed society’s view that they are disposable. The search for the possible is a search for human worth that society has already denied them.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n14">Norfolk: “The Lost Corner of England”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n15">At Hailsham, the students develop a joke that Norfolk is “the lost corner of England” — the place where all lost things end up. Ishiguro transforms this childhood joke into the novel’s most powerful <dfn class="term" data-def="A recurring image, idea, or symbol that develops significance across a text through repetition and variation.">motif</dfn>. Norfolk is where they search for Ruth’s possible (and fail), where Tommy finds Kathy a replacement copy of the Judy Bridgewater tape, and where Kathy stands at the wire fence in the final scene, imagining her losses might return.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Norfolk represents hope — specifically, the human need to believe that what is lost can be recovered. But Ishiguro never allows this hope to be fulfilled. The possible is not Ruth’s original. The replacement tape is not the same as the one Kathy lost. The losses cannot be undone. Norfolk is the place where hope and reality coexist without resolution, and this tension gives the novel its emotional power.</p>

This is one of the novel’s tenderest moments — Tommy remembers that Kathy lost her tape and has been searching for a replacement. His gift shows genuine care and attention. But Ishiguro undercuts the tenderness: the replacement is not the original. What matters is not the object but what it represents — the desire to restore what has been lost, even when restoration is impossible.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n19">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n20">Ruth’s mimicry of the veterans shows the clones constructing identity from borrowed fragments — they have no authentic cultural roots.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n21">The failed search for Ruth’s possible, and her outburst that clones are “modelled from trash,” reveals internalised oppression and the desperate search for human worth.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n22">Norfolk as “the lost corner of England” represents the human need to believe lost things can be recovered — a hope the novel acknowledges but never fulfils.</li></ul>
