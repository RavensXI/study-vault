<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Kathy H.: The Passive Narrator</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Kathy is the novel’s narrator and emotional centre. Her voice is characterised by restraint, digression, and constant self-qualification: “I’m not sure about this,” “I might have this wrong.” These hesitations serve multiple purposes. They mimic how real memory works — uncertain, reconstructed, shaped by emotion. They reflect the clones’ conditioning: Kathy has been taught to doubt her own perceptions. And they make her an <dfn class="term" data-def="A narrator whose account of events may be distorted by personal bias, limited understanding, or deliberate omission. Kathy’s constant qualifications make the reader question what she chooses to tell and what she leaves out.">unreliable narrator</dfn>, filtering events through her own biases and blind spots.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Kathy never expresses anger about her fate. She describes losing Tommy and Ruth with the same measured tone she uses for childhood anecdotes. This <dfn class="term" data-def="Calm acceptance of one’s situation, even when that situation is unjust. Kathy’s passivity is both her defining characteristic and the novel’s central provocation.">passivity</dfn> is both her strength and the novel’s great tragedy. As a carer, she is competent and compassionate, described as “one of the best carers around.” But being a good carer means serving the system that will kill her. Kathy’s quiet competence is the novel’s most unsettling feature: she cannot see that her compliance enables the very institution she should resist.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and explain three effects of Kathy’s self-qualifying narration: ‘I’m not sure about this’, ‘I might have this wrong.’">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Kathy’s qualifying narration creates three effects: it mimics real memory’s uncertainty, reflects the clones’ conditioned self-doubt, and makes her unreliable — the reader must question what she reveals and what she withholds. Her calm acceptance is both admirable and tragic.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Tommy: Emotion, Art, and Sacrifice</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">Tommy is the novel’s most emotionally expressive character. As a child, he has violent <dfn class="term" data-def="Sudden, uncontrollable outbursts of anger or frustration. Tommy’s childhood tantrums become significant when contrasted with his adult restraint and final scream.">tantrums</dfn> when teased about his inability to create art. His rages terrify and fascinate the other students. The other children’s cruelty towards Tommy foreshadows how society treats the clones: those who do not conform — who cannot produce the ‘right’ kind of expression — are mocked and excluded.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">As an adult, Tommy undergoes a transformation. He begins drawing small, intricate animals — “tiny, meticulously detailed creatures” — believing his art might prove he has a soul and help secure a deferral. These drawings represent hope: Tommy is literally trying to draw his way to survival. When Miss Emily reveals the deferral is a myth, Tommy’s scream at the roadside is the novel’s most powerful moment. His crucifixion-like posture — “His arms were stretched out on either side of him, his head was tilted back” — positions him as a <dfn class="term" data-def="A person who suffers or dies for a cause or on behalf of others. Tommy’s posture during his scream echoes the crucifixion, framing him as a figure destroyed by a system he did not create.">sacrificial figure</dfn>, destroyed by a system he never chose.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">Ruth is the novel’s most complex character. She is manipulative, jealous, and controlling — she deliberately keeps Kathy and Tommy apart because she wants Tommy for herself. At the Cottages, she copies the veterans’ gestures, invents a fantasy career working in an office, and constructs a persona of sophistication that collapses under scrutiny.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Yet Ruth is also deeply vulnerable. Her fantasies are a desperate attempt to construct an identity in a world that denies her one. When the search for her ‘possible’ fails, she lashes out: “We’re modelled from trash.” This outburst reveals <dfn class="term" data-def="When a marginalised group absorbs and believes the negative views the dominant society holds about them, turning society’s contempt inward.">internalised oppression</dfn> — Ruth has absorbed society’s contempt for the clones. Her deathbed confession, admitting she kept Kathy and Tommy apart, is the novel’s most emotionally honest moment. Ishiguro refuses to make Ruth a simple villain; her selfishness is a survival mechanism in a world that offers nothing.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Miss Emily and Madame: The Failed Saviours</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Miss Emily founded Hailsham to prove that clones deserve humane treatment. She is well-intentioned but ultimately ineffective: her campaign changed nothing. Society preferred not to know. Madame collected the Gallery artwork and was “afraid of us” — she could not overcome her revulsion even while fighting for the clones’ rights. Ishiguro uses these characters to critique liberal good intentions: caring about a marginalised group is not enough if the system that exploits them remains unchanged.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: why does Ishiguro make Madame afraid of the clones, even though she is fighting for their rights?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Madame’s fear of the clones despite advocating for them is Ishiguro’s sharpest critique: even those who fight for justice may harbour the very prejudices they oppose. Miss Emily and Madame represent the audience — people who feel sympathy but do not act to dismantle the system.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Miss Lucy: The Guardian Who Broke Ranks</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14"><dfn class="term" data-def="A guardian at Hailsham who believes the students should be told the truth about their purpose directly. She is dismissed after her outburst in the sports pavilion.">Miss Lucy</dfn> is the only guardian who refuses to participate in the system of evasion. Her outburst — “Your lives are set out for you” — is the novel’s first moment of plain truth. She believes the students deserve honesty. But Ishiguro complicates this: Miss Lucy’s truth has no effect. The students have been so thoroughly conditioned that even direct knowledge cannot provoke action. Miss Lucy is dismissed, and the system continues. Ishiguro suggests that truth alone is insufficient to change a society that has decided not to listen.</p>

    <svg class="collapsible-icon" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2"><polyline points="6 9 12 15 18 9"/></svg>
  </button>
  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n15">The Kathy–Tommy–Ruth triangle drives the novel’s emotional core. Kathy and Tommy share a genuine connection from childhood, but Ruth claims Tommy first. Ruth’s jealousy keeps them apart for years. When Ruth finally confesses, Kathy and Tommy have only a few months together before Tommy “completes.” Ishiguro uses this truncated relationship to argue that love’s value is not diminished by its brevity — but also that time lost to manipulation and fear cannot be recovered.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n17">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n18">Kathy’s passive, self-qualifying narration reflects the clones’ conditioning and makes her unreliable — the reader must question what she reveals and withholds.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n19">Tommy’s arc from childhood tantrums to intricate art to his final scream traces a journey from uncontrolled emotion to purposeful creativity to futile rebellion.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n20">Ruth’s manipulation masks deep vulnerability, and her deathbed confession is the novel’s only moment of full moral honesty.</li></ul>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n16">For character-focused questions, avoid simply describing what characters do — analyse <em>how</em> Ishiguro presents them through language choices. For Kathy, focus on her qualifying, self-doubting narration. For Tommy, contrast his childhood tantrums with his adult restraint and final scream. For Ruth, explore how her mimicry and aggression mask vulnerability. Always link characterisation to Ishiguro’s wider themes (AO4).</p>
