<h2 data-narration-id="n1">The World Shakespeare Wrote For</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">When Shakespeare wrote <em>Macbeth</em> around 1606, England was ruled by King James I — a monarch genuinely fascinated by, and terrified of, witchcraft. James had written his own book on the subject, <em>Daemonologie</em> (1597), and had personally overseen witch trials in Scotland. For a <dfn class="term" data-def="Relating to the reign of King James I of England (1603–1625), whose beliefs about witchcraft and kingship heavily influenced Shakespeare.">Jacobean</dfn> audience, the witches in <em>Macbeth</em> were not fantasy figures — they represented a genuine, terrifying threat.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">Understanding this context is essential For the exam . The play was written partly to flatter James I and reflect his worldview. Every scene involving the supernatural would have provoked a far stronger reaction from Shakespeare’s original audience than it does from a modern one.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall what King James I wrote in 1597 and which trials he attended that influenced Shakespeare's Macbeth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>King James I wrote <em>Daemonologie</em> in 1597 and personally attended witch trials. Shakespeare wrote <em>Macbeth</em> partly to appeal to the king’s interests and to warn against the dangers of unchecked ambition and supernatural interference.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">Jacobean Attitudes to Witchcraft</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In the early seventeenth century, most people believed that witches were real. The 1604 Witchcraft Act made it a capital offence to invoke evil spirits. Witches were thought to be servants of the Devil who could predict the future, control the weather, and cause disease. Their <dfn class="term" data-def="Animal companions believed to be given to witches by the Devil, such as cats, toads, or ravens.">familiars</dfn> — animals like cats and toads — were considered proof of a pact with Satan.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Shakespeare taps directly into these fears. The witches in <em>Macbeth</em> speak in <dfn class="term" data-def="A metrical pattern of a stressed syllable followed by an unstressed syllable, creating an unnatural, chant-like rhythm.">trochaic tetrameter</dfn> rather than the iambic pentameter used by other characters, making them sound inhuman. Their chant “Double, double toil and trouble” would have genuinely disturbed an audience that believed such spells could work.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n8">James I firmly believed in the <dfn class="term" data-def="The belief that a monarch's authority comes directly from God, making regicide not just murder but a sin against the divine order.">Divine Right of Kings</dfn> — the idea that monarchs are appointed by God and answerable only to Him. This means that killing a king (regicide) is not simply murder but a sin against the natural and divine order. When Macbeth murders Duncan, he is committing the ultimate crime in Jacobean eyes.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n9">Shakespeare reinforces this through the <dfn class="term" data-def="The Elizabethan and Jacobean belief that everything in the universe had a fixed, God-given place in a hierarchy: God, angels, monarch, nobles, commoners, animals.">Great Chain of Being</dfn>. After Duncan’s murder, the natural world descends into chaos — horses eat each other, the sky darkens, and an owl kills a falcon. These unnatural events signal that Macbeth has ruptured the divinely ordained order.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n10">Act 1, Scene 1: “Fair Is Foul, and Foul Is Fair”</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n11">The play opens with the three witches meeting on a desolate heath amid “thunder and lightning.” Their very first scene establishes the theme of moral inversion. The paradox “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” tells the audience that nothing in this play will be as it seems — appearances will deceive, and moral boundaries will collapse.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n12">Notice that Macbeth’s first words in Act 1, Scene 3 echo this line: “So foul and fair a day I have not seen.” Before he has even met the witches, he speaks their language. Shakespeare suggests that Macbeth is already connected to the supernatural — the seeds of ambition may already exist within him.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n13" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and name the literary device used in 'Fair is foul, and foul is fair' and explain how Macbeth echoes this before meeting the witches.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>“Fair is foul, and foul is fair” is a <dfn class="term" data-def="A figure of speech in which the order of words in one clause is reversed in the next, creating a mirror effect that emphasises contrast.">chiasmus</dfn> — a mirror structure that reverses meaning. It establishes the play’s central theme: appearance versus reality. Macbeth echoes this language before meeting the witches, linking him to evil from the start.</p>
</div>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n14">Shakespeare uses <dfn class="term" data-def="A literary device where the weather or natural environment reflects the emotional state of characters or the moral state of the world.">pathetic fallacy</dfn> throughout the play to signal the presence of evil. The witches always appear in storms and darkness. After Duncan’s murder, “the night has been unruly” with “strange screams of death.” The disruption of the natural world mirrors the disruption of the political order.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n15">This technique was powerful for a Jacobean audience who believed weather could be influenced by supernatural forces. Shakespeare uses the setting to create an atmosphere of dread that permeates the entire play.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n16">The Role of the Witches: Catalyst or Cause?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n17">A central debate in studying <em>Macbeth</em> is whether the witches cause Macbeth’s downfall or simply reveal ambitions he already possesses. Shakespeare deliberately leaves this ambiguous. The witches prophesy that Macbeth will become Thane of Cawdor and then king, but they never tell him to murder anyone. It is Macbeth and Lady Macbeth who decide to act on the prophecies.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18">In the AQA exam, exploring this ambiguity is what separates a good response from an excellent one. You might argue that the witches are a dramatic device through which Shakespeare externalises Macbeth’s inner desires, or that they represent the genuine power of evil to corrupt even a brave and loyal subject.</p>

<h3 data-narration-id="n23">Key Takeaways</h3>
<ul><li data-narration-id="n24">Shakespeare wrote <em>Macbeth</em> for a Jacobean audience that genuinely feared witchcraft and believed in the Divine Right of Kings — context that intensifies every supernatural scene.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n25">The witches’ paradox “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” establishes the central theme of appearance versus reality and is echoed by Macbeth before he even meets them.</li>
<li data-narration-id="n26">Whether the witches cause Macbeth’s downfall or merely reveal his existing ambition is a key debate — exploring this ambiguity in essays will strengthen your analysis.</li></ul>
