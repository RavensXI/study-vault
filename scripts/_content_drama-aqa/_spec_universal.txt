UNIVERSAL DRAMA SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (AQA GCSE Drama 8261, version 1.7, 26 April 2022)

This slice covers the parts of Section 3.1 "Understanding drama" that apply to ALL students regardless of which set play they study:
- Theatre roles, stagecraft and theatrical conventions (used in Unit 1 — Theatre Roles & Stagecraft)
- Drama practitioners and styles (used in Unit 2 — Practitioners & Styles)
- Live theatre production analysis (used in Unit 3 — Live Theatre Review)

Plus the AQA command-word taxonomy and the per-question-type guidance.

---

3.1.1 KNOWLEDGE AND UNDERSTANDING (universal — applies to all set plays and to live theatre review)

Students must develop knowledge and understanding of the following:

CHARACTERISTICS OF PERFORMANCE TEXT(S) AND DRAMATIC WORK(S)
- genre
- structure
- character
- form
- style
- language
- sub-text
- character motivation and interaction
- the creation of mood and atmosphere
- the development of pace and rhythm
- dramatic climax
- stage directions
- the practical demands of the text

SOCIAL, CULTURAL AND HISTORICAL CONTEXTS
- the social, cultural and historical context in which the performance texts studied are set
- the theatrical conventions of the period in which the performance texts studied were created

HOW MEANING IS INTERPRETED AND COMMUNICATED
- performance conventions
- use of performance space and spatial relationships on stage
- actor and audience configuration
- relationships between performers and audience
- design fundamentals such as scale, shape, colour, texture
- the design of props and the design of sets such as revolves, trucks, projection, multimedia, pyrotechnics, smoke machines, flying
- the design of costume including hair and make-up
- the design of lighting such as direction, colour, intensity, special effects
- the design of sound such as direction, amplification, music, sound effects both live and recorded
- performers' vocal interpretation of character (accent, volume, pitch, timing, pace, intonation, phrasing, emotional range, delivery of lines)
- performers' physical interpretation of character (build, age, height, facial features, movement, posture, gesture, facial expression)

DRAMA AND THEATRE TERMINOLOGY AND HOW TO USE IT APPROPRIATELY

Stage positioning:
- upstage (left, right, centre)
- downstage (left, right, centre)
- centre stage

Staging configuration:
- theatre in the round
- proscenium arch
- thrust stage
- traverse
- end on staging
- promenade

Students should have a general understanding of the implications of the above stage configurations on the use of the performance space.

THE ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES OF THEATRE MAKERS IN CONTEMPORARY PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE
Roles:
- playwright
- performer
- understudy
- lighting designer
- sound designer
- set designer
- costume designer
- puppet designer
- director
- stage manager
- technician
- theatre manager

For each role, students should know:
- the activities each may undertake on a day-to-day basis
- the aspect(s) of the rehearsal/performance process each is accountable for (their contribution to the whole production being a success)

3.1.3 AREA OF STUDY 2 — LIVE THEATRE PRODUCTION (used in Unit 3)

Students must learn how to analyse and evaluate the work of live theatre makers (performers and/or designers).

Students should aim to understand productions in terms of the relevant content listed in Knowledge and understanding (3.1.1) and in addition consider:
- how the play has been interpreted in the production seen and what messages the company might be trying to communicate
- the skills demonstrated by the performers and how successfully meaning was communicated to the audience by the performers
- the design skills demonstrated in the production and how successfully meaning was communicated to the audience through design

To aid their analysis students should carry out background research into the production.

They may read the play and reviews of the production and should develop an understanding of:
- the plot and characters
- specific features or hallmarks of the style/genre of the production
- the context of the play/production

Live theatre could include:
- plays
- physical theatre
- theatre in education
- musical theatre

Productions may be professional or amateur (not peer).

The performance must:
- be a minimum of 50 minutes in duration excluding any intervals or breaks
- include at least two actors
- include dialogue
- include a range of production values (lighting, sound, set and costume)

3.4 GUIDANCE ON THEATRICAL SKILLS

3.4.1 PERFORMER
Performers are expected to develop:
- vocal skills and techniques: clarity of diction, inflection, accent, intonation and phrasing; pace, pause and timing; projection, pitch; emotional range; song and/or choral speaking
- physical skills and techniques: movement, body language, posture, gesture, gait, co-ordination, stillness, timing, control; facial expression; eye contact, listening, expression of mood; spatial awareness; interaction with other performers; dance and choral movement
- an appropriate performer/audience relationship sustained throughout the performance

3.4.2 LIGHTING DESIGNER
Designers are expected to develop the ability to design and realise:
- lighting that contributes positively to the overall effect of the performance and communicates intended meaning
- lighting capable of establishing the location, time and/or to enhance mood or atmosphere
- a range of lighting effects through colour, gobos, gels, filters, gauzes, projections and lighting states (intensity, fading and cross-fading, blackout, shadow, directional lighting)
- selection of equipment: profile, Fresnel, flood, moving light, birdies, strobes; rigged, floor and side lights; angle and focus of lanterns
- understanding of how to apply rigging, positioning, angling and focusing

3.4.3 SOUND DESIGNER
Sound designers are expected to develop the ability to design and realise:
- sounds that contribute positively to the overall effect and communicate intended meaning
- sounds capable of establishing the location, period, time and/or to enhance mood or atmosphere
- selection of equipment: microphones, amplifiers, software, musical or percussion instruments
- different types of sounds: live and recorded sound, directional sound, music
- a range of sound effects: audio effects, edited and/or mixed sound, distortion, reverb, echo, volume/amplification, fades, soundscapes

3.4.4 SET DESIGNER
Set designers are expected to develop the ability to design and realise:
- sets that contribute positively to the overall effect and communicate intended meaning
- sets capable of establishing the location, period, time and/or to enhance mood or atmosphere
- selection of materials and equipment: flats, cyclorama, floor coverings, drapes, furnishings, projections/media
- sets showing understanding of stage configuration, performer/audience relationship, action taking place, positioning of entrances/exits, effective, smooth and fluent scene changes, use of space, scale, levels
- sets selecting as appropriate dressings, furnishings, material, colour, texture, props

3.4.5 COSTUME DESIGNER
Costume designs may include masks, hair and make-up as well as clothing.
Costume designers are expected to develop the ability to design and realise:
- costumes that contribute positively to the overall effect and communicate intended meaning
- costumes capable of establishing the character, period, location and/or contributing to mood or atmosphere
- selection of appropriate materials: fabrics, textures, trimmings, accessories
- costumes showing understanding of colour, shape, appropriate fit, period detail, ethnic authenticity, movement constraints

Where students include make-up and hair:
- make-up: grease-paint, liquids, powders, facial hair, eyelashes, gum, putty, prosthetics, stage blood
- character through make-up: aging, fantasy characters
- hairstyles appropriate to character, period, age, ethnicity

3.4.6 PUPPET DESIGNER
Puppet designers are expected to develop the ability to design and realise puppets with a well-defined character and audience appeal.

DRAMA PRACTITIONERS AND STYLES (used in Unit 2)

The specification does not prescribe a closed list of practitioners. Centres typically teach the following as the canonical four because they cover the major modern stylistic traditions students will need to apply to their set play:

CONSTANTIN STANISLAVSKI (1863–1938)
- Russian actor-director who developed the first systematic acting "system".
- Naturalism / realism on stage: psychological truth, the actor inhabits the character.
- Key concepts: emotion memory (drawing on personal experience), given circumstances (the world of the play), the magic if (acting "as if" the situation were real), objectives and super-objectives, method of physical actions, the fourth wall.
- Founded the Moscow Art Theatre with Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko (1898).
- Influence: realist and naturalist theatre; modern screen acting; the "Method" tradition in the United States.

BERTOLT BRECHT (1898–1956)
- German playwright and director who developed Epic Theatre.
- Theatre as a tool for social and political change; the audience should think, not just feel.
- Key concepts: Verfremdungseffekt (alienation effect / making the familiar strange), gestus (a physical attitude that reveals social position), breaking the fourth wall, direct address, placards, songs interrupting action, visible stage lights, episodic structure, narrators.
- Plays: Mother Courage and Her Children, The Caucasian Chalk Circle, The Threepenny Opera.

ANTONIN ARTAUD (1896–1948)
- French theorist who proposed the Theatre of Cruelty.
- Theatre should overwhelm the senses and shock the spectator into awareness.
- Key concepts: total theatre, ritual, sensory assault, non-verbal communication, sound and light as primary languages, the audience surrounded by the action.
- Influence: physical theatre, immersive theatre, the early work of Peter Brook.

PHYSICAL THEATRE COMPANIES (FRANTIC ASSEMBLY, COMPLICITE, DV8, BERKOFF)
- Movement-led storytelling that communicates without (or alongside) words.
- Key techniques: ensemble work, multi-roling, lifts, chair duets, "round-by-through-under" movement vocabulary (Frantic Assembly's signature exploration of physical contact), tableau, soundscape-led movement.
- Frantic Assembly co-developed Things I Know to Be True with Andrew Bovell (2016).
- Complicite are known for object transformation and storytelling theatre.

OTHER PRACTITIONERS WHOSE WORK MAY BE TAUGHT
- Joan Littlewood (Theatre Workshop, devised methods, premiered A Taste of Honey 1958).
- Peter Brook (the empty space, ritual theatre, the carpet show).
- Augusto Boal (Theatre of the Oppressed, forum theatre, the spect-actor).
- Steven Berkoff (heightened physical style).

AQA COMMAND-WORD TAXONOMY (universal — applies across all units)

Students should expect the following command words on the written paper. StudyVault question types map to these as follows:

- "Identify" / "Name" / "Give" → 1 mark — Identify (1 short factual answer)
- "Define" / "What is meant by" → 2 marks — Define (definition with one feature OR two-sentence definition)
- "Explain" / "Describe the effect of" → 4 marks — Explain Effect (a point with developed explanation of effect on audience)
- "Analyse" (short) → 4 marks — Short Analysis (a point + analytical development of method or meaning)
- "How would you (as a performer) interpret…" → 8 marks — Interpret as Performer (vocal AND physical choices, anchored to a named moment)
- "How would you (as a designer) design…" → 8 marks — Interpret as Designer (specific design elements: lantern type, gobo, fabric, colour, fitting; anchored to a named moment; effect on audience)
- "Analyse the playwright's intentions" / "Analyse what X is trying to make the audience think" → 12 marks — Analyse Intentions (multi-paragraph, references to dramatic methods, analysis of effect)
- "Discuss how you would direct/stage…" / "How would you, as performer or designer, bring this extract to life" → 20 marks — Extended Staging Response (the highest-stakes set-play question; requires multi-skill staging across performer + design + audience effect)
- "Analyse and evaluate the live theatre production you have seen" → 32 marks — Live Theatre Review (Unit 3 only; specific performer choices, specific design choices, directorial intention, effect on audience, substantiated evaluative judgement)

When writing practice questions and mark schemes:
- Use the StudyVault rubric (Mastering / Secure / Developing / Emerging) for 8+ mark levels-based questions.
- Use point-by-point credit allocation for 1-, 2- and 4-mark questions ("1 mark for naming a vocal skill; 1 mark for explaining its effect on mood…").
- NEVER use AQA's "Level 1 / 2 / 3 / 4" descriptors. NEVER use "Award N marks for". NEVER use "Nothing worthy of credit".
