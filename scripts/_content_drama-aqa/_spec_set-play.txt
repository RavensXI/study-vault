3.1.2 AREA OF STUDY 1 — SET PLAY

Students must study and explore practically one set play chosen from the prescribed list:

1. The Crucible by Arthur Miller (Methuen Student Editions)
2. Blood Brothers by Willy Russell (Methuen Modern Classics)
3. Noughts and Crosses by Malorie Blackman / Dominic Cooke (Nick Hern Modern Plays)
4. Around the World in 80 Days by Jules Verne / Laura Eason (Nick Hern Modern Plays)
5. Things I Know to Be True by Andrew Bovell (Nick Hern Modern Plays)
6. Romeo and Juliet by William Shakespeare (Palgrave Macmillan)
7. A Taste of Honey by Shelagh Delaney (Methuen Drama)
8. The Great Wave by Francis Turnly (Methuen Drama)
9. The Empress by Tanika Gupta (Methuen Drama, Student Editions)

Study should be targeted at:
- developing knowledge and understanding (as described in 3.1.1) of the characteristics and context of the whole play
- exploring ideas for how the play may be interpreted practically

The exam will include one compulsory short answer question for all students linking design and context and/or theatrical conventions. Students do not need practical experience of design to answer this.

One part of the set-play question section will offer students the choice of answering as a performer or designer (lighting, sound, set, costume, puppets).

Students must not write about the same play in the set-play questions and the live-theatre review question — the live production seen cannot be the set play.

3.1.1 KNOWLEDGE AND UNDERSTANDING — applied to your set play

For YOUR chosen set play, you should be able to discuss:

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE PERFORMANCE TEXT
- genre (e.g. tragedy, social realism, kitchen-sink drama, dystopian political drama, comedy of adventure, hybrid Frantic-Assembly-influenced drama)
- structure (act/scene divisions; framing devices like the Narrator in Blood Brothers; episodic vs linear; non-linear flashbacks)
- character (protagonists and antagonists; how each is constructed through dialogue, action, stage directions)
- form and style (naturalistic vs stylised; verse vs prose; multi-roled ensemble vs fixed cast; documentary vs poetic)
- language (heightened idiom; dialect; verse; bilingual exchanges)
- sub-text (what is implied or unsaid; emotional undercurrents)
- character motivation and interaction (what drives each character; how relationships shift)
- the creation of mood and atmosphere (how the playwright builds a specific emotional climate scene by scene)
- the development of pace and rhythm (how the play's momentum changes; comic timing; tension building)
- dramatic climax (how the play's high points are constructed and where they fall)
- stage directions (what the playwright tells you about the set, costume, lighting, sound, blocking)
- the practical demands of the text (cast size; multi-roling; technical requirements)

SOCIAL, CULTURAL AND HISTORICAL CONTEXTS
- the social, cultural and historical context in which the play is set (Salem 1692, Liverpool 1980s, Salford 1958, Verona Renaissance, contemporary Adelaide, Victorian London, 1980s–2000s Japan and DPRK)
- the theatrical conventions of the period in which the play was written and first performed (Elizabethan playhouse for R&J; Theatre Workshop for A Taste of Honey; National Theatre house style for The Great Wave; West End musical theatre for Blood Brothers; RSC ensemble for Noughts and Crosses and The Empress)

HOW MEANING IS INTERPRETED AND COMMUNICATED — applied to your set play

You should think and write about every set-play moment as a PERFORMER and as a DESIGNER:

PERFORMER
- vocal interpretation: accent, volume, pitch, timing, pace, intonation, phrasing, emotional range, delivery of lines
- physical interpretation: build, age, height, facial features, movement, posture, gesture, facial expression
- characterisation: bringing the character's inner life into vocal and physical specifics

DESIGNER
- set design: scale, shape, colour, texture, period, materials, levels, configuration, transitions; props as symbols
- lighting design: direction, colour, intensity, special effects (gobos, gels, filters, gauzes), states (intensity, fading and cross-fading, blackout, shadow, directional lighting); choice of lanterns (profile, Fresnel, flood, moving light, birdies, strobes); rigged, floor and side lights
- sound design: live and recorded sound, directional sound, music, sound effects (audio effects, edited and/or mixed sound, distortion, reverb, echo, volume/amplification, fades, soundscapes)
- costume design (including hair and make-up): fabrics, textures, trimmings, accessories, colour, shape, fit, period detail, ethnic authenticity, movement constraints; aging and character through make-up

STAGE POSITIONING
- upstage (left, right, centre)
- downstage (left, right, centre)
- centre stage

STAGING CONFIGURATION
- theatre in the round
- proscenium arch
- thrust stage
- traverse
- end on staging
- promenade

You should be able to discuss the implications of any of these configurations on how meaning would be communicated for your chosen set play.

ASSESSMENT OBJECTIVES (universal but applied to set play in this section)

AO1: Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of how drama and theatre is developed and performed.
AO2: Apply theatrical skills to realise artistic intentions in live performance.
AO3: Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of how meaning is interpreted and communicated.
AO4: Analyse and evaluate their own work and the work of others.

For the WRITTEN paper (set-play and live-theatre questions): AO3 (32%) and AO4 (8%) dominate. AO1 (sup­porting knowledge) underpins all answers. AO2 is assessed practically in coursework, not on the written paper.

---

QUESTION TYPES ON THE SET-PLAY SECTION

Students should expect a mix of:

- Short multiple-choice on theatre roles and stagecraft (4 marks total — uses our 1-mark Identify type).
- Short answer linking design and context for the set play (a 2- or 4-mark question — uses our 2-mark Define or 4-mark Explain Effect types).
- Performer / designer choice questions on a named extract — students choose to answer "as a performer" OR "as a designer" (8 marks — uses our 8-mark Interpret as Performer or 8-mark Interpret as Designer types).
- Analysis of playwright's intentions on a moment of the play (12 marks — uses our 12-mark Analyse Intentions type).
- Extended staging response on a longer extract or a key moment, with directorial breadth across performer and design choices (20 marks — uses our 20-mark Extended Staging Response type).

The 32-mark Live Theatre Review question is NOT used in set-play units — it is reserved for Unit 3 (Live Theatre Review) and is about a different production from the set play.

---

WHAT YOUR ANSWERS SHOULD DEMONSTRATE

- Specific knowledge of the named moment / scene of the play (no plot reproduction; reference moments by name).
- Concrete performer choices: vocal AND physical, anchored to that moment.
- Concrete design choices: lighting / sound / set / costume — with named elements (lantern type, gobo, fabric, colour, fitting, instrument, soundscape texture).
- Justification: how the choice creates meaning for the audience at that moment.
- Awareness of context: social, cultural, historical — and how that context shapes the choices a 21st-century company might make.
- For 12-, 20- and 32-mark questions: a substantiated judgement that weighs interpretive choices against each other and reaches a directorial decision.

---

AQA COMMAND-WORD TAXONOMY (set-play context)

- "Identify" / "Name" / "Give" → 1 mark — Identify
- "Define" / "What is meant by" → 2 marks — Define
- "Explain" / "Describe the effect of" → 4 marks — Explain Effect
- "Analyse" (short) → 4 marks — Short Analysis
- "How would you (as a performer) interpret…" → 8 marks — Interpret as Performer (vocal AND physical, named moment)
- "How would you (as a designer) design…" → 8 marks — Interpret as Designer (specific design elements; named moment; effect on audience)
- "Analyse the playwright's intentions" → 12 marks — Analyse Intentions
- "Discuss how you would direct/stage…" / "How would you bring this extract to life" → 20 marks — Extended Staging Response

When writing mark schemes for the StudyVault rubric:
- Mastering: full range of points; every choice concrete (named lantern/gobo/fabric/proxemic/vocal quality); every choice anchored to a named moment with a named effect on audience or character; substantiated judgement.
- Secure: most points present; generally concrete; at least one named design and one performer choice; generally accurate effect.
- Developing: relevant points but generic ("dark lighting", "sad music"); few or no named moments; effect on audience implied not stated.
- Emerging: basic listing with little explanation; descriptive rather than analytical.

NEVER use AQA's "Level 1 / 2 / 3 / 4". NEVER use "Award N marks for". NEVER use "Nothing worthy of credit".

COPYRIGHT AND QUOTATION DISCIPLINE

Specific editions are prescribed for each play. Reference the edition (Methuen Student Editions, Nick Hern Modern Plays, Palgrave Macmillan, Methuen Drama Student Editions) for orientation only — do NOT reproduce editorial notes, page numbers or footnote material in lesson content.

- Romeo and Juliet — public domain; quote freely but keep quotes short and purposeful.
- All eight other plays — IN COPYRIGHT. Hard cap of 15 words per quotation, maximum once per lesson, in quotation marks. Prefer paraphrase. Never reproduce dialogue blocks.
- Reference moments by name ("the linen scene", "Mickey's discovery", "the balcony scene") rather than reproducing them.

The play-specific `unit_level_teaching_brief` provides a detailed list of named scenes you can refer to without reproducing dialogue.
