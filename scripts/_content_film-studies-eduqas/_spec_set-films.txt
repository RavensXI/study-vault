SET-FILM SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (WJEC Eduqas GCSE Film Studies C670QS / WJEC 3670QS, version 7, May 2025)

This slice covers Section 2.1 (Component 1 — US film) and Section 2.2 (Component 2 — Global Film) as they apply to ANY of the set film options. Each set-film unit's batch JSON also includes a substantive `unit_level_teaching_brief` containing per-film synopsis, characters, themes, production context, critical reception, filmic methods, named scenes for micro-analysis, most-relevant theory and copyright status — read both this spec slice AND that brief together.

The spec is identical for English Eduqas centres (C670QS) and Welsh WJEC centres (3670QS). Lessons must work for both audiences.

---

WHAT EVERY SET-FILM ANSWER MUST DEMONSTRATE

Every set-film question rewards three things in proportion:

1. AO1 — knowledge and understanding (factual): correct names of director, cinematographer, year, country, principal collaborators, production context, critical reception, named sequences. 30% of qualification overall.
2. AO2 — analysis and application: how a specific filmic choice creates meaning for the spectator at a specific named moment. 50% of qualification overall and the most reliably under-performed objective at GCSE.
3. Specialist film vocabulary in extended writing: cinematography, mise-en-scene, editing, sound, narrative, genre, representation, aesthetic, context-related terms, theory terms used precisely.

Examiner-reported failure modes (apply to every set-film answer):
- describing what happens on screen instead of analysing how meaning is created (AO2 gap)
- naming a technique without anchoring it to a specific named sequence
- producing textbook narrative-theory descriptions in the abstract instead of applying the theory to a named moment
- writing two parallel single-film descriptions instead of a true comparative line (Component 1 Section A Q3)
- summarising a critical source instead of using it to support analysis of a specific moment (Component 1 Section C)

THE FOCUS AREA EACH SET FILM CARRIES

Each set film has a core focus area in addition to the universal toolkit (cinematography / mise-en-scene / editing / sound / narrative / genre / representation / context).

US FILM COMPARATIVE STUDY (Unit 2)
- Focus: integrated study with emphasis on GENRE, NARRATIVE and CONTEXT.
- Each pair sits in one genre (horror, musical, romantic comedy, teen film, science fiction).
- Comparative method: matched moments — pick a comparable narrative beat in each film and analyse how the same beat is staged differently because of when each film was made.

US INDEPENDENT FILM (Unit 3)
- Focus: SPECIALIST WRITING ON FILM (film criticism / cinematography writing / film review).
- Three sources are set every three years on the WJEC secure website: one source on cinematography, one source on US independent film, one film review (one set per independent film option).
- The platform teaches the SKILL of applying a critical source generically — quote a 5-10 word fragment, name the writer, then use it to support analysis of a specific moment in the chosen film. The actual sources rotate and live on the WJEC secure website; students access them via their teacher.

GLOBAL ENGLISH-LANGUAGE FILM (Unit 4 part 1)
- Focus: NARRATIVE.
- How the chosen film tells its story: structure (linear / non-linear / frame narrative / flashback), cause and effect, ellipsis, withholding/releasing, three-act conventions, plot points, character function.

GLOBAL NON-ENGLISH-LANGUAGE FILM (Unit 4 part 2)
- Focus: REPRESENTATION.
- How the chosen film represents people and ideas — gender, ethnicity, age, culture. Where applicable at GCSE: stereotype and counter-type, who is looking at whom in a frame, how representation positions the spectator.

CONTEMPORARY UK FILM (Unit 4 part 3)
- Focus: AESTHETIC QUALITIES OF FILM.
- Distinctive look, signature framing, palette, production design, the spectacle-vs-narrative balance, what is typical or atypical of the film's style.

QUOTATION AND COPYRIGHT DISCIPLINE

NEVER reproduce more than 15 words of dialogue from any film still in copyright (any film not made before 1956), and only once per lesson, in quotation marks. Better to paraphrase the situation than to quote.

NEVER paraphrase the plot scene-by-scene. Reference moments by name only:
- GOOD: "the closing dance number"
- GOOD: "the boat-arrival sequence"
- GOOD: "the 'how can I miss you...' confrontation in the diner" (descriptive label, no quoted dialogue)
- BAD: a 5-paragraph synopsis
- BAD: "and then he says '[long quote]' and she says '[long quote]'"

NEVER reproduce critical writing from copyrighted study guides (Studio Canal pedagogical packs, the Eduqas Resources hub, any third-party study guide). Reference only neutral factual sources for production context: Wikipedia, BFI, Britannica, IMDb (facts only), the films' theatrical releases. The specification itself is the only authority on content scope, and even the specification's wording must not be reproduced verbatim.

NEVER ingest or paraphrase Eduqas/WJEC mark schemes, examiner reports beyond their general findings, or past papers. The build relies on the spec for content scope only and produces all examples, analysis and questions as original work.

Public-domain status by set film:
- Likely public domain in the UK (films released >70 years before 2026 — i.e. before 1956): none of the current set films qualify. Dracula (1931) is the earliest and remains in copyright in the UK under the 70-years-after-author's-death rule (Tod Browning died 1962, so Dracula enters UK PD in 2033). Treat ALL set films as in copyright.
- All other set films IN COPYRIGHT — strict 15-word quotation cap, paraphrase preferred, never reproduce plot.

THE FREE-TIER, NO-IMAGE-OR-CLIPS PROBLEM

Free-tier Film Studies has no still images and no clips. Prose must do the dual-coding work itself: describe shots specifically and spatially so students build mental images they can then revisit when studying.

GOOD (specific, spatial, technical):
"The shot opens on a low-angle close-up of the protagonist's face, the camera tilted up so that he fills the upper third of the frame. Hard side-lighting carves a deep shadow down one cheek; the rest of the room sinks into low-key gloom."

BAD (vague, generic):
"There is a close-up of the character that is moody and dark."

Every set-film lesson should describe at least three named sequences in this specific, spatial, technical register.

WHAT A SET-FILM LESSON MUST CONTAIN

Each set-film lesson should include:

1. A 1-2 sentence orientation — what the film is, who made it, when, where (NOT a plot summary).
2. Production context — director, year, country, principal cinematographer/editor/composer where notable, audience, critical reception in 1-2 lines. From the unit-level teaching brief.
3. The lesson's micro/macro topic anchored to the set film — e.g. "cinematography in [film]", "narrative structure in [film]", "representation in [film]".
4. THREE TO FIVE NAMED SEQUENCES the lesson will draw on, each labelled descriptively (e.g. "the closing dance number on the platform", "the ladder shoot-out", "the boat-arrival sequence") — set up each in one sentence, then spend the rest of the time on filmic choices within it.
5. Worked examples that explicitly link MICRO choices (a specific shot, cut, lighting state, sound design choice) to MACRO meaning (theme, genre, representation, narrative function).
6. Practice questions whose stems ask about specific filmic choices on specific named sequences — never plot-recall.
7. At least 6 inline glossary terms (`<dfn class="term">`) — Film Studies is technical-vocabulary heavy.
8. Glossary array entries for 6+ terms.
9. A revision-style key-fact box and at least two collapsibles per the lesson template.

---

WHAT A SET-FILM LESSON MUST NOT CONTAIN

- No scene-by-scene plot summary.
- No reproduced dialogue beyond 15 words across the whole lesson, and at most one quotation block.
- No reference to specific Eduqas/WJEC question stems, paper codes, component codes, section letters, mark scheme tier names, "Award N marks for", "Level 1/2/3", or "Nothing worthy of credit".
- No reproduction of editorial notes, critical interpretations or page numbers from any third-party study guide.
- No real production references for Unit 3 worked examples that exceed factual production-context (e.g. "filmed in 16mm by [DOP] at [studio]" is fine; "[critic A] argued that [film] subverted the indie aesthetic" is NOT — that's reproduced critical writing).
- No images, no clips, no embedded video — free tier is text-only. Do not reference visual material that is not present.
