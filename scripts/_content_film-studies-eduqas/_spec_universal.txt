UNIVERSAL FILM-FORM SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (WJEC Eduqas GCSE Film Studies C670QS / WJEC 3670QS, version 7, May 2025)

This slice covers the parts of Section 2.1 "Subject content" that apply UNIVERSALLY to every set film, regardless of which option a learner chooses. It is the toolkit lessons in Unit 1 ("Film Form and Language") draw on, and which Unit 5 ("Key Developments in Film and Film Technology") returns to in historical-development form.

Note: the spec is identical for English Eduqas centres (C670QS) and Welsh WJEC centres (3670QS). Lessons must work for both audiences. Do not write "in England" or "in Wales" framing.

---

CORE STUDY AREAS (apply to every film)

Two core areas apply to every film studied across both written components:

1. Key elements of film form
2. The contexts of film

The ability to analyse how the key elements of film form create meaning — both in isolation and together — is a core skill in Film Studies at all levels. AO2 (analyse and apply) is 50% of the qualification.

---

1. KEY ELEMENTS OF FILM FORM

CINEMATOGRAPHY (including lighting)
- camera shots: extreme close-up, close-up, medium shot, long shot, extreme long shot
- camera angles: high angle, low angle, 60-degree (off-centre) angles, tilt and aerial shots
- camera movement: pan, tracking, dolly, Steadicam
- lighting: position and intensity; high-key vs low-key; side, back, top and under-lighting; chiaroscuro; motivated vs unmotivated; natural vs artificial; the colour and warmth of light
- framing: created through distance, angle, level and mobility; how framing directs spectator attention
- the typical meanings associated with each cinematographic choice
- how cinematography contributes to the portrayal of characters and settings
- how cinematography combines with mise-en-scene or sound to underline meaning
- how each choice generates spectator responses

MISE-EN-SCENE
- settings: built sets, location shooting, real-world environments
- props: functional, symbolic, signature
- costume and make-up: period, character signifier, transformation
- the typical meanings associated with each aspect
- how mise-en-scene conventionally conveys realism
- how settings contribute to the themes and issues a film raises
- how props contribute to character and/or narrative development
- how costume and make-up convey character
- how each aspect generates spectator responses

EDITING
- types of edit: cut, dissolve, fade, jump cut, match cut, shot-reverse-shot
- principles of continuity editing (180-degree rule, eye-line match, axis of action)
- cross-cutting / parallel editing
- pace of editing (slow, fast, accelerating)
- visual effects created in post-production
- the typical meanings associated with each editing choice
- how continuity editing establishes relationships between characters
- how cross-cutting contributes to the portrayal of character and tension
- how editing contributes to narrative development
- how editing, including visual effects, generates spectator responses

SOUND
- diegetic sound: dialogue, ambient sound, sound effects, sounds within the world of the film
- non-diegetic sound: score, voice-over, sound effects added in post-production
- sound bridges across cuts
- the typical meanings associated with diegetic sound, including how it contributes to character and narrative
- the typical meanings associated with non-diegetic sound, including how music motifs convey character and how different musical styles contribute to narrative
- how non-diegetic sound contributes to the themes and issues a film raises
- how sound (diegetic or non-diegetic) generates spectator responses

2. THE CONTEXTS OF FILM

Films reflect and respond to the contexts in which they are made:
- social context (aspects of society and its structure reflected in the film)
- cultural context (ways of living, beliefs and values of groups of people reflected in the film)
- historical context (society and culture at the time when films are made and, where relevant, when they are set)
- political context (political issues, when relevant)
- technological context (technologies reflected in production and, in some cases, narrative)
- institutional context (how films are funded, how budget affects the kind of film made, the main stages of film production)
- key aspects of the history of film and film technology

Learners must be introduced to the most significant developments in the history of film and film technology, including at least:
- the first moving images and silent film
- the rise of Hollywood and the development of sound
- the introduction of colour film
- the emergence of widescreen technology and 3D film
- the development of portable cameras and Steadicam technology
- the role of computer-generated imagery in film
- digital cinematography, streaming and citizen filmmaking (added to the timeline for assessment from 2024)

Lessons in Unit 5 cover the historical sweep. Lessons in Units 2-4 (set-film units) anchor a film to its institutional and historical moment in the production-context section of the lesson.

3. NARRATIVE (structural element of film form)

Narrative is studied as a focus area for the global English-language film (Component 2 Section A). Universal concepts:

- the distinction between plot and story (fabula and syuzhet)
- the role of the screenplay in conveying plot
- the role of character and character function in narrative
- cause and effect as a structural principle of narrative
- narrative techniques: ellipsis, withholding/releasing information, frame narrative, non-linear structure
- narrative conventions in screenwriting: three-act structure, plot points, inciting incident, climax, resolution
- interpretation of the narrative (themes and issues raised)
- how narrative generates spectator responses

GCSE-appropriate narrative theorists used sparingly:
- Tzvetan Todorov (equilibrium / disruption / new equilibrium)
- Vladimir Propp (character functions: hero, villain, donor, helper, princess, dispatcher, false hero)
- David Bordwell (narration as the formal organisation of story information)

Use named theory once a lesson at most, applied to a named moment, never as a whole-lesson framework.

---

4. GENRE (structural element of film form)

Genre is studied as a focus area for the US film comparative study (Component 1 Section A). Universal concepts:

- the concept of genre
- conventions of genre based on iconography, including mise-en-scene, characters, narratives and themes
- the significance of genre to film producers and film audiences
- genre cycles and genre evolution (e.g. how horror in 1931 differs from horror in 1987)
- generic hybrids and subversion

---

5. REPRESENTATION

Representation is the focus area for the global non-English-language film (Component 2 Section B). Universal concepts:

- films are never simply "pictures of reality" — they always offer points of view
- representations of gender, ethnicity, age and culture
- stereotype and counter-type
- spectator positioning by representation choices
- where appropriate at GCSE level: Mulvey's gaze theory (used at GCSE-appropriate framing — i.e. who is looking at whom in a given shot, and what that constructs for the spectator)

---

6. AESTHETIC QUALITIES OF FILM

The aesthetic qualities of film are the focus area for the contemporary UK film (Component 2 Section C). Universal concepts:

- film as an art form
- how all aspects of film form combine artistically
- distinctive "look" or style across a whole film
- spectacle vs narrative — where the aesthetic engages spectators more than the story
- how filmmakers create aesthetic effects through specific film sequences

ASSESSMENT OBJECTIVES

AO1 — Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of film. 30% of qualification.
AO2 — Analyse and apply knowledge and understanding of film. 50% of qualification.
AO3 — Apply knowledge and understanding to film production and screenwriting. 20% of qualification (NEA / coursework only — out of platform scope).

Free-tier revision content covers the 80% written-paper share. Coursework production is the teacher's domain.

COMMAND-WORD TAXONOMY (questions on the written papers)

Short factual questions:
- identify
- name
- list
- give
- state

Define:
- define
- what is meant by

Explain:
- explain how
- explain why
- explain the effect of

Analyse:
- analyse
- explore
- examine

Compare:
- compare
- contrast
- compare and contrast

Evaluate / extended writing:
- evaluate
- discuss
- to what extent
- how successfully
- justify

Practice question stems used by the platform mirror this taxonomy. They never reproduce verbatim Eduqas/WJEC question wording.
