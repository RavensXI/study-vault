## HIGH Severity

### Telescopic L10 — Hertzsprung-Russell Diagram and Variable Stars
**Claim:** "Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered in **1908** that the pulsation period is directly related to the star's luminosity (the period-luminosity relation)"

**Verdict: WRONG DATE — HIGH**

Leavitt's 1908 paper (Harvard College Observatory Circular 173) catalogued 1,777 variable stars in the SMC and noted that brighter variables tended to have longer periods, but **did not formally state the period-luminosity law**. The quantitative period-luminosity relationship was published in her **1912** paper (Leavitt & Pickering, Harvard College Observatory Circular 173, 1912). The 1908 publication is the observation that prompted the 1912 law.

**Fix:** Change "discovered in 1908" to "published the period-luminosity relationship in 1912 (first noted the pattern in 1908)".

## MEDIUM Severity

### Telescopic L2 — The Sun: Structure, Fusion and Atmosphere
**Claim:** "The chromosphere extends to about 2,000 km above the photosphere and reaches temperatures of **20,000 K**"

**Verdict: UNDERSTATED — MEDIUM**

The chromosphere's upper boundary temperature before the transition region is commonly cited as **~50,000 K**, not 20,000 K. At ~2,000 km altitude the temperature has already risen far above 20,000 K. NASA and ESA solar physics resources give the upper chromosphere at 50,000 K. Stating 20,000 K significantly undersells the temperature gradient and may confuse students in comparison questions.

**Fix:** "…reaching temperatures of up to approximately 50,000 K at its upper boundary"

### Telescopic L12 — Stellar Evolution: Birth, Life and Death of Stars
**Claim:** "The brightest star in a constellation is labelled with the Greek letter alpha, the second brightest with beta" — followed by the example "beta Orionis (Rigel) is the second-brightest in Orion"

**Verdict: OVERSIMPLIFIED / SELF-CONTRADICTING — MEDIUM**

Bayer's 1603 assignment was roughly by apparent brightness but has many exceptions. The lesson's own example undermines the stated rule: **Rigel (β Orionis, apparent magnitude ~0.13) is actually brighter than Betelgeuse (α Orionis, magnitude ~0.42–1.60 variable)**, making α Ori the dimmer of the two. Rigel is β despite being the brighter star in modern photometry. The rule should be qualified as approximate/historical.

**Fix:** Add a caveat — e.g., "Bayer assigned Greek letters roughly by brightness at the time of his atlas (1603), but many exceptions exist. For example, Rigel (β Ori) is brighter than Betelgeuse (α Ori) in modern measurements."

### Telescopic L13 — The Milky Way and Other Galaxies
**Claim (key-fact box):** "The Milky Way is an **SBc** — a loose-armed barred spiral"  
(Main text earlier states the Milky Way is classified as **SBb**)

**Verdict: INTERNAL CONTRADICTION / CLASSIFICATION ERROR — MEDIUM**

The lesson contains two different Hubble types for the Milky Way within the same lesson: SBb in the galaxy-types list and SBc in the closing key-fact box. The majority of peer-reviewed sources classify the Milky Way as **SBbc** (intermediate, e.g. Rix & Bovy 2013) or SBb (e.g., earlier studies). SBc implies looser spiral arms than current structural models support. SBb or SBbc is the consensus.

**Fix:** Standardise both occurrences to **SBbc** (or SBb) and remove the contradictory SBc.
