TITLE: Reading & Analysing an Unseen Poem
SLUG: reading-and-analysing-an-unseen-poem  LESSON_NUMBER: 1  STATUS: live

--- DESCRIPTION ---
A step-by-step method for approaching any unseen poem with confidence in the exam.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">What Is the Unseen Poetry Section?</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Section C of AQA English Literature Paper 2 tests your ability to analyse poetry you have never seen before. Unlike the anthology section, you cannot prepare specific quotations or contextual knowledge. Instead, you must demonstrate your skills as a reader: your ability to identify what a poem is about, analyse how the poet creates meaning through language, structure, and form, and respond to the poem with your own interpretation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n3" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: How many questions are in Section C, what are their numbers, mark values, and suggested timings?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Section C contains <strong>two questions</strong>. Question 27 asks you to analyse a single unseen poem and is worth <strong>24 marks</strong>. Question 28 asks you to compare the first poem with a second unseen poem and is worth <strong>8 marks</strong>. You should spend approximately <strong>25 minutes</strong> on Q27 and <strong>20 minutes</strong> on Q28.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n4">Step 1: Read the Poem Three Times</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n5">The single most important thing you can do is <strong>read the poem carefully</strong>. Many students rush into writing and miss the poem’s meaning entirely. Use the first three minutes to read the poem three times:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n6"><strong>First read:</strong> Get a general sense of the subject, tone, and mood. What is the poem about on the surface? Who is speaking? What is the situation?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7"><strong>Second read:</strong> Look for <dfn class="term" data-def="The underlying ideas, messages, or concepts explored beneath the surface level of a poem. Themes might include love, loss, identity, power, nature, or memory.">themes</dfn> beneath the surface. What deeper ideas is the poet exploring? What feelings does the poem create? Note any shifts in tone or direction.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n8"><strong>Third read:</strong> Begin annotating. Circle key words, underline striking images, note any techniques you recognise. Pay special attention to the opening and closing lines — they often contain the poem’s most important ideas.</p>

  <div class="collapsible-content"><div class="collapsible-inner">
    <p data-narration-id="n9"><strong>Language:</strong> Powerful individual words (especially verbs and adjectives), <dfn class="term" data-def="Language that creates pictures in the reader’s mind, including metaphors, similes, personification, and sensory descriptions.">imagery</dfn> (metaphors, similes, personification), sound devices (alliteration, sibilance, onomatopoeia), semantic fields (groups of related words).</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n10"><strong>Structure:</strong> How the poem is organised. Does the mood shift? Is there a turning point (<dfn class="term" data-def="A shift or turning point in a poem where the tone, argument, or direction changes. In a sonnet, the volta traditionally occurs between the octave and sestet.">volta</dfn>)? Does the poem build towards a climax or fade away? How does the ending relate to the beginning?</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n11"><strong>Form:</strong> The overall shape of the poem. Is it a sonnet? Free verse? Regular stanzas? Does it rhyme? What effect do the poet’s choices about line length, stanza breaks, and rhythm create?</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n12">Step 2: Identify the Poem’s Message</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n13">Before you write, summarise the poem’s message in one sentence. This keeps your response focused. For example: “The poet presents memory as something beautiful but painful, using natural imagery to show how the past fades over time.” Every paragraph you write should connect back to this central interpretation.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n14" data-revision-tip="Cover this box and recall: Which two Assessment Objectives does AQA assess for unseen poetry and what is each worth in marks?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>AQA assesses unseen poetry across two Assessment Objectives: <strong>AO1</strong> (12 marks) — informed personal response with textual references; <strong>AO2</strong> (12 marks) — analysis of language, form, and structure. There are <strong>no marks for context</strong> (AO3) in the unseen section — focus entirely on your own interpretation and analysis of the poet’s methods.</p>
</div>



<h2 data-narration-id="n15">Step 3: Write Your Response</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n16">Structure your response in <strong>three paragraphs</strong>, each analysing a different aspect of the poem. The <dfn class="term" data-def="A structure for analysis paragraphs: What (identify the technique or feature), How (quote and analyse the specific words), Why (explain the effect on the reader and how it connects to the poem’s message).">What-How-Why</dfn> method works well for each paragraph:</p>
<p data-narration-id="n17"><strong>What:</strong> Identify a technique or feature the poet uses (e.g. “The poet uses a metaphor comparing memory to water”).</p>
<p data-narration-id="n18"><strong>How:</strong> Quote the specific words and analyse them closely. What do individual word choices suggest? What sounds, rhythms, or patterns do you notice?</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19"><strong>Why:</strong> Explain the effect on the reader. How does this technique contribute to the poem’s overall message or mood? This is where your personal interpretation matters most.</p>

    <p data-narration-id="n20"><strong>Example:</strong> The poet presents nature as a powerful but indifferent force. The metaphor “the sea swallowed the shoreline whole” personifies the sea as a predator, with the violent verb “swallowed” suggesting consumption and destruction. The adverb “whole” emphasises the completeness of this erasure — nothing is left. This creates a sense of helplessness in the reader, reinforcing the poem’s message that humanity is insignificant compared to the natural world.</p>
    <p data-narration-id="n21">Notice how this paragraph: (1) makes a clear point about the poem’s message, (2) embeds a quotation, (3) analyses specific word choices (“swallowed,” “whole”), (4) identifies a technique (personification, metaphor), and (5) explains the effect on the reader. This is what Level 5-6 analysis looks like.</p>

</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n22">Common Mistakes</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n23"><strong>Retelling the poem:</strong> The most common mistake is simply describing what happens in the poem. “In the first stanza the poet talks about a bird, then in the second stanza they talk about the sky” — this is summary, not analysis. Always explain the <em>effect</em> of the poet’s choices.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n24"><strong>Feature-spotting:</strong> Listing techniques without analysing their effect (“The poet uses alliteration, a metaphor, and enjambment”) will not earn high marks. You must explain <em>why</em> the poet chose that technique and what it makes the reader feel or understand.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n25"><strong>Ignoring form and structure:</strong> Many students only write about language. The examiners specifically reward analysis of form (what type of poem? regular or irregular? rhyme scheme?) and structure (how the poem develops, shifts in tone, the significance of the opening and ending).</p>

--- EXAM_TIP_HTML ---
<p data-narration-id="n26">For Q27 (24 marks), aim for three well-developed paragraphs in 25 minutes. It is better to write three paragraphs with detailed, close analysis of specific words than five paragraphs with surface-level comments. Quality beats quantity. Always refer to the poet as “the poet” — not “the author” or “the writer.”</p>

--- CONCLUSION_HTML ---
<h3 data-narration-id="n27">Key Takeaways</h3><ul><li data-narration-id="n28">Read the poem three times before writing: first for general meaning, second for themes, third for annotation.</li><li data-narration-id="n29">Summarise the poem’s message in one sentence before you start writing — this keeps your response focused.</li><li data-narration-id="n30">Use the What-How-Why structure for each paragraph: identify a technique, quote and analyse the specific words, then explain the effect on the reader.</li></ul>
