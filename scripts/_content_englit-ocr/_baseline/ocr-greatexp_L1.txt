TITLE: Context: Victorian Class & Social Mobility
SLUG: lesson-01  LESSON_NUMBER: 1  STATUS: live

--- DESCRIPTION ---
Victorian class divisions, social mobility, and Dickens’s critique of the gentleman ideal.

--- CONTENT_HTML ---
<h2 data-narration-id="n1">Dickens and the Class System</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n2">Charles Dickens published <em>Great Expectations</em> as a weekly serial in <em>All the Year Round</em> between December 1860 and August 1861. Victorian Britain was rigidly divided by class. At the top sat the aristocracy and landed gentry — families who inherited wealth. Below them, a growing middle class of merchants and lawyers aspired to climb higher. At the bottom, the working class struggled in conditions Dickens knew firsthand: when he was twelve, his father was imprisoned for debt, and young Charles was forced to work in a <dfn class="term" data-def="A factory producing boot-blacking (shoe polish). Dickens worked in one as a child, shaping his lifelong anger at class injustice.">blacking factory</dfn>.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n3">This autobiography matters. Pip’s shame about his working-class origins, his desperate desire to become a gentleman, and his eventual realisation that wealth does not equal worth all mirror Dickens’s own complicated relationship with class.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n4" data-revision-tip="Cover this and recall: what three social classes existed in Victorian Britain, and how did Dickens’s childhood connect to Pip’s story?">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Victorian society had three rigid tiers: aristocracy/gentry (inherited wealth), middle class (earned wealth), and working class/poor. Dickens drew on his own childhood poverty when creating Pip’s shame about his origins at the forge.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n5">The ‘Gentleman’ Ideal</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n6">In Victorian culture, a <dfn class="term" data-def="A man of good social standing who does not work with his hands. Dickens questions whether these external markers reflect true moral worth.">gentleman</dfn> was defined by wealth, education, manners, and the absence of manual labour. This ideal shaped everything from marriage prospects to legal rights.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n7">Dickens uses <em>Great Expectations</em> to interrogate this ideal ruthlessly. Pip believes becoming a gentleman will make him worthy of Estella’s love. Instead, his ‘great expectations’ make him a snob — ashamed of Joe Gargery, the man who raised him with genuine kindness. The true gentleman of the novel is Joe himself: no wealth, no education, no status, but absolute moral integrity.</p>

<h2 data-narration-id="n9">Social Mobility and Self-Improvement</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n10">Victorian Britain offered limited pathways for <dfn class="term" data-def="The ability to move between social classes, constrained in Victorian Britain by class prejudice.">social mobility</dfn>. Samuel Smiles’s <em>Self-Help</em> (1859) promoted the idea that effort could raise anyone. But Dickens complicates this: Pip’s rise is funded by a convict’s money, and his ‘improvement’ makes him worse — more snobbish, less kind.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n11">Biddy represents genuine self-improvement. She educates herself, becoming a schoolteacher through intelligence and determination, without losing her kindness. Where Pip’s rise corrupts him, Biddy’s enriches her.</p>

<div class="key-fact" data-narration-id="n12" data-revision-tip="Test yourself: list three ways Dickens contrasts Pip’s social mobility with Joe’s and Biddy’s moral worth.">
  <div class="key-fact-label">Key Fact</div>
  <p>Dickens challenges the belief that social climbing equals moral improvement. Pip’s wealth makes him worse. Joe and Biddy — who remain working class — are the novel’s moral centres. True worth comes from character, not class.</p>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n13">Crime, Punishment, and the Law</h2>
<p data-narration-id="n14">The criminal justice system pervades the novel. It opens on the <dfn class="term" data-def="Flat, marshy land near the Thames Estuary in Kent, associated with danger and criminality.">marshes</dfn> near a prison hulk. Magwitch is an escaped prisoner. Jaggers washes his hands after dealing with criminals. Newgate Prison looms over London.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n15">Transportation to <dfn class="term" data-def="British colonies in Australia to which criminals were transported. Magwitch is transported for life.">penal colonies</dfn> was a common sentence. Magwitch is transported for life; returning illegally risks hanging. Dickens exposes the brutal disproportion of Victorian punishment.</p>

Serialised weekly, <em>Great Expectations</em> needed cliffhangers, but beyond entertainment Dickens wanted middle-class readers to question their assumptions. First-person narration forces the reader to share Pip’s shame, ambition, and painful realisation.</p>
  </div></div>
</div>

<h2 data-narration-id="n17">Structure: The <dfn class="term" data-def="A novel following moral and psychological growth from youth to adulthood.">Bildungsroman</dfn></h2>
<p data-narration-id="n18">The novel divides into three stages: Stage 1 (Chs 1–19) — childhood at the forge; Stage 2 (Chs 20–39) — gentleman in London; Stage 3 (Chs 40–59) — disillusionment and moral recovery. The structure mirrors innocence, corruption, and redemption.</p>
<p data-narration-id="n19">Dickens uses first-person retrospective narration. The adult Pip looks back with regret and wisdom, commenting on his younger self’s mistakes even as we experience them.</p>
