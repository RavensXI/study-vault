PAPER 2: SOCIO-CULTURAL INFLUENCES AND WELLBEING — SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (AQA GCSE Physical Education)

Overview
This part of the course concentrates on sports psychology, socio-cultural influences, and health, fitness and wellbeing, with use-of-data skills assessed alongside. Quantitative skills (interpreting tables, bar charts, line graphs and pie charts) are assessed on this paper too.

The four sections are:
- 3.2.1 Sports psychology
- 3.2.2 Socio-cultural influences
- 3.2.3 Health, fitness and wellbeing
- 3.2.4 Use of data (shared with Paper 1; same techniques applied to socio-cultural and wellbeing data)

---

3.2.1 SPORTS PSYCHOLOGY

Students should develop knowledge and understanding of the psychological factors that can affect performers in physical activity and sport.

3.2.1.1 Classification of skills (basic/complex, open/closed)

Definitions of skill and ability.

Basic definitions of the following skill classifications, taught as continuums (not binaries):
- basic / complex
- open / closed
- self-paced / externally paced
- gross / fine

Students should choose and justify the appropriate classifications in relation to sporting examples.

Definitions of types of goals:
- performance goals (personal performance / no social comparison)
- outcome goals (winning / result)

Appropriate performance and/or outcome targets for sporting examples.

3.2.1.2 The use of goal setting and SMART targets to improve and/or optimise performance

The use and evaluation of setting performance and outcome goals in sporting examples.
Performance and outcome goals can be combined; outcome goals should generally be avoided as they rely on factors that cannot be controlled (e.g. other performers). Beginners should avoid outcome goals because failure can demotivate or because winning may be unrealistic.

SMART targets:
- specific
- measurable
- accepted
- realistic
- time-bound

3.2.1.3 Basic information processing

The basic information processing model — role of each stage:
- input — information from the display (senses); selective attention
- decision making — selection of appropriate response from memory; role of long-term and short-term memory
- output — information sent to muscles to carry out the response
- feedback — received via self (intrinsic) and/or others (extrinsic)

Students should be able to draw (in box format) and/or explain the stages of a basic model of information processing, and apply the model to skills from sporting examples.

3.2.1.4 Guidance and feedback on performance

Types of guidance — evaluation of effectiveness for beginners and elite-level performers:
- visual (seeing — e.g. a demonstration)
- verbal (hearing)
- manual (assisting movement physically)
- mechanical (use of objects/aids)

Students should choose and justify which types of guidance are appropriate for beginners and/or elite performers.

Types of feedback — evaluation with specific links to beginners and elite-level performers:
- positive / negative
- knowledge of results / knowledge of performance
- extrinsic / intrinsic

Students should explain what each type of feedback entails and choose and justify which types are appropriate for beginners and/or elite performers.

3.2.1.5 Mental preparation for performance

Definition of arousal.

Inverted-U theory — the shape of the inverted-U placed on a graph with y axis (performance level — low to high) and x axis (arousal level — low to high). Students should be able to draw an inverted-U graph with both axes labelled.

The relationship between arousal level and performance level (under-arousal = low performance; over-arousal = low performance; optimal arousal = peak performance). Link appropriate arousal level (high/low) to gross/fine skills in sporting actions. Link skills (not sports) to an appropriate arousal level (e.g. a tackle in rugby will need a high arousal level).

Stress management techniques to control arousal:
- deep breathing
- mental rehearsal / visualisation / imagery
- positive self-talk

Students should explain how these techniques are carried out, using sporting examples.

Aggression — direct vs indirect:
- direct aggression involves physical contact (e.g. a rugby tackle)
- indirect aggression does not involve physical contact (e.g. a fast bowler aiming a ball)

Personality types:

Characteristics of an introvert:
- shy/quiet
- thoughtful
- enjoy being on their own
Tend to play individual sports when concentration/precision (fine skill) is required and low arousal is required.

Characteristics of an extrovert:
- enjoy interaction with others / sociable / aroused by others
- enthusiastic / talkative
- prone to boredom when isolated
Tend to play team sports when there is a fast pace, concentration may need to be low, and gross skills are used.

Motivation:
- intrinsic motivation — from within (pride / self-satisfaction / personal achievement)
- extrinsic motivation — from another source/person:
  - tangible (certificates, trophies, medals)
  - intangible (praise, feedback, applause)

Evaluation of the merits of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in sport — intrinsic is generally more effective; overuse of extrinsic can undermine intrinsic; the performer can become reliant on extrinsic; intrinsic is more likely to lead to continued effort and participation; extrinsic rewards may result in feelings of pride / self-satisfaction.

3.2.2 SOCIO-CULTURAL INFLUENCES

Students should develop knowledge and understanding of the socio-cultural factors that impact on physical activity and sport, and the impact of sport on society.

3.2.2.1 Engagement patterns of different social groups in physical activity and sport

Engagement patterns can differ between different social groups.

Factors that contribute to engagement patterns in the following social groups:
- gender
- race / religion / culture
- age
- family / friends / peers
- disability

Justifiable links between the following factors and their relevance to engagement patterns:
- role models
- attitudes
- accessibility (to facilities, clubs, activities)
- media coverage
- sexism / stereotyping
- culture / religion / religious festivals
- family commitments
- available leisure time
- familiarity
- education
- socio-economic factors / disposable income
- adaptability / inclusiveness

3.2.2.2 Commercialisation of physical activity and sport

Definition of commercialisation. The relationship between sport, sponsorship and the media (the "golden triangle").

Definitions of sponsorship and the media.

Types of sponsorship:
- financial
- clothing and equipment, including footwear
- facilities

Types of media:
- television
- radio
- the press
- the internet
- social media

Positive and negative impacts of commercialised activity (sponsorship and the media) on:
- performer
- sport
- official
- audience / spectator
- sponsor / company

Students should justify why each impact is positive or negative.

Positive and negative impacts of technology on:
- performer
- sport
- official
- audience / spectator
- sponsor / company

The focus is on technology generically (its uses and impacts) rather than on specific named systems.

3.2.2.3 Ethical and socio-cultural issues in physical activity and sport

Conduct of performers — definitions and sporting examples of:
- etiquette
- sportsmanship
- gamesmanship
- contract to compete

Categories of prohibited substances, including the basic positive effects and negative side effects:
- stimulants
- narcotic analgesics
- anabolic agents
- peptide hormones (EPO)
- diuretics

Prohibited methods (blood doping):
- removal of blood a few weeks prior to competition; blood is frozen and re-injected just before competition
- leads to increased red blood cell count
- benefits — increased oxygen-carrying capacity (relevant to endurance performers)
- side effects — thickening of blood (viscosity); potential infection; potential heart attack; embolism (blockage of vessel)

Drugs subject to certain restrictions — beta blockers:
- reduce heart rate, muscle tension and blood pressure
- reduce the effects of adrenaline
- improve fine control / preciseness
- side effects — nausea, weakness, heart problems
- should be prescribed by a medical professional

Which type of performers may use different types of performance-enhancing drugs (PEDs), with sporting examples:
- stimulants — alertness
- narcotic analgesics — pain killers from over-training
- anabolic agents — muscle mass
- diuretics — lose weight
- peptide hormones — oxygen-carrying capacity
- blood doping — oxygen-carrying capacity
- beta blockers — for fine motor control

Advantages of taking PEDs (for the performer):
- increased chances of success
- fame
- wealth
- a level playing field (where peers are also doping)

Disadvantages of taking PEDs (for the performer):
- cheating / immoral
- associated health risks
- fines
- bans
- reputational damage

Disadvantages to the sport/event:
- damage to reputation
- damage to credibility

Spectator behaviour — positive influences of spectators at matches/events:
- creation of atmosphere
- home-field advantage (for home team / individuals)

Negative influences of spectators at matches/events:
- negative effect on performance as a result of increased pressure
- potential for crowd trouble / hooliganism
- safety costs / concerns
- negative effect on participation numbers among younger performers

Reasons why hooliganism occurs:
- rivalries
- hype
- fuelled by alcohol/drugs
- frustration (e.g. at officials' decisions)
- gang culture
- display of masculinity

Strategies employed to combat hooliganism / spectator behaviour:
- early kick-offs
- all-seater stadia
- segregation of fans
- improved security
- alcohol restrictions
- travel restrictions / banning orders
- education / promotional activity / campaigns and high-profile endorsements

Students should evaluate the effectiveness of these strategies (e.g. high costs of security versus safety of spectators).

3.2.3 HEALTH, FITNESS AND WELLBEING

Students should develop knowledge and understanding of the benefits of participating in physical activity and sport to health, fitness and wellbeing.

3.2.3.1 Physical, emotional and social health, fitness and wellbeing

Reasons for participation in physical activity, exercise and sport, and how performance can increase health, wellbeing and fitness.

Physical health and wellbeing:
- improves heart function
- improves efficiency of the body systems
- reduces the risk of some illness
- able to do everyday tasks
- helps avoid obesity

Mental health and wellbeing:
- reduces stress / tension
- release of feel-good hormones (serotonin)
- able to control emotions

Social health and wellbeing:
- opportunities to socialise / make friends
- cooperation
- teamwork
- helps meet essential human needs (food, shelter, clothing — through community)

Fitness:
- improves fitness
- reduces the chances of injury
- can aid in the physical ability to work (e.g. on your feet all day, manual labour)

3.2.3.2 The consequences of a sedentary lifestyle

Definitions of sedentary and lifestyle.

Possible consequences of a sedentary lifestyle:
- weight gain / obesity
- heart disease
- hypertension
- diabetes
- poor sleep
- poor self-esteem
- lethargy

Definition of obesity. Obesity and how it may affect performance in physical activity and sport:
- limits stamina / cardiovascular endurance
- limits flexibility
- limits agility
- limits speed / power

Causes ill health (physical):
- cancer
- heart disease / heart attacks
- diabetes
- high cholesterol

Causes ill health (mental):
- depression
- loss of confidence

Causes ill health (social):
- inability to socialise
- inability to leave home

Somatotypes — definitions of the following body types:
- endomorph
- mesomorph
- ectomorph

Students should identify the most suitable body type for particular sports (or positions within a sport) and justify their choice.

3.2.3.3 Energy use, diet, nutrition and hydration

Energy is measured in calories (Kcal) and is obtained from the food we eat.
The average adult male requires 2,500 Kcal/day; the average adult female requires 2,000 Kcal/day, but this is dependent upon:
- age
- gender
- height
- energy expenditure (exercise)

Nutrition — reasons for having a balanced diet:
- there is no single food that contains all the nutrients the body needs
- a balanced diet contains lots of different types of food to provide suitable nutrients, vitamins and minerals
- unused energy is stored as fat, which could cause obesity (particularly saturated fat)
- suitable energy can be available for activity
- the body needs nutrients for energy, growth and hydration

Nutrition — role of carbohydrates, fat, protein, vitamins and minerals:
- a balanced diet contains 55–60% carbohydrate, 25–30% fat, 15–20% protein
- carbohydrates are the main and preferred energy source for all types of exercise, of all intensities
- fat is also an energy source — provides more energy than carbohydrates but only at low intensity
- protein is for growth and repair of muscle tissue
- vitamins and minerals are for maintaining the efficient working of body systems and general health (no specific vitamins/minerals required)

Reasons for maintaining water balance (hydration). Definition of dehydration.

Dehydration results in:
- blood thickening (increased viscosity), which slows blood flow
- increases in heart rate / heart has to work harder / irregular heart rate (rhythm)
- increase in body temperature / overheat
- slowing of reactions / increased reaction time / poorer decisions
- muscle fatigue / cramps

Students should understand and evaluate the consequences of dehydration to performance in different sporting activities.

3.2.4 USE OF DATA (shared with Paper 1)

Students should develop knowledge and understanding of data analysis in relation to key areas of physical activity and sport — including socio-cultural participation data, health and wellbeing statistics, and lifestyle data.

Quantitative data deals with numbers. Methods for collecting quantitative data: questionnaires, surveys.
Qualitative data deals with descriptions. Methods for collecting qualitative data: interviews, observations.

How to present data in tables, bar charts and line graphs. How to label x and y axes.

Interpretation of data presented in basic:
- tables
- bar charts
- line graphs
- pie charts

Students should be able to read values from a chart, compare two data points, calculate simple differences or percentages, and explain what the data shows about a sporting or wellbeing situation. Common Paper-2 data scenarios:
- participation rates by gender, age or socio-economic group
- spectator numbers / commercial audience figures
- nutritional intake data and energy expenditure
- BMI / body composition trends in a population

NOTES FOR CONTENT GENERATION

Calculation/data formulae that may appear in this paper:
- Cardiac output (Q) = stroke volume × heart rate (when applied to wellbeing data)
- Maximum heart rate (max HR) = 220 − age (used in wellbeing/fitness contexts)
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100
- Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values
- Range = highest − lowest

Data interpretation rule of thumb on this paper:
- Always compare at least two figures from the data set
- Always state the unit (bpm, %, kg, kcal, participants, % share of media revenue, etc.)
- Always explain what the comparison means in a sporting or wellbeing context — examiners reward the inference, not the figure read.
