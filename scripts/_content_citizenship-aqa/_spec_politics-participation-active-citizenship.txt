UNIT 1: POLITICS, PARTICIPATION AND ACTIVE CITIZENSHIP — SPECIFICATION EXTRACT (AQA GCSE Citizenship Studies)

Overview
This part of the course concentrates on the nature of political power in the UK and the core concepts relating to democracy and government. It covers how government operates at its various levels within the UK, how decisions are made and how the UK Parliament works and carries out its functions. It also looks at the role of political parties, the election system, how other countries govern themselves and how the citizen can bring about political change. Active citizenship is the overarching principle of the qualification — students study how citizens have made a difference (case studies of others) and undertake their own citizenship action investigation.

The sections covered in this unit are:
- 3.1 Citizenship skills, processes and methods (overarching skills, applied across the qualification)
- 3.4 Politics and participation
- 3.5 Active citizenship — the actions of others, plus the student's own investigation

---

3.1 CITIZENSHIP SKILLS, PROCESSES AND METHODS

Through studying GCSE Citizenship Studies students will:

- gain the ability to form their own hypotheses, create sustained and reasoned arguments and reach substantiated conclusions about citizenship issues
- understand the range of methods and approaches that can be used by governments, organisations, groups and individuals to address citizenship issues in society, including practical citizenship actions
- formulate citizenship enquiries, identifying and sequencing research questions to analyse citizenship ideas, issues and debates
- select and organise their knowledge and understanding in responses and analysis, when creating and communicating their own arguments, explaining hypotheses, ideas and different viewpoints and perspectives, countering viewpoints they do not support, giving reasons and justifying conclusions drawn
- present their own and other viewpoints and represent the views of others, in relation to citizenship issues, causes, situations and concepts
- plan practical citizenship actions aimed at delivering a benefit or change for others in society
- critically evaluate the effectiveness of citizenship actions to assess progress towards the intended aims and impact for the individuals, groups and communities affected
- show knowledge and understanding of the relationships between the different citizenship aspects studied, using the concepts to make connections, identify and compare similarities and differences in a range of situations from local to global

3.4 POLITICS AND PARTICIPATION

In this theme students look at the nature of political power in the UK and the core concepts relating to democracy and government. This includes how government operates at its various levels within the UK, how decisions are made and how the UK Parliament works and carries out its functions. It also looks at the role of political parties, the election system, how other countries govern themselves and how the citizen can bring about political change.

3.4.1 Where does political power reside in the UK and how is it controlled?

- The concept of democracy and different forms of democracy, including representative democracy.
- The values underpinning democracy: rights, responsibilities, freedoms, equality, the rule of law.
- The institutions of the British constitution: the power of government, the Prime Minister and cabinet; the sovereignty of Parliament; the roles of the legislature, the opposition, political parties, the Monarch, citizens, the judiciary, the police and the Civil Service.
- How the relationships between the institutions form an uncodified British constitution and examples of how this is changing.

3.4.2 What are the powers of local and devolved government and how can citizens participate?

- The role and structure of elected local government; the services provided by local government for citizens in local communities; roles and accountability of councillors.
- The nature and organisation of regional and devolved government: Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and England.
- How powers are organised between the Westminster Parliament and the devolved administrations in Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales; how relations are changing between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; the debate about "English votes for English laws".
- Who can stand for election and how candidates are selected.
- Who can and cannot vote in elections and why; debates about the voting age.
- Issues relating to voter turnout, voter apathy and suggestions for increasing voter turnout at elections.
- How public taxes are raised and spent by government locally and nationally.
- The practice of budgeting and managing risk and how it is used by government to manage complex decisions about the allocation of public funding.
- Different viewpoints and debates about how governments and other service providers make provision for welfare, health, the elderly and education.

3.4.3 Where does political power reside: with the citizen, parliament or government?

- The nature of the "First Past the Post" system based on parliamentary constituencies; the frequency of Westminster elections.
- Other voting systems used in UK elections, including proportional systems and the advantages and disadvantages of each.
- The difference between the executive, the legislature, the judiciary and the monarchy. The nature of the bicameral Westminster Parliament, the respective roles of and the relationship between the House of Commons and the House of Lords and the role of the monarch.
- The major political parties contesting UK general elections; key philosophical differences between the political parties operating in UK general elections.
- How parliament works: scrutinising government and making it accountable; parliamentary questions, committees, debates.
- The role of MPs; representing their constituencies, debating policy; scrutinising legislation.
- Ceremonial roles including Black Rod; key parliamentary roles including the Speaker, whips, front bench and back bench MPs.
- The legislative process; parliamentary debates and deliberation of public issues and policy.
- The formation of government by the leader of the political party with a majority in the House of Commons, or by a coalition of parties.
- The role of the Prime Minister, cabinet and ministers; the power of the Prime Minister and cabinet.
- The organisation of government administration into departments, ministries and agencies; role of the civil service.

3.4.4 How do others govern themselves?

- Key differences in how citizens can or cannot participate in politics in one democratic and one non-democratic political system that is outside the UK.

3.4.5 How can citizens try to bring about political change?

- How citizens can contribute to parliamentary democracy and hold those in power to account.
- How digital democracy, social media and other measures are being developed as a means to improve voter engagement and the political participation of citizens.
- The different forms of action citizens can take to hold those in power to account for their actions; how the citizen can contribute to public life by joining an interest group or political party: standing for election; campaigning; advocacy; lobbying; petitions; joining a demonstration; volunteering.
- The roles played by public institutions, public services, interest and pressure groups, trade unions, charities and voluntary groups in providing a voice and support for different groups in society.

3.5 ACTIVE CITIZENSHIP

This specification is developed around the overarching principle of how citizens can try to make a difference in society. The three content-based themes (Life in Modern Britain, Rights and Responsibilities, Politics and Participation) develop students' citizenship knowledge base; the active citizenship element enables students to explore through case studies and their own actions how citizens are able to try to make a difference.

3.5.1 Citizenship action — the actions of others

This element draws upon the fifth key question in each of the three content themes:
- How can citizens make their voice heard and make a difference in society? (from Life in Modern Britain)
- How do citizens play a part to bring about change in the legal system? (from Rights and Responsibilities)
- How can citizens try to bring about political change? (from Politics and Participation)

Students should know two contrasting examples of how citizens working together — alone or through groups — have attempted to change or improve their communities through actions to address public policy, challenge injustice or resolve a local community issue. Examples may be local, national or international and should span the political spectrum so students see a balanced range of civic action. Examples in common use include the Hillsborough families' campaign for justice, the Stephen Lawrence campaign and resulting Macpherson Report, the Marcus Rashford free school meals campaign, the disability-rights campaigns leading to the Equality Act, the suffrage movement and the civil rights movement.

3.5.2 The investigation: taking citizenship action

Taking citizenship action is defined as a planned course of informed action to address a citizenship issue or question of concern, aimed at delivering a benefit or change for a particular community or wider society. It applies citizenship knowledge, understanding and skills in a real out-of-classroom context.

The investigation requires students to practise:
- research and enquiry, including interpretation of primary and secondary sources
- planning, collaboration and problem-solving
- advocacy and campaigning
- evaluation of impact

Stages typically followed:
- Stage 1 — Deciding the question or issue. The issue must arise from the specification content; it can be local, national or international.
- Stage 2 — Carrying out initial research using primary and secondary sources.
- Stage 3 — Planning the action, drawing further questions and conclusions from the research.
- Stage 4 — Taking the action. Forms of action include letter writing, petitioning, e-media campaigns, volunteering or establishing a group to promote a change.
- Stage 5 — Assessing the impact of the action, including reflection on approach and outcome.
- Stage 6 — Evaluating the whole process — what went well, what could have been done differently.

The investigation is assessed via questions in the active citizenship section of the qualification and accounts for 15% of total GCSE marks.

---

ASSESSMENT OBJECTIVES (apply to all questions)

- AO1: Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of citizenship concepts, terms, and issues. (30%)
- AO2: Apply knowledge and understanding of citizenship concepts, terms and issues to contexts and actions. (30%)
- AO3: Analyse and evaluate a range of evidence relating to citizenship issues, debates and actions, including different viewpoints, to develop reasoned, coherent arguments and make substantiated judgements. (40%)

AO3 is the largest single AO, which is why levels-based extended-response questions (8-mark Analyse / Discuss, 12-mark Evaluate) carry the most weight. Source-interpretation questions (4 marks) draw on AO2 and AO3.

---

COMMAND-WORD TAXONOMY (the 9 registered question types)

Short answer:
- "1 mark — Multiple Choice" — pick one option from four. Tests recall.
- "2 marks — Identify Two" — name two distinct items. Two qualitatively different points required; near-duplicates score once.
- "2 marks — State" — give a brief defined statement (1-2 lines). Specificity required where the topic demands a specific named item.

Mid-tariff:
- "4 marks — Describe" — give two clearly described features OR two developed points. Avoid evaluation; description only.
- "4 marks — Explain" — point + reason + consequence. A single fully developed point with cause-and-consequence often outscores two undeveloped points.
- "4 marks — Source Interpretation" — interpret a source, supported by direct reference to the source plus the candidate's own knowledge. Reach a substantiated judgement about what the source shows.

Extended response (use StudyVault Mastering / Secure / Developing / Emerging rubric):
- "8 marks — Analyse" — break down a topic, weighing evidence on each side, with a substantiated judgement. Two-sided analysis essential; one-sided caps in Developing.
- "8 marks — Discuss" — present and weigh contrasting viewpoints with connectives (however, on the other hand, in contrast). Naming a specific real-world example (legislation, campaign, court case) lifts the answer into Secure.
- "12 marks — Evaluate" — full two-sided argument with at least one named real-world example per side and a substantiated overall judgement in the conclusion. Phrases like "it depends" without supporting criteria do not earn evaluation marks.

Mark-scheme rule of thumb:
- Always include a named real-world example (an Act, a campaign, a treaty, a named institution) in 8- and 12-mark answers — examiners reward the named anchor.
- Always reach a substantiated judgement in 12-mark Evaluate — list of points without evaluation does not access the top band.
- For 4-mark Source Interpretation — combine the source with own knowledge; pure summary of the source caps in Developing.
