The Berlin Wall: Building a Cold War Symbol

How a wall of concrete became the most visible symbol of a divided Europe.

On 13 August 1961, in the early hours of a Sunday morning, soldiers and police of the German Democratic Republic began rolling out barbed wire across the streets of Berlin. By dawn the city was split. Within weeks, the wire had been replaced with concrete, watchtowers, and a death strip. The Berlin Wall would stand for twenty-eight years and become the most recognisable symbol of the Cold War in Europe.

Why the Wall was built

Between 1949 and 1961, around two and a half million East Germans fled west, most of them through Berlin. Many were young, educated, and skilled — exactly the people the East German economy could not afford to lose. The communist government called this haemorrhage of talent the Republikflucht, or "flight from the republic". Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev and East German leader Walter Ulbricht agreed that something had to stop it.

Closing the border was risky. It would prove, dramatically and in public, that people had to be locked in rather than persuaded to stay. But the alternative was watching East Germany hollow out. In the end, Ulbricht's regime decided the political cost of a wall was lower than the economic cost of continued emigration.

The night the city was split

The operation began at midnight. Roads were torn up. Telephone and S-Bahn links between the two halves of the city were cut. Families who lived on opposite sides of a single street woke up to discover that they could no longer cross to see each other. The Western Allies — Britain, France and the United States — issued protests, but did nothing to physically intervene. Khrushchev had gambled correctly: the West would not start a war over Berlin.

In the months that followed, the wire was upgraded into a concrete wall up to 3.6 metres tall, with a parallel inner wall, anti-vehicle trenches, and a wide patrolled "death strip" between them. Border guards were given orders to shoot anyone trying to cross. Over the wall's lifetime, at least 140 people were killed attempting to escape.

A symbol the West used

For Western politicians the wall was a propaganda gift. When US President John F. Kennedy visited West Berlin in June 1963, he stood beside the wall and declared "Ich bin ein Berliner" — I am a Berliner. The line, broadcast worldwide, made plain that Western democracy stood with the city. The wall framed the Cold War as a moral contest: not just communism versus capitalism, but freedom versus an ideology that needed concrete to hold its own people in.

The wall did achieve what its builders wanted. After 1961, the flood of refugees slowed to a trickle. But the political damage to the East was enormous, and the image of a divided Berlin would shape Western thinking about the Soviet bloc for the rest of the Cold War — right up to the moment, on 9 November 1989, when the wall came down.
