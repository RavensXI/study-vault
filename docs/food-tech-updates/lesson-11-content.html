Why Do We Cook Food?

Cooking is one of the most important processes in food preparation. We cook food for several reasons: to make it safe by killing harmful bacteria, to improve its flavour and texture, to increase its shelf life, and to add variety to our diet. Raw flour, for example, is inedible — but once baked into bread, it becomes a staple food.

Cooking changes the appearance of food (browning of bread, colour change in meat), its smell (aromas released during roasting), its texture (softening of vegetables, crisping of pastry), and its palatability — how pleasant food is to eat. Some nutrients are also affected: water-soluble vitamins like B and C can be lost through heating and dissolving into cooking water.

Key Fact

We cook food to make it safe (killing bacteria above 75°C), improve flavour and texture, extend shelf life, increase variety, and make nutrients more accessible. However, cooking can also destroy heat-sensitive vitamins such as vitamin C and B1 (thiamin).

Heat Transfer in Cooking

Heat is transferred to food in three ways: conduction, convection, and radiation.

Conduction is the transfer of heat through direct contact. When you heat a saucepan on a hob, heat passes from the metal base of the pan into the food touching it. Metals are good conductors — this is why pans are made from stainless steel or copper. The food itself also conducts heat inward, from the outer layers to the centre.

Convection occurs in liquids and gases. When you heat a pan of sauce, the liquid at the bottom warms up, becomes less dense, and rises. Cooler liquid sinks to take its place, creating a convection current. This is how an oven heats food too — hot air circulates around the food. A fan oven speeds up convection by forcing air to move faster.

Radiation does not require direct contact. Heat travels as infrared waves from a heat source to the food. Grilling and toasting use radiation — the grill element radiates heat directly onto the surface of the food. Radiation only heats the surface, so food must be turned or the interior is cooked by conduction from the outside in.

Key Fact

When you heat a saucepan of sauce on a hob, all three methods of heat transfer are at work: conduction passes heat from the hob through the metal pan into the sauce touching the base; convection circulates the sauce as warm liquid rises and cooler liquid sinks; radiation from the hob’s element emits infrared energy to the pan’s base.

Cooking Methods

Boiling — food is submerged in water at 100°C. Fast but can destroy water-soluble vitamins. Used for pasta, rice, and vegetables.

Simmering — liquid is kept just below boiling point (around 85–95°C). Gentler than boiling, used for soups, stews, and sauces.

Poaching — food is cooked in liquid at a low temperature (around 70–80°C). Ideal for delicate foods like eggs and fish. No added fat, so it is a healthier method.

Steaming — food is cooked by steam rising from boiling water below. The food does not touch the water, so fewer water-soluble vitamins are lost. Excellent for vegetables and fish.

Blanching — food is briefly plunged into boiling water and then transferred to iced water to stop cooking. Used to preserve colour and texture of vegetables before freezing, or to loosen skins (e.g. tomatoes).

Braising — food is first seared at a high temperature and then slow-cooked in a small amount of liquid in a covered dish. Ideal for tough cuts of meat, which become tender over long cooking times.

Baking — food is cooked in an oven by convection. Used for bread, cakes, pastries, and casseroles. No added fat is required (though recipes may include it).

Roasting — similar to baking but usually involves coating the food in fat or oil. Used for meat, poultry, and root vegetables. Produces a crisp, golden exterior through Maillard browning.

Grilling — food is cooked by radiated heat from above (or below on a barbecue). Fat drips away from the food, making it a lower-fat method. Good for meat, fish, and vegetables.

Dry frying — food is cooked in a pan without added fat, relying on the food’s own fat content. Used for spices, nuts, and fatty meats like bacon.

Shallow frying — food is cooked in a small amount of oil or fat in a frying pan. Gives a crisp, golden finish. Used for eggs, fish fillets, and pancakes.

Stir frying — food is cut into small, even pieces and cooked very quickly in a small amount of oil over high heat, stirring constantly. Retains colour, texture, and nutrients because of the short cooking time.

The cooking method you choose affects the nutritive value of food. Water-based methods can leach vitamins B and C into the cooking water. Fat-based methods add extra calories. Steaming and stir frying are generally the best methods for retaining nutrients because of minimal water contact and short cooking times. Using marinades can add flavour and begin to break down protein in meat through acid-based denaturation.

Functional Properties of Proteins

Proteins change their structure when heated, mixed, or combined with acid. These changes are essential in cooking.

Denaturation — heat, acid, or agitation causes protein molecules to unwind from their coiled shape. This is the first stage of cooking protein. For example, when you heat an egg, the clear albumen turns white as the protein denatures.

Coagulation — once denatured, protein molecules bond together and set into a solid. This is why eggs become firm when fried or boiled. Coagulation occurs at around 60–70°C for eggs and is irreversible.

Gluten formation — when wheat flour is mixed with water and kneaded, two proteins (glutenin and gliadin) combine to form gluten. Gluten is elastic and stretchy, which allows bread dough to trap gas bubbles and rise. Without gluten, bread would be flat and crumbly.

Foam formation — when egg whites are whisked, the protein denatures and traps air bubbles, creating a foam. This is used in meringues and soufflés. The foam sets when heated because the protein coagulates around the air bubbles.

Key Fact

Protein has four key functional properties in cooking: denaturation (egg white turning from clear to white), coagulation (egg setting firm when heated), gluten formation (kneading flour and water to make elastic dough), and foam formation (whisking egg whites into stiff peaks for meringue).

Functional Properties of Carbohydrates

Gelatinisation occurs when starch is heated in a liquid. Starch granules absorb water, swell, and eventually burst, releasing starch molecules that thicken the liquid. This is how sauces, custards, and gravies thicken. The ratio of starch to liquid determines the final viscosity — more starch produces a thicker result. Cornflour, for example, produces a smooth, glossy sauce.

Dextrinisation is the browning of starch when exposed to dry heat. When you toast bread, the starch on the surface breaks down into dextrins, producing a golden-brown colour and a slightly sweet flavour. It also occurs on the crust of baked bread and roasted potatoes.

Caramelisation is the browning of sugar when heated above its melting point. Different sugars caramelise at different temperatures — sucrose at around 160°C. The result is a golden-brown colour and a rich, slightly bitter-sweet flavour. Caramelisation is used in crème brûlée, caramel sauce, and caramelised onions.

Functional Properties of Fats and Oils

Shortening — when fat is rubbed into flour, it coats the flour particles and prevents long gluten strands from forming. This creates a “short” (crumbly) texture in pastry and biscuits. Butter, lard, and margarine are common shortening fats.

Aeration — when fat and sugar are creamed together, air is trapped in the mixture. This is the basis of the creaming method used in cakes. The air expands during baking, helping the cake rise.

Plasticity — fats like butter and margarine are plastic at room temperature, meaning they can be spread and shaped. This makes them ideal for rubbing into flour or creaming with sugar. At fridge temperature they are hard; at high temperatures they melt completely.

Emulsification — fats and water do not normally mix, but an emulsifier can hold them together in a stable mixture called an emulsion. Lecithin in egg yolk is a natural emulsifier. It holds oil and vinegar together in mayonnaise, and butter and lemon juice together in hollandaise sauce.

Key Fact

Fats have four functional properties: shortening (creating crumbly pastry), aeration (trapping air when creaming), plasticity (being spreadable at room temperature), and emulsification (holding oil and water together, as in mayonnaise). Lecithin in egg yolk is the key emulsifier.

Fruit and Vegetables: Enzymic Browning and Oxidation

Enzymic browning occurs when the flesh of cut fruit or vegetables is exposed to oxygen. Enzymes in the cells react with oxygen in the air, producing brown compounds. Apples, bananas, potatoes, and avocados are all susceptible.

You can prevent enzymic browning by: adding lemon juice (the acid deactivates the enzyme), blanching (heat destroys the enzyme), keeping the food submerged in water (blocking oxygen contact), or wrapping tightly in cling film.

Oxidation also causes the loss of vitamins — particularly vitamin C — when food is cut, heated, or stored for long periods. To minimise vitamin loss, prepare fruit and vegetables just before cooking, use a sharp knife (less cell damage), and cook for the shortest time possible.

Raising Agents

Raising agents introduce gas into doughs and batters, making baked products light and airy. There are four types: chemical, mechanical, steam, and biological.

Chemical raising agents produce carbon dioxide (CO&sub2;) when they react with heat and/or moisture. Bicarbonate of soda (sodium bicarbonate) needs an acid (such as lemon juice or buttermilk) to react. Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda plus an acid (cream of tartar), so it reacts with just moisture and heat. Self-raising flour has baking powder already mixed in.

Mechanical raising agents incorporate air through physical action. Whisking (egg whites for meringue), beating (batter), creaming (fat and sugar), sieving (flour), folding (gentle mixing to keep air in), and rubbing in (fat into flour) all trap air bubbles that expand when heated.

Steam — when water in a mixture turns to steam during baking, it expands and pushes the mixture upward. This is the main raising agent in choux pastry, Yorkshire puddings, and puff pastry.

Biological raising agents — yeast is a living organism that feeds on sugar through fermentation, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol. The CO&sub2; makes bread dough rise. Yeast works best at around 25–30°C and is killed above 55°C.

Key Fact

The four types of raising agent are: chemical (baking powder, bicarbonate of soda), mechanical (whisking, creaming, sieving), steam (choux pastry, Yorkshire puddings), and biological (yeast in bread). Chemical agents produce CO&sub2;; mechanical methods trap air; steam expands water; yeast ferments sugar.
