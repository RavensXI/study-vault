What Is Harmony?

Harmony refers to the combination of notes sounding together at the same time. When you hear a chord being played on a piano or guitar, you are hearing harmony. In the Eduqas GCSE Music listening exam, you need to identify chords, describe chord progressions, and explain how harmony creates mood and atmosphere.

Harmony is closely linked to tonality. While harmony is about which chords are used, tonality is about the overall key — whether the music sounds major or minor, and which note feels like “home.”

Primary and Secondary Chords

Every major or minor key has seven chords built on each degree of the scale. These are labelled with Roman numerals. The three most important are the primary chords:

  • Chord I (the tonic) — built on the first note of the scale. This is the “home” chord.
  • Chord IV (the subdominant) — built on the fourth note.
  • Chord V (the dominant) — built on the fifth note.

For example, in the key of G major the primary chords are G (I), C (IV) and D (V). In a major key, all three primary chords are major chords.

Secondary chords are built on the remaining degrees of the scale: ii, iii, vi and vii. In a major key, chords ii, iii and vi are minor, while chord vii is diminished. Pop and rock music often uses chord vi heavily — for example, the chorus of Africa by Toto uses the progression vi–IV–I–V (F#m–D–A–E in A major), which is a very common pop chord pattern.

Key Fact

In a major key, primary chords (I, IV, V) are all major. The most commonly used secondary chords in pop music are ii (minor), iii (minor), and vi (minor). Chord vii is diminished and is rarely used on its own in popular music.

Upper-case Roman numerals (I, IV, V) indicate major chords. Lower-case numerals (ii, iii, vi) indicate minor chords. A small circle after the numeral (vii°) means the chord is diminished. In the exam, you may see either format. For example, in Africa by Toto, the intro chords in B major are written as ♮VII–vi–ii (A–G#m–C#m). The natural sign (♮) before VII tells you the chord has been flattened from what the key signature would normally give.

Chord Progressions in Popular Music

A chord progression is the order in which chords are played. Many pop songs are built on short, repeating chord loops. Here are some of the most common progressions you will hear:

  • I–V–vi–IV — an extremely common “four-chord” pop progression.
  • vi–IV–I–V — the same four chords starting on the minor chord. This is the progression used in the chorus of Africa by Toto (F#m–D–A–E in A major).
  • I–IV–V — the simplest primary-chord-only progression, common in rock and blues.

The rate at which chords change is called the harmonic rhythm. In the verse sections of Africa, the harmonic rhythm is mostly one chord per bar. In the chorus, the same one-chord-per-bar pattern continues. Faster harmonic rhythm (more chord changes per bar) can create excitement or tension; slower harmonic rhythm feels more stable and relaxed.

Key Fact

In the chorus of Africa by Toto, the chord progression vi–IV–I–V (F#m–D–A–E) is a typical pop song chord pattern. The first three phrases of the chorus all use this same four-bar loop before the final phrase changes chords to prepare the key shift back to B major.

Power Chords

In rock music, guitarists often use power chords instead of full major or minor chords. A power chord contains only the root and the fifth — there is no third. Because the third is the note that makes a chord sound major or minor, a power chord is neither. This gives rock music its raw, aggressive sound. Power chords are often played on electric guitar with distortion.

Diatonic and Chromatic Harmony

Diatonic harmony means the chords use only the notes that belong to the key. Most popular music is diatonic. The background notes for Africa by Toto describe the harmony as “diatonic throughout,” meaning the chords stay within the notes of the key in each section.

Chromatic harmony uses notes from outside the key. This can sound surprising, mysterious, or tense. In film music, composers like John Williams use chromatic harmony to create an otherworldly atmosphere — for example, Hedwig’s Theme uses “unusual, non-functional chromatic chords” to create a sense of magic.

A chord is in inversion when a note other than the root is placed in the bass (the lowest sounding note). In Africa, the verse uses several inversions such as B/F# (chord I with the fifth in the bass, written as Ic) and A/E (♮VII with the fifth in the bass, written as ♮VIIc). Inversions create smoother bass lines and more interesting movement between chords.

Tonality — Major and Minor Keys

Tonality describes the key of a piece — whether it sounds bright and happy (major) or dark and sad (minor). You need to be able to identify tonality by ear in the listening exam.

  • Major — sounds bright, positive, confident. Example: the chorus of Africa is in A major.
  • Minor — sounds dark, sad, mysterious. Often used in horror film music to build tension.

Some pieces change key during the music. This is called modulation. In Africa by Toto, the majority of the song is in B major, but the choruses all shift to A major. This modulation gives the chorus a different, brighter feel compared to the verses.

Intro/Links (B major): Three chords only — ♮VII (A), vi (G#m) and ii (C#m). These are presented in two riffs, each lasting two bars. Riff a uses a syncopated rhythm pattern; Riff b uses a pentatonic ostinato over a sustained chord ii.

Verses (B major): A longer 9-bar chord sequence: I–iii–vi–Ic–♮VIIc–IV–vi–♮VII–vi–ii (B–D#m7–G#m7–B/F#–A/E–E/F#–G#m7–A–G#m7–C#m). This repeats with variations. Note the use of chord inversions (B/F#, A/E, E/F#) to create a smooth bass line.

Choruses (A major): A typical pop chord pattern — vi–IV–I–V (F#m–D–A–E). The final phrase alters to vi–IV–I–iii–V–vi to prepare the shift back to B major for the next section.

Cadences

A cadence is a pair of chords that marks the end of a musical phrase. Cadences function like punctuation in language — they tell the listener whether a musical sentence has finished, paused, or been interrupted. You must be able to identify four types of cadence by ear for the listening exam.

Perfect cadence (V → I): This is the strongest ending in music. It moves from the dominant chord to the tonic chord, creating a powerful sense of resolution and finality. Think of it as a full stop. Almost every piece of tonal music ends with a perfect cadence.

Imperfect cadence (any chord → V): This cadence ends on the dominant chord, which sounds unfinished and questioning — like a comma in a sentence. It creates the expectation that more music will follow. Any chord can lead into an imperfect cadence, but the most common progressions are I → V and ii → V.

Plagal cadence (IV → I): Sometimes called the “Amen” cadence because it is the progression heard at the end of many hymns when the congregation sings “Amen.” It moves from the subdominant to the tonic and sounds gentle, warm, and complete — but less dramatic than a perfect cadence.

Interrupted cadence (V → vi): This is the surprise cadence. The listener expects chord V to resolve to chord I (as in a perfect cadence), but instead it moves to chord vi (the relative minor). The effect is unexpected and can sound poignant, dramatic, or even humorous depending on the context.

Key Fact

Perfect (V → I) = finished, like a full stop. Imperfect (any → V) = unfinished, like a comma. Plagal (IV → I) = gentle ending, the “Amen” cadence. Interrupted (V → vi) = surprise, the unexpected twist. In the exam, listen for phrase endings and ask: does it sound finished (perfect or plagal), unfinished (imperfect), or surprising (interrupted)?

Listen for the moments where a musical phrase feels like it reaches a resting point. Then ask yourself: does the phrase sound finished (like the end of a sentence) or unfinished (like a pause before continuing)? If it sounds finished and strong, it is likely a perfect cadence (V → I). If it sounds finished but softer and more gentle, it could be a plagal cadence (IV → I). If it sounds unfinished and you feel the music wants to continue, it is probably an imperfect cadence (ending on V). If it sounds like a surprise — you expected the phrase to end but it took an unexpected turn — it is likely an interrupted cadence (V → vi).

Modulation in Detail

You already know that modulation means changing key during a piece. Now let’s look at the two most common types of modulation you need to recognise in the exam.

Modulation to the dominant: This means moving from the home key to the key built on the fifth note of the scale. For example, from C major to G major, or from D major to A major. This is the most common modulation in Western music. It creates a brighter, more energised feel because the new key is closely related to the original. In sonata form and binary form, music often modulates to the dominant in the first section.

Modulation to the relative minor: This means moving from a major key to its relative minor — the minor key that shares the same key signature. For example, from C major to A minor, or from G major to E minor. This modulation creates a darker, more melancholic mood while remaining harmonically close to the original key.

How do you spot a modulation? Listen for three clues: (1) new accidentals — sharps or flats that were not in the original key, (2) a new tonal centre — a different note now feels like “home,” and (3) cadences in the new key — if you hear a perfect cadence (V → I) and the “I” chord is in a different key from where the piece started, a modulation has occurred.

Key Fact

The two most common modulations: (1) to the dominant (e.g. C major → G major) — sounds brighter; (2) to the relative minor (e.g. C major → A minor) — sounds darker. Spot modulation by listening for new accidentals, a shift in tonal centre, and cadences in the new key.

Other Tonality Terms You Need to Know

The exam may test you on several related tonality terms beyond major and minor:

  • Atonal — music with no sense of key at all. It sounds unsettled and is often used in horror film scores.
  • Modal — music based on modes (scales from medieval music) rather than standard major or minor scales. Modal music has a distinctive, sometimes ancient-sounding quality.
  • Pentatonic — based on a five-note scale. In Africa, Riff b in the intro uses the E major pentatonic scale. Pentatonic melodies and harmonies are common in African, Chinese, and folk music traditions.
Key Fact

In Africa by Toto, the tonality shifts between B major (verses, intro, links, instrumental, outro) and A major (all three choruses). The harmony is described as diatonic throughout, meaning all chords stay within the notes of whichever key the section is in.
