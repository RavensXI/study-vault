What Are Musical Devices?

Musical devices are techniques that composers use to develop, decorate, and add interest to their music. In the Eduqas GCSE Music specification for AoS1, you need to know six key devices: sequence, ostinato, imitation, pedal, drone, and canon. These devices appear across Baroque, Classical, and Romantic music — the whole span of the Western Classical Tradition (1650–1910) that AoS1 covers.

Being able to identify these devices by ear and describe how they are used is essential for the listening exam. You should also consider using them in your own compositions, as the free composition briefs for AoS1 specifically ask you to “make use of sequence” and other devices.

Sequence

A sequence is when a musical pattern — usually a short melodic phrase — is immediately repeated at a higher or lower pitch. If the pattern moves up in pitch each time, it is called an ascending sequence. If it moves down, it is a descending sequence.

Sequences are very common in Baroque music. In Vivaldi’s Winter from The Four Seasons, the solo violin uses ascending sequences during the second section to portray “biting, stinging winds.” The motif is heard three times, each time at a higher pitch, creating a sense of rising intensity. Sequences help composers extend a short idea into a longer passage without introducing completely new material.

Key Fact

A sequence repeats a melodic or rhythmic pattern at a different pitch level. Listen for the same shape of notes stepping up or stepping down. In the exam, state whether the sequence is ascending or descending and give a bar number if you can.

Ostinato

An ostinato is a short musical pattern — melodic, rhythmic, or both — that repeats continuously throughout a section or an entire piece. It acts like an anchor, giving the music a strong sense of pulse and unity while other parts change above or around it.

A famous example is the introduction to Toto’s Africa, where “Riff b” (bars 3–4) uses an ostinato pattern of five notes based on the E major pentatonic scale over a sustained chord ii (C♯m). The ostinato groupings cut across the 2/2 time signature, creating syncopation rooted in African musical traditions. In the Baroque period, an ostinato bass line (sometimes called a ground bass) was a common feature, with a repeating bass pattern supporting changing harmonies and melodies above.

In popular music, a repeated pattern is often called a riff. In classical and exam contexts, the correct term is ostinato. They describe a very similar idea — a short, catchy pattern that loops — but “ostinato” is the term the exam expects. A riff might only appear in certain sections (like a guitar riff in the chorus), whereas a true ostinato tends to repeat persistently throughout a section or the whole piece.

Imitation

Imitation is when a musical idea is heard in one part and then copied closely — but not exactly — by another part a short time later. Think of it as one instrument “echoing” what another has just played. The copy usually enters before the first part has finished, so the two parts overlap.

Imitation is a key feature of contrapuntal texture, which was especially important in the Baroque era (c.1600–1750). Composers like Bach used imitation extensively in fugues, where a subject is introduced in one voice and then imitated by other voices entering one by one. In the listening exam, if you hear a melody appear in one instrument and then a similar version starting shortly after in a different instrument, you are likely hearing imitation.

Pedal

A pedal (also called a pedal note or pedal point) is a note that is sustained or continuously repeated, usually in the bass, while the harmonies and melodies above it change freely. The most common pedal notes are the tonic pedal (based on the home note of the key) and the dominant pedal (based on the fifth note of the key).

In Vivaldi’s Winter, a tonic pedal appears in the opening section as the orchestral strings create a cold, still atmosphere. The held bass note grounds the harmony while the upper parts create gentle dissonance above it. A dominant pedal is often used to build tension before a final cadence, because it creates anticipation that the music will resolve back to the tonic.

Key Fact

A pedal note is usually in the bass, but it can also appear in the highest part — this is called an inverted pedal. In the exam, always state which type of pedal you hear (tonic or dominant) and where it is (bass or upper part).

Drone

A drone is one or more sustained notes that sound continuously beneath a melody. Unlike a pedal, which is typically a single note in the bass that underpins changing harmony, a drone usually involves two notes (often the tonic and the dominant) and tends to last for very long stretches of music — sometimes an entire piece.

Drones are commonly associated with folk music traditions, such as the sound of bagpipes, where a continuous low note hums while the melody is played on top. In Western classical music, drones appear at the beginnings and endings of pieces to establish the key firmly. Think of a drone as a constant, unchanging sound bed that gives the music a sense of stability or even monotony.

Students often confuse pedal and drone because both involve sustained notes. Here is a simple way to tell them apart. A pedal is a single sustained or repeated note (usually tonic or dominant) in the bass, and the harmonies above it change — sometimes clashing with the pedal note. A drone usually involves two sustained notes (tonic and dominant together) and typically lasts much longer — sometimes the whole piece. If the held note is short-lived and the harmony moves around it, call it a pedal. If it is continuous and unchanging throughout, call it a drone.

Canon

A canon is a device where two or more parts play exactly the same melody but start at different times, so they overlap. The most well-known example is Frère Jacques — when sung as a round, each voice enters with the same tune a few bars apart. That is a canon.

In classical music, Pachelbel’s Canon in D is the most famous example. Three violin parts play the same melody, each entering a few bars after the previous one, over a repeating bass line (an ostinato). Canon creates a rich, layered texture because multiple versions of the melody sound simultaneously. It is closely related to imitation, but the key difference is that in a canon the melody is copied exactly, whereas in imitation the copy may be altered slightly.

Both canon and imitation involve one part copying another, but they are not the same thing. In a canon, the second part copies the melody exactly — note for note, rhythm for rhythm. In imitation, the second part copies the idea closely but not identically — it may change some notes, intervals, or rhythms. If the exam asks you to identify which device is being used, listen carefully: is the copy exact or approximate?

Repetition and Contrast

Repetition is the most fundamental compositional tool in music. When a composer restates a musical idea — whether it is a melody, a rhythm, or a chord pattern — the listener recognises it and feels a sense of unity and familiarity. Without repetition, music would sound random and impossible to follow.

Contrast is the opposite: introducing something new and different. This might be a change of key, texture, dynamics, rhythm, or melodic character. Contrast creates variety and keeps the listener’s attention.

The two devices work hand in hand. Too much repetition and the music becomes boring. Too much contrast and the music sounds incoherent — like a series of unrelated ideas. Every well-crafted piece of music balances repetition and contrast. In binary form (AB), section A is stated and section B provides contrast. In ternary form (ABA), the return of A uses repetition to frame the contrasting B section.

Key Fact

Repetition creates unity and familiarity. Contrast creates variety and interest. Together, they form the backbone of every musical structure. In the exam, you can describe how a composer uses repetition to unify a piece and contrast to keep it engaging.

Broken Chord, Arpeggio and Alberti Bass

A broken chord is created when the notes of a chord are played separately rather than all at once. Instead of striking all three (or four) notes together, the performer plays them one at a time. This creates a flowing, rippling effect often used in accompaniments.

An arpeggio is a specific type of broken chord where the notes are played in order — from the bottom note to the top (or top to bottom). Think of it as running up or down the notes of a chord like a ladder.

The Alberti bass is a very specific broken chord pattern that was hugely popular in Classical-era keyboard music. Named after the Italian composer Domenico Alberti (c.1710–1740), it plays the notes of a chord in the pattern: bottom – top – middle – top. For example, if the chord is C major (C–E–G), the Alberti bass plays C–G–E–G, C–G–E–G, over and over in the left hand.

Mozart and Haydn used the Alberti bass constantly in their piano sonatas. It provides a gentle, flowing harmonic accompaniment beneath a singing melody in the right hand. If you hear a rippling left-hand pattern in a Classical keyboard piece, it is very likely an Alberti bass.

Key Fact

Broken chord = notes of a chord played separately. Arpeggio = broken chord played in order (low to high or high to low). Alberti bass = a specific pattern of bottom–top–middle–top, common in Mozart and Haydn piano music. In the exam, listen for a repeating left-hand pattern beneath a melody — if it follows the bottom–top–middle–top shape, call it an Alberti bass.

Regular Phrasing and Motifs

Regular phrasing means the melody is organised into balanced phrases of equal length — most commonly 4-bar or 8-bar phrases. These phrases often come in pairs known as antecedent and consequent (or question and answer). The first phrase ends on an unfinished-sounding chord (like the dominant), and the second phrase responds by finishing on the tonic — giving a sense of completion.

Regular phrasing is characteristic of Classical-era music (Mozart, Haydn, Beethoven) and gives the music a sense of order and elegance.

A melodic motif is a short, memorable melodic idea — sometimes just two or three notes — that forms the building block of a composition. A rhythmic motif is a short rhythmic pattern that serves the same function. Motifs are the DNA of a composition: composers repeat them, vary them, put them in sequence, invert them (turn them upside down), and combine them in new ways to build an entire piece from a tiny seed.

Beethoven’s Symphony No. 5 is the classic example: the famous four-note motif (short–short–short–long) drives the entire first movement, appearing in different keys, dynamics, and instruments throughout.

Key Fact

Regular phrasing = balanced 4-bar or 8-bar phrases, often in question-and-answer pairs. A motif is a short musical idea (melodic or rhythmic) used as a building block. Composers develop motifs through repetition, sequence, inversion, variation, and augmentation/diminution. In the exam, if a short idea keeps reappearing in different forms, call it a motif.

How These Devices Appear Across the Eras

The six devices are not limited to one era. However, some are more closely associated with particular periods:

Baroque (c.1600–1750)

Imitation, Sequence, Pedal, Canon, Ostinato

Contrapuntal texture was central to Baroque music, so imitation and canon were extremely common. Composers like Bach and Vivaldi used sequences to develop melodic ideas, pedal notes (especially tonic pedals) to anchor harmonies, and ground bass ostinatos to unify whole movements.

Classical (c.1750–1820)

Sequence, Pedal

Classical composers like Mozart and Haydn favoured homophonic texture and balanced phrases, but they still used sequences to extend melodic ideas and dominant pedals to build tension before cadences. Imitation appears less frequently but is still present in development sections.

Romantic (c.1820–1900)

Pedal, Ostinato, Sequence

Romantic composers used pedal notes and ostinatos to create dramatic tension and sustain emotional moods. Sequences were used alongside chromaticism to build intensity. Drones appear in pieces influenced by folk music traditions.

Spotting Devices in the Listening Exam

In the Eduqas listening exam, you may be asked to identify a device by name, give a bar number where it occurs, or explain how a device is used to create a particular effect. Here is a quick checklist for each device:

Key Fact

Quick identification guide: Sequence — same pattern, different pitch. Ostinato — short pattern repeating over and over. Imitation — one part echoes another closely but not exactly. Pedal — one held or repeated bass note while harmonies change. Drone — continuous sustained notes (often two) throughout. Canon — exact copy of a melody starting at a different time.
