Four Roles, Every Ensemble

In AoS2, you need to understand not just which instruments are in an ensemble, but what each instrument is doing. Every ensemble — whether it is a string quartet or a big band — divides the musical work into four key roles:

  • Melody — plays the lead part or the main tune.
  • Accompaniment — may play countermelodies, imitation lines, or rhythmic ideas that support the melody.
  • Harmonic support — fills in inner parts of chords, giving the music its harmonic colour.
  • Rhythm section — keeps time and sets the groove.
Key Fact

The same instrument can take on more than one role within a piece. For example, the piano in a piano trio can play the melody with the right hand while providing harmonic support and rhythmic drive with the left hand.

Let’s look at how these four roles are shared across the four ensembles covered in this unit: the string quartet, wind quintet, piano trio and big band.

String Quartet

The string quartet comprises four instruments: 1st violin, 2nd violin, viola and cello. Because all four instruments belong to the same family, they blend together closely, but each has a distinct role.

Melody: The 1st violin often plays the main tune. It sits in the highest register, so it naturally cuts through the texture.

Accompaniment: The 2nd violin and viola may provide countermelodies, rhythmic figures, or imitation of the melody.

Harmonic support: The viola and cello fill in the inner parts of chords, giving the music richness and depth.

Rhythm section: There is no dedicated rhythm section. Instead, the pulse is maintained through articulation and bowing techniques such as pizzicato or tremolo.

Wind Quintet

The wind quintet comprises five instruments: flute, oboe, clarinet, French horn and bassoon. Unlike the string quartet, these instruments come from different families (woodwind and brass), so they produce a wider range of timbres.

Melody: The flute, oboe and clarinet often share melodic ideas, passing the tune between them.

Accompaniment: The bassoon and French horn provide countermelodies and harmonic lines underneath the melody.

Harmonic support: The French horn, bassoon and clarinet fill chords and inner parts, adding warmth to the harmony.

Rhythm section: There is no dedicated rhythm section. The pulse is maintained through articulation and phrasing — for example, staccato quavers in the bassoon can create a sense of rhythmic drive.

Piano Trio

The piano trio comprises three instruments: piano, violin and cello. The piano is unique because it can play melody, chords and bass all at the same time, making this a very flexible ensemble.

Melody: The violin is the main melodic instrument. The cello sometimes takes the tune, and the piano’s right hand may also share melodic ideas.

Accompaniment: The piano’s left hand and the cello provide broken chords and rhythmic figures beneath the melody.

Harmonic support: The piano’s left hand and the cello underpin the harmonies, filling in the bass register.

Rhythm section: There is no dedicated rhythm section. Piano articulation — such as repeated chords or arpeggiated patterns — maintains the pulse.

Big Band

The big band is much larger than the other ensembles studied in this unit. It consists of a large horn section and a rhythm section.

The horn section typically includes:

  • Saxophones: 2 alto, 2 tenor, 1 baritone
  • Trombones: 3 tenor, 1 bass
  • Trumpets: 4 (sometimes including a flugelhorn)

The rhythm section typically includes drum kit, electric guitar, bass guitar (or double bass), and piano.

Key Fact

In a big band, the melody is usually played by the lead trumpet or lead alto saxophone. Other horns often double or harmonise the melody, or play riffs and stabs in a call-and-response style.

Melody: The lead trumpet and lead alto sax often carry the main tune. Other horns may double the melody or provide harmonised versions of it.

Accompaniment: Other horns play stabs, riffs, or call-and-response figures behind the lead.

Harmonic support: The piano, guitar and trombones reinforce the harmonic structure by playing chords and filling inner parts.

Rhythm section: The drums, bass, guitar and piano maintain tempo and groove. This is the first ensemble we have studied with a dedicated rhythm section.

Sonatas

A sonata is a composition written for one or two instruments. In its most common form, a sonata is for a solo instrument plus keyboard (such as a violin sonata with piano accompaniment) or for piano solo. The term has been used across the Baroque, Classical, and Romantic eras, though the structure and style evolved over time.

In the Baroque era, the trio sonata was one of the most important chamber music forms. Despite its name, a trio sonata actually involves three or four players: two melody instruments (such as two violins, or a flute and an oboe) plus the basso continuo (harpsichord and cello). The “trio” refers to the three written parts (two melody lines and one bass line), not the number of performers.

In the Classical and Romantic eras, the sonata became a work for a solo instrument with piano, or for piano alone. The roles were clearly defined: the soloist carries the melody and provides expressive, virtuosic playing, while the keyboard provides harmonic support, accompaniment patterns, and sometimes shares melodic ideas. Mozart, Beethoven, and Chopin all wrote famous piano sonatas and sonatas for violin and piano.

Key Fact

Sonata = a composition for 1–2 instruments (solo instrument + keyboard, or piano alone). Baroque trio sonata = 2 melody instruments + basso continuo (3–4 players, despite “trio” in the name). In the exam, remember that “trio sonata” does not mean three players — it means three written parts.

Expression and Communication in Ensembles

Knowing the roles is only half the picture. For the exam, you also need to explain how ensembles use the elements of music to communicate emotions and moods. Here is how some key elements are used expressively:

  • Dynamics: A sudden shift from piano (quiet) to fortissimo (very loud) can create shock or drama. A gradual crescendo builds tension and anticipation.
  • Tempo: A fast tempo can suggest excitement or anger, while a slow tempo often creates sadness or tenderness.
  • Articulation: Staccato notes can feel playful or agitated; legato playing sounds smooth and lyrical, often evoking love or calm.
  • Melody: A rising, conjunct (stepwise) melody may feel hopeful. A falling, disjunct (leaping) melody can feel unsettled or dramatic.
  • Harmony: Major keys and consonant chords suggest happiness. Minor keys and dissonant chords suggest sadness, fear or tension.
  • Texture: A thin texture (e.g. a solo instrument) can feel vulnerable. A thick, layered texture creates power and grandeur.
Key Fact

In the exam, always describe how the parts interact, not just what each instrument is doing individually. AoS2 is about Music for Ensemble, so the examiner wants to see that you understand how musicians work together to create an effect.

For example, in Defying Gravity by Stephen Schwartz (from the musical Wicked), the orchestra builds drama through a crescendo, thickening texture and rising vocal melody. In Prologue by Stephen Sondheim (from Into the Woods), anticipation is created through repeated rhythmic patterns, a gradual build in dynamics, and the layering of different vocal parts.

In chamber ensembles (string quartet, wind quintet, piano trio), there is no dedicated rhythm section. The pulse is maintained through articulation, bowing techniques, or the piano’s rhythmic patterns. Roles are flexible — instruments often swap between melody and accompaniment.

In a big band, the rhythm section (drums, bass, guitar, piano) is a permanent, dedicated part of the ensemble. The horn section is also divided into clearly defined groups (saxophones, trumpets, trombones) that often play together in homophonic blocks of harmony.
